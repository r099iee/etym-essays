The plural anglers continues one of the oldest fishing words in English, rooted in the hook itself. Its ancestor is reconstructed PIE *h₂enk- meaning bent or curved, source of a large family of hook and angle words: Greek ankylos crooked, Latin angulus corner and ancus bent, Old Norse angi point, and Germanic *angulaz meaning hook and by extension fishhook. The semantic logic is purely visual: a bent piece of bone, thorn or metal that catches in the mouth, and the sharp bend or corner it forms.

In Proto-Germanic *angulaz denoted both the implement and the shape — hook, barb, angle, corner — a polysemy still visible in modern angle as both fishing and geometry. Old English inherited it as angel, glossed specifically as fishhook in Ælfric and other sources, distinct from hōc hook in general. The fisherman was originally anglere in Middle English, formed with the agent suffix -ere from the hook noun, first attested c. 1300 in the sense one who fishes with an angle. Early citations already contrast the anglere with net-fishers and spear-fishers, establishing angling as hook-and-line fishing as opposed to netting, trapping or stupefying — an irony in this passage, where the Tahitian fish are gathered by hand after poisoning yet still said to be caught at the anglers will, using anglers loosely for fishermen in general.

Sound changes are regular and well documented:

  • PIE to Germanic: PIE *h₂enk- yields Germanic *ang- by loss of laryngeal with a-colouring and Grimm devoicing patterns; the -ul- extension forms the instrumental noun *angulaz hook.
  • Old to Middle English: OE angel (pronounced angel with hard g) develops to ME angel, angle for the hook and anglere for the person; French influence does not alter the native stem.
  • Early Modern stabilisation: spelling settles as angle for hook and corner and angler for the fisherman; pronunciation of angler as /ˈæŋɡlə/ with hard g before l preserves the Germanic consonant, unlike the softened angel messenger borrowed from Greek via Latin.
  • Plural formation: regular -s plural anglers from the 16th century onward; no umlaut or irregularity, unlike fishermen which retains the -men plural.

Semantic drift shows narrowing, broadening and cultural elevation in turn. Old and Middle English used angler neutrally for any hook-fisherman, often poor or subsistence. Izaak Walton Compleat Angler (1653) transformed the word socially, making the angler a contemplative, genteel sportsman and turning angling into a moralised pastoral pastime. Nineteenth-century sport codified coarse angling versus game angling, fly-angling, and sea-angling, with clubs, licences and literature. In parallel, American English broadened angler to commercial hook-and-line fishers, while biology borrowed it for anglerfish, whose dorsal lure resembles a rod and bait — a metaphorical extension from human technique to animal anatomy.

Socio-cultural contexts reinforced the word. Medieval sumptuary and poaching laws distinguished anglers with rods (often permitted) from netters (often restricted), giving the hook a legal privilege. Victorian muscular Christianity celebrated angling as patient masculine virtue. Twentieth-century tourism made the angler a holiday figure, and modern conservation discourse recasts anglers as stakeholders in river ecology, catch-and-release ethics and invasive-species control. The passage here predates that sport ethic, using anglers in the older generic sense of men who get fish by any artifice, including narcotics.

Literary attestations anchor each stage:

  • Old English glosses (c. 1000): angel glosses Latin hamus hook, establishing the fishing implement.
  • Middle English (c. 1390s): Chaucerian and de Deguileville-adjacent angling metaphors for souls hooked by preaching.
  • Treatyse of Fysshynge wyth an Angle (1496, Wynkyn de Worde): first English fishing manual, fixing angle as rod, line and hook together.
  • Walton (1653) and Cotton (1676): canonising the contemplative angler and the technical vocabulary of flies, baits and rivers.
  • Modern compounds: anglerfish (19th c.), angling club, angling licence, angling match (19th–20th c.), and figurative angling for compliments, angling for votes, where the hook becomes a metaphor for elicitation.

The plural in this card — anglers will — is thus historically loose: strictly, an angler uses hook and line, not plant poison, yet the author extends it to any fishermen gathering stupefied fish at will. The extension is licensed by the older generic sense fisherman and by the possessive idiom at will, meaning at pleasure or discretion, which here stresses effortless gathering of floating, drunk fish.
