Delacroix primarily designates Ferdinand Victor Eugène Delacroix (1798–1863), widely regarded as the supreme master and standard-bearer of French Romanticism in painting. Rising in fiery opposition to the rigid, sculptural Neoclassicism championed by Jacques-Louis David and Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, Delacroix established an aesthetic revolution that privileged expressive color, visible and vibrating brushstrokes, psychological turbulence, and exotic, emotionally charged subject matter. His iconic canvases—such as The Barque of Dante (1822), The Massacre at Chios (1824), The Death of Sardanapalus (1827), and Liberty Leading the People (1830)—radically altered the trajectory of Western visual culture, directly anticipating Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, and modern abstraction.

In addition to identifying the historical artist himself, the name Delacroix functions polysemously across art criticism, aesthetics, literature, and social discourse through several distinct extensions:

  • Metonymic Designation for Artworks: In museum curatorship, auction houses, and art historical prose, a Delacroix denotes any original oil painting, watercolor, lithograph, or sketchbook produced by his hand (e.g., "The Louvre acquired a previously uncatalogued Delacroix depicting an Arab horseman").
  • Aesthetic Archetype of Romantic Colorism: Within aesthetic philosophy and art theory, the name serves as the definitive symbol for color-driven, emotionally liberated painting (*le coloris*). Delacroix stands as the 19th-century heir to the Venetian colorists (Titian, Veronese) and Flemish Baroque masters (Peter Paul Rubens). Historically, it forms the pole of the famous 19th-century artistic feud: the expressive *Delacroix* colorists versus the linear, austere *Ingres* classicists.
  • Emblem of Political and Republican Liberty: Because of his world-famous allegorical masterpiece La Liberté guidant le peuple (commemorating the July Revolution of 1830), Delacroix became permanently entwined in French civic identity, postage, currency, and republican iconography with revolutionary fervor, barricade heroism, and artistic freedom.
  • Literary and Critical Modernity: In literary circles, particularly through the influential art criticism of Charles Baudelaire (in Curiosités esthétiques), Delacroix was canonized as the archetypal "modern" genius—a melancholic, fiercely intellectual creator who translated human suffering, poetic grandeur, and spiritual anguish into pigment.
  • Color Theory and Technical Precursor: In optical theory and technical painting treatises, Delacroix is cited for his pioneering use of complementary color juxtaposition, optical blending, and colored shadows (learned in part from John Constable and his own North African notebooks), which directly instructed Claude Monet, Paul Cézanne, Vincent van Gogh, and Georges Seurat.

Semantic Reasoning and Polysemous Drift: The shift of a proper surname into an aesthetic category and metonym is governed by cultural canonization. Originally an ordinary French toponymic surname meaning "of the Cross" (indicating residence near a wayside cross or calvary), the surname underwent hyper-specialization in the 19th century due to Eugène Delacroix's seismic reputation. Because his style so vehemently challenged the French Academic establishment's doctrine of linear draftsmanship (*le dessin*), his very name became shorthand for passionate chromatic freedom, dramatic movement, and the modern artistic spirit.
