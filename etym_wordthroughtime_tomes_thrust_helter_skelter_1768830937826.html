- 16th century (tome): enters English meaning "a single volume, one bound section of a larger written work," the word remembering its birth as a slice cut from a longer roll
- 17th–18th century: specialization toward size and gravity — a large, heavy, imposing book, a monument rather than a pamphlet
- 19th century: "great tome" as august, dignified weight; the reading-desk word for serious learning
- Modern: still insists on size, age, and authority — a book substantial enough to be a piece of the cosmos

- Middle English (thrusten): enters English meaning "to press, push" — sudden physical force applied in a single directed motion
- 13th–14th century: to pierce, to stab — a sword thrust; the weapon sense dominates
- Figurative extension: to thrust oneself forward, to be thrust upon someone — intrusion and imposition
- Modern: a sudden, deliberate push (noun and verb), and a thrust as a strategic or political drive forward

- 1593 (helter-skelter, first recorded): "in headlong disorder" — a rhyming reduplication built on dialectal skelter, "to scurry, dash"
- 17th–18th century: general confused haste, pell-mell, things scattered in every direction
- 19th century: adjective and adverb of disorder — a helter-skelter life, a headlong careering course
- 20th century: transferred to the fairground's spiral slide — the name itself enacts the dizzy, tumbling rush
