Stage 1 — PIE *lem- "to break; broken, crippled" (reconstructed, per Watkins; the "break/shatter" root). Cognates across the family: Old Church Slavonic lomiti "to break," Russian lomat' "to break, smash," Lithuanian luomas "lame." Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *lama- "weak-limbed; literally 'broken'" (reconstructed; the broken → lame derivation). Old English lama "lame, crippled" (attested) → English lame; Old Norse lami "lame, maimed"; German lahm, Dutch lam "lame." Stage 3 — Old Norse lami "lame, maimed" → Scandinavian dialectal loma "to move slowly, walk heavily" (Swedish dialect loma "to shamble"). Stage 4 — Middle English lomere "to move clumsily" (c. 1300, borrowed from a Scandinavian source) → English to lumber "to move heavily and clumsily" (with an unetymological -b-, cf. humble, nimble). Stage 5 — English noun lumber "heavy, clumsy, useless objects; disused household goods" (1550s; derived from the verb on the notion of awkward bulk). Stage 6 — American English lumber "timber sawn into rough boards or planks for use" (c. 1660s, Massachusetts; a narrowing of the "bulky goods" sense to the sawn timber trade) — this card's sense; the full modern family includes lumber-yard (1777), lumber-mill (1830), lumberjack (1830s), and the back-formed verb to lumber "to cut forest trees" (1809). NOTE ON THE LOMBARD THREAD (debated/nuanced): A well-known but UNPROVEN folk-historical association links the "junk" sense of lumber to the Lombard bankers and pawnbrokers of medieval London (Lombard Street), whose shops filled with unredeemed pledges — on this view a lumber-room was a "Lombard-room," a pawnshop. Modern scholarship treats this as an association rather than a derivation: the OED notes that lumber-room is not attested before the noun lumber itself, so the pawnshop story is an attractive guess that grew up around an already existing word. It is noted here as a genuine but rejected or at best secondary strand, not as an etymon. NOTE ON ROOT/ANTI-MERGE: The PIE "timber" family (tree, timber < *deru- "wood") is deliberately EXCLUDED: it never reaches lumber, and including it would falsely merge two unrelated roots. No Romance or Indo-Iranian reflex of *lem- survives in the documented record; the Germanic *lama- and Balto-Slavic cognates shown above are the attested family. The dating order is attested: verb (c. 1300) → British noun "clutter" (1550s) → American noun "sawn timber" (c. 1660s).
