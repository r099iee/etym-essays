• invective: Similar because it names the core of a philippic, language of violent abuse aimed at a person or cause; differs because invective is a mass noun for the abusive style itself, whereas a philippic is a countable, composed speech with a beginning, argument and peroration.
  • diatribe: Similar because both are long, hostile and rhetorical; differs because a diatribe is often monologic and peevish, and need not be delivered in public or aimed at a named political opponent, while a philippic implies a public figure and a formal occasion.
  • tirade: Similar because it denotes a prolonged outburst of denunciation; differs because a tirade is uncontrolled and emotional, whereas a philippic is crafted, allusive and meant to persuade an audience.
  • harangue: Similar because both are speeches of attack delivered to a crowd; differs because a harangue is loud and bullying, often improvised, and carries no suggestion of classical eloquence.
  • denunciation: Similar because it is the act of publicly condemning someone; differs because it is neutral and general, covering a single sentence or a formal charge, and lacks the literary and rhetorical flavour of philippic.
  • polemic: Similar because it is a hostile argument in speech or writing; differs because a polemic attacks a doctrine or position rather than a person, and it is typically written rather than declaimed.
  • jeremiad: Similar because it is a long, impassioned denunciation, often with a moralising tone; differs because a jeremiad laments and prophesies disaster, whereas a philippic accuses and prosecutes.
  • broadside: Similar because it is a fierce public attack, originally a printed sheet; differs because it is essentially a printed text, often brief and anonymous, without the oratorical architecture of a philippic.
  • screed: Similar because it can mean a long, vehement piece of writing; differs because a screed is dismissive and implies tedium or incoherence, while a philippic implies skill.
  • Catilinarian: Similar because it names another set of Cicero's speeches of accusation; differs because the Catilinarian orations were delivered in the Senate against a domestic conspirator, while the Philippics were aimed at a rival commander and were partly written rather than spoken.
