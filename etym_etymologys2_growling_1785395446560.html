Imitative / expressive Germanic base *gr- (a low, rolling, guttural rumble; sound-symbolic, shared across the West Germanic family by groan, grunt, grumble, and groul; NO secure PIE etymon can be reconstructed — the OED classes the family as words 'formed naturally to express a deep guttural sound') → Middle English groulen (15th century; 'to rumble, to mutter, to growl'; first recorded of thunder and of the belly) → English growl (from the 16th century applied to the threatening throat-sound of dogs and wolves; figurative 'to growl an answer' later) → English growling (present participle, formed with the -ing suffix of continuous action; the sustained, unbroken menace in the text) → GROWLING Note on debate: a Proto-Indo-European etymon is sometimes sought for this imitative family, but no accepted PIE root has been established; the words are best treated as a native Germanic sound-symbolic formation.
