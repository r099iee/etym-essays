Stockade is in its founding sense a defensive barrier or enclosure built of stout upright posts or stakes driven into the ground side by side — a palisade wall ringed around a fort, settlement, prison camp, or cattle pen.

In the source fragment — He opened the ______, but the instant they were — the word wears this enclosing sense mid-action: a gated barrier being opened, with captives, animals, or defenders pressing at the threshold. The sentence breaks off at the moment of release, and stockade supplies the creaking gate, the sharpened posts, and the held breath before the rush.

Core meaning in detail: The classic stockade is a ring of logs or squared timbers, eight to twelve feet high, sharpened or loopholed at the top, often backed by an earth bank and fronted by a ditch, with one or two heavy gates. Roman legions threw up a vallum of stakes nightly on campaign; American frontier forts, Spanish colonial presidios, South African laagers of wagons and posts, and Civil War prison pens all reuse the design, because upright timber is the cheapest wall an axe-wielding garrison can raise. Building one is communal muscle-work — felling, trimming, hauling, tamping — and defending one is a siege in miniature.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Palisade fort or defended enclosure (primary, from 17th c.): frontier forts, trading posts, and besieged settlements ringed with posts; Stevenson’s blockhouse stockade in Treasure Island is the type specimen.
  • Military prison or prison camp (American, from 19th c.): Andersonville’s infamous Confederate pen, modern brigs — to be in the stockade is to be locked behind military posts. The bridge is the enclosure turned inward: the same wall that keeps raiders out keeps prisoners in.
  • Cattle pen or animal enclosure (ranching): stockaded corrals for sorting herds. The bridge is function — posts that hold bodies — scaled from soldiers to steers.
  • Any formidable barrier of uprights (extended): a stockade of spears, of masts, of skyscrapers. The bridge is visual metaphor: anything tall, close-set, and bristling borrows the fort’s menace.
  • To stockade — to fence with posts (verb): settlers stockaded the village against raids. The bridge is the ordinary noun-to-verb promotion of tools and structures.
  • Log-house or blockhouse quarters (loose frontier use): occasionally the buildings inside the wall share the name. The bridge is whole-for-part: the enclosure lends its name to what it encloses.

Register and connotation: stockade smells of pine sap, gunpowder, and westerns — heroic when it shelters pioneers, grim when it pens prisoners. Treasure Island gilds it with adventure; Andersonville stains it with suffering. As metaphor it is unyielding: a stockade of silence, of lawyers, of objections. Whether keeping danger out or inmates in, the word always draws the same line in the earth — posts up, sides chosen.
