mortifying descends from the Latin word for death made into a Christian verb for killing — and then released into surgery and society. Every stage keeps the corpse audible.

It begins in PIE *mer-, to die, to disappear, to wither, one of the darkest and most widespread roots: Sanskrit mriyate (dies), mrta (dead), Avestan miryeite, Greek brotos (mortal, with mb- from *mr-), ambrotos (immortal, whence ambrosia), Latin mori (to die), mors, mortis (death), mortalis (mortal), immortalis, Russian umeret', Old English morth (murder), morthor, modern murder, mortal, mortuary, mortgage (a death-pledge). The extended noun *morti- gives directly Latin mors. From the same root English also keeps mere in mermaid? No — but it keeps murder and mortal as everyday reminders that mer- still kills in plain sight.

  • Late Latin mortificare (3rd–5th c.): early Christian writers, translating St Paul's Greek nekroō (put to death) and thanatoō, coin morti-ficare — death-make — from mors, mortis plus -ficare (to make, from facere, PIE *dheh₁- to do, put). Jerome's Vulgate commands mortificate membra vestra (mortify your members, Colossians 3:5): kill lust, greed, and pride as one would kill a limb. Monastic rules elaborate mortificatio carnis — fasting, vigils, scourging, silence — as the technology of holiness. Phonologically mortificare keeps classical mort- intact; the suffix -ficare with short i regularly weakens in Romance.
  • Old French mortifier and Middle English mortifien (12th–14th c.): crusading surgeons and confessor-priests carry the verb north. Old French mortifier already shows the fateful doubling: to mortifier la char is both to afflict the flesh ascetically and to describe flesh that is dying of a wound. Middle English borrows both: Chaucerian surgery speaks of mortified membres (gangrened limbs), while devotional prose speaks of mortifiynge of pryde. The present participle mortifying is attested in both registers by 1400, spelled mortefiynge before the Great Vowel Shift fixes modern /ˈmɔːtɪfaɪɪŋ/.
  • Early modern surgery's key term: before antisepsis, every compound fracture, strangulated hernia, frostbite, or ergot-poisoned limb risked mortification: the part grew cold, livid, then black, exhaling a sweet putrid smell as bacteria invaded dead capillaries. Ambroise Paré, Percivall Pott, and John Hunter all write chapters Of Mortification; Pott's 1770s accounts of crushed legs at London hospitals read exactly like Ruskin's grandfather at Croydon — a fall, a contusion against stone, swelling, discoloration, fever, death within days. The hurt's mortifying is therefore not a metaphor but a diagnosis, uttered in a world that had no antibiotics and little safe amputation.
  • From flesh to face (17th–19th c.): as manners grow polite, to be mortified slides from the surgeon's table to the drawing room: pride, like flesh, can die. Restoration comedy already jokes that a wit is mortified by a better jest; by Austen mortification is the standard word for social shame (her spirits were mortified). Evangelical households like Ruskin's, where sermons daily command the mortifying of vanity, accelerate the shift: children learn that to lie is to deserve both the birch and the blush. By the 1880s both senses coexist on the same page of Praeterita — legs mortify, mothers are whipped, girls are pattern pupils — and the reader is expected to hear the corpse in all of them.

Modern medicine has retired mortification to the history shelf in favour of Greek gangrene (an eating) and necrosis (a deadening), leaving mortifying to blushes and memoirs. But Ruskin's sentence preserves the older distribution, when one participle could still describe a dying leg and a dying reputation with equal exactness — because both, to his grandfather's generation, were the wages of a fall.
