In the passage in question, 'He managed with deceptive ease to translate the catastrophes of nature into parables of human nature,' the word 'parables' describes the rhetorical method of the historian Gibbon. A parable is a short narrative constructed on a likeness, told in order to teach a moral or spiritual lesson. Here the author's point is that Gibbon treats natural catastrophes — earthquakes, plagues, storms — not simply as physical events to be chronicled but as moral texts, 'translated,' as one translates from a foreign language, into stories that illuminate human conduct. The adverb phrase 'with deceptive ease' is the key to the tone: it is knowing and lightly ironic, admiring of Gibbon's artfulness, because this translating of disaster into instruction is a feat of literary sleight-of-hand that conceals its own difficulty. The plural 'parables' is significant: each catastrophe becomes its own little instructive tale, and together they form a scripture of human nature written in the language of natural disaster. CORE SENSE AND DEFINITION:
  • Primary sense: a short, simple story used to illustrate a moral or spiritual lesson, typically told in the form of an everyday or realistic scene whose deeper meaning lies hidden in a likeness — the archetypes being the parables of Jesus in the Gospels (the sower, the prodigal son, the good Samaritan).
  • Distinctive feature: the parable works by setting a familiar scene 'beside' a truth (the word's etymology: Greek paraballein, 'to throw beside, to set alongside, to compare'), so that the story and the lesson run parallel and the listener must make the application.
  • Register: religious, didactic, and literary; the word is also used more broadly and loosely of any instructive tale or, as in Gibbon, of the rhetorical reduction of events to moral lessons.
FULL POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • Biblical/religious sense (dominant): one of the stories told by Jesus, or any similar story in religious teaching — a short realistic narrative concealing a moral or spiritual truth (the sower, the mustard seed, the lost sheep, the prodigal son, the good Samaritan).
  • General instructive-tale sense: any simple story told to point a lesson, moral or otherwise; a saying or narrative that illustrates a principle.
  • Rhetorical/literary sense (as in Gibbon): a natural event, historical episode, or circumstance 'read' as an instructive tale — the reduction of raw events to moral texts; 'to translate X into parables of Y' means to convert events into lessons.
  • Loose/figurative sense: anything that serves as an instructive comparison or object-lesson — 'his life is a parable of ambition.'
  • Older derived senses now rare or obsolete: (a) an enigma or dark saying (a sense inherited from the Gospel usage 'without a parable spake he not unto them'); (b) a proverb or common saying — a sense preserved in the Latin parabola and the Romance words for 'word' (Italian parola, French parole, Spanish palabra).
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE (how the senses connect): The word's career is one long metaphor of 'setting beside.' Greek paraballein meant 'to throw beside, to set alongside'; its noun parabolē meant 'a placing beside, a comparison.' In Greek rhetoric the parabolē was an extended simile — a familiar scene set alongside a truth to illuminate it — and this is the sense that the Greek New Testament took up for Jesus' teaching stories. Because the Gospels use the word for stories whose meaning lies hidden in a likeness, 'parable' acquired its modern sense of the short instructive narrative. A parallel Latin development pushed the word toward 'speech, word, talk' (Vulgar Latin parabola 'speech, word'), producing the Romance words for 'word' and the English 'parole'; and a learned re-borrowing of the Greek mathematical sense gave 'parabola,' the conic section, named because its curve is, as it were, 'thrown beside' its diameter. The modern word thus carries all three lines within it: the instructive likeness (religious), the 'word' (Romance), and the geometric curve (learned). CONTEXTUAL READING (Gibbon's sentence in full critical detail): The sentence comes from a critical essay or commentary describing the method of the historian Gibbon. He 'managed with deceptive ease to translate the catastrophes of nature into parables of human nature.' The word is doing precise rhetorical work. The catastrophes of nature — earthquakes, plagues, storms — are events without moral content; to turn them into 'parables of human nature' is to read them as instructive tales about human conduct, as the divines of Gibbon's own sentence on the deluge read earthquakes as punishments for heresy. The word 'parables' imports the whole apparatus of religious instruction into historiography: like Jesus teaching in parables, the historian sets natural disaster beside human nature so that the one illuminates the other. And the phrase 'with deceptive ease' governs the tone: the translation looks effortless — a mere change of frame — but it is in fact a feat of art, a sleight-of-hand that conceals its own difficulty and, perhaps, its own tendentiousness. The plural is pointed: not one parable but many, each catastrophe a separate little instructive tale, together forming a scripture of human nature written in the language of natural disaster. COLLOCATION AND SYNTACTIC BEHAVIOUR:
  • Countable noun: 'a parable,' 'the parables,' 'a parable of/about X.'
  • Religious collocations: 'the parable of the sower,' 'the parable of the prodigal son,' 'to speak in parables,' 'to teach by parables.'
  • Metaphorical collocations: 'a parable of vanity/ambition/decay' — the pattern 'a parable of + [abstract noun]' for an instructive instance.
  • The word is most often plural in religious reference ('the parables of Jesus') and singular in the pattern 'a parable about/of.'
  • Verbs: 'to tell/utter/interpret/expound a parable'; 'to speak in parables' (i.e., cryptically, allusively).
RELATION TO THE REST OF THE WORD FAMILY: Parable descends from Greek parabolē, 'a placing beside, a comparison,' the noun of paraballein, 'to throw beside' (para-, 'beside,' + ballein, 'to throw'). The same Greek verb ballein 'to throw' is the ancestor of a vast English family: symbol and symbolic (Greek symbolon, 'a token thrown together'), problem (problēma, 'a thing thrown before one'), hyperbole (hyperbolē, 'a throwing beyond'), embolism (embolē, 'an insertion'), and even devil (Greek diabolos, 'the slanderer,' the one who 'throws across' accusations). Through Latin the word branched into the Romance words for 'word' (Italian parola, French parole, Spanish palabra, Portuguese palavra) and into English parole; through a separate learned route the Greek mathematical sense gave English parabola and parabolic (17th century). The word's deep root is the PIE *gʷelH-, 'to throw, strike, hurl,' reconstructed for Greek ballein.
