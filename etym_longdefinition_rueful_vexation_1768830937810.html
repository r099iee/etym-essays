rueful and vexation name two poles of emotional experience — a wry, self-aware regret on the one hand, and a sharp, jolting annoyance on the other — that Jack Vance fuses into a single compound state in his sentence: "Across the room was a cage; into this Turjan gazed with rueful vexation." The phrase captures the magician's complex feeling before his flawed creation: regret tinged with irony at what he has made, and a shaking irritation at the whole predicament.

Part 1: RUEFUL — Definition

rueful (adjective): feeling, showing, or expressing sorrow, regret, or repentance, almost always accompanied by a wry, self-aware, or dryly humorous edge. It is the distinct emotional posture of a person who recognizes their own folly or minor misfortune and acknowledges it with a half-smile rather than weeping or raging.

Polysemous Spectrum of RUEFUL

  • 1. Primary Wry Regret (Modern General Usage): expressing mournful regret tempered with subtle humour, self-awareness, or irony. Contextual example: "He gave a rueful shrug as he surveyed the charred remains of his first sourdough loaf." This is the dominant modern sense, and the one Vance uses.
  • 2. Pitiable or Deplorable State (Archaic-Literary Sense): inspiring pity, sorrow, or compassion; wretched or mournful in appearance. Contextual example: "The defeated army presented a rueful spectacle as it marched home through the rain." This sense survives in poetic and historical registers.
  • 3. Regretful over One's Own Fault (Penitential Sense): the original 14th-century sense, full of rue in its archaic meaning of "repentance" — a solemn, often religious sorrow for wrongdoing. It survives in fossilised phrases such as "rue the day."
  • 4. Botanical Homonym and Herbal Association: historically entangled in literature with the bitter herb rue (Ruta graveolens), a medieval emblem of regret and repentance — "the bitter herb of grace." The coincidence enriched the word's melancholy colouring, though the etymologies are unrelated.

Historical Semantic Bridge (RUEFUL)

The word travelled from solemn penitential grief (sense 3) to pitiable wretchedness (sense 2) to the modern wry, self-deprecating regret (sense 1). The bridge is the gradual secularisation and lightening of European emotional vocabulary: as religion's grip on everyday language loosened, the heavy guilt of "full of rue" softened into the knowing half-smile of the modern rueful — grief that has learned to laugh at itself without ceasing to be grief.

Part 2: VEXATION — Definition

vexation (noun): the state of being annoyed, troubled, worried, or irritated; also the cause of such a state — the thing that disturbs or agitates the mind or body. It implies a jolting, shaking quality inherited from its Latin ancestor vexāre, "to shake, jolt, toss about," so that vexation is annoyance that rattles the composure rather than merely pricking it.

Polysemous Spectrum of VEXATION

  • 1. Mental Annoyance and Worry (Primary Modern Sense): the state of being irritated, worried, or troubled. Contextual example: "To her great vexation, she missed the last bus."
  • 2. The Cause of Annoyance: by metonymy, the thing or event that provokes the feeling — "the parking fines were a constant vexation."
  • 3. Physical Affliction or Distress (Archaic-Literary Sense): bodily torment, harassment, or affliction; a "vexation of the spirit" (Ecclesiastes) is any trouble that wears at the soul.
  • 4. Legal Usage — Vexatious Litigation: the formal adjective vexatious describes lawsuits brought to harass or oppress rather than to win a genuine claim; the juridical transfer of "jolting, troubling" from persons to proceedings.
  • 5. Archaic Technical Usage: in old medicine, a vexation could be a troublesome symptom or perturbation; the word retained its sense of shaking disturbance well into the 18th century.

Historical Semantic Bridge (VEXATION)

The word entered English in the 14th century meaning physical torment or harassment — the jolting of a rough cart ride. Over the 16th–18th centuries the sense moved inward: vexation became a settled mental state of annoyance and worry, the mind rattled rather than merely pricked. The modern meaning — an irritation that disturbs composure — preserves the old image of being shaken out of one's calm.

Combined Note

In Vance's sentence the two words work as one emotional portrait: the regret is rueful because it is self-aware and ironic, and it is vexation because it jolts the calm. Together they name the peculiar feeling of a maker disappointed in his own creation — a grief with a half-smile and a trembling irritation underneath.
