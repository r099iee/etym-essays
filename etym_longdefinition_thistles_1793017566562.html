Thistles, the plural of thistle, are prickly herbaceous plants of the daisy family, above all the spear, creeping, and cotton thistles, with spiny winged leaves, bristling purple tufts of florets, and parachutes of thistledown that drift for miles.

In the source sentence they close the weed catalogue beside the nettles: rampant nettles and thistles. The pairing is the classic alliance of the waste place, sting beside prickle, both armed, both shunned, both thriving where nothing tender survives. To walk that ground is to be punished at every step, which is exactly the scene's inhospitable point.

Core meaning in detail: A true thistle stands stiffly upright on winged spiny stems, each leaf tipped and edged with yellow needles, each flower head a tight urn of spines opening into a shaggy crown of crimson-purple florets rich in nectar for bees and goldfinches. After flowering the head ripens into the famous down: hundreds of plumed seeds, each on its own silken parachute, released to float across parishes on the wind. The plant is biennial or perennial, deep-rooted, and fiercely competitive; creeping thistle spreads by underground runners that defeat the hoe, which is why farmers speak the word with such feeling.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Prickly composite weeds, Cirsium, Carduus, and kin (primary sense, from Old English): the source sense, spear thistle in the cornfield corner and creeping thistle in the pasture. The bridge to glory is national: one of these wayside weeds became the badge of Scotland.
  • The national emblem of Scotland: the stylised thistle crowned and mottoed, worn by regiments, stamped on coins, carved over doorways. The bridge is legend: barefoot Norse raiders betrayed by a night-time thistle, or so the story runs, and the weed that saved a kingdom became its honour.
  • Any prickly weed by extension: country speech lends the name to teasels, burrs, and sow-thistles, armed plants of similar temper. The bridge is guilt by spine: whatever pricks like a thistle may be called one.
  • A prickly person or a thorny problem, figuratively: as in a thistle of a committee chairman, or the thistles of a negotiation. The bridge is character: the human or difficulty that wounds every hand laid on it.
  • Thistledown, the seed parachute, metonymically: poets write of thistles drifting silver across autumn fields when they mean the down alone. The bridge runs from plant to seed to weather: the fiercest weed ends as the gentlest floating thing.

Register and connotation: thistles in the plural is usually the farmer's or walker's word, practical and slightly resentful, naming an enemy of socks and stockings. The singular can turn ceremonial in a Scottish context, where the Thistle means honour, regiments, and rugby. Poets split the difference: Burns made the thistle tender without blunting it, and every writer of waste places since has known that nettles sting but thistles hold, the difference between a slap and a grip. Donkeys, famously, are said to relish them, the one compliment the plant's enemies allow.
