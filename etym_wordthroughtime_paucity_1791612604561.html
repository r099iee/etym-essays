- 14th c. – Borrowed into Late Middle English from Old French paucité and Latin paucitas; utilized in theological and scholastic treatises to denote numerical fewness, such as the small number of the elect, the brevity of human lifespan, or the fewness of apostolic companions.
- 15th–16th c. – Retained in Renaissance humanism, legal disputation, and early natural philosophy; used by Sir Thomas More and Elizabethan essayists to characterize the scarcity of reliable witnesses or the fewness of foundational laws.
- 17th–18th c. – Solidified in Enlightenment historiography, scientific treatises, and neoclassical essays (e.g., Francis Bacon, Samuel Johnson, Edward Gibbon); utilized as the dignified, formal opposite of multitude or copiousness to lament the lack of documentary evidence from antiquity.
- 19th c. – Maintained as an elevated literary and forensic standard across Victorian jurisprudence, parliamentary debate, and philosophical critique; widely paired with nouns like evidence, facts, intellectual resources, and sympathy.
- 20th–21st c. – Pervasive in academic peer-reviewed literature, legal opinions, clinical medicine, and art theory (e.g., Rudolf Arnheim's diagnostic critique of the modern 'paucity of ideas'); functions as the quintessential scholarly term for acute empirical or conceptual deficit.
