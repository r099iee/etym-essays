Danes continues one of the oldest Germanic ethnonyms, yet its deepest root is still debated. Proto-Germanic *daniz (plural) is reconstructed from Old Norse Danir, Old English Dene (also Dæne), Old High German Teni, and Latin Dani (Gregory of Tours, Jordanes, 6th c.). The singular Dane in modern English is a back-formation from the plural plus Scandinavian Danr/Dansk. A popular etymology links *dan- to PIE *dhen- "flat, low, valley" (cf. English den, dene "valley"), making Danes "lowlanders" and Denmark "lowland border" (mark = borderland) — geographically apt for flat Jutland and the isles, but phonologically and historically unproven. The honest statement is: the Viking-Age forms are certain; the pre-migration source is a tribal name of unknown meaning. Later folk-etymologies invoking King Dan (medieval Danish chronicles) are foundation myths, not linguistics.

Historically Danes moves through four phases. In Roman and Migration sources Dani are a southern Scandinavian tribe. In Viking-Age England Dene means terrifying sea-raiders and then settlers of the Danelaw (9th–11th c.) — the Danes of Alfred, Æthelred, Cnut. In high-medieval England Danes becomes a historical memory (Dane-geld, Hocktide). In modern English Danes simply means citizens of Denmark, with the Viking echo revived by romanticism.

  • Proto-Germanic to Common Norse (pre-600 CE): *daniz as self-name; runic and Jordanes's Dani. Small tribal confederation in Zealand, Scania, Jutland.
  • Viking Age (800–1050): Old Norse Danir vs. Old English Dene. In England Dene carries terror: Great Heathen Army (865), Danelaw partition, Æthelred's St Brice's Day massacre (1002), Cnut's North Sea empire (1016–1035). Eleventh-century Danes in Durant = these late Viking settlers and conquerors, Christianising but still "barbarian" to Normanising chroniclers.
  • Middle English (1100–1500): Danes as ancestors and villains in chronicles (Geoffrey of Monmouth, Layamon). Danegeld (tribute) enters fiscal vocabulary. Meaning shifts from present threat to historical other.
  • Early modern to modern (1500–present): Dane = native of Denmark; Danish = language, pastry, bacon. Neutral demonym. Great Danes (dogs) and Danish furniture extend the brand far from Vikings.
  • Durant's eleventh-century Danes: Durant singles out the last pagan-tinged generation — Sweyn Forkbeard, Cnut — to extend cannibalism charges into the Middle Ages. No credible evidence supports institutional Danish cannibalism in the 11th century; Christian Denmark (Harald Bluetooth Christianised c. 965) practised inhumation funerals, directly contradicting "funerals were unknown." The claim reflects early-anthropology credulity toward saga slander and classical topoi, not archaeology (Danish graves of the period are rich and well documented).

Formally the plural -s is English; the stem Dane- is Norse-English. Pronunciation /deɪnz/ reflects the Great Vowel Shift (Middle English /daːnəs/ > modern /deɪn/ + /z/). Culturally the word travelled from fear (Dene invaders) to neighbour (EU Danes) — with Durant freezing it at the fear-stage to argue for a savage past that modern Denmark, with its Viking museums and Lutheran churchyards full of ordered graves, visibly refutes.
