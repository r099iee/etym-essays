PORK-PIE is a compound of two independent root words, PORK and PIE; each has its own derivation chain. CHAIN 1 — PORK (the pig-meat strand): 1. Proto-Indo-European *pórḱos / *porḱo- “young pig, piglet” (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE; the swine-breeding word of the western Indo-European farming world, attested indirectly through many branches; sometimes written *porços). 2. Latin porcus “pig, swine” (attested from the 2nd c. BCE, e.g. Cato, Pliny; the ordinary farm-animal word; the root is a shared Italo-Celtic inheritance, cf. Oscan and Old Irish orc “piglet”). 3. (Vulgar Latin) Latin porcus passing into the Gallo-Romance of late antiquity (no recorded form change; the vowel quality shifted in speech). 4. Old French porc “swine, pig’s flesh” (attested from the 9th–11th c.; the word names both the live animal and its meat, a French habit inherited by English). 5. Anglo-Norman / Middle English pork “pig’s flesh as food” (attested from the 13th–14th c., borrowed into English after the Norman Conquest, 1066; in English the word was at first restricted to the meat, while the living animal kept the native word pig/swine). 6. Modern English pork “the flesh of the pig used as food” (14th c. onward). 7. English pork in the fixed compound the card’s PORK-PIE (the compound attested from the early 18th c., e.g. 1732). CHAIN 2 — PIE (the pastry strand): 1. Latin pīca “magpie” (attested from the 1st c. BCE), the pied black-and-white bird that gathers odds and ends; the bird-name’s deeper origin is debated (possibly imitative of its chatter, or from an adjective “pied, painted”; not securely traced to PIE). 2. Old French pie “magpie” (9th–11th c.). 3. Middle English pie “baked pastry with a mixed filling” (attested c. 1300); the standard etymology is a nickname: the miscellaneous contents were likened to a magpie’s gathered hoard. The derivation is traditional but debated (some prefer a word for “pasty, folded”); the magpie route remains the usual scholarly answer. 4. Modern English pie (14th c. onward), the general pastry-case word. 5. English pie in the compound the card’s PORK-PIE (attested c. 1732).
