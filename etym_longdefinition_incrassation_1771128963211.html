Incrassation is a noun of Latin origin naming the process or result of becoming thick, dense, or viscous. It derives from the post-classical Latin noun incrassatio, formed from the verb incrassare 'to make thick', which in turn comes from in- (intensive or directive prefix) + crassus 'thick, fat, dense, solid'. In the sentence about vital humours, incrassation describes a creeping, pathological thickening of the bodily fluids — a loss of vitality in which the very substance of life turns dense, sluggish, and unyielding.

Core senses and polysemy. The word is rare but shows a clear spectrum of senses across its history:

  • Humoral-medical sense. In the theory of the four humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile), incrassation denoted the pathological thickening or coagulation of a bodily fluid, regarded as a cause of disease, sluggishness, and decay. This is the sense the prompt sentence draws on, and it is the word's most historically important meaning.
  • Alchemical and early chemical sense. In alchemy and early chemistry, incrassation referred to the gradual densifying or inspissation of any substance — a syrup boiled down, a mixture left to thicken, a solution reduced — without the moral weight of the medical sense.
  • Botanical and anatomical sense. In natural history and botany, incrassation describes the thickening of plant parts, membranes, or tissues (a leaf blade growing thicker and fleshier, a seed coat becoming dense), a neutral descriptive use that parallels the adjective 'incrassate'.
  • Generalised and figurative sense. Rarely, incrassation is used figuratively of anything that gradually grows heavier, denser, or more sluggish — bureaucracy, atmosphere, or habit — though this extension is uncommon and mostly poetic.
  • Related family of words. The same root gives the adjective incrassate ('thickened; made thick or viscous'), the verb incrassate ('to thicken'), and the English word crass, which descends from crassus through the sense of 'thick, coarse, unrefined' applied to the intellect.

Metaphoric drift and historical bridges. The bridge from 'grow' to 'thicken' is crucial. The ultimate root, Latin crassus 'thick, dense, fat', carries the idea of accumulated substance. Because thickness was associated in humoral medicine with stagnation — the opposite of the healthy, flowing, mobile humours — incrassation took on the clinical colour of pathology: it was the process by which the body's vital fluids lost their motion and became the cause of 'obstruction' and disease. In alchemy the same process was neutral or even desirable (concentrating essences), so the word's emotional tone depends entirely on context. In modern English the word is archaic and scholarly, often used deliberately to evoke the creaking world of humoral medicine, or mock-scientifically for any slow congealing or loss of fluidity.

Register, grammar and collocation. Incrassation is a rare, formal, learned noun, essentially confined to historical medicine, alchemy, early science, and literary pastiche; the everyday word is 'thickening'. It is used as a mass or count noun ('the gradual incrassation of the humours', 'an incrassation of fluids'), typically followed by 'of' + the substance thickened. Its synonyms with different nuances include thickening, coagulation, congealment, condensation, and inspissation — of which inspissation is the closest learned equivalent, both meaning 'the making or becoming thick'.

Nuances against near-synonyms. Thickening is the plain, colloquial equivalent, universally understood but lacking the clinical gravity and humoral history of incrassation. Coagulation specifically denotes the clotting of blood or protein, often a sudden, necessary physiological response to injury, whereas incrassation is gradual, pathological densifying. Condensation usually means a gas becoming liquid, or a reduction of volume, and misses the organic, viscous sludginess of incrassation. Congealment involves the loss of fluidity, but typically through cooling, whereas incrassation is a creeping thickening independent of temperature. Inspissation is the closest synonym, but is even rarer and purely technical. Incrassation alone carries the full weight of humoral pathology and gradual, unhealthy decay.
