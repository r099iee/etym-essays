gloom — the word in this passage and in the language. In the source sentence — "their torch-beams tearing a dotted gloom that had perhaps never known the intrusion of light in all its former cycles" — gloom carries two simultaneous loads. Its primary, oldest settled sense is "partial or total darkness; dimness; the absence or near-absence of light in a place." That is the literal meaning the passage exploits, but the verb "tearing" forces a second, more physical reading: the gloom is treated as a tangible substance, a fabric or veil that the torch-beams must rip through rather than simply illuminate. The darkness here is not a mere absence; it is a presence — an ancient, settled shadow that has accumulated its own density over the "former cycles" of a cave that "had perhaps never known the intrusion of light." The phrase locks the sense to something permanent and near-substantial: gloom as a condition of a place, not a temporary shading. The tone is ominous and claustrophobic — darkness conceived as a hostile, or at least indifferent, thing that human light must forcibly breach.

1. Primary sense — darkness. In its physical core meaning, gloom is partial or total darkness, dimness, obscurity, the state of a place from which light is absent or almost absent. Unlike "darkness," which is a neutral fact, gloom implies a settled, pervasive weight: it is heavy, oppressively deep, and often connotes thickness or murkiness of air — the gloom of a forest, of a vault, of a fog-bound day. The word is still fully current in this sense in literary and descriptive prose, though everyday speech now prefers "darkness" for the plain fact and keeps "gloom" for the atmospheric, mood-charged version. It is this sense, magnified by "former cycles" and the cave's sealed antiquity, that the source passage primarily exploits.

2. Secondary sense — melancholy. Since the mid-18th century gloom has also meant "a state of depression, despondency, or melancholy; a feeling of sadness, hopelessness, or foreboding." This is now the statistically dominant use in everyday language: "a mood of gloom," "economic gloom," "market gloom," "gloom and doom," "to cast a gloom over a company," "the gloom lifted." The semantic bridge is historical and profound: in the older worldview the darkening of the sky and the darkening of the heart were continuous — both were the fading of a light, outer or inner — so the same word names a dim place and a dimmed spirit. Gloom is one of the few English words that names a physical darkness and the emotion of sadness with equal naturalness, because the emotion is itself imagined as the darkening of the spirit. The full mechanism of this bridge is set out in the etymology and wordThroughTime fields.

3. Tertiary senses and related formations.
  • As a verb, to gloom (and gloom over/about): to look sullen or dejected, to frown, to be gloomy; the verb preserves the oldest recorded life of the stem (Middle English gloumen, "to look sullen," c. 1300) and survives chiefly in literary use.
  • Gloomy (adjective): dark, dim, murky; also dismal, melancholy, pessimistic. The adjective is far commoner in speech than the noun: "a gloomy day," "a gloomy room," "a gloomy forecast," "he looked gloomy."
  • Gloomily (adverb) and gloominess (noun): the derived forms, both standard.
  • Gloaming (noun): "twilight, dusk" — an etymological sibling of gloom (both from the Germanic "glow" family's fading-light grade), now literary or dialectal, occasionally confused with gloom itself.
  • Figurative and extended uses: "the gloom of the underworld" (Milton's Tartarus), "the gloom of a ruined abbey" (Gothic fiction), "gloom-monger" and "gloomster" (rare, playful agents of pessimism), "doom and gloom" (idiomatic collocation for unrelieved pessimism).
  • In weather and meteorology: a "gloomy" day or sky is murky, heavily overcast, and lowering — the physical sense shading into mood once more.

4. The semantic bridge, stated plainly. Gloom is a word of light that became a word of darkness and then a word of sadness: "glow" → "fading light, twilight" → "darkness" → "the darkening of the spirit." Each new sense did not replace the old but was layered on top of it, so that today the same word, with no change of form, can describe a cave that never knew the intrusion of light and the melancholy of a disappointed heart — which is precisely the double work the source passage asks of it.

5. The synonym field, finely graded.
  • Darkness: the neutral, factual term — mere absence of light, without weight or mood.
  • Shadow: a specific, projected shape cast by an object, implying light present elsewhere; shadows move and lengthen, gloom does not.
  • Murk: thick, obscuring, often damp or smoky darkness (fog, smog, dirty air); emphasizes impenetrability rather than desolation.
  • Dusk and twilight: temporal terms tied to the time of day, soft and often beautiful; gloom is timeless and oppressive.
  • Dimness: merely reduced light, a quantitative condition without weight.
  • Obscurity: darkness as a consequence of being hidden or hard to see, often abstract ("the obscurity of the past").
  • Shade: a cool area sheltered from light, often pleasant (the shade of a tree).
  • Penumbra: the partial shadow at the edge of a total shadow — technical, of eclipses.
  • Overcast: of a sky, covered with cloud — a meteorological near-synonym for gloomy weather.
  • On the mood side: melancholy, dejection, despondency, depression, sadness, sullenness — the affective set, differing in intensity and duration; gloom is the least clinical and the most atmospheric, hovering between a dark place and a dark mood.
