- 8th–10th Century: Old English Sensory Specialization - Originated as the present participle *stincende* of Old English *stincan*, shifting from an earlier neutral Germanic meaning of emitting any scent toward an exclusively foul and offensive odor.
- 11th–14th Century: Religious & Moral Condemnation - Flourished in Middle English homilies, biblical translations, and devotional poetry as an epithet for sin, demonic presence, moral corruption, and the torments of hell.
- 15th–17th Century: Botanical & Zoological Nomenclature - Adopted systematically into English vernacular natural history to name foul-smelling plants and animals used for defense (e.g. *stinking iris*, *stinking horehound*, *stinking badger*).
- Late 19th–20th Century: Intensive Colloquial Adverb - Emerged in working-class and popular slang as a superlative intensifier denoting offensive excess or intoxication, producing immortal idioms like 'stinking rich' and 'stinking drunk'.
- 21st Century: Multi-Register Versatility - Continues as an everyday olfactory adjective, an established botanical classification marker, an aggressive personal insult, and an emphatic intensifier.
