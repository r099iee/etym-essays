ROOT WORD 1 of 4: INCANTATIONS
PIE *kan- (to sing, resound) — earliest known stage
├── Latin: canere (to sing)
│ ├── Latin frequentative: cantāre (to sing, chant, sing repeatedly)
│ │ ├── incantāre (in + cantāre: to chant over or upon someone, to enchant)
│ │ │ └── Latin noun: incantātiō (a chanting, an incantation, a spell)
│ │ │ └── Old French: incantation
│ │ │ └── English: incantation (from the 14th century) → incantations (plural) ◄ TARGET
│ │ └── English relatives from cantāre: chant, enchant, canticle, cantor
│ └── Cognates of *kan-: Old Irish canaid (sings), Welsh canu (to sing)
└── Note: The chain is entirely secure; incantation is a transparent Latin compound (in + cantare + -tion). The PIE root *kan- (to sing) is well attested in the Celtic and Italic branches.

ROOT WORD 2 of 4: CANTRAPS
Scots cantrip / cantraip (a charm, spell, or trick) — origin obscure and DEBATED
├── Scots: cantrip / cantraip / cantrap (magic charm, spell, trick)
│ └── English (literary, from the 18th century, popularized by Sir Walter Scott): cantrap / cantrip → cantraps (plural) ◄ TARGET
├── Proposed etymologies (all debated): (1) from Scots cant (a toss, a trick; also to speak in set phrases) + the -rap/-rip of trap or trip; (2) from Middle English cant, corner or edge, with the idea of boundary magic; (3) a blend with the -ripper sense of something trifling
└── Note: ZERO GUESSWORK — the word's origin is genuinely uncertain. The earliest recorded form is Scots cantrap/cantraip (early modern Scots), and no single etymology commands scholarly agreement; it is safest to report the Scots family and flag the obscurity. The modern fantasy sense (a minor spell) is a 20th-century revival, especially in role-playing games.

ROOT WORD 3 of 4: RUNES
PIE *rew- (to roar, murmur, whisper; some handbooks write the IE base *ru-n- with a nasal) — earliest known stage, itself debated
├── Proto-Germanic: *rūnō (secret, mystery, whispered counsel; also runic letter)
│ ├── Old English: rūn (secret, counsel, mystery; runic letter)
│ │ └── Middle English: rūn / rune
│ │ └── English: rune — the LETTER sense was re-established in the 17th century by antiquarian scholarship (Old Norse rún had meant both secret and letter) → runes (plural) ◄ TARGET
│ ├── Old Norse: rún (secret, rune); Gothic: rūna (mystery); Old Irish: rún (secret; a Celtic loan from Germanic); Welsh: rhin (secret, charm)
├── Attestation: Wulfila's Gothic Bible uses rūna to translate Greek mystērion (mystery)
└── Note: The PIE stage is speculative — *rew- (to murmur) is the common proposal, but several handbooks reconstruct only a Germanic *rūnō without a secure Indo-European etymon; the connection of the word to the root meaning roar/murmur/whisper is widely accepted but not absolutely certain. The Germanic stage and its meaning (secret, counsel) are secure.

ROOT WORD 4 of 4: THAUMATURGIES
Greek thauma (wonder, marvel; etymology DEBATED — possibly a Pre-Greek substrate word) + ergon (work, deed; from PIE *werg- to do, make, work) — compound roots
├── Greek: thaumatourgós (adjective and noun: working wonders, a wonder-worker; thauma + -ourgós, worker)
│ └── Greek abstract noun: thaumatourgía (the working of wonders, miracle-working)
│ └── Late Latin: thaumaturgia
│ └── English: thaumaturgy (learned borrowing, from the 16th century; plural thaumaturgies) ◄ TARGET
├── PIE *werg- cognates: English work, wrought; Greek ergon; Sanskrit várjas? (in varcas, vigor); the same root in English organ, erg, energy (through Greek)
└── Note: Only the ergon half of the compound has a clear PIE etymology (*werg- to work). The Greek noun thauma (wonder, marvel) has no agreed Indo-European derivation — Beekes suggests a Pre-Greek substrate origin, and older proposals linking it to PIE *dʰau- or *dʰew- (to become, to strike wonder) are speculative; this is reported here as an open question rather than a settled chain.
