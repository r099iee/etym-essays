The biography of blithering is a story of noise turning into insult. At the base stands the Proto-Indo-European root *bhle-, "to blow," the ancient equation of speech with breath and of talk with wind — an equation every language makes, since we speak by blowing. From that root, or from the same imitative impulse that makes babble sound like babbling, came a scatter of expressive formations across the family. In Italy, Latin blaterāre, "to babble, to talk foolishly," was a comic verb used by Plautus for the garrulous and the drunk — the Roman stage's word for a mouth running on without sense. In the Germanic north, Old Norse blaðra, "to babble, to mutter," stood beside Old English blǣdrian, "to blather," and together they fed Middle English blathren, the medieval verb that survives today as blather and blether — the sound of a mouth full of noise without thought. Old English also possessed blǣdre, "a wind-filled bag," the bladder, belonging to the same root-world; the ancient image is of talk as a bladder of wind — inflated, hollow, bursting with air. (The Sanskrit bhaṣate, "he speaks," is sometimes brought into this family, but its origin is uncertain and it must be set aside; and bleat, the imitative cry of sheep, shares the expressive atmosphere without being an ancestor.) For centuries blather and blither were interchangeable dialectal forms of the same foolish verb, and the speech sense was the only sense there was: to blither was to talk nonsense. In the eighteenth century blither hardened into a distinct variant, and from it came the participle blithering, at first simply meaning "babbling, driveling." Then came the decisive semantic leap of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the point at which the word's whole history condensed. In the famous collocation "blithering idiot," popularized by the comic writing of the early twentieth century, blithering stopped describing talk and became a pure intensive: it no longer meant "talkative" but "utter, complete, hopeless." The babbling sense collapsed into a single blast of contempt — the fool's blather transferred to the fool himself and then to anything foolish. The word's reach widened accordingly: any noun could now be blithering, the adverb blitheringly following, and the intensifier became the stock dismissal of English comic and colloquial speech. That is the form Maspic uses in the passage. His "blithering sandstorm" is not a storm that talks; it is a storm that is utterly, idiotically excessive — a force so mindless it seems to rave, a blast of wind that behaves like a fool with the volume turned up to madness. The word's whole history — breath, babble, bladder, blather, idiot, utter — is comprehended in that one transferred adjective: the ancient equation of speech with wind replayed as weather, the storm reduced to a blathering fool, all noise and no intelligence. The comic personification is perfect because the word has always been about wind — wind in a bag, wind in the mouth, wind in the desert sky — and the sandstorm is, in the oldest sense of the root, pure blithering: hollow, inflated, bursting with sound, and empty of sense. The journey from a Latin comic verb for drunken chatter, through the wind-bag vocabulary of the Germanic north, to a twentieth-century intensifier of idiocy is one of the most compressed semantic arcs in the language — and in a single transferred adjective it lands, still full of wind, on the desert.
