English haunches is a gift of the Norman settlement to the body lexicon. The singular haunch enters Middle English in the 14th century as haunche / hanch, borrowed from Old French hanche 'hip, haunch'. That Old French form is itself a loan from a Germanic source, most plausibly Frankish *hanka (Old Low Franconian), continuing a Proto-Germanic *hankō / *hanhō 'hip, haunch, the bent hind part'. The Germanic pedigree is confirmed across the family: related Germanic words for the hip or the hind part of a quadruped appear in continental dialects, and the same Germanic word was borrowed into several Romance languages, giving French hanche, Italian anca, and Spanish and Portuguese anca — a coherent 'anca / hanche' cluster long recognized as a Frankish-era loan. A deeper descent from PIE *kenk- 'to gird, bend, loop' is sometimes proposed, because a hip is after all a bend of the body; but the link is not securely established in the handbooks, and the honest chain stops at Germanic.

HOW THE WORD GOT ITS SOUND

  • The initial h- is Germanic and was kept in the French and English loans (compare the Romance forms that dropped it, such as Italian anca, where the h- was lost).
  • The vowel: Old French hanche to Middle English haunche shows the French a-vowel accommodated to English spelling habits; later pronunciation shifts brought the modern /hɔːntʃ/.
  • The medial cluster -nch- preserves the palatalized affricate of the Old French spelling -che; English kept the cluster while the vowel quality drifted under the Great Vowel Shift and later lowering.

THE SEMANTIC DRIFT

  • In the source languages the core sense was the hip and the hind region of a body — in animals the powerful muscular hindquarter, in humans the hip-and-buttock area.
  • English preserved the anatomical sense and built three extensions on it: (1) the postural idiom to sit / squat on one's haunches, describing the weight borne back through hips and thighs; (2) the culinary sense, a haunch of venison / mutton, the prized joint of the hind limb, standard in the game and butchery trade; and (3) a technical sense in building, the haunch of an arch, the hip-like swelling where an arch springs from its support.
  • The register of the word has been stable: solid, physical, and slightly coarse. Applied to a person, haunches keeps an animal and market-place after-taste; in hunting, kitchen, and veterinary prose it is ordinary professional vocabulary.

SOCIAL AND LITERARY LIFE OF THE WORD

  • Because the word arrived through Anglo-Norman and the Francophone elite, it circulated in the manorial and courtly worlds of hunting and feasting — precisely the worlds of the haunch of venison and of horse anatomy — before spreading into general English.
  • In late medieval and early modern English it appears in cookery and game accounts, in descriptions of horse and hound, and in narrative prose where a human posture is described with physical directness.
  • The plural form haunches became the default anatomical spelling, and the idiomatic phrase 'on his haunches' remained available to writers who wanted a squat, a crouch, or a half-sit more earthy than 'on his knees'.

CONCLUSION

The word in the passage does exactly what its history prepares it for: when the usher 'perches one of his haunches sideways on the edge of a chair', the Germanic-French body word, still faintly of the stable and the spit, undercuts gentlemanly polish. The usher has stepped toward a gentlemanly living, but his body performs deference in the language of the hindquarters — a half-seat that is simultaneously claim and apology.
