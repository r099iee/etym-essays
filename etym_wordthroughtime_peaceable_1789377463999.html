- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed from Anglo-French *paisable* (from Old French *paisible*), originally describing law-abiding, orderly citizens or quiet, undisturbed monastic communities free from civil strife.
- 16th to 17th Century (Tudor & Stuart Eras) - Codified in influential religious and legal texts (notably the 1611 King James Bible, James 3:17; and the Book of Common Prayer), establishing a peaceable temper as a prime Christian virtue in an age of violent sectarian conflict.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment & Common Law) - Enshrined in legal jurisprudence and constitutional theory, notably defining the doctrine of "peaceable assembly" without riot or public disorder.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely employed in Victorian narrative literature to delineate modest, domestic, and non-contentious characters, as exemplified by Oscar Wilde's portrait of the Duck in *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888).
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as a standard legal term in constitutional law (peaceable assembly) and property rights (peaceable possession), while remaining a prized descriptor in mediation, psychology, and personal ethics.
