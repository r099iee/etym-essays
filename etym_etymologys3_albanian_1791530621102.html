The name Albanian begins with a small Illyrian tribe and ends as the name of a modern nation and an independent branch of Indo-European. The earliest attestation is in the Geography of Ptolemy of Alexandria (second century CE), who mentions the Albanoi and their city Albanopolis in the Roman province of Macedonia, near modern Krujë in central Albania. The tribe is usually classified as Illyrian, and the city name combines Albanoi with Greek polis "city". The deeper meaning of Alb- is uncertain: one proposal connects it with an Indo-European word for a mountainous terrain, also seen in Alps; another with "white"; another simply treats it as an unrecoverable Illyrian name. There is no secure etymology, and the ancient attestation does not by itself prove continuity of population.

From Albanoi to Arbanitai. The name reappears in Byzantine sources in the eleventh century. Michael Attaliates, writing about the year 1043, mentions Albanoi; later Byzantine historians use Arbanitai and Arbanites. The alternation of Alb- and Arb- reflects a rhotacism (l to r) that is also seen in the Albanian endonym arbën / arbër and in the Italian Arbëreshë and Greek Arvanites. Latin and Italian documents from the fourteenth century onward use Albanenses, Albanesi, and Albanese. The Turkish form Arnavut derives from the same stem. English Albanian enters in the sixteenth century, through Latin and French, and gradually displaces older learned forms. The modern Albanian endonym shqiptar is later, emerging between the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and is now standard inside the Albanian-speaking world.

The endonym Shqip. The origin of shqip is debated. One old suggestion connects it with Albanian shqip "clear, understandable, plain", from a verb meaning "to speak clearly"; another with Latin excipere "to except" or with a root meaning "to speak". None is conclusive. What is clear is that shqip and shqiptar became national markers during the Rilindja, the Albanian national awakening of the nineteenth century, and that they coexist with the older arbërisht and with the exonym Albanian. The double naming is a common Balkan pattern: a local self-designation, a regional name, and a learned classical name all refer to the same people.

Language history and attestation. Albanian is an independent Indo-European branch. Its relationship to Illyrian, Dacian, or Thracian is debated; the Illyrian hypothesis is traditionally favoured, but the evidence is indirect. The language is first attested in the Formula e Pagëzimit of 1462, a short baptismal formula in the Gheg dialect, and in Gjon Buzuku's Meshari (1555), a translation of the Catholic missal. Frang Bardhi's Latin-Albanian dictionary (1635) and the works of Pjetër Budi and Pjetër Bogdani mark the early modern literary tradition. The nineteenth-century Rilindja produced a national literature and a standardized alphabet, fixed at the Congress of Manastir in 1908. The Congress of Orthography in 1972 codified the modern standard, based mainly on Tosk. The communist period under Enver Hoxha (1944-1985) isolated the country and imposed a rigid literary norm; after 1991, contact with Italy, Greece, and the diaspora brought new lexical and stylistic influences.

Sound changes and dialect variation. Albanian phonology has undergone substantial changes from Indo-European. Proto-Indo-European o became a, e became o, and the stop system was reshaped; final consonants were often lost, and many clusters were simplified. Latin loanwords show the working of these changes over two millennia. The Gheg dialects preserve nasal vowels and final consonants that Tosk has lost; Tosk has rhotacism of intervocalic n to r, a feature that distinguishes the two groups. The Shkumbin River is the traditional boundary, though the continuum is gradual. The Arbëreshë of Italy and the Arvanites of Greece speak archaic Tosk varieties that preserve features lost in the standard language.

Socio-cultural context and modern usage. Albanian identity has been shaped by Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman, and communist rule. The national hero Skanderbeg (Gjergj Kastrioti) led a revolt against the Ottomans in the fifteenth century and became a symbol of resistance. The League of Prizren (1878) and the declaration of independence in 1912 were milestones of nation-building. Under Hoxha, Albania became a Stalinist state and declared itself the world's first atheist country; churches and mosques were closed, and travel was severely restricted. The collapse of communism in 1991 brought mass emigration, political instability, and the Kosovo war of 1998-1999. Today Albanian is the language of a EU candidate state and of a large diaspora, and the name is central to debates about Kosovo's recognition, the protection of minorities in North Macedonia and Greece, and the cultural heritage of the Arbëreshë.

  • Earliest attestation - Ptolemy's Albanoi and Albanopolis, second century CE.
  • Medieval forms - Byzantine Albanoi / Arbanitai; Latin Albanenses; Turkish Arnavut.
  • Endonym - shqip, shqiptar; older arbën / arbër.
  • Language - independent Indo-European branch; Gheg and Tosk; standard 1972.
  • Modern charge - national identity, Kosovo, diaspora, and the communist legacy.
