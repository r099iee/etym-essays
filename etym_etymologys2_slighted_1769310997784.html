Stage 1 — PIE root *sleyǵ- (also reconstructed *slei-, *sleib-, *sleig-) "to be smooth, slick, slippery, to glide" (reconstructed, c. 4500-3000 BCE): the root of the smooth-and-glide family; its daughters include English sleek and slick (smooth, glossy), slip and slide (to glide). There is scholarly variation in the exact shape of the root (with or without the final velar), and this nuance is noted below. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *slihtaz "even, smooth, level, plain" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE-100 CE): formed from the PIE root with the Germanic adjective suffix *-t-; the ancestor of Old Norse sléttr, Old English sliht, and Old High German sleht. Stage 3 — Old Norse sléttr "smooth, even, level" and Old English sliht "smooth, level, plain" (Viking Age, 8th-11th c.): the Old Norse form is the one that entered English during the Scandinavian settlements; the native Old English form is attested in glosses and place-names. Stage 4 — Middle English slight "smooth, flat, level; then plain, ordinary; then small, slender, of little account" (14th-15th c.): the sense drifted in successive steps within Middle English from surface smoothness to smallness of size and worth. Stage 5 — Early Modern English slight "small, slender, insignificant" (adjective) and the new verb to slight "to treat as of little account, to disparage" and noun a slight "an act of neglect or disdain" (16th c.): the adjective's "of little account" sense generated the verb and noun of social belittlement. Stage 6 — English slighted "treated as slight, insulted by neglect" (past participle, 16th c. onward): formed from the new verb; in the 16th-17th c. also used with the older physical sense "levelled, demolished" of fortresses (to slight a fortress = to raze it). Stage 7 — Modern English SLIGHTED "insulted by indifference or neglect" (the participle's social sense dominant from the 17th c. onward). Note on the debate: the PIE root is sometimes cited with the palatal velar (*sleyǵ-) and sometimes without (*slei-); the velar-extended form is the one generally connected to English slight, while the plain *slei- sits behind slip and slide. The German cognate Old High German sleht "straight, even" famously pejorated into modern German schlecht "bad" — a striking parallel to the English drift from "plain" to "insignificant".
