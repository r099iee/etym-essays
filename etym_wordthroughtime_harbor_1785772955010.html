- Early Middle English: Closely tied to lodging and army/traveler shelter from the old *here-beorg* compound—practical, protective, institutional (inn, quarters).
- Later Middle English: Verb "to harbor/herberwe" normal for giving lodging; noun begins strong association with safe places for people and, increasingly, ships.
- Early Modern English: Maritime "harbor" as port is culturally central; figurative "harbor thoughts/doubts" becomes literary and psychological common stock.
- 18th–19th centuries: Legal and moral discourse highlights harboring criminals or runaway slaves; suspicion can color the verb when the object is illicit.
- 20th–present: Dual everyday life—ports and safe-harbor metaphors; still standard for refugees, pathogens, and grudges. Perception ranges from humanitarian (harbor the needy) to accusatory (harbor terrorists) depending on object and politics.
