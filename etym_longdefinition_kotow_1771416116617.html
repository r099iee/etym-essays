Kotow (also spelled kowtow) names the supreme gesture of filial piety and social submission: the act of kneeling and knocking the forehead against the ground in the presence of a superior. In this passage, Dai-yu has just arrived at the Rongguo mansion and enters the private chamber of Grandmother Jia for the first time. The narrator tells us that she “would have fallen on her knees and made her kotow,” and the reader must understand how much social work this single gesture performs. The kotow is not a bow and not a curtsey; it is the physical enactment of rank itself. For Dai-yu it is a vital social performance—the visible proof of her virtue, her upbringing, and her recognition of Grandmother Jia’s absolute authority as the matriarch of the house. Her grandmother’s snatching her up “in her arms” before she can complete the prostration is therefore a moment of enormous emotional and social power: love momentarily suspends the iron protocol of hierarchy, and the two meet as grandmother and grandchild rather than as sovereign and subject. The transitive phrasing (“made her kotow”) also hints that the ritual is an obligation performed on demand, a duty owed rather than a spontaneous courtesy. The word must be distinguished from a cluster of neighbouring expressions. A bow is a mere inclination of the head or body, reversible and light, exchanged between near-equals; a curtsey is a feminine gesture of deference that never requires the forehead to touch the floor; a prostration is an abject full-length lying flat that can signal grief or religious ecstasy as much as submission; a genuflection bends only the knee, a liturgical gesture native to European churches; while an obeisance is a general, rather bookish word for any reverent gesture. The kotow is stricter and more total than all of these: it is a complete surrender of the vertical, ritualized into a countable, repeated act (the “three kneelings and nine prostrations” of court protocol) that encodes hierarchy at every level of Qing society—subject before emperor, child before parent, wife before husband, and junior before elder. Lexically, kotow operates in formal, historical, and journalistic registers. In eighteenth- and nineteenth-century travel and diplomatic writing it denoted Chinese court protocol (the famous 1793 refusal of Lord Macartney to kotow to the Qianlong emperor); today it survives as an idiomatic verb meaning to grovel or defer abjectly, as in “the minister kotowed to the chairman,” and as a noun for that act. It collocates with make, perform, refuse to, be forced to, and the prepositions to or before; common collocations include “to kotow to authority,” “the ritual kotow,” and “a perfunctory kotow.” In literary Chinese and English fiction alike, it is a gravity well of social weight—the moment a character literally lowers the body to fix their place in the cosmic and household order.
  • Primary sense (the literal ritual): Kneeling and knocking the forehead against the ground in worship or submission, a graded prostration performed before the emperor, parents, elders, or deities, with the depth and number of knockings calibrated to rank.
  • Transferred sense (deference and servility): Any act of abject self-abasement before authority—the court sense extending to “to kotow to” as a general verb of grovelling surrender of dignity.
  • Register and context: Formal, historical, journalistic; at home in diplomatic memoirs, political commentary, and literary narrative, and nearly always now figurative and mildly dismissive.
  • Orthographic variants: kowtow is the most common spelling; kotow is a standard respelling recorded by dictionaries through the twentieth century and is the rootWord of this card; both are accepted as verb and noun.
Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings: In political commentary and diplomacy, kowtow means subservient grovelling or bowing to authority. In business and social life, to “kowtow to the banks,” “to fashion,” or “to public opinion” is to defer abjectly and without principle. As a noun it can mean the specific gesture, the diplomatic act, or, loosely, any display of excessive deference. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: Chinese kē tóu (磕頭, 'knock head') transferred from the Imperial court forehead-touching ritual to an international diplomatic idiom for humiliating submission, and then in twentieth-century journalism to any surrender of dignity before power, money, or opinion. The word’s power in the novel is that it is simultaneously a precise court gesture and a moral verdict: Dai-yu’s readiness to perform it certifies her virtue, and Grandmother Jia’s interception of it certifies her love.
