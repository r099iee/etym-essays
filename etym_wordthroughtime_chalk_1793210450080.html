- Old English — building and burning: cealc names the white stone the Romans taught Britain to burn for mortar and spread for crops.
- Medieval — the landmark rock: chalk pits, chalk hills, and white cliff coasts fix the word in place-names and parish boundaries.
- 16th c. — the marking stick: sawn chalk becomes the schoolman's and tailor's tool, and white marks on dark cloth spread the word indoors.
- 17th–18th c. — scores and slates: taverns chalk debts and darts on boards, and chalk up begins its long march from the slate to the scoreboard.
- 19th c. — idiom's golden age: chalk and cheese, by a long chalk, and chalk figures cut into hillsides make the word proverbial.
- Today — the classroom ghost and the living hill: blackboards yield to whiteboards while the Downs still whiten every cutting, and chalk grassland is prized as England's richest wildflower habitat.
