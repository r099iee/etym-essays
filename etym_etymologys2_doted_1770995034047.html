Proto-Germanic *dutōną "to be dazed, to behave foolishly, to be dull of mind" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-200 CE; origin uncertain - see debate note) → Middle Dutch doten "to be silly, to act foolishly, to dote" (attested c. 13th century CE) → Middle Low German doten "to be foolish, to be out of one's wits" (attested c. 13th-14th centuries CE) → Middle English doten "to behave foolishly, to be weak-minded or senile" (attested c. 1200-1225) → Middle English doten (extended sense) "to be blindly and excessively fond" (attested 14th century CE; the love sense develops from the idea of a mind clouded by affection) → English dote "to show excessive, uncritical fondness (on/upon)" → doted (past tense and past participle) TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The Germanic chain (Middle Dutch and Middle Low German doten → Middle English doten) is well attested and secure. The deep pre-Germanic origin of the Germanic root is uncertain. One frequently cited hypothesis connects it to the PIE root *dheu- "to smoke, cloud, vapor", on the idea that a "clouded" or "smoked" mind underlies the sense "to be foolish, dazed" - the same root family that gave English deaf (a clouding of hearing), dust (a cloud of particles), and fume (a cloud of gas). This is, however, a speculative root-link, not a demonstrated descent, and should not be treated as certain. The noun dotage ("senile decay; excessive fondness") and the adjective doting ("blindly affectionate") are native English formations on the same verb.
