The etymology of adder is one of the most celebrated case studies in English historical linguistics, illustrating the powerful phonetic mechanism of metanalysis (also known as faulty juncture, rebracketing, or resegmentation), while simultaneously tracking the zoological narrowing of an ancient Indo-European term for serpents.

1. Indo-European Ancestry and Germanic Cognates:

The ancient origin of adder traces back to the Proto-Indo-European noun *nē-tr- or *nə-tr-, designating a water snake, crawling serpent, or poisonous reptile. This root produced cognate forms across multiple Indo-European branches, including Latin natrīx ("water snake"), Old Irish nathair ("snake"), and Welsh neidr ("adder, viper").

In Proto-Germanic, this became *nēdrō or *nadrō ("snake, serpent"). This Germanic parent word developed into Old High German nātara (modern German Natter, meaning grass snake or adder), Old Norse naðra, and Gothic nadrs.

2. Old English 'Nǣdre' and General Serpent Meaning:

In Old English, the word appeared as nǣdre, nǣddre, or nēdre. Importantly, during the Anglo-Saxon period, nǣdre was not restricted to a specific biological species; it served as a general term for any snake, serpent, viper, or mythical reptilian monster. In the epic poem Beowulf and Old English translations of the Gospels, nǣdran describes the deceitful serpent of Eden as well as fire-breathing dragons. The venomous bite of the creature was widely feared, and Anglo-Saxon medical texts (such as Bald's Leechbook) contained herbal charms and salves specifically designed against nǣdran slite ("adder's bite").

3. The Linguistic Phenomenon of Metanalysis (Resegmentation):

During the Middle English period (roughly between 1300 and 1450), English underwent a profound boundary shift in spoken acoustic perception. Because the indefinite article an ended in an /n/ and the noun naddre began with an /n/, phrases like "a naddre" and "an addre" sounded completely identical in connected, rapid speech (/ə ˈnad.rə/).

Listeners and speakers systematically misbracketed the word boundary, transferring the initial /n/ of the noun onto the indefinite article. Consequently, a naddre was reanalyzed as an addere. Exactly the same phonetic process occurred in other English words: a napron became an apron, a noumpere became an umpire, and a nauger became an auger (while the reverse process turned an ekename into a nickname and an ewte into a newt).

4. Biblical Canonization and Zoological Specialization:

By the 16th century, the spelling had standardized to adder. The Great Bible (1539) and the King James Bible (1611) immortalized the term in English literature, translating Hebrew pethen in Psalm 58:4 as the "deaf adder that stoppeth her ear." Medieval bestiaries explained this folklore by claiming the adder pressed one ear against the ground and plugged the other with its tail to resist the enchanter's music.

In early modern scientific taxonomy, British naturalists restricted adder primarily to Vipera berus, distinguishing it from non-venomous grass snakes. P.G. Wodehouse, steeped in classical and biblical schooling, frequently had Bertie Wooster cite the "deaf adder of Scripture" to humorously reproach stubborn companions who refused to heed his sensible plans.
