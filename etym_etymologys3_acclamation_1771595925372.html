The biography of acclamation begins in the primal cry — the human voice raised to cross a distance, to summon, to call out. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *kelh₁-, “to call, to shout, to summon,” the root of the herald and the mother’s call. From this root the ancient languages built their vocabulary of the raised voice. Greek kaleîn meant “to call, summon, name,” and from it came klêsis, “a calling,” and ekklēsía — literally “the assembly of those called out” — the citizens summoned from the city by the herald’s voice, which became the Church: from it come ecclesiastical, ecclesiastic, and ecclesiology, so that the language of religion and the language of acclamation are siblings. In Latin the root split into two important verbs: calāre, “to announce, summon, proclaim,” which produced kalendae, the Calends — the first day of the month when the pontifex called out the moon’s name and the calendar was announced — and concilium, “the assembly called together,” whence council; and clāmāre, “to shout,” the root of our word, with its noun clāmor, “a shout,” the ancestor of English clamour. The decisive branch for our word is clāmāre, “to shout.” Roman political life was built on the shout: the senate, the forum, the circus crowd, the soldiers on a triumph — all of them clamoured. From clāmāre Latin compounded a remarkable family: exclāmāre (to exclaim), prōclāmāre (to proclaim), reclāmāre (to cry out against — to reclaim), dēclāmāre (to declaim), and clāmōsus, “shouting” — the ancestor of clamorous. Most important of all is acclāmāre, from ad “toward” and clāmāre: to shout at, or toward, someone. By the late Republic and Empire, acclāmātiō had hardened into a political and ceremonial institution — the ritual, chanted approval of the senate, of soldiers, of circus crowds, of the multitude at an emperor’s appearance or a bishop’s election. Historians record the stylized cries that hailed a triumph or acclaimed a new ruler; the shout of acclamation was, in effect, the Roman ballot, the collective voice by which public opinion registered itself. The word’s ceremonial gravity was therefore ancient before it ever reached English: acclamation was not just applause but the institutional voice of a whole body, the shout that made a ruler, a general, or a decree legitimate in the ears of Rome. From Rome the institution and its word travelled into the medieval world. The church adopted the acclamation — the cries of ‘Amen,’ ‘Alleluia,’ and the popular approval that could elect a pope or a saint by the voice of the faithful — so that in ecclesiastical Latin acclāmātiō kept its constitutional force: election by acclamation became a recognized, if debated, form of assent. In the secular vernaculars the word entered Old French as acclamation in the fourteenth century and passed into English in the fifteenth, borrowed from the French and Latin forms and carrying the full ceremonial weight of its Roman past. In early English use acclamation meant the loud, collective shout of approval — the acclamation of a monarch, a victor, a public act — and it kept the political and constitutional colouring it had worn in Rome: an election or decree ‘by acclamation’ was approved by the unanimous voice of the assembly, without ballot or vote. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries broadened the word in two directions. On the one hand, acclamation became the standard literary and journalistic term for any unanimous, enthusiastic reception — the acclamation of a great work, a great performer, a great cause — and this sense survives in phrases like critical acclamation and universal acclamation. On the other hand, the word kept its institutional home in politics and the church, where decisions carried ‘by acclamation’ remain a recognized procedure: a motion passed by acclamation, an office accepted by acclamation. It is this double life — the crowd’s shout and the assembly’s vote — that gives the word its peculiar gravity: acclamation is at once the most spontaneous and the most formal of approvals, the voice of the many and the verdict of the body. It is against this whole history that John Ruskin’s phrase “vain acclamation” in the Preface to Modern Painters (1843) must be read: “I shall shrink from no labour... to the honour of those great living Masters whom we now neglect, only to exalt the dead with vain acclamation.” Acclamation is the ancient, constitutional shout of honour — the voice of the senate, the triumph, the people — and to call it “vain” is to say that the Victorian art-public has emptied the most solemn instrument of collective honour of all meaning: the shout is raised, but it is raised at the dead, who neither hear it nor require it. The word’s Roman, ceremonial, vote-like weight makes the vanity all the more damning; acclamation is sound that ought to make things true — a ruler crowned, a hero honoured, a motion passed — and here it signifies nothing. Ruskin’s irony depends entirely on the word’s history: only a word so constitutional, so laden with the collective voice of Rome and the church and the assembly, can make the modern noise of praise seem so hollow. Today acclamation remains a living, high-register word. It survives in procedural contexts — election or approval by acclamation — in the vocabulary of public enthusiasm — the acclamation of a performer, a book, a cause — and in the memory of its Roman past. Its distinctive meaning is still the collective, vocal, spontaneous approval of a body: applause may be individual hands, but acclamation is the voice of the many raised together, the shout that honours and the shout that votes. The word that began as the primal cry of the human voice has carried, across four millennia, the whole weight of public honour — from the Roman triumph to the Victorian press — and in Ruskin’s sentence it shows its subtlest power: emptied of meaning, the loudest voice in the language of honour is turned into the sound of vanity.
