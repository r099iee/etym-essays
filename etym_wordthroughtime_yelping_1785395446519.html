- [Old English–Middle English: boastful origin] - The ancestor form gielpan/gilpan meant 'to boast, exult, glory in oneself' — a proud, self-regarding cry of human triumph, closer to bragging than to pain.
- [Middle English–16th century: descent of register] - The boastful sense withered; the verb instead named any loud, sharp shouting, whether of man or beast, and the word slid down the social ladder from the speech of praise to the mere noise of the creature.
- [17th–19th century: the cry of the beast] - 'Yelp' settled firmly on the short, sharp, high-pitched cry of dogs and other animals in pain or alarm; applied to humans it became deliberately belittling — the language of the kennel and the farmyard.
- [Contemporary sense] - The involuntary, staccato cry of sudden pain, surprise, or distress; in narrative it often serves comic deflation, stripping menace from its subject (as Tolkien uses it of the routed goblins), with an observational, slightly derisive register.
