Diachronically is the adverb of diachronic: in a manner that follows developments through time, comparing successive stages of a language, culture, or institution rather than freezing one moment for inspection. Where the synchronic view slices across a single period, the diachronic view runs lengthwise down the centuries, asking how earlier forms became later ones.

In the source sentence the word is paired with its gloss, or historically: scholars must approach orality and literacy not only side by side but also diachronically, tracking the long evolution from oral mind to manuscript to print to electronics. The adverb carries the whole methodological claim — that literacy is a historical succession, not merely a gallery of coexisting types, and only the through-time lens reveals it.

Core meaning in detail: Coined for linguistics by Ferdinand de Saussure and diffused across the human sciences, diachronically signals the historical axis of explanation: to study English vowels diachronically is to follow the Great Vowel Shift; to study religion diachronically is to watch ritual mutate across reformations. The Greek parts say it plainly — dia-, through or across, plus khronos, time — thinking that goes through time rather than standing inside one date.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Across successive historical stages (primary linguistic sense, from 19th c.): as in studying sound changes diachronically from Old to Modern English. The bridge is the coinage itself: Saussure needed an adverb for the historical axis, and Greek supplied it.
  • Through historical development generally (extended humanities sense): tracing institutions, genres, or technologies diachronically. The bridge runs from language outward by analogy: if phonemes have histories, so do parliaments, novels, and media.
  • As a narrative of origins and transformations (historiographical sense): explaining the present by its antecedents rather than its contemporaries. The bridge is causal: diachronic accounts answer how it came to be, where synchronic ones answer how it works now.
  • Developmentally in biology and psychology (borrowed scientific sense): following a species, embryo, or child diachronically through growth stages. The bridge carries the historian lens into the laboratory: ontogeny and phylogeny are both through-time series.
  • Longitudinally in data and policy (modern applied sense): tracking cohorts, prices, or literacy rates diachronically across decades. The bridge is statistical: the panel survey is the diachronic method wearing numbers.
  • As opposed to comparatively at one date (contrastive methodological sense, the source sense): diachronically or historically versus synchronically side by side. The bridge is the famous pair itself: every serious account needs both axes, and the adverb marks which one is in play.

Register and connotation: diachronically is academic through and through — lecture halls, methods chapters, and footnotes, seldom heard beyond the campus. It dignifies its user with structuralist lineage while promising depth: the synchronic view may be elegant, but the diachronic view knows where things came from. The source sentence uses it as a term of art, softened at once by the plain gloss that follows.
