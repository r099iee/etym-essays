Pinch is a medieval French gift to the English fingers and to English idiom. Its story runs from an Indo-European root of pricking and stabbing, through Latin and through the French of the twelfth century, into Middle English, and then outward in one of the richest semantic radiations in the language — ending, on this card, with a dwarf praising a hobbit who is "fierce as a dragon in a pinch." 1. Indo-European and Latin foundations: pricking and piercing. The deepest recoverable layer is an Indo-European root in the area of *peuĝ-, "to prick, stab, cut," the same root that produced Latin pungō "I prick, pierce, sting" (with its participle punctum "a prick, a point") and Greek pugmḗ "fist." Latin pungere itself went on to generate a vast and familiar family: punctum gave point, punctuation, punctual, and punctilious — all descending from the same "sharp point" image. The pinching sense enters through a Latin-derived formation meaning "to press sharply with the fingers," a small-scale, frequentative version of the pricking idea: from the needle to the fingertip.
  • Root: PIE *peuĝ- "prick, stab, cut."
  • Latin: pungō, pungere "prick, pierce, sting"; punctum "prick, point."
  • Wider family: point, puncture, punctuation, punctual, compunction — all from the same sharp-point root.
2. Vulgar Latin and Old French. Between classical Latin and the Romance languages, a Vulgar Latin verb in the area of *pinctiāre developed the meaning "to pinch, to nip, to compress sharply with the fingers." This form is inferred rather than attested — it is the standard reconstruction that bridges Latin pungere and the Romance pinch-words — and some scholars instead look to a Celtic or imitative source for the French verb. What is secure is that Old French had pincier and Old North French pinchier, "to pinch, to squeeze with the fingers, to nip," attested from the twelfth century. It is the northern French form that crossed the Channel. 3. Entry into English: Middle English pinchen. English borrowed the verb as Middle English pinchen around 1300, from Old North French pinchier. The sound of the loan was assimilated to English habits: the infinitive ending was dropped, and the word settled as pinch /pɪntʃ/, with the initial cluster and final affricate giving the word its sharp, compressed feel. From the start the verb meant to squeeze or grip with the fingers, and the noun followed directly: the act of pinching, and then — very quickly in the history of the word — the amount pinched and the pressure felt. 4. The great semantic radiation. From the single image of a finger-and-thumb squeeze, pinch generated its extraordinary family of senses over the following centuries:
  • The physical act (14th c.): to squeeze between finger and thumb; also of cold or shoes that "pinch" — sharp compression, painful or not.
  • The small measure (16th c.): "a pinch of salt" — what the fingertips can hold, a culinary idiom that remains standard.
  • Scarcity and want (16th c.): "to feel the pinch" — the economic squeeze, the pressure of poverty or shortage on a household.
  • Stinginess (16th c.): "to pinch pennies," and the compound pinch-penny for a miser — squeezing every coin.
  • The critical situation (16th c.): "in a pinch" (and British "at a pinch") — a tight spot, a moment when you are squeezed and tested; this is the idiom of the card.
  • Theft (17th–18th c. slang): "to pinch" — to steal, to lift something with a quick sharp grasp of the fingers.
  • Arrest (19th c. slang): "a pinch" — a police arrest, the criminal seized, caught in the grip of the law.
  • Compound formations: pinchbeck (a cheap gold-colored alloy, after its inventor), pinch-penny, pinchpoint — the word keeps manufacturing new nouns.
5. The idiom "in a pinch." The set phrase that governs this card freezes the situational sense: "in a pinch" (British also "at a pinch") means "when hard pressed, if necessary, when it comes to the push." Its metaphor is the physical one — the circumstances press in on you as a hand presses on a nerve — and it has been current in English since the sixteenth century. It is the ideal word for the dwarves' judgment of Bilbo: his value is not constant pomp but performance under compression. 6. On this card. In the dwarves' praise, "as fierce as a dragon in a pinch," the noun is precisely the idiomatic pinch of trial — the crisis-squeeze that proves character. Bilbo, for all his queer fits, is trusted to harden when the grip of circumstance closes. The card must preserve that bare noun of the set phrase — the emergency, the tight spot — not merely the kitchen gesture of pinching spice.
