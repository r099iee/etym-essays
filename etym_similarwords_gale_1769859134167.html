• Gust: Similar because both name winds of notable force; differs because a gust is a sudden, brief burst of wind, while a gale is sustained over hours and across a wide area.
  • Squall: Similar because both denote strong wind; differs because a squall strikes suddenly and briefly, typically with rain, snow, or thunder, while a gale persists and need not bring precipitation.
  • Tempest: Similar because both denote violent weather; differs because tempest is a grand, literary word for a raging storm (often at sea or of emotion), while gale is a precise, practical term for wind strength.
  • Storm: Similar because both describe violent atmospheric conditions; differs because storm is the general term for any severe disturbance, and on the Beaufort scale a storm (Force 11+) is actually stronger than a gale.
  • Breeze: Similar because both name moving air; differs because a breeze is mild and pleasant, while a gale is strong, sustained, and dangerous.
  • Wind: Similar because both are air in motion; differs because wind is the generic word for any air movement, while gale specifies a particular, powerful strength band.
  • Blast: Similar because both convey violent air movement; differs because a blast is a sudden, explosive gust of short duration, while a gale is prolonged and wide-ranging.
  • Hurricane: Similar because both are powerful winds; differs because a hurricane is a specific tropical cyclone with sustained winds of Force 12+ (64+ knots) and a defined circular structure, far stronger than a gale.
  • Cyclone: Similar because both are meteorological wind phenomena; differs because cyclone names a rotating storm system, while gale names a wind strength rather than a storm system.
  • Blizzard: Similar because both denote severe weather; differs because a blizzard specifically combines strong winds with heavy snowfall and poor visibility, whereas a gale is defined purely by wind strength.
  • Onslaught: Similar because both evoke an overwhelming, sustained force; differs because onslaught is a metaphor for an attack and is not a meteorological term, while gale is literal weather.
  • Rush: Similar because both can describe a surge of force or sound (as in a rush/gale of laughter); differs because rush is a general word for sudden movement, carrying no fixed wind strength.
