Archway is a compound noun — arch + way — denoting a passage or entrance formed by an arch: an opening through a wall, bridge, gatehouse, or building whose top curves over rather than running flat, so that people, traffic, or water pass through it beneath a span. In everyday use an archway is the opening itself, usually tall enough to walk or drive through, and the word is at home in two settings: the architectural (the stone archways of cathedrals, colleges, and bridges) and the experiential (walking through an archway from one space into another). The single word is comparatively modern — first recorded in 1774 — but it joins two very old English words: arch, from Old French and ultimately Latin arcus, and way, from Old English weg, “road, path, course”.

Primary sense: the arched passage. An archway is defined structurally by its arch and functionally by its passage. Unlike a plain doorway, it need not have a door; unlike a tunnel, it is short, open, and usually part of a wall or facade; unlike a gateway, which implies an entrance with a closing device, an archway is the opening itself, whether or not anything can be shut. Architecturally the arch may be semicircular (Romanesque and classical), pointed (Gothic), horseshoe (Moorish), segmental, or flat-arched (the so-called jack arch), and its components — the voussoirs (wedge-shaped blocks), the keystone at the crown, the springing where the curve begins, and the intrados and extrados (inner and outer surfaces) — form the standard vocabulary of arch-building. An archway typically carries the wall or superstructure above it while opening a passage below: bridge archways let river water and boats through; arched openings in arcades are loosely called archways; a covered passage vaulted above is more strictly an arcade or vaulted passage, though casual speech mixes the terms.

Secondary and extended senses.

  • Landscape and garden archways — free-standing arches of wrought iron, timber, or trimmed hedging (a “rose archway”) designed as ornamental entrances or as foci of garden vistas; here the word shifts from engineering to ornament.
  • Figurative use — the threshold — writers speak of “the archway of life” or of “passing through an archway into a new world”: the arched passage becomes a symbol of transition, of leaving one space (and one state) for another, because walking under an arch is an instantaneous change of place without a door’s obstruction.
  • Place-name use — “Archway” (capitalised) names the north-London district and its famous road under the Archway, a tunnel opened in 1813; the word has become a proper noun, a rare honour for an architectural term.

Boundaries and related terms. The arch alone is the curved construction; the archway adds the passage. A gateway emphasises closure and control (it implies a gate); a portal is grand, ceremonial, and usually ornate; a doorway is small, domestic, and centred on its door; an arcade is a row of arches, not a single opening; a tunnel is long, enclosed, and usually cut through rock or earth. The semantic bridge from arch to archway is simple: an arch is a shape; an archway makes that shape a route. In the story — “Under the archway of a bridge two little boys were lying” — the word does exactly its plain job: a curved opening under a bridge, wide enough for two children to shelter in, the passage that nobody is using as a passage.
