In Walter Pater's educational aesthetics, susceptibility names the cultivated openness of mind and sense through which impressions are received—the depth and variety with which one can be affected by beauty in art, landscape, and human character. In the sentence preserved on this card, Pater argues that education becomes complete in proportion as susceptibility to these impressions increases in depth and variety. The word is therefore not a symptom of fragility but the very goal of the aesthetic life: a trained, educable power of being seized by experience, the opposite of bluntness or anesthesia of taste. Complete education is proportionate to the growth of this receptive power, not to the mere accumulation of facts. The tone is affirmative, developmental, and normative: susceptibility here is a virtue of the critic and of the cultured self, the capacity that turns the mere beholder into the connoisseur. Core sense and register. At its root, susceptibility is the capacity or liability of a thing or person to be affected, acted upon, or influenced by an external agent. Abstractly it names a quality: the state or character of being easily affected, sensitive, or open to impression. The register is Latinate, formal, and analytical, spanning philosophy, psychology, medicine, and high literary prose. It typically governs the preposition to: susceptibility to impressions, influence, cold, flattery, or disease. It differs from mere sensitivity by emphasizing the passive potential of being taken or seized, and from vulnerability by emphasizing openness to impression and influence rather than exposure to injury. Polysemy and modern senses. Susceptibility has branched into several distinct senses across disciplines:
  • Aesthetic and psychological sense (the Paterian core): the refined openness of the mind to aesthetic impressions—the trained capacity to be moved by beauty, art, and human character. Here it is a positive virtue and the measure of a finished education.
  • General openness to influence: the disposition to be affected by persuasion, flattery, suggestion, or emotional appeal; close to suggestibility but without the clinical narrowing of that term.
  • Medical and clinical sense: liability to contract disease or to be harmed by an agent (susceptibility to infection, susceptibility to cold), usually opposed to immunity or resistance, and central to epidemiology and immunology.
  • Physics and materials science: magnetic susceptibility (the degree of magnetization a material acquires in an applied magnetic field) and electric susceptibility (the dielectric response to an electric field)—quantitative measures of how readily a medium responds to an external influence, preserving the root idea of response to what comes from outside.
  • Epidemiological and mathematical sense: in SIR-type compartment models, "susceptible" labels individuals who may still be infected, so susceptibility becomes a precise technical parameter of population dynamics and disease transmission.
  • Everyday and figurative use: susceptibility to flattery, advertising, superstition, or charm; a constitutional tendency regarded neutrally or negatively according to context, sometimes with a slightly old-fashioned flavor.
Historical bridge. All these senses flow from one Latin stem: suscipere, "to take up, receive, undertake," built from sub- ("up from under") and capere ("to take, seize"). The physical act of taking something up was metaphorically extended to receiving impressions, admitting influences, and being able to be affected; the quantitative senses of physics inherit the same image of a medium that "takes in" or responds to a field. The semantic logic is constant across two thousand years: from grasping to being-graspable, from seizing to being-seizable, from active taking to passive openness. Collocations and usage. Common collocations: susceptibility to (impressions, influence, cold, infection, flattery), a man of great susceptibility, nervous susceptibility, aesthetic susceptibility, magnetic susceptibility, increased susceptibility. In medical and negative contexts the word implies weakness or risk; in aesthetic and psychological contexts it implies refinement and openness. The antonymic field includes immunity, resistance, insusceptibility, impassivity, and anesthesia. Writers of Pater's generation prized the word precisely because it could carry both the clinical liability and the aesthetic capacity in a single Latinate form, letting the refined sense glow against the medical one.
