• belt: Similar because both denote an encircling strap worn around the waist; differs because belt is the standard, modern utilitarian word for an everyday leather or fabric strap with a buckle, whereas girdle is more historic, poetic, formal, or specialized (anatomical, botanical, foundation garment).
  • sash: Similar because both are bands worn around the body; differs because a sash is a wide decorative strip of cloth draped over the shoulder or tied loosely around the waist with hanging ends, whereas a girdle is a tight, secure fastening or complete enclosing perimeter.
  • cincture: Similar because both refer to a rope, belt, or sash worn around a robe; differs because cincture is an ecclesiastical and liturgical term specifically describing the cord worn by priests and monks around an alb or cassock, or an architectural ring molding on a column.
  • band: Similar because both describe a continuous strip encircling an object; differs because band is a broad, generic geometric and physical term without the anatomical, historical clothing, or waist-oriented specificity of girdle.
  • coronet: Similar because in floral and poetic contexts a girdle of petals forms a circular crown around a center; differs because a coronet is specifically a small crown or circular headpiece worn by nobility, emphasizing royal stature.
  • ring: Similar because both represent a closed circular loop or boundary; differs because ring is a universal geometric and jewelry term (finger ring), whereas girdle emphasizes the act of enclosing, binding, or supporting a three-dimensional body.
  • corset: Similar because both are supportive torso-shaping garments; differs because a corset is a rigid, heavily boned, laced bodice extending from the chest to the hips, whereas a 20th-century girdle is an elasticized, pull-on lower-torso foundation garment.
  • cummerbund: Similar because both are wide fabric waistbands; differs because a cummerbund (from Persian kamarband) is a specific pleated silk or satin sash worn horizontally with a tuxedo in formal menswear.
  • zone: Similar because both can denote an encircling geographic or celestial belt; differs because zone (from Greek zōnē "belt") has evolved primarily into an abstract administrative, climatic, or spatial boundary.
  • perimeter: Similar because both define the enclosing outer boundary of an area or object; differs because perimeter is a sterile, mathematical and geometrical term measuring boundary length, lacking organic or tactile connotations.
