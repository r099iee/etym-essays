Warrant in the source sentence is a verb of personal guarantee: But I ______ you haven't seen them doing it means I assure you, I promise you, take my word for it — the speaker staking his own credit on the truth of the claim. This assuring sense, centuries old and still alive in confident speech, is the beating heart from which all the word's sterner legal meanings grew.

In ordinary modern use the verb has two great branches. The first is the guarantee branch: I warrant the goods for a year, this plan warrants attention, nothing could warrant such cruelty — to promise, to justify, to vouch that a thing is sound or deserved. The second is the authorisation branch: a warrant as noun is a paper that empowers — an arrest warrant, a search warrant, a death warrant — and to warrant is to license or empower the bearer to act. Both branches share one image: a trustworthy backer putting weight behind a claim or an act, whether the backer is a neighbour's word or a judge's seal.

Core meaning in detail: As a verb of assurance, warrant is stronger than think and warmer than certify: I warrant he'll come carries a countryman's cheerful confidence, the speaker as surety. As a verb of justification, it measures desert — the results warrant the expense — turning approval into a quasi-legal verdict. As a noun of law, it is the state's written permission slip: police may not search or seize without one, which makes the warrant simultaneously an instrument of power and a leash on it, much argued over in every free country's courts.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To assure or promise confidently (Tolkien's sense; oldest verbal use, from the 13th c.): I warrant you haven't seen it — I give you my word. The bridge from the guarantor noun is direct: as a warrantor backs goods with his bond, so the speaker backs his statement with his honour, and the financial surety becomes a conversational one.
  • To justify or deserve, to call for (primary modern verb sense): the case warrants investigation, his rudeness warrants no reply. The bridge runs through authorisation: what is warranted is licensed by the facts themselves — the evidence issues its own paper, empowering action, and the judge is reality.
  • A legal writ empowering arrest, search, or seizure (primary modern noun sense): arrest warrant, search warrant, bench warrant. The bridge extends the guarantor's sealed paper into state power: the sovereign's guarantee (we warrant this constable to act) becomes the citizen's protection too, since power that needs paper leaves a trail.
  • A voucher or certificate of right (commercial and military): dividend warrant, share warrant, travel warrant — papers entitling the bearer to money, stock, or passage. The bridge is the same empowering paper shrunk to commerce: a promise made portable, signed, and redeemable.
  • Official authorisation in the ranks (military): a warrant officer holds his authority by warrant rather than commission — senior, specialist, and essential. The bridge is institutional: the paper that once empowered a single arrest now empowers a whole career between the non-commissioned and commissioned worlds.
  • To empower or license (verb, formal): warranted by statute, a warranted search. The bridge closes the circle: the noun's paper hardens back into a verb, so that acts themselves can be warranted — clothed in paper authority — as persons and statements were before them.

Register and connotation: warrant is confident and slightly old-fashioned in the mouth — farmers, judges, and Shakespeare's heroes warrant things; teenagers rarely do. The assurance sense carries bluff rural warmth (I warrant he'll pull through), the legal sense carries iron formality (warrant for arrest), and the justification sense carries editorial gravity. Together they give the word a rare double glow: the neighbour's handshake and the judge's seal in one syllable, which is exactly why Tolkien gives it to a hobbit — plain speech with an iron backbone.
