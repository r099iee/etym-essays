Fastness carries two great meanings that look like opposites but spring from one root. In its oldest and most poetic sense — the sense Tolkien uses — a fastness is a stronghold, a fortress, a secure refuge set among mountains or walls where defenders can hold out: his ancient fastness in the Dark Tower. In its everyday modern sense it is the abstract quality of being fast in the old sense of the word — firm, fixed, speedy, or unfading: the fastness of a dye, a friendship, or a runner.

The source sentence uses the fortress sense at full power: Sauron has returned to his ancient fastness in the Dark Tower of Mordor. Rumour, exile, and return gather round one noun: the fastness is not merely a building but a seat of power waited for and reclaimed, deep-walled, mountain-guarded, evil made architectural. Tolkien the philologist chose the word with complete awareness — Old English fæstnes meant exactly this kind of fortified refuge — and its archaism lends dread: modern English would say stronghold, but fastness sounds older, deeper, harder to take.

Core meaning in detail: Sense one (stronghold) pictures high stone walls, narrow gates, and ground that favours the defender — mountain fastnesses, forest fastnesses, desert fastnesses. The landscape itself does half the defending, which is why the word collocates with wild places: a fastness is safety borrowed from geography as much as from masonry. Sense two (firmness) divides further: (a) fixedness — being tied, locked, or held so it cannot move; (b) speed — covering ground quickly; (c) permanence — of colours, fidelity that does not wash out or fade. All three share the old adjective fast meaning firm, fixed: what is tied fast cannot slip, so it holds (fixedness), holds true (fidelity), and, by a famous paradox, the runner held least by the ground runs fastest (speed).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A fortified stronghold or secure refuge (oldest noun sense, Old English fæstnes): a mountain fastness; the rebels retreated to their forest fastness. The bridge is direct inheritance: Old English fæst meant firm, fortified, and -nes made it a place — literally the fortified place. Fantasy and history writing keep it alive.
  • The state of being fixed, firm, or secure (abstract sense): the fastness of the knot; the fastness of his grip. The bridge strips the fortress down to its quality: what makes a stronghold safe — immovability — becomes a general noun for tightness and security.
  • Speed, swiftness, rapidity (modern everyday sense): the fastness of the new train drew gasps. The bridge runs through the famous fast paradox: the Old English sense firm produced hold fast, and from running fast by (firmly past?) came the modern sense of speed; the noun followed the adjective into velocity.
  • Permanence of colour against washing or light (textile and dyeing term): this dye is guaranteed for fastness; colour-fastness ratings. The bridge is fidelity: a fast colour holds fast to the cloth and refuses to run — the fortress idea miniaturised into a fibre.
  • Steadfastness of loyalty or resolve (literary): the fastness of their friendship survived exile. The bridge carries fixedness from walls into hearts: a fast friend is a fortified friend, one who does not give way under siege.
  • Density or impenetrability of terrain (extended fortress sense): the fastnesses of the jungle swallowed the expedition. The bridge lets landscape itself be the fortress: trackless forest and swamp defend their inhabitants as surely as ramparts, hiding them in green stone.

Register and connotation: The stronghold sense is deliberately archaic and lofty — it belongs to epic, scripture, and fantasy, and using it lends any hideout the gravity of a kingdom. The speed and colour senses are plain and technical — laboratory words, catalogue words. Context therefore flips the tone entirely: his mountain fastness promises banners and sieges, while colour fastness promises laundry instructions. Both, however, promise holding: nothing in a fastness slips, runs, fades, or yields, whether it is a Dark Lord or a dyed shirt.
