Harum-scarum is a rhyming compound word — “a harum-scarum,” as the verse-portrait of Bao-yu in Chapter 3 of Dream of the Red Chamber puts it, “to all duty blind.” As a noun it names a person of reckless, impetuous, flighty energy; the word pictures someone who dashes through life headlong, zigzagging like a chased hare, heedless of the scares of consequence or duty. In the poem the word is the first blow of the jeering couplet, setting the tone for everything that follows: Bao-yu is a “doltish mule” disinclined to study, a wild creature who cannot be caught by the world of decorum. The tone is affectionate-condemning — the half-admiring mockery of a schoolmaster or an exasperated aunt — and the very sound of the rhyming compound, tripping and tumbling over its own syllables, mimics the erratic, undignified motion it describes.

The compound works on three grammatical levels at once, and that versatility is part of its comic energy:
  • Noun: “a harum-scarum” names the person as a type — a reckless, impetuous, heedless fellow. This is the sense at work in the poem, where the word stands in apposition to the jeering couplet: Bao-yu is pronounced “a harum-scarum, to all duty blind.” The noun use is attested from the early nineteenth century (c. 1803).
  • Adjective: “harum-scarum ways,” “a harum-scarum lad,” “a harum-scarum scheme” — qualifying conduct, character, or undertakings that are wild, disorderly, and entered upon without thought. This is the earliest use, recorded from the 1670s.
  • Adverb: “to go rushing harum-scarum about the town,” “to run harum-scarum down the street” — describing a headlong, helter-skelter manner of motion. The adverb converts the compound into a picture of how the person moves, not merely of what the person is.
  • Predicative complement: “he is quite harum-scarum” — describing a person’s settled temperament, with the compound functioning like a bare adjective after a copula.

Polysemy and family relations. Beyond its three syntactic uses, harum-scarum belongs to a large expressive family of English rhyming reduplications that picture disorder — helter-skelter, pell-mell, hurly-burly, higgledy-piggledy, topsy-turvy, hubble-bubble. These words are structural siblings rather than etymological relatives: each is built on a jingling vowel alternation (i-a, u-a) that makes the sound of the word mimic the motion it names, and each conveys the same half-laughing judgement of chaos. In metaphor the compound reaches beyond people: “the harum-scarum of war” or “harum-scarum politics” applies the same headlong, undirected energy to events, campaigns, or enterprises, so that a whole historical process can be dismissed as a wild, aimless chase. The semantic reasoning is consistent: whatever is harum-scarum dashes forward without plan, like a hare pursued, frightening others and being frightened in turn, and the word always retains the grin of the schoolmaster who is half-impressed by the wildness he condemns.

Register and collocation. Collocations show its informal, dated, chiefly British register: “a harum-scarum lad or fellow,” “harum-scarum ways,” “to go rushing harum-scarum about the town,” used predicatively and adverbially as well as as a noun. The word belongs to the same expressive family of rhyming reduplications that English loves for disorderly motion, and like them it is more often met in nineteenth-century fiction than in modern speech, where its mock-scholarly Latin flourish has faded into pure comic bounce. In the poem it does double work: it condemns Bao-yu’s wildness with mock-indulgent laughter, and its bouncing rhythm mirrors the very unruliness the adult world complains of, so that even the metre seems to shrug off duty. The historical bridge from “harrying-and-scaring” to “a reckless person” is transparent: the man who spends his life harrying and being scared, chasing and fleeing, is by definition the man who can never settle to duty or study — the perfect nickname for Bao-yu, whose “acts outlandish” and “nature queer” make him the household’s own lovable harum-scarum.
