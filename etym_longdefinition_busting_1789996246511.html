busting is the present participle of the verb bust — in this context a colloquial variant of burst — meaning to break open, break apart, or burst energetically into somewhere or into action. In your context — He's driven me frantic busting into things on his own initiative — it means bursting in, barging in, charging into situations uninvited: rushing into rooms, missions, or affairs on impulse, without orders or permission, with more energy than discretion. The irritation (driven me frantic) comes from well-meaning but uncontrolled initiative.

Bust / burst is one of English's most tangled families because two historically distinct words — (1) bust = burst, to break and (2) bust = torso sculpture — converged in spelling while remaining unrelated in origin. Only the first is relevant here, but the second must be disambiguated so learners do not confuse busting into things (bursting in) with busting (sculpting or arresting).

  • 1. Bursting into a place (your context — busting into things): busting into rooms, busting into meetings, busting in unannounced. Means to enter suddenly, forcibly, or without knocking — pushing doors, interrupting, surprising occupants. The bridge from burst is kinetic: as a dam bursts inward, so a person bursts into a room. Often with in, into, through: bust in, bust into, bust through the door. Connotation: energetic, rude, or heroic depending on viewpoint — a rescuer busts in to save; a subordinate busts in to annoy.
  • 2. Breaking apart or open (general bust = break): busting a lock, busting a pipe, busting open a crate, busting a balloon. Colloquial equivalent of bursting, breaking. Past forms vary: busted / bust for this sense (he busted the door) versus burst in formal style. The bridge is physical rupture by pressure from inside or blow from outside.
  • 3. Bursting with fullness or effort (intransitive): busting with pride, busting a gut (laughing or labouring), busting at the seams. Here the subject swells until it nearly ruptures. Busting a gut splits into two branches: (a) to work extremely hard (busting a gut to meet the deadline), (b) to laugh violently (busting a gut laughing). Both image the belly straining to bursting.
  • 4. To arrest, raid, or catch (slang, chiefly US): busted for drugs, drug-busting cops, bust a criminal. From bust as a sudden breaking-in (police busting down a door) extended to the arrest itself, then to the raid as noun (a drug bust). Past: busted. Register: police procedural and street slang, now mainstream. The bridge is the violent entry of a raid.
  • 5. To demote, ruin, or go bankrupt (figurative breaking): busted to private, bust a bronco (to tame by breaking), go bust, bust the bank. Military bust (reduce in rank) images breaking stripes; financial go bust images a broken enterprise; gambling bust (exceed 21 in blackjack) images a broken hand. All share rupture of status or fortune.
  • 6. To hit or punch (slang): bust him in the nose, bust a move (originally a sharp dance move). From breaking by blow. Bust a move travelled from 1980s hip-hop (to break into motion) to general take action.
  • 7. HOMOGRAPH TO DISAMBIGUATE — bust = torso sculpture (unrelated): a marble bust of Caesar, bust portrait. From Italian busto (torso, trunk), from Latin bustum (funeral monument). No sense of breaking; pronunciation identical (/bʌst/) but etymology and meaning wholly separate. Learners must not read busting into things as sculpting. Similarly busty (having a large bust) belongs to this sculpture / bodice family, not to bursting.
  • 8. Grammatical note: bust vs burst vs busted. Formal burst – burst – bursting (burst the balloon, burst into tears) versus colloquial bust – busted/bust – busting (bust the balloon, bust into the room, busted flat). In progressive busting, the two verbs are nearly interchangeable, but busting is more informal, American, and physical. Your science-fiction dialogue chooses busting for voice: a harassed superior complaining in spoken style, not writing a report with bursting.

Why so many senses? Because breaking is a universal human experience — doors, balloons, bellies, banks, ranks, laws — and bust/burst supplies its most abrupt, noisy, and vivid verb. Once bust split from burst by metathesis (swapping /r/ and vowel), it became the demotic twin: shorter, punchier, freer to colonise slang (raids, demotions, bankruptcy, punches) while burst kept the literary and emotional ground (burst into tears, burst with joy). Your busting into things sits at the intersection: physical bursting-in that annoys precisely because it is energetic and unregulated.

Register: informal to neutral in this participial use; authoritative complaint softened by familiarity. To say an operative is busting into things is to call him keen but undisciplined — a headstrong junior whose initiative outruns his orders.
