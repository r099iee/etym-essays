Bulging is the present participle of the verb bulge, used here as an adjective: swelling or protruding outward beyond the normal level of a surface, forming a rounded, convex protuberance. In the literary context of this note, from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), it describes "big bulging blind eyes" — the eyes of the slimy, nameless creatures the traveller imagines wriggling in the dark water of the underground lake: eyes that stick out roundly from their sockets and, being blind, see nothing at all. The word carries a deliberately grotesque and repellent tone: a bulge is a swelling that should not be there, a distension of a normally flat surface, and applied to eyes it conjures an image of unnatural, almost diseased protrusion. Combined with big and blind it is the very stuff of horror and disgust. The participial form suggests a permanent state — the eyes do not swell and subside; they simply are bulging — and the word's heavy rounded vowels and hard g's give it a bloated, waterlogged sound suited to the slimy things of the deep.

Primary sense (literal). The core meaning is physical and visual: to swell out or project beyond a surface or container wall because of internal pressure, as a full sack, a stuffed pocket, a swollen wall, or a straining muscle bulges. The image is always of a flexible boundary holding contents that press it outward — hence the everyday idiom of a "bulging wallet" or "bulging suitcase."

Secondary senses and modern uses.
  • Anatomy and medicine: bulging eyes (proptosis), a bulging disc, a bulging vein or fontanelle — protrusion of bodily structures, often with pathological overtones.
  • Cartography and geometry: a bulge is any outward-curving protuberance on a line or surface — a bulge in a wall, the bulging horizon, a bulge in a curve on a graph.
  • Demography and statistics: a "population bulge" or "baby bulge" — a temporary excess of one age group in a population structure, a sense that treats the data itself as a swelling surface.
  • Figurative transfer (content): "bulging with" means filled to capacity with something — a notebook bulging with ideas, a year bulging with events; the historical bridge is the image of an overfull container pressing at its sides.
  • Register: the word is informal, vivid, and tactile; it lacks the clinical coolness of protuberant and the neutral technicality of protruding.

Grammar note. As an adjective formed from a present participle, bulging denotes an ongoing or inherent state rather than a completed action; it stands before the noun (bulging eyes) and, because the underlying verb is intransitive in this sense, it never takes an object. The noun bulge coexists with the adjective and, in the twentieth century, gained a famous military sense: the Battle of the Bulge (1944–45), named for the outward salient the German offensive drove into the Allied lines — the same image of a swelling protuberance on a surface applied to the front line of a war.
