thrust — pushed or driven with sudden, concentrated force in a specific direction; the past of thrust. In “Four directions was he thrust, then one,” the verb is the engine of the whole sentence’s geometry: the wizard is not thrown but thrust, four times, each time in a different compass direction, and then once more — the verb is a vector, describing not the flight but the directing of it, a force as exact and impersonal as a piston or a lance. Thrust is the weapon-word of the family (the thrust of a sword or spear), and in this passage it makes the directions themselves the blade: the cloud shoves Turjan along four lines of the compass like a coin pushed across a board, and the word’s brevity and hardness — a monosyllable that stops dead on the -t — mime the sudden stop-start of each shunt.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. Thrust descends from PIE *treud-, “to press, push, squeeze,” through Old Norse þrysta, “to press, squeeze, thrust,” borrowed into Middle English around 1300. The semantic bridge is pressure: the root named the force of one body bearing upon another, and the English word has kept that meaning of directed, concentrated pressure — a push along a line, not a swing. Its past tense is identical to its present (thrust, thrust, thrust), a native strong-verb pattern that suits a force over in the same moment it begins.

Primary senses of thrust.
  • Pushed with sudden, concentrated force in a specific direction (he thrust the sword forward).
  • To force one’s way or one’s presence into a place (thrust oneself forward; thrust into prominence).
  • To pierce or stab with a pointed weapon (a thrust of the lance).
  • As a noun: the main point or drive of something (the thrust of the argument); the mechanical push of engines and rockets (rocket thrust).

hurled — thrown with great force and rushing momentum; the past of hurl. In “at last a great blow hurled him from the cloud,” the blow itself is the thrower: Turjan is not thrown by a hand but by a strike, so that the hurling is the trajectory of a body hit with immense force — a stone from a catapult, a man from the fist of a god. The literary tone is pure ballistic physics: the verb compresses the whole journey into one syllable of release, after which the body is simply in flight.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. Hurl descends from an imitative Germanic base *hurr-, “rapid motion; roaring sound,” with no established PIE ancestry — the word is probably onomatopoeic, built from the rush and roar of something moving at speed. Middle English hurlen first meant “to run against, to collide” (c. 1225), then “to throw forcibly” (c. 1300). The OED’s classic distinction is worth keeping: in hurl the notion is forcible projection, in hurtle forcible collision.

Primary senses of hurled.
  • Thrown with great force (he hurled the spear).
  • Projected by a violent blow or impact (a great blow hurled him from the cloud).
  • Directed with violence, especially of words: hurled abuse, hurled accusations.
  • As a noun or in compounds: a violent throw; the Irish game hurling; hurly-burly, a commotion (1530s).

sprawled — spread out or lying with the limbs flung open in an awkward, ungainly way; the past of sprawl. In “a great blow hurled him from the cloud, sprawled him into Embelyon,” the word completes the sentence’s third and final stage: after the thrusting (the vectoring) and the hurling (the projection), the sprawled is the landing — the body arriving without grace, arms and legs scattered every which way, like a dropped puppet.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. Sprawl descends from PIE *sper-, “to scatter, spread,” through Proto-Germanic *spreaw-, and Old English sprēawlian, “to move convulsively, to writhe.” The semantic bridge runs from convulsive writhing (the earliest sense) to the ungainly spread posture (the modern sense): a body that writhes without control comes to rest sprawled, limbs thrown out in every direction, and from the posture the word extended to anything that spreads without plan — urban sprawl, the sprawl of a city across the land.

Primary senses of sprawled.
  • Lying or fallen with the limbs spread out in an awkward, ungraceful way (he sprawled on the sofa).
  • Moved or writhed convulsively (the earliest recorded sense).
  • Spread out irregularly over a wide area (sprawling city; urban sprawl).
  • As a noun: an unplanned, disorganized extension or spread (the sprawl of the suburbs).

How the three work together. The sentence is a three-act physics of violent transport: thrust is the exact, directed push along the lines of the compass; hurled is the great blow that projects him from the cloud; sprawled is the ungainly arrival. Together they move Turjan from the ordered geometry of directions to the scattered chaos of the landing — from vector to trajectory to collapse.
