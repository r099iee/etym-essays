Origin debated between two routes; the standard account for English derives the word from Greek via Vulgar Latin, with a parallel Germanic form: - Greek tŷphos (τῦφος) 'steam, smoke, vapour; heat' (attested Classical Greek, 5th c. BCE) — candidate source of the 'steam' element in the Romance base. - Vulgar Latin *extūfare 'to heat in steam, to give a vapour-bath' (reconstructed, late antique / early medieval period; from ex- plus the *tūf- element and the Greek-derived idea of steam; the exact derivation is debated) — the source of the Romance forms. - Old French estuve (noun) 'a heated room, sweating-room, vapour-bath, bath-house' (attested 11th–12th c.) and estuver (verb) 'to bathe in hot water or steam; to stew (food)' (attested 12th c.). - Middle English stewe (noun) 'a heated room, a bath-house, a sweating-house' (attested 13th c.; borrowed from Old French estuve, with the initial vowel lost). - Middle English stewes (plural) 'the bath-house brothels of a city; a brothel district' (attested late 14th–15th c.; the licensed stews of Southwark, London). - English stews 'brothels; a place or district of ill repute' (historical). The same stem separately yields the culinary noun 'stew' and verb 'to stew' (14th c., via Old French estuver 'to cook slowly in moist heat') and the figurative verb 'to stew' = 'to fret' (19th c.). Debate note: two competing origins are proposed for the Romance base. (1) The Graeco-Latin hypothesis derives Vulgar Latin *extūfare from Greek tŷphos 'steam, smoke' (via ex- + *tūf-); (2) an older view derives the whole family from a Germanic root *stubō 'heated room' (source of German Stube 'room, parlour' and English stove, originally 'heated room'), with the Romance words borrowed from Germanic. Both routes converge on the same meaning ('heated room / bath'), and the exact path into Old French is not directly attested. The fish-pond sense of 'stew' is from a different Old French word, estui 'enclosure.'
