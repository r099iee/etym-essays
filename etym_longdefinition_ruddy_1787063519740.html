Ruddy is an adjective meaning (1) having a healthy reddish color, especially of the face or complexion; and (2) red or reddish in general hue, especially with a warm, glowing, or vigorous quality. In the source sentence the dragon's hoard lies bathed in "the ruddy light" — a red-gold glow, warm and fiery, that fits the word's core sense of luminous redness perfectly. It is the color of embers, sunsets, autumn, and the flush of health rather than the red of danger or blood.

Primary senses:

  • Of a person or face: having a healthy red color. A ruddy complexion, ruddy cheeks, a ruddy face — the redness of good health, vigor, wind, exercise, or hearty embarrassment. This is the oldest and most central sense, going back to the very origins of the word in Old English.
  • Red or reddish in color generally. Ruddy light, a ruddy sky, a ruddy sunset — a warm, glowing, sometimes firelit red, often with brown or golden undertones rather than a pure crimson.
  • Of objects: reddish-brown. Ruddy soil, ruddy iron, ruddy wood, ruddy sand — the coloring of earth, rust, autumn leaves, and weathered materials.

Secondary and specialized senses:

  • British minced oath. Ruddy is used in British English as a mild intensifier replacing the stronger "bloody": "you ruddy fool," "ruddy well done." This use, recorded from the early twentieth century, treats the word as a euphemistic disguise for bloody, playing on shared force while remaining printable — a color adjective turned into an everyday swear-substitute.
  • Ornithological and zoological names. Ruddy appears in taxonomic names of reddish species: the ruddy duck, the ruddy turnstone, the ruddy shelduck — a plain color adjective pressed into scientific naming.
  • Archaic: vigorous, hearty, stout. Older use could extend the health-idea of the complexion sense to a person's whole build — a ruddy, sturdy yeoman — the complexion sense writ large.

Register and connotation. Ruddy is warm and vigorous. It is the color of health, fire, sunrise, and autumn rather than of danger or blood (unlike crimson or scarlet). Its affectionate overtones make it a favorite of descriptive prose: a ruddy face is honest and hearty, ruddy light is cozy and treasure-like. In the Tolkien passage, ruddy light makes the hoard glow like embers, linking the word to both fire and health.

Etymology in brief. Ruddy descends from Old English rudig, formed from rūd (redness, a ruddy complexion) with the adjective suffix -ig, and is a close cousin of the plain word red — both descend from the Indo-European color root for red.

Historical and cultural depth. Ruddy has always carried the stamp of the living, warm world. In poetry and prose alike it belongs to faces, hearth-fires, harvests, and sunsets — the red of things that glow with life rather than the red of wounds. This warmth is exactly why British slang borrowed it as a mild, affectionate-sounding substitute for a stronger swear word: even as an expletive, ruddy keeps a trace of its healthy, hearty origins, softening profanity into something almost rustic and good-natured.
