- Early 15th Century - 'Incur' first appears in English, derived from Old French 'encourir' and Latin 'incurrere.' Its initial sense was often quite literal: 'to run into, rush upon, attack.' Connotation: Active, sometimes aggressive, physical movement towards something.
- 16th Century - The meaning shifts and specializes towards the figurative sense of 'to fall into, meet with, bring upon oneself,' particularly negative consequences like danger, displeasure, or debt. Connotation: Increasingly negative, implying responsibility for an undesirable outcome.
- 17th-19th Centuries - The word solidifies its primary usage in contexts of financial burdens (incur debt, expenses), legal penalties (incur a fine, liability), and social disapproval (incur wrath, criticism). Connotation: Formal, often legalistic or economic, consistently associated with adverse results.
- 20th Century to Present - 'Incur' remains a standard term in formal, business, legal, and academic language. Its meaning is stable, almost exclusively referring to bringing about or becoming subject to something undesirable. Connotation: Unchanged, precise, and formal, used to denote direct causation of negative outcomes.
