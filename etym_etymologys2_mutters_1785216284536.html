The etymology of MUTTERS reaches back to a sound, not a meaning: the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European imitative base *mut- "to grunt, mutter." Proto-Indo-European imitative base *mut- "to grunt, mutter" (reconstructed; a sound-symbolic base formed with compressed lips — the very posture of mumbling — with no ordinary non-imitative Proto-Indo-European reflex; no certain date, prehistoric c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Germanic branch: Middle English moteren "to mumble, to utter words in a low tone with compressed lips" (early 14th century; a frequentative formation, the -er suffix giving the sense of repeated, habitual action as in stammer and wander) → Modern English mutter (spelling fixed by the end of the Middle English period) → English mutters (third-person singular present tense, formed with -s; the noun mutter "a murmur" follows in 1630). NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: the base *mut- is imitative, so the Germanic and Italic words are parallel creations from the same expressive root rather than direct descendants of a single spoken ancestor. In the Italic branch the same imitative base produced Latin muttire "to mutter, mumble, murmur" → Late Latin muttum "a grunt, a word" → Italian motto "word, saying" → (via Old French) English motto (1580s) — an independent line, not an ancestor of mutter. Old Norse muðla "to murmur" and Old High German mutilon "to murmur, mutter" (whence dialectal German muttern) are likewise parallel imitations, not stages of the English word. No Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia cognates are attested for *mut-. The English chain itself is short: imitative base → moteren (early 14th c.) → mutter → mutters.
