Alboin (prop. n.) — King of the Lombards (reigned c. 560–572 CE) who led his Germanic nation out of Pannonia, across the Alps into Italy in 568, conquered the Po Valley, and established the Lombard Kingdom (capital Pavia) before his assassination in a legendary domestic conspiracy. On this card the word appears in the sentence "All the chiefs who founded Teutonic dynasties in the continental provinces of the Roman empire, Alaric, Theodoric, Clovis, Alboin, were zealous Christians": the name stands in the dignified roll-call of the Germanic founder-kings who, unlike the still-pagan rulers of Wessex, embraced Christianity.

Sense 1 — The historical king (the primary sense). Alboin succeeded his father Audoin as king of the Lombards, defeated the Gepids with Avar help (the source of the famous skull-cup legend — he was said to have drunk from the skull of the slain Gepid king Cunimund), and then in 568 led the Lombards into Italy, overwhelming the Byzantine position and seizing Pavia as his capital in 572. His reign marks the end of Byzantine rule in northern Italy and the beginning of Lombard rule, which endured until Charlemagne conquered the kingdom in 774.

Sense 2 — The legendary and literary sense. In Germanic heroic tradition Alboin is remembered as a generous warrior-king: the Old English poem Widsith mentions him as a lord who gave treasure, and the Old English form of his name, Elfwine ("elf-friend"), makes him the structural twin of his own Germanic name. The legend of his murder — instigated by his wife Rosamund, daughter of the slain Gepid king, after the skull-cup outrage — made him the archetype of the tragic barbarian king in medieval chronicle and in modern historiography alike.

Sense 3 — The onomastic sense. The name means "elf-friend," a classic two-element Germanic anthroponym: alb- "elf" (literally "the white, shining being") + -win "friend, beloved," the same second element found in the Old English poetic word wine "friend" of the comitatus.

  • Register: a historical proper name used in chronicles, historiography, and the study of the migration period; it has no everyday modern sense.
  • Collocations: King Alboin, the Lombards under Alboin, Alboin's invasion of Italy (568), the skull-cup legend of Alboin, Paul the Deacon's History of the Lombards.
  • Historical bridge for the name's meaning: a pagan Germanic "elf-friend" name became, through its bearer and the medieval legends around him, the emblem of the barbarian founder-king — remembered less for conversion than for the great descent into Italy that gave the Lombards their kingdom.
  • Distinction from Theodoric and Alaric: those are Gothic rulers (Visigoth and Ostrogoth respectively); Alboin is the Lombard, and his invasion (568) is the last of the great Germanic migrations into the western empire.
  • Geographic note: the region Alboin conquered is still called Lombardy (from the medieval Latin name of the Lombards, Langobardia), so the king's name and his people's name survive today in the geography of northern Italy — a permanent toponymic trace of his conquest.
  • Source note: the principal early source for Alboin is Paul the Deacon's History of the Lombards (8th century), which preserves both the historical record and the legends (including the skull-cup story and Rosamund's revenge) that made the king a literary archetype.
