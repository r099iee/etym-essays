expatiating is the present participle of the verb expatiate, meaning speaking or writing at great length and in comprehensive detail about a subject; enlarging upon or dilating over a topic as though roaming freely across a wide space. The word's core image is spatial: to expatiate is etymologically to step outside the enclosure and walk freely — Latin exspatiari, 'to wander out of bounds, to roam beyond the course.' The subject is treated like open country, and the speaker ranges across it with freedom and relish, covering every feature of the ground, the opposite of the narrow, clipped, businesslike statement.

In the literary context of this card the word captures an obsessive, tireless enthusiasm: John Stuart Mill 'was never weary of expatiating on all the advantages of so singular a relationship.' The participle points to an action repeated without exhaustion — he went on, again and again, ranging over every benefit of his friendship with Harriet Taylor. The spatial ancestry animates the image: expatiating is not merely praising at length but roaming across a subject with freedom and relish, the rhetorical equivalent of a long walk through open country, and the phrase 'was never weary of' underlines the relish — for Mill the topic was not a chore but an inexhaustible ground to wander over.

The senses and uses may be catalogued:

  • Rhetorical core sense: to speak or write at length and in detail upon a subject, ranging freely over its parts — 'to expatiate on/upon a topic'; the standard preposition is on or upon.
  • Original literal sense (now rare): to roam, wander, or range widely in space — 'to expatiate through the countryside'; survives chiefly in older poetry and prose and in the rhetorical memory of the word.
  • Of rivers and natural force: (of a river) to overflow its banks, to spread out of its course — a classical Latin usage (exspatiari) occasionally echoed in English.
  • Intellectual and conversational use: to dilate at large over a favourite theme; to be expansive, discursive, and generous in treating a subject; often implies enthusiasm, pleasure, and leisure.
  • Register: formal, literary, and slightly old-fashioned; used in biography, essay, and rhetorical prose. The noun expatiation and the agent noun expatiator exist but are rare.

expatiating must be separated from its near-synonyms. Elaborating means working a point out in careful structural detail, and implies constructive labour rather than expansive discourse. Dilating (dilate upon) means to expand or amplify at length and is a genuine synonym, but it is now rare in this sense and is more familiar from the unrelated sense 'to widen' (of the pupils); it lacks the image of free movement. Enlarging upon a topic is close, but it pictures growth of the subject itself rather than the speaker's roaming. Digressing is the negative counterpart: to wander away from the subject into irrelevancies, a deviation to be excused, whereas expatiating stays on the subject while covering it exhaustively and is a positive rhetorical performance. Discoursing is formal and reasoned but not necessarily expansive, while rhapsodizing adds a note of effusive, emotional transport that does not fit an intellectual catalogue of 'advantages.' The distinctive nuance of expatiating is the blend of length, freedom, and relish: the speaker roams at large across the whole subject with evident pleasure.
