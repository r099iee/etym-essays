1. Ancient Egyptian and Afroasiatic origins. The etymology of ivory represents one of the ancient trading journeys from Pharaonic Egypt across the Mediterranean basin into Western European languages. The ultimate source is the Ancient Egyptian word ꜣbw (vocalized as âb or âbu), meaning both "elephant" and "ivory". The ancient trade island of Elephantine on the Nile (Egyptian Ybwt) served as the primary mercantile customs post through which elephant tusks from Nubia and sub-Saharan Africa entered the Mediterranean world. This Afroasiatic root is also reflected in Coptic ebou ("elephant") and Biblical Hebrew shenhabbīm (literally "teeth of elephants", 1 Kings 10:22, where habbīm borrows the Egyptian root).

2. Transmission into Classical Latin and monumental arts. The Egyptian term was adopted into Latin during the early Roman Republic as the neuter noun ebur (genitive eboris), denoting raw ivory tusks and carved artifacts. In ancient Roman and Greek culture, ivory was among the most revered artistic materials:

  • In Classical Greece, Phidias created the monumental "chryselephantine" (gold and ivory) colossal statues of Zeus at Olympia (one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World) and Athena Parthenos inside the Parthenon.
  • Roman patricians and consuls used ivory for curule magistrate chairs (sella curulis), consular diptychs (folding writing tablets presented to dignitaries), and luxurious furniture inlays.

3. Vulgar Latin adjectival mutation and Old French transition. In Vulgar Latin, the neuter noun ebur was supplemented by the adjectival neuter derivative eboreum ("of ivory"), which underwent fronting and vowel dissimilation in Western Romance to *eborium and then *ivorium, raising the initial unaccented vowel from /e/ to /i/. This yielded Old French yvoire (Modern French ivoire), Spanish marfil (borrowed from Arabic ‘aẓm al-fīl "elephant bone"), and Italian avorio (preserving the /a/ variant from Arabic/Latin interactions).

4. Middle English adoption and Gothic devotional art. Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman scribes introduced the word into Middle English around 1250 as ivori or yvory. During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Paris became the international capital of Gothic ivory carving. Master ateliers produced portable devotional triptychs, miniature Madonna and Child statues, mirror backs depicting courtly romances, and oliphants (hunting horns, celebrated in The Song of Roland). To medieval minds, ivory symbolized virgin purity, spiritual incorruptibility, and royal dignity.

5. Scriptural iconography, poetry, and the "Ivory Tower". The literary and theological symbolism of ivory was deeply influenced by the Vulgate Bible:

  • In the Song of Songs (7:4), the bride's beauty is praised: "Collum tuum sicut turris eburnea" ("Thy neck is as a tower of ivory"), establishing the classic poetic link between smooth ivory and radiant physical perfection.
  • In the Roman Catholic Litany of Loreto, Turris eburnea became one of the devotional titles of the Virgin Mary, symbolizing unblemished spiritual purity.
  • In 1837, the French literary critic Charles Augustin Sainte-Beuve famously secularized this phrase in his poem Pensées d'Août, applying tour d'ivoire ("ivory tower") to Alfred de Vigny to describe the poet's aristocratic withdrawal from public politics into pure art. This phrase permanently entered international idioms to denote academic and aesthetic seclusion.

6. Victorian Aestheticism and Oscar Wilde. In the late nineteenth-century Aesthetic Movement, ivory became a favourite sensory and visual motif for writers like Walter Pater, Théophile Gautier, and Oscar Wilde. In Wilde's "The Nightingale and the Rose" (1888), the student's face is compared to "pale ivory": "his brow is like the hyacinth-blossom, and his lips are red as the rose of his desire; but passion has made his face like pale ivory, and sorrow has set her seal upon his brow." Here, ivory functions not merely as a colour, but as an emblem of passion sublimated into artistic and physical exhaustion—the pale, statuesque martyr of romantic idealism.

7. Modern ecological ethics and conservation. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the global perception of ivory underwent a profound ethical transformation. The devastating impact of the ivory trade on African elephant populations led to the 1989 CITES international commercial ban. Today, the noun retains its historical, literary, and colour associations, while the material itself stands as a catalyst for global wildlife preservation and anti-poaching jurisprudence.
