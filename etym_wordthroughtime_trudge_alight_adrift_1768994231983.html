- trudge
- Expressive birth (16th c., Scotland & the north) - Surfaces as an imitative coinage for walking laboriously; the blunt tr- and heavy -udge perform the meaning — feet planted in mud; a word of the walking poor, soldiers, servants, and laborers.
- Plain narrative spread (17th–19th c.) - Spreads through humble prose; the noun (a long trudge) and the fixed phrase trudge on fix the sense of weary, unavoidable endurance — a walk that must go on.
- The homely verb (20th c.–present) - Stays plain and informal, almost never used of vehicles or speed; its very homeliness lets the poet lend it to machines, making great steamboats 'hoot and trudge past' like exhausted men.
- alight
- Kindled (Old English) - Built from a- 'on' + the verb to light, 'to kindle'; the adjective of ignition, a thing set burning.
- Predicative fire (Middle English–18th c.) - Settles as a literary word meaning 'ablaze, illuminated' — set alight, caught alight — kept alive in poetry while the kitchen says the plain 'lit.'
- Steady illumination (19th c.–present) - Extends from literal fire to the maintained glow of windows, faces, and rooms (eyes alight with joy); keeps a faintly archaic, ceremonial radiance in descriptive prose.
- adrift
- Broken loose (16th–17th c.) - Coined from a- 'in' + drift: purely nautical — floating at random, cut from its moorings, at the mercy of current and wind; a failure of seamanship.
- Sea & weather (18th–19th c.) - Serves the logbook and the forecast — set adrift, cast adrift, a raft adrift — the language of vessels that have lost their hold on the land.
- Modern uprooting (19th–20th c.–present) - Goes psychological and social: a person adrift in the city, adrift from family and friends; the nautical helplessness becomes the master-metaphor for a life unmoored.
