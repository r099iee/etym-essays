ISLES is the plural form of isle, denoting "islands" — bodies of land surrounded by water on all sides and smaller than a continent. It is the archaic and poetic counterpart of the everyday plural islands. Both words mean the same thing in the strictest geographic sense, but isles is freighted with literary resonance: it appears in romantic verse, in fixed place names, and in formulaic collocations such as "the British Isles", "the Isles of Greece", and "the Western Isles".

  • Primary sense: two or more islands considered together as a group, often scattered across a sea, lake, or river system. In the source sentence, small islands dot the marshes and pools of a flooded river plain.
  • Poetic and rhetorical sense: islands as figures of refuge, solitude, romance, or exotic remoteness — "an isle of calm", "the Isles of the Blessed" of classical myth.
  • Place-name and geographic sense: fixed in proper nouns such as the British Isles, the Channel Isles, the West Indies (historically "the West Isles"), and the Hebrides or "Western Isles".
  • Historical and administrative sense: in British usage "the Isles" can elliptically mean the British Isles, or the islands of a given archipelago or country (e.g. the islands of Scotland).

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: English inherited isle from Old French and ultimately from Latin insula "island". The plural isles emerged in Middle English alongside the singular. Because the word was reintroduced in its Latin-influenced spelling during the Renaissance, it acquired a bookish, elevated flavour that never attached to the native island, which continued as the plain everyday word. Consequently isles developed a stylistic secondary meaning — "literary, archaic, or formal" — rather than a wholly new dictionary sense. Register, not reference, became its defining feature.

Collocations and usage domains:

  • Geography: "the isles of the archipelago", "isles of the sea".
  • Literature: Byron's "The Isles of Greece"; the isles of Tolkien, Homer, and medieval romance.
  • Heritage and ornithology: "Isle of Man", "Faroe Isles" (though "Faroe Islands" is now standard).
  • Metaphor: "a sea of isles", "isles of cloud" extend the word to any clustered, isolated mass.

Secondary and figurative meanings today:

  • Figurative refuge: a person or place isolated from its surroundings is called an isle — "an isle of sanity", "isles of greenery" in a city.
  • Cluster metaphors: "isles" of vegetation, of light, or of population in geography and urban planning, transferred from land to any isolated patch.
  • Historical toponymy: the spelling with silent -s- (from Latin insula) marks the word as bookish; earlier English wrote ile, and the modern plural occasionally appears as iles in names, as in the "Lofoten Isles".

Register and nuance: isles is never technical. Cartographers and geologists write islands, islets, or archipelagos. Isles belongs to elevated prose, verse, songs, and rhetorical flourishes, and using it in plain speech deliberately creates a poetic, nostalgic, or grandiose tone. It is rarer in American than in British English, where it lingers in royal and imperial collocations ("the home isles", "the British Isles").

Grammar and form: isle is a regular count noun; the plural isles is formed with -s and is pronounced identically to iles (/aɪlz/). It can appear attributively ("isles resorts") only in promotional or poetic language. There is no difference in meaning between singular and plural beyond count.

Distinguishing from near-synonyms: islets are very small islands; islands is the neutral generic term; archipelago is the collective noun for a group of isles; atolls, keys, and cays are specific low coral or sand islands; skerries are bare rocky islets. Isles overlaps all of these in denotation but adds the marked literary register.
