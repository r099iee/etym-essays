The compound handcarts joins two histories of very different temper: hand, one of the oldest and most stubbornly native words in English, whose deeper origins remain honestly unknown, and cart, a well-travelled wanderer that rolled from an Indo-European verb for running through Celtic chariots and Roman wagons into Norse streets before reaching the Shire.

First element — hand: Old English hand or hond continues Proto-Germanic *handuz, attested across every Germanic language from Gothic handus to Old Norse hǫnd. Beyond Germanic, however, standard etymology stops: unlike foot or heart, hand has no agreed Indo-European pedigree. Some older handbooks linked it tentatively to roots meaning to seize or grasp, but modern references, including the OED, leave it explicitly of unknown or disputed origin, a reminder that even the commonest body-words can keep their secrets. What is certain is its combining power: once fixed, hand- becomes English's favourite prefix for the manual — hand-mill, hand-barrow, hand-loom — preparing the way for hand-cart.

  • Sound and form: Proto-Germanic *handuz loses its ending by Old English to give hand, and the spelling with o in early texts records a rounded vowel that later unrounds, leaving the rock-solid monosyllable every child learns first.
  • Sense growth: from the body-part, hand extends to handling, skill, power, and agency — a hand at work, a lend a hand — so that hand- in compounds means not just the limb but human power itself as opposed to horse, wind, or steam.

Second element — cart: the cart-word's journey is the opposite, documented at almost every turn. The deepest root is PIE *kers-, to run, the running that wagons do. Celtic speakers built on it *karros, a wagon or chariot, the vehicle famous enough that Julius Caesar's armies borrowed it as Latin carrus in the first century BCE, giving Romance a wagon-word outright. Northward, the Germanic world took the same vehicle-name, plausibly through contact with Celtic neighbours, yielding Old Norse kartr and possibly an Old English cræt of matching sense. When Norse settlers filled the Danelaw with their wagons, Middle English cart, recorded around 1200, carried a double inheritance, native and Norse at once, which is why its vowel and consonants look Scandinavian while its use feels fully English.

  • c. 1200 — Middle English cart: two-wheeled vehicles for farm and town, horse-drawn or hand-drawn without lexical distinction yet.
  • Sound shape: the hard c- and clipped -art survive Viking influence where southern voicing might have softened them, so that cart still sounds Norse and northern eight centuries later.
  • Kin and rivals: the same Celtic wagon-word also gives chariot and car through French and Latin, making the family saloon, the Roman war-wagon, and the porter's handcart distant cousins around one ancient axle.

The compound: seventeenth-century English, busy distinguishing hand-mills from horse-mills and hand-looms from water-frames, coins hand-cart for the porter and hawker sort, and the hyphen slowly falls out as the thing becomes everyday. The plural handcarts in Tolkien's lane is thus a small museum of English history: an orphan body-word of unknown depth pushing a wagon-word that has run since the Bronze Age, both still earning their keep.
