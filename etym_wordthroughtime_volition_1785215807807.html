- mid-17th century philosophical adoption - enters English from scholastic Latin as a technical term of the philosophy of mind, naming the individual act of willing; its home is logic and metaphysics, not conversation.
- 17th-19th century psychology - becomes the standard word in English philosophical psychology for the executive step that turns desire and intention into chosen action; it is contrasted with reason, desire, and habit.
- 19th-20th century narrowing - while the philosophy of the will flourishes, everyday English increasingly prefers "will" and "choice," leaving volition chiefly in formal and literary prose.
- Contemporary use - survives almost entirely in the fixed phrase "of one's own volition," meaning freely and by one's own choice; outside that idiom it is distinctly bookish.
