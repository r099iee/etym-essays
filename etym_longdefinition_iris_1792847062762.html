In the source sentence iris carries its botanical sense: the flowering marsh plant growing in great beds beside the reeds of the Gladden Fields. These are wild flags, most fittingly the yellow flag that haunts slow rivers and water-meadows, with tall sword-like leaves and bold blooms rising above the shallows where the boat drifts past.

Core botanical meaning in detail: Iris is a large genus of perennial herbs, numbering some three hundred species, grown from rhizomes or bulbs and instantly known by sword-shaped basal leaves and intricate flowers built on a plan of threes: three drooping outer segments called falls, often bearded or blotched with gold, and three upright inner segments called standards. The wild British kinds are the yellow flag of wet places and the stinking iris of shady banks, while gardens boast bearded irises in nearly every shade, Siberian irises, and delicate bulbous kinds. Tolkien, the philologist, chose the word with double pleasure, for the Gladden Fields take their very name from the Old English word for this flower: they are the fields of yellow flag, the river running gold with blooms each early summer.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The flowering plant (primary in this context, from antiquity): any member of genus Iris, wild flag or garden favourite. The bridge from the rainbow is colour itself: the blooms span violet, blue, yellow, white, and bronze, so the Greeks named the flower for their rainbow goddess.
  • The coloured ring of the eye (anatomy, from the 16th century in English): the pigmented muscular diaphragm surrounding the pupil, blue, brown, green, or grey, which widens and narrows to admit light. The bridge is again colour and circle: the ring paints each human eye a different hue, like a small private rainbow encircling the dark pupil.
  • The rainbow, and the goddess who personifies it (classical, Homeric): Iris the swift messenger of the gods, trailing her rainbow bridge between heaven and earth. This is the founding sense from which all others flow, and poets still invoke it whenever light breaks through rain.
  • An adjustable diaphragm regulating light (optics and photography): the iris diaphragm of a camera lens or microscope, a ring of overlapping metal leaves opening and closing exactly like the pupil of an eye. The bridge runs through the anatomical sense: engineers borrowed the eye's own shutter to name their mechanical copy.
  • A play of rainbow colours on surfaces (mineralogy and glass): as in iris glass, iris quartz, or the iridescence of oil on water. The bridge is visual resemblance: shifting spectral hues recalling the rainbow, so that even stone and glass may carry the goddess's name.
  • A woman's given name (modern): Iris as a first name, popular in waves from the Victorian flower-name fashion to the present day. The bridge is admiration: parents lend daughters the flower's beauty and the goddess's swiftness at once.

Register and connotation: iris is graceful and learned in every sense: gardeners speak it with affection, anatomists with precision, poets with awe. In the wildflower sense it smells of wetlands and summer rivers; in the ocular sense it is intimate and individual, since no two irises match exactly. Grammatically it keeps the classical plural irides in anatomy alongside everyday irises for flowers, a small reminder of its long journey from Greek epic to the water-meadow.
