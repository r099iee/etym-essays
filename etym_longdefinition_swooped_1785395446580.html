SWOOPED is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb to swoop: to descend rapidly through the air in a smooth, sweeping, curved trajectory, usually of a bird of prey or of an aircraft striking a target, or more loosely of any sudden, aggressive, downward rush upon something. The word fuses speed, grace, and inevitability in a single continuous arc: the swooping creature does not simply fall, it drives itself downward with wings half-folded and with momentum that seems to accelerate as it nears the target, striking at the last instant with its talons or, in extended use, with its full force. In Tolkien's sentence - 'Over them swooped the eagles; the dark rush of their beating wings smote them to the floor or drove them far away; their talons tore at goblin faces' - the verb launches the Eagles' counterattack in the Battle of Five Armies: they come out of nowhere, in a rushing, wheeling descent that overwhelms the goblins before they can rally. The nuance is predatory yet heroic; the swoop is the raptor's trademark, so the intervention of the Eagles reads as the return of wild, high justice descending from the mountains. Unlike a plunge or a drop, a swoop is never out of control: the bird arcs, accelerates, and strikes in one continuous movement, and the past tense swooped makes the whole descent a single completed event, an attack from a position of absolute advantage that arrives with the finality of a hawk's stoop. CORE SENSES AND POLYSEMY
  • Primary sense (ornithological/predatory): of a bird of prey, to descend rapidly from a height to seize prey, often in a curved dive - the hawk swoops down on a field mouse.
  • Extended sense (military/raiding): of persons, aircraft, or organized forces, to descend upon or attack suddenly - the police swooped on the warehouse; fighter jets swooped over the coast.
  • Sense of sudden arrival or seizure (informal): to arrive or move suddenly and catch someone or something unawares - death swooped without warning; a rival bidder swooped up the deal.
  • Figurative/moral sense: a sudden, overwhelming descent of fortune, emotion, or silence - sorrow swooped over him; a great calm swooped down upon the valley.
  • As a noun: swoop, a single act of descending so as to seize or attack; by extension, a raid or sudden decisive action; the idiom 'in one fell swoop' (from Shakespeare's Macbeth, 1605) means all at once, in a single devastating stroke.
  • Jargon and technical uses: in falconry, the swoop (or stoop) is the steep kill-dive of a trained hawk; in aviation and military slang, a 'swoop' is a low-level high-speed attack or surveillance pass; in sport and finance, a 'swoop' is a sudden takeover or recruitment raid.
HISTORICAL AND SEMANTIC BRIDGES
  • The word descends from the Old English verb swapan, 'to sweep, drive, move rapidly', whose past-tense stem swop- became a distinct verb. The connection between 'sweep' and 'swoop' is still felt: a swoop is a violent, downward kind of sweeping.
  • The predatory specialization belongs to the 16th-17th centuries, when falconry was the sport of the nobility; the hawk's kill-dive was the archetypal swoop, and the sense then generalized to raids by soldiers, ships, and police.
  • The noun developed in parallel with the verb, and the Shakespearean idiom 'one fell swoop' (1605) cemented the sense of a single, sudden, devastating stroke.
DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • Dive: a steep, headlong plunge, often head-first into water, lacking the curved, wing-controlled grace of a swoop.
  • Plummet / drop: gravity taking over, uncontrolled and simply downward, without wings or intent.
  • Pounce: the final leap of an ambush predator sprung from a crouch - a single bound, not a descent through the air.
  • Stoop: the true falconry term for a hawk's high, steep kill-dive, essentially a specialized vertical swoop.
  • Descend: the neutral, general verb for coming down, with no predatory force, speed, or suddenness.
USAGE AND REGISTER Swoop is a vivid, kinetic word, at home in literary prose, nature writing, journalism (police swoop, dawn swoop), and informal speech (swoop in, swoop up). It always implies speed, a height or distance covered, and a sudden seizing of something - whether prey, a building, or an opportunity. The past tense swooped in Tolkien's line converts that whole motion into a single finished event, giving the Eagles' attack its instant, decisive quality.
