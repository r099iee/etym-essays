The tale of trussed is the tale of the bundle: how medieval France taught England to pack, and how one packed bundle then lent its name to fowls, prisoners, hernias, hay, and bridges.

Old French — tucking up skirts and packs: Twelfth-century trousser meant to pack, bundle, or tuck up, used for loading mules, tucking gowns for the road, and stuffing quivers, the noun trousse naming the resulting case or bundle. A lady troussed her skirts to ride; a valet troussed his master's baggage; an archer's arrows rode in a trousse at his hip. The verb smelled of travel, haste, and competence, and the Normans' heirs carried it across the Channel with their pack-saddles.

Middle English — the pedlar's art: By 1200 trussen is at work in England, packing, binding, and loading, while trusse the noun settles into three great labours: the traveller's pack, the truss of hay or straw weighing some fifty pounds for army horses, and the truss of flowers or rushes. Langland's pilgrims truss their bundles; household accounts buy trusses of hay by the hundred; and the verb already stretches toward people, servants trussed out of doors, debtors trussed into pledges.

Kitchen and sickroom — binding bodies: Tudor cooks made trussed a term of art, skewering wings and legs close for the spit so the bird roasted even and carved handsome, and every cookbook since has trussed its poultry. Parallel ran the surgeons, whose truss, a padded steel-and-leather belt, held hernias against the belly wall for five centuries, advertised in every newspaper's back pages beside the corsets. Same cord-work, opposite ends of dignity: the bird prepared for honour, the body held together for decency.

Scaffold and battlefield — binding the unwilling: The verb's shadow side grew with the state's power: rebels trussed for hanging, thieves trussed to the cart-tail, Guy Fawkes effigies trussed of straw for November fires. Military engineers added the noblest sense, the truss of triangulated timbers that cannot rack, raising roofs over cathedrals and bridges over rivers on packer's logic, triangles being bundles that hold. Palladio praised such frames; British sappers built them across India and Canada.

Modern survival — the competent bundle: Zip, buckle, and velcro have stolen the verb's daily work, yet trussed holds its corners: the chicken, the hay bale's descendant the shrink-wrapped round bale, the walker's pack, the Christmas turkey trussed with string in a million kitchens. Its milestones read:

  • French packing verb: trousser and trousse giving England both the act and the bundle, with a debated deeper root honestly asterisked.
  • Medieval triple noun: traveller's pack, army hay-truss, and surgeon's belt, one strapped shape serving road, stable, and sickroom.
  • Tudor kitchen: the trussed fowl becoming cookery's standard, neatness going to table.
  • Engineering triangle: the braced truss raising roofs and bridges on binding logic, from Palladio to the railway age.
  • Empire's luggage: soldiers and emigrants trussing kit around the world, the participle an emblem of readiness.
  • Modern corners: poultry, packs, and bales keeping the participle alive while buckles do the easy work.

So the packs trussed up again after breakfast carry eight centuries of packing in their straps: French mule-trains, Tudor spits, surgeons' belts, and engineers' triangles, all hauled taut in one morning knot.
