Promontory — a high point of land or mass of rock that projects outward into a sea, lake, or river, rising steeply from the water while remaining joined to the mainland on one side.

In the physical landscape a promontory is one of the most dramatic of landforms. It thrusts forward like a headland, but the word lays its stress on elevation and on rocky boldness: a promontory is not merely a point of land but a high one, often a cliff or a great shoulder of stone standing out against the water. In the passage from The Hobbit that supplies this card's context, the promontory is "a promontory of rock" jutting into the surface of a lake; it breaks the swirl of the entering river and so creates a calm bay on which the town of Lake-town can safely stand. That protective function is typical of the word's physical sense: promontories shelter harbours, anchor fortresses, and command the surrounding sea.

  • Primary sense — physical geography: an elevated, rocky projection of land into a body of water; e.g. the Rock of Gibraltar, a promontory of the Iberian coast. The term is standard in coastline description, geology, and cartography.
  • Secondary sense — navigation and seafaring: a promontory is a landmark of the first order. Because it is high and thrusts into open water it can be seen from far off, and it marks the division of one stretch of coast from another; promontories were historically crowned with beacons, signal towers, and lighthouses, and many harbour cities grew up in their shelter.
  • Tertiary sense — anatomy: by a direct metaphorical transfer from landform to body, promontory names rounded projections of bone. The most important are the promontory of the sacrum, the projecting anterior edge of the sacrum at the back of the pelvis, and the promontory of the middle ear, a prominence on the medial wall of the tympanic cavity. Anatomists saw a bony "headland" jutting into a cavity and reached for exactly this word.
  • Domain-specific extensions: in geology, "promontory" may name any resistant mass left standing where surrounding softer rock has eroded; in archaeology and military history a "promontory fort" is a fortification built on a spur of high land, defended on one side by a rampart and on the others by the water or cliffs below; in rhetoric and astronomy the word is used figuratively for anything that juts out from a surrounding "plain".
  • Figurative and literary uses: the word is a favourite of elevated prose. It can stand for any exposed, isolated, or commanding position — a "promontory of thought", or the "sterile promontory" of Shakespeare's Hamlet, where the earth itself is imagined as a bare headland in a dark sea.

Semantic bridges: every sense of promontory radiates from a single image of projection. The Latin base prōminēre means "to jut forward"; a promontory is literally what juts. The navigational importance is a practical consequence of projection: the jutting land is the visible, commanding land. The anatomical sense is a purely visual metaphor: anatomists looked at the sacrum and the base of the skull and saw miniature headlands. The figurative sense extends the same picture to any lonely, thrusting feature of the conceptual landscape. The word never acquired colloquial or slang senses: it is a formal, learned term, so that in ordinary conversation speakers prefer "headland", "cliff", or "cape", while "promontory" retains a bookish, epic flavour.

Typical collocations: "a rocky promontory", "a windswept promontory", "the promontory of Gibraltar", "to stand upon a promontory", "the castle crowns the promontory", "a promontory of rock". In the Tolkien sentence the phrase "a promontory of rock" identifies the material as well as the form, underlining that the feature is stone, not merely high ground.

Boundaries against related words: a promontory is attached to the land, so it is not an island; it is high and rocky, so it is not a low sandy point; it projects into water, so it is not an inland cliff or ridge; it is joined by a broad base, so it is not a narrow-necked peninsula. In short: elevation, rock, and projection into water define it.
