- Egret enters English in the 14th–15th c. as the name of the white heron, a word from the world of fowling, heraldry and gentry tables, where the plumed bird was prized for its feathers.
- By the 16th–17th c. the word fixes on the snowy-plumed heron specifically, and English keeps 'aigrette' as a close doublet for the ornamental plumes that gave the bird its name and its value.
- In the 19th c. the fashion for 'aigrette' plumes drives a plume trade that nearly exterminates egrets, and the word enters the language of conservation, outrage and the millinery trade.
- In the 20th–21st c., after plume bans and recovery, 'egret' becomes a word of clean light and stillness — in poetry a votive whiteness, in ornithology the narrowest and most delicate of the heron words.
- Rookery follows its own path: in the 16th–17th c. it means a breeding colony of rooks; by the 18th c. it stretches to colonies of any gregarious bird, and in the 19th c. to crowded slum tenements packed like nests.
- In the modern register the slum sense recedes and 'rookery' returns to its root meaning: a noisy, crowded nesting colony of birds.
