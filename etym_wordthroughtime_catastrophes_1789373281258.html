- 16th Century - Enters Renaissance English theatrical and literary criticism from French and Latin (*catastrophe*), strictly denoting the concluding resolution or dramatic overturning that ends a play.
- Early to Mid-17th Century - Broadens in Jacobean drama and rhetoric to refer to any unfortunate, abrupt conclusion or unhappy ending to a personal enterprise or human life.
- 18th Century - Extends into natural philosophy and history to describe sudden, immense natural disasters, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and cosmic collisions.
- 19th Century - Becomes a central scientific battleground in geology and evolutionary biology through Georges Cuvier's doctrine of "catastrophism," before emerging in social criticism as an emblem of revolutionary collapse.
- Late 20th Century to Present - Formalized in mathematics through René Thom's "Catastrophe Theory," while remaining a dominant term in climate science, financial risk management (cat bonds), and literary aesthetics for the inevitable unraveling of human pretension.
