Pother is a rare case in English etymology: a word whose entire recorded history is contained within the modern period, with no ancient root to trace and no certain ancestor in any other language. When it first appears in print, around 1590, it denotes a choking cloud of dust or smoke — the spluttering puff of debris kicked up in a dry road, the steam and soot of a smoking hearth. Its very shape suggests its origin: the syllables sound like a soft explosive exhale, a "poth" of breath and particles, and the Oxford English Dictionary accordingly classifies the word as of uncertain origin and most probably imitative (echoic). To claim a Proto-Indo-European ancestor for pother would be an invention: this is a word born in the open air of early-modern English, not a descendant of Greek, Latin, or Sanskrit.

Within a generation the image was turned inward, from the physical world to the social and mental one. By the early seventeenth century pother (alongside its earliest form pudder) means "a commotion, a bustle, a fuss" — exactly as the cloud raised by a struggle is the visible signature of the struggle itself. The idiom "to be in a pother" meant to be agitated and flustered; "to make a pother" meant to make a disproportionate fuss; the exclamation "what a pother!" became a standard cry of exasperation over trifles. A verb developed alongside the noun:

  • "to pother" someone — to harass, perplex, bewilder, or worry them (to envelop them, metaphorically, in the choking cloud);
  • "to pother about" — intransitively, to bustle about making a fuss.

The word was noticeably unstable in form, and this instability is itself part of the story. Within the space of a century it was written pudder, pudher, potther, and pother; "pudder," the earliest shape, is thought by many scholars to be the true original, with "pother" a later reshaping. Two possible sources have been canvassed by lexicographers. First, a Continental origin: Middle Low German and Dutch pudderen, "to splash, to kick up water or dust," is the most plausible candidate and remains the standard conjecture — but it is a conjecture, not a proof, and the dictionaries print it with caution. Second, native influence: the English verb potter, "to poke about, to trifle," was close in sound and meaning, and some scholars hear an echo of bother in the modern form. None of these is certain; the safest verdict is that pother is a native creation, probably echoic, with possible Continental reinforcement.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries pother was ordinary, vigorous, everyday English. The prose of Dryden and Swift uses it without ceremony; it appears in drama and satire as the natural word for a noisy commotion out of proportion to its cause; and it survives in the idioms that still ring true today — "to be in a pother," "to make a pother." Its tone throughout is dismissive and slightly comic: a pother is a fuss, and usually a fuss about nothing much. Over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word retreated from speech into print, surviving as an old-fashioned, faintly bookish item — the kind of word a writer reaches for deliberately when he wants the flavour of an earlier century.

That is precisely why C. S. Lewis uses it in the passage on this card, where the castle of the sick Queen is filled with "bustle and pother." The two words work as a doublet: bustle supplies the energetic, purposeful stir of servants and officials hurrying about their duties, while pother contributes the sense of a fuss — a cloud of needless, worried agitation swirling around the royal sickbed. The word's original dust-cloud rises one final time in a fairy-tale castle, carrying with it all the meanings the centuries have layered upon it: the physical puff of kicked-up debris, the social commotion, and the mental fluster, all wrapped in a single archaic, slightly contemptuous syllable.
