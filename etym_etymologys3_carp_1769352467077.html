The word carp is really two biographies wearing the same name, and a proper study of it must tell both. The first biography belongs to a fish; the second belongs to a verb of complaining. They share no ancestor, no route, and no meaning; their identity in modern English is a pure accident of sound — a homonymic coincidence that any dictionary must treat as two separate entries. Because the card's definition and context concern the fish, that story comes first.

The fish. Unlike most English fish-names, which descend from Old English, carp is a late arrival and a loanword from the south and east. The common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is native to the slow, warm, muddy rivers of the Danube and the Black and Caspian Sea basins, and the people who first named it were not Indo-European speakers: the word is reconstructed as a pre-Indo-European substrate term, *karpa-, the name used by the older Danubian population whom the Indo-Europeans encountered. It is, in short, one of the oldest surviving European fish-names of non-Indo-European origin. When Rome's empire spread into the Danube region, Latin borrowed the substrate word as carpa, first attested in the fifth century CE in the letters of Cassiodorus, who praised the fish of the Danube as a delicacy. From Latin it passed into the medieval fish-pond world: the Church, whose monks farmed fish in artificial ponds to supply meat-free Lenten meals, carried the carp from the Danube basin across Western Europe. Old French adopted it as carpe, and with the expansion of monastic fish culture after the Norman Conquest the word reached England, recorded in Middle English as carpe about 1400.

Independently of the Romance route, the same substrate term was borrowed early by the Germanic-speaking peoples of central Europe, who had their own version, Proto-Germanic *karpa-; from it descend Old High German karpho and modern German Karpfen. The two routes — Romance and Germanic — thus carried one fish-name north and west on parallel paths, converging again in the fish-markets and fish-ponds of medieval Europe. The fish itself then travelled even further in human company: Chinese fish farmers had domesticated carp relatives more than two thousand years ago, and the Japanese bred the ornamental koi (a word that simply means "carp" in Japanese) from the common species; in the twentieth century silver, grass, and bighead carp were introduced to North America, where they became notorious invasive species in the Mississippi basin. The word, meanwhile, has never changed its reference: a carp is a carp across every century and every language that received it.

  • Angling and literature: the carp entered English letters as a sporting and table fish. Izaak Walton's The Compleat Angler (1653) crowned it "the Queen of Rivers," a reputation that shaped British coarse-angling for centuries; in Britain "carp fishing" is now a vast specialist culture, and the shy, powerful old fish of ponds and lakes is the most prized catch of the coarse fisherman.
  • Symbolism: in East Asia the carp is a symbol of perseverance and success — in the Chinese legend the carp that swims up the Yellow River and leaps the Dragon Gate becomes a dragon, an image of passing examinations and rising in the world; the koi appears throughout Japanese art and festival.
  • Modern ecology: in environmental and regulatory contexts "carp" often means an invasive pest; barrier projects and removal programmes along North American rivers are built around the bighead, silver, and grass carps.

The verb. The second biography is shorter but no less interesting. Old Norse, the language of the Vikings who settled northern England, had the verb karpa, "to brag, boast, talk noisily, wrangle." It was a plain word for loud, self-important speech. English borrowed it in the fourteenth century as carpen, and at first the word was completely neutral: to "carpen" was simply to speak, to talk, to tell a tale — the verb appears in medieval romances meaning "to say" or "to relate." The narrowing came later, in the sixteenth century, when carp began to mean "to find fault, to complain, to speak critically." Scholars believe this shift was reinforced from a different direction: the Latin verb carpere meant "to pluck, to pick, to pull to pieces," and in classical usage it already had the figurative sense of "to slander, to assail a person's reputation by picking at him" — the same image we keep when we say someone "picks at" another's faults. The Viking word for "wrangle" and the Roman word for "pick to pieces" blended in English speech, and the modern verb carp — petty, persistent, peevish complaint — was the result. Its use in the great period of English satire and criticism ("carping critics," "carping at everything") fixed it in the literary register it still occupies.

Put the two stories together and the word becomes a lesson in how etymology works. The fish-noun has no Indo-European past at all — it begins in the mists of a vanished Danube culture, travels through Rome and the cloister, and arrives in English as a medieval loanword. The verb has an Indo-European past only indirectly (through the Old Norse and Latin words that fed it), and it changed its meaning from "talk" to "complain" inside English itself. The two words fell together by chance because speakers who inherited or borrowed them heard the same syllables; no ancient speaker would have recognised the fish in the fault-finder or the fault-finder in the fish. In C. S. Lewis's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, the context of this card, the word appears in its oldest and purest form: "a big carp rose to the surface of the pool, gulped air and then sank mysteriously into the dark water again" — the quiet, heavy, deep-dwelling fish of the Narnian wood, a creature whose name has swum up from the Danube to the English page without ever changing its meaning.
