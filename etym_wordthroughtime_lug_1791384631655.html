- 14th c. "to pull by the hair or ears" - Middle English luggen, originally a rough colloquial verb meaning to pull or tear at someone's hair, ears, or limbs (as fighting boys or hunting dogs baiting bears).
- Late 15th c. "the human ear / projecting handle" - Northern English and Scottish dialects nominalize lug to mean the ear (the flapped handle of the head) and subsequently projecting handles on vessels and pots.
- 16th–17th c. "to haul or drag heavy burdens" - general semantic shift from pulling ears to pulling, hauling, or carrying any heavy, cumbersome load with difficulty.
- 19th c. "mechanical tabs & lugsails" - industrial revolution applies lug to mechanical projections, brackets, flanged fittings, and lug nuts.
- Early 20th c. "engine strain & clumsy men" - mechanical engineers use lugging for engines laboring under load; American slang adopts "lug" for a burly, good-natured, or thick-headed man.
- Present "everyday physical haul & engineering standard" - universally used for awkward carrying, while firmly established in automotive mechanics and electronics.
