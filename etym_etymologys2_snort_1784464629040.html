Deep stage: no single secure PIE verbal root is reconstructable for the whole set. English snort belongs to the expressive Germanic sn- cluster of nasal-and-breath words (snore, sniff, snuff, snivel, snarl, snuffle), which historical linguists treat as largely imitative or sound-symbolic — the initial sn- iconically suggests nasal friction and the sharp passage of breath. [DEBATED: a loose link to an Indo-European root *sner- "to hiss, whisper, snore" (seen in Greek snórchō / snérkhō "to snore") is sometimes floated, but most specialists treat the English forms as onomatopoeic reinforcement rather than strict inherited cognates, so a clean PIE pedigree cannot be honestly drawn.] → Middle English snorten (to snore; to breathe heavily through the nose; to snort), attested from the 14th century CE; reinforced by Middle English snoren (to snore) and the surrounding sn- nose-sound set → Early Modern English snort (verb: to force air loudly through the nose; noun: the sound so made), 16th century CE → Modern English SNORT — the card's target form (verb and noun). NOTE on the debate: the origin is essentially expressive and onomatopoeic; inventing a single linear PIE derivation would be fabrication, so the honest attested chain stops at the Germanic/English record, with the wider *sner- link flagged as merely plausible.
