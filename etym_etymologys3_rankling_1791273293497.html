The strange beauty of rankling is that it began as maggots. Middle English ranklen meant to breed, to fester with worms in a wound, to suppurate. Its source is Old North French rancler, raoncler to fester, from noun rancle, draoncle a festering sore, ulcer. That noun in turn continues Latin dracunculus little serpent, dragonet, used by physicians for a painful ulcer or Guinea worm, diminutive of draco serpent, from Greek drakon. The loss of initial d- (draoncle to raoncle) is a regular dissimilation when two r-like sounds crowded. Thus a serpent-shaped sore gave English a word for festering, which gave modern psychology a word for resentment. Few etymologies wind so visibly from body to mind.

In Middle English medical writing rankle is literal and disgusting: wounds rankle when they gather corruption. The verb could also mean to breed in the sense of maggots rankling in carrion. By the late fourteenth century the metaphor to moral corruption was available: sin rankles like an ulcer. Shakespeare completes the emotional transfer. In Hamlet, Richard III, Coriolanus and elsewhere, injuries, malices, and shames rankle in breasts and minds, no longer suppurating pus but resentment. The King James Bible and Prayer Book reinforced the image of festering guilt. Seventeenth-century spelling stabilised as rankle, losing any memory of the French ao, and present participle rankling became the favoured literary form because it names duration: what rankles is still at work.

Phonetically the word benefited from confusion with native rank meaning overgrown, foul, strong-smelling (from Old English ranc proud, strong). Speakers heard rankling sores as rank sores, reinforcing the sense of foul persistence even though the two rank words are historically distinct. This folk-etymological support helped rankle survive when many Anglo-Norman medical terms died. Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines to rankle as to fester, to breed corruption, then figuratively to cause rage or inquietude, capturing both stages. Victorian novelists use rankling for class slights, jealousies, and colonial grievances, making it a standard term for respectable resentment that cannot be voiced directly and therefore festers.

  • Serpent to sore: Latin diminutive for worm-like ulcer narrows in Old French to any festering boil, losing zoology but keeping pain.
  • Sore to soul: medieval moral physiology, which read body and soul as parallel humoral systems, licenses transfer from pus to bitterness without sense of metaphor.
  • Verb to participle prominence: finite rankles yields to rankling / rankled as adjectives of memory, fitting modern interest in lingering trauma and grievance.
  • Folk support from rank (foul): homophony with Germanic rank foul anchors the word in native soil and prevents obsolescence.

Wodehouse's use — rankling in my bosom — is deliberately archaic-bathos, reviving Tudor breast-language for a 1930s bedroom farce. That it still reads instantly shows how fully the metaphor has naturalised: every speaker knows a rankling remark even without knowing draoncles or Guinea worms. Contemporary therapy language of rumination, triggers, and unprocessed hurt maps neatly onto rankling, ensuring the old ulcer-word a continued clinical afterlife in ordinary speech.

Today rankling endures as the most bodily word for a mental ache, preserving medieval medicine inside modern feeling. Its evolution from little serpent to festering sore to festering grudge illustrates English at its most metaphorical: we heal minds as we once healed wounds, by finally drawing what rankles.
