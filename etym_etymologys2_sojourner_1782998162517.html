SOJOURNER is built from two elements, sojourn + the agent suffix -er; each has its own chain. Chain for the element SOJOURN: 1. PIE *dyew- (also *dyeu-, "bright sky, heaven, day") — the root of the Indo-European sky-god *Dyeus, reconstructed from cognates including Latin dies "day," Greek Zeus, and Sanskrit dyaus "heaven"; not directly attested, c. 4th millennium BCE. 2. Latin dies "day" (attested 3rd-2nd c. BCE in Plautus; a derivative of the PIE root, with acc. diem). 3. Latin diurnus "of the day, daily" (adjective, 1st c. BCE, Cicero; formed from dies with the suffix -rnus, as in hesternus "of yesterday"). 4. Late Latin diurnum "a day's time, a daily allowance; (in monastic use) a day-service or breviary" (neuter noun, 4th-6th c. CE; common in Christian writers). 5. Vulgar Latin *subdiurnare "to spend the day, stay for the day in a place" (reconstructed: sub- "up to, during" + the denominal verb diurnare; not attested in writing, inferred from Romance outcomes). 6. Old French sojorner "to remain, stay for a time, dwell temporarily" (attested 11th-12th c.; the Chanson de Roland, c. 1100, uses sojorner and sojorn of rest at court; from *subdiurnare, with the prefix assimilating to soj- and the internal /d/ weakening). 7. Middle English sojornen "to stay temporarily, abide, dwell for a time" (verb; attested c. 1290). 8. sojourn "a temporary stay or residence" (noun; back-formation from the verb, attested 14th c., e.g. c. 1375). 9. sojourner "one who sojourns, a temporary resident" (Middle English sojornere, attested 14th c., e.g. in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, c. 1387) → Modern English SOJOURNER. Chain for the suffix -ER: 1. Latin -arius (denominal suffix of belonging or occupation; e.g. mercarius "merchant," tabernarius "shopkeeper"; Classical Latin). Its ultimate PIE derivation is debated — often compared with the adjectival suffix *-ro-/*-aro- — and should be treated as uncertain. 2. Proto-Germanic *-arijaz (agent-noun suffix borrowed from Latin -arius; e.g. Gothic bokareis "scribe, letter-writer"; reconstructed). 3. Old English -ere (agent suffix; e.g. fiscere "fisher," writere "writer"; attested). 4. Middle English -er(e) → Modern English -er, attached to sojourn to form SOJOURNER.
