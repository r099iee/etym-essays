“Were-” entered English as a prefix through compounds like “werewolf,” built on an old word for “man” — so a were-creature is something part-man, part-beast, a shape-shifter of folklore.
From the werewolf model, English coined other “were-” beasts, and “worm” (the old word for “serpent” or “dragon”) was attached to make “were-worm” — a man-shaped or man-related dragon-serpent, a monster of myth and wonder.
In modern fantasy and Tolkien’s usage, “were-worms” are fabulous desert dragons at the edge of the map — creatures of legend and hyperbole rather than natural history, a word that sounds dreadfully old and strange.
“Were-worms” today functions as an archaic-sounding, mock-heroic compound: listeners understand “were-” as a horror-prefix and “worm” as an old word for dragon, without any real beast existing in the zoology — the name itself carries the weight of dread and wonder.
