1. The Indo-European root: *streig- “to stroke”. The chain begins with the Indo-European root *streig-, “to stroke, rub, press, draw a line”. Its reflexes outside Germanic are debated but probable: Latin stringere “to touch lightly, graze; to draw tight” (whence English stringent and strain via the “tighten” sense) is commonly — though not universally — adduced, together with Latin striga “furrow, line”. More firmly, the Slavic family shows the root in the sense “to shear, cut”: Proto-Slavic *strigti (Russian strichʹ, Polish strzyc “to cut, shear”), which preserves the rubbing/cutting edge of the root. The cautious statement of the record is this: the root is securely reconstructed for Germanic and Slavic; the Latin connection, though widely accepted in outline, carries scholarly caveats that an honest etymology must flag.

2. Proto-Germanic and Old English: a gentle verb. In Germanic the root produced the class I strong verb *strīkaną, “to stroke, rub; to pass over, move swiftly”. Old English strīcan (attested from the ninth–tenth century) preserved the gentle meanings: “to stroke, smooth, rub” (a hand stroking a surface), and “to go, move on, glide” (a ship or person passing quickly over water or land — one of the great Old English verbs of motion). Nothing in the word’s Old English life suggests violence; the sense development that made strike a verb of hitting belongs to the period after the Norman Conquest and is central to the word’s history. The professed chain from stroke to city-smiting runs through Middle English, where the Old Norse contact — strjúka means both “to stroke, rub” and, in Norse usage, “to strike” — is generally credited with giving the English verb its new, violent force. That Norse reinforcement is plausible and widely repeated, though the semantic change could equally have arisen natively; the sources agree that by the 13th century striken meant “to hit, smite, deal a blow”, and from there the modern verb’s entire career follows.

3. Middle English: the blow and the participle. The fourteenth century is the great divide. Middle English striken (with past strook, strake, and past participle stricken) explodes into productivity: to strike with a sword, a rod, a messenger’s blow; to strike a coin (to stamp it); to strike a harp (to play); to strike sail; to strike the hour — the full medieval semantic field of hitting, pressing, stamping and sounding. The participle stricken appears at once in adjectival use: a man “stricken” is a man hit, smitten, wounded — and, by immediate extension, a man afflicted (stricken with sickness, stricken to the heart). Chaucer and the mystery plays use both the verb and the participle freely, and the participle has already begun its drift away from the grammar of the active verb: Middle English writers say “stricken in age” for far advanced in years, using the participle of the sense “to pass, press on”, and “stricken” for “struck with” in every moral and physical sense.

4. The biblical age: 16th–17th centuries. The King James Bible (1611) fixed the word in the national memory: “old and stricken in years” (Joshua 13:1), “well stricken in years” (Luke 1:7, of Zacharias and Elisabeth), and the great messianic prophecy of Isaiah 53:4 — “we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted” — where stricken and smitten echo together as the vocabulary of divinely inflicted suffering. From scripture the participle passed into sermon, hymn and devotional prose, carrying with it the sense of blows sent by God: plague-stricken cities, conscience-stricken sinners, heart-stricken mourners. The seventeenth century also gives “stricken dumb” its modern home — the phrase for being silenced as if struck — and the “stricken field” (battlefield) becomes a stock image of heroic prose and verse down to the Victorians.

5. The nineteenth century: the compound machine. In the 1800s stricken became the most productive participle of catastrophe in English: awe-stricken and terror-stricken (early), then the great Victorian set — panic-stricken, grief-stricken, horror-stricken, famine-stricken, poverty-stricken, conscience-stricken — the readings of a century obsessed with emotional extremity and social misery. Dickens uses “poverty-stricken” as a social diagnosis; Victorian journalism reports “cholera-stricken” towns and “distress-stricken” parishes. The same period begins the relevant lexical split: daily usage increasingly prefers the short participle struck (struck down, struck out, struck gold), while stricken is promoted to the formal, mournful register where it still lives.

6. The twentieth century to today. Modern English shows the division complete. Struck handles all ordinary grammar — the clock struck, the deal was struck, struck by lightning, struck from the list, struck out (in baseball and law). Stricken is now an adjective of catastrophe: the stricken region, the stricken family, the stricken face, stricken with grief — permanent resident of headlines after earthquakes, floods, famines and wars, and of the frozen moment of shock in narrative prose, as when Tolkien writes that Thorin “was stricken dumb with amazement and confusion”. The legal idiom “stricken from the record” preserves the participle in courtroom procedure, and the old poetic “stricken in years” survives as a deliberate archaism. What remains constant across nine hundred years is the blow: struck in the body by a sword, struck in the mind by grief, struck in the will by God — the participle still reports that someone, somewhere, was hit, and is still reeling.

7. Phonology in brief. The class I strong verb shows the regular Germanic -ei- > -ī-: Old English strīcan kept long ī, which Middle English preserved; the Great Vowel Shift then raised and diphthongised the accented infinitive and past forms to modern /aɪ/ (strike), but the participle stricken had a short vowel and kept it — so strike and stricken have rhymed with different vowels for five hundred years. The -en ending is the old Germanic participial suffix (compare broken, spoken, forgiven), and the doubled consonant in the spelling records the Middle English short vowel. The word is thus also a fossil of English sound history: its very vowel is the reason it survived as a distinct adjective rather than merging with struck.
