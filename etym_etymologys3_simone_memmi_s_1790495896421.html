PART A: Simone (from Hearing to Siena). The forename Simone begins far from Tuscany, in the Bronze-Age Semitic verb to hear, hearken, obey. Biblical Hebrew forms shama (hear) and the theophoric name Shimon (he who hears, God has heard, son of Jacob and Leah, Genesis 29:33). The name travels with the Septuagint into Greek as Symeon and Simon, borne in the New Testament by no fewer than eight figures including Simon Peter, Simon the Zealot, and Simon of Cyrene. Latin Vulgate fixes Simon, Simeonis as a saintly, apostolic name; medieval Christendom multiplies Simons: popes, martyrs, crusaders, scholastics. Old French makes Simon, Middle English Simond, Symon (Simon de Montfort).

Italian vernacular in Dante's age gives Simone (masculine, stressed on O: see-MO-neh), with feminine Simona. It is a common Tuscan baptismal name by 1280, borne by notaries, wool-merchants, and painters. Parish books in Siena record Martino's son Simone; guild rolls list him as Symon Martini. Phonologically Latin Simon-em (accusative) loses final -m and raises -on to -one in Tuscan, parallel to Leone. No consonant shift obscures the chain; the interest is semantic: from a descriptor of divine hearing to an individual tag to, by antonomasia, the Simone of art history.

  • Hebraic theophoric stage: name as prayer-testimony (God heard Leah's affliction). Tribal eponym for Simeonites. Sacred and communal.
  • Hellenistic-Jewish diffusion: Maccabean Simon Thassi, rabbinic Shimon bar Yochai; Greek spelling stabilises international form.
  • Christian sanctification: apostles and martyrs make Simon unavoidable; baptismal frequency soars; compounds like Simone di Filippo emerge.
  • Tuscan vernacularisation (13th century): Simone as everyday Sienese forename; workshop Latin still writes Simon, volgare writes Simone.
  • Art-historical antonomasia (14th century to present): Simone unqualified in an art book means Martini, as Michelangelo means Buonarroti. Forename absorbs surname's fame.

PART B: Memmi (from William to Workshop Kin, 3500 characters). Memmi looks exotic but is homely Tuscan. Its deepest reconstructible source is Germanic wiljo (will, desire) plus helmaz (helmet, protection), compounded as Wiljahelmaz, Frankish Willahelm, Old High German Willehelm, Norman French William and Guillaume, Italian Guglielmo (with characteristic g- prothesis and -iel- diphthong). Normans carry William to England (1066) and to Sicily and Italy, where Lombard and Angevin chanceries naturalise Guglielmo as a prestige Christian name. Tuscan speech, impatient with four syllables, clips and reduplicates: Guglielmo to Memo, Memmo (compare Beppe for Giuseppe, Lapo for Jacopo, Checco for Francesco). Diminutive gemination (-mm-) signals affection: Memmo equals little Will, our Will.

Sienese notarial practice then forms patronymics by adding -i (originally Latin genitive plural, sons of): Memmo to Memmi equals son, descendants, house of Memmo, exactly as Medici, Bardi, Tolomei. The painter Memmo di Filippuccio (active c. 1300, Sienese frescoes in San Gimignano) fathers Lippo Memmi (c. 1291-1356) and a daughter Giovanna, who marries Simone son of Martino c. 1324. Simone and Lippo share a bottega, sign jointly (SYMON DE SENIS ET LIPPUS MEMMI ME PINXERUNT on the 1333 Annunciation). Archival Catasto and Opera del Duomo payments keep them distinct: Symoni Martini versus Lippo Memmi. Vasari, working from hurried notes and oral tradition 200 years later, collapses the pair into Simone Memmi, perhaps misreading LIPPUS MEMMI as surname for both. The error is then canonised by print: every edition, translation, and Murray guide repeats Memmi until 19th-century German source-criticism (Crowe and Cavalcaselle, Milanesi's Vasari commentary) restores Martini from documents.

  • Phonetic path: Willehelm to Guglielmo to Memmo to Memmi with patronymic -i. No irregular sound law; purely Tuscan hypocoristic morphology with reduplication and gemination.
  • Socio-cultural context: bottega kinship (marriage alliance cements workshop), guild matriculation by father's name, Sienese pride in lineage versus Florentine individualism.
  • Historiographic afterlife: Vasarian error productive for 300 years; Pre-Raphaelites love Simone Memmi as mellifluous; modern databases cross-reference both forms to avoid loss.
  • Possessive 's coda: from PIE genitive through Old English -es (daeges, kinges) to Middle English his-genitive folk-etymology (king his head) to printer's apostrophe (king's). By Ruskin's day it is a phrasal clitic attachable to any noun phrase, including foreign names ending in -i (Memmi's). Its nuance here is attributive-style, not ownership.

English 's completes the string: from PIE genitive through Old English -es to Middle English to printer's apostrophe. By Ruskin's day it is a phrasal clitic attachable to any noun phrase. Together Simone plus Memmi plus 's compresses Hebrew theology, Germanic war-names, Tuscan workshop love, Vasarian print culture, and English inn-sign jokes into three syllables, which is why Ruskin could hang so much affection on so small a phrase when he wished his Croydon grandmother gilded.
