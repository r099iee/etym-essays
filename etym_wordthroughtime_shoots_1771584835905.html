- [Old English verb: to dart forth] - 'Shoot' first means to discharge, hurl, or thrust out with force - the word of arrows and missiles; from this violent motion the image of anything that 'shoots' forth is born.
- [Middle English extension: things that dart out] - The verb's force transfers to nature: stems, buds, and sprouts that shoot up from the ground; the noun 'shoot' names the young growth itself, and connotation is fresh, vigorous, and new.
- [Early modern to contemporary: the tender poetic growth] - 'Shoots' settles as the botanical term for new young stems; connotation turns delicate and literary - tender, flexible, easily bruised - the image of youth and spring, as of a willow shoot caressed by the wind, set apart from the sturdier, established growth of twig and branch.
