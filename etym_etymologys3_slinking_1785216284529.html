The lineage of slinking is short but crooked. It begins in Proto-Indo-European with the root *slenk-, "to creep, crawl, wind," a movement-root of the kind used for serpents and vermin — slithering, gliding, sinuous motion — and from it Proto-Germanic built the verb *slinkaną, "to creep, crawl," a nasalized formation whose -nk- cluster still sounds its slithering origin. The nasal infix (slink rather than a plain *slik-) gives the word its body: it is the same pattern seen in Germanic verbs of gliding and withdrawing, where the -n- marks an ongoing, continuous motion.

Old English and Middle English. In Old English the verb appears as slincan, used of creeping and gliding movement, especially of serpents and other crawling creatures; the sense was neutral and purely physical — to move along close to a surface, without any moral colouring. By Middle English the verb had become slinken, "to creep, move stealthily," and here the crucial change began: the neutral sense "crawl" began to acquire the notion of moving quietly, so as not to be seen. The word's semantic career from this point is one of steady narrowing and darkening.

The moral turn of the 16th–17th centuries. The pivotal shift took place in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, as the sense "move stealthily" hardened into "move with an air of guilt or shame." To slink came to mean moving so as to avoid notice, and then — irreversibly — moving as one who knows, or feels, that he has no right to be seen: the crouched, self-diminishing gait of one caught or defeated. This moralization of a movement-word is the biography's central event, and it is visible in the idioms the language built around it. The most famous is to slink off with one's tail between one's legs, an image borrowed from a beaten dog and applied to human defeat and humiliation; the phrase fixes the word's habitat in the territory of shame, retreat, and disgrace. Around the same time a second, independent branch developed in rural stock-raising: by the seventeenth century a slink calf was a calf born prematurely, the animal having "slipped away" before its time — the same root sense of gliding, withdrawing motion turned to the idea of something that escapes before its season. Both branches, the moral and the rural, grow from the single root idea of gliding away unseen.

The participle in modern English. The present participle slinking, formed with the participial suffix -ing, has been in continuous use since the word's period of semantic darkening, and it is the participle that does the atmospheric work in narrative prose. Because the -ing form denotes an ongoing or habitual state rather than a single act, slinking converts furtive motion into a permanent condition: someone who is slinking is always slinking, never merely did it once. This is exactly the effect J.R.R. Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit (1937): the "original owners" of the ancient caves are "still there in odd corners, slinking and nosing about." The participle makes their stealth a lasting attribute, not an event — they are not walking, standing, or watching; they are slinking, in corners, forever, the low, guilty, gliding motion of things that have never left the dark.

Cognates and limits. Cognates elsewhere in Germanic confirm the root's antiquity: Old High German slinkan, "to creep, crawl," and Middle High German slinken, "to glide away," attest the same base across the western branch of the family. Beyond Germanic, however, no certain cognates have been established in Romance or Indo-Aryan, and honest etymology says so rather than inventing links. The root *slenk- may itself be related to the family of English sling, but the connection is uncertain and is not pressed.

Key sound shifts and forms.
  • *slenk- → Proto-Germanic *slinkaną: nasalized present stem in -n-, the continuing form of the root.
  • *slinkaną → Old English slincan: regular Anglo-Frisian development of the Germanic stem.
  • slincan → Middle English slinken: regular loss of the Old English infinitive ending, giving the Middle English form.
  • slinken → slink: Modern English contraction; the semantic shift "crawl" → "move stealthily" → "move guiltily" is complete by the 17th century.
  • slink → slinking: the -ing participle, used as adjective and verbal noun from the 16th century onward.
