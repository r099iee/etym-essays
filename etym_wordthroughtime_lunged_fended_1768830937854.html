lunged:
- The fencer's thrust (18th c.) - 'lunge' enters English from the French word for 'an extension': the deep forward drive of the leading leg with the weight thrown behind the blade - a precise, athletic term of the fencing school
- From sword to creature (19th c.) - anything lunges - a boxer, a tiger, a desperate man - the whole frame thrown forward in one explosive, extended reach
- The overcommitted attack (20th c.) - narrative prose keeps the word's dark logic: because a lunge is all-in, it is exactly the move a skilled opponent can punish; discipline and predatory intent travel together
- Contemporary - both the fencing technique and the everyday verb of sudden forward movement, with the root idea of 'lengthening' still felt in the stretched, linear posture

fended:
- The clipped heart of defend (Middle English) - 'fend' emerges as the shortened form of defend, keeping the full sense of its ancestor: to strike a blow away, to ward off
- Workmanlike self-reliance (16th-19th c.) - 'to fend for oneself', 'to fend off hunger, cold, blows': the word lives in plain, daily language of meeting one's own needs and beating off what threatens
- Sharp physical deflection (modern) - in combat prose, fending is the active, economical deflection - one sharp movement that turns an attack into an opening
- Contemporary - mostly reflexive and phrasal (fend for oneself, fend off); the word keeps its bare, muscular core - the immediate act of striking a threat away
