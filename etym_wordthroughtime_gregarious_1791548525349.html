- Mid-17th Century (1660s): Purely zoological and natural history classification - Perceived as an objective scientific descriptor denoting animals, birds, or insects that naturally flock, herd, or swarm together.
- Late 18th to 19th Century: Extended to human sociology and anthropology - Used to characterize tribes, societies, or communal tendencies, emphasizing humanity's innate biological and psychological drive to form collective groups.
- Early to Mid-20th Century: Interpersonal personality trait and psychological characterization - Acquired a predominantly positive, vibrant connotation denoting an extroverted, approachable, and highly sociable individual who thrives in social gatherings.
- Late 20th to 21st Century: Modern personality and organizational vernacular - Retains strong acclaim in workplace dynamics and social settings, though occasionally juxtaposed critically with inward contemplation, solitary focus, or deep introversion.
