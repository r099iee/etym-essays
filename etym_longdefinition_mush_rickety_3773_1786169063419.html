EXHAUSTIVE LEXICOGRAPHICAL & POLYSEMOUS ANALYSIS: MUSH & RICKETY

The compound mush and rickety binds physical pulpiness to structural fragility, and in the Vance passage the two work as a single ghastly image: the creature's mouth refuses the spoon, and the mush trickles down its glazed skin to fall on the rickety frame of its body. Below is the full polysemous exegesis of both words.

SECTION 1: MUSH — THE NOUN

1. Primary culinary sense: boiled cornmeal porridge. Mush is a thick, soft porridge of boiled cornmeal (maize meal) cooked in salted water or milk — for centuries the staple breakfast and supper of rural North America.

  • Frontier history: 17th–18th century American pioneers ate cornmeal mush daily; 'fried mush' (slices of chilled, set mush fried in fat) is still a traditional Southern and Appalachian dish. The word entered English in 1670 as a dialectal variant of 'mash', possibly reinforced by German Mus 'soft food, porridge' brought by Pennsylvania settlers.
  • Regional survival: 'mush' is still the ordinary word for cornmeal porridge in the American South and Midwest, equivalent to England's porridge and Scotland's brose.


2. Secondary physical sense: any soft pulpy mass. By extension, mush names any soft, shapeless, crushed or semi-liquid mass: melting snow mixed with mud ('break-up mush'), the crushed remains of fruit or vegetable matter, soft pulp of any kind.

  • Geophysical: river 'mush ice' or 'slush' — ice broken into soft fragments; spring thaw turns frozen ground to mush.
  • Organic: soft decayed tissue, wet sawdust, rotted leaves — anything that has lost its internal structure and yields to pressure.


3. The interjection and verb: 'Mush!' In dog-sledding, mush! is the command to a sled team to go — an Americanism of the 19th century, often said to be a corruption of French marchez! ('walk!', 'go!'), blended with the slurred sound of mushers urging their dogs.

  • Verb: 'to mush' is to travel by dog-sled, and a 'musher' is a dog-sled driver; the command is one of the most famous words of the Yukon gold rush and of Alaskan idiom.


4. Figurative sense: cloying sentimentality. From the softness of the food comes 'mushy' — exaggerated, mawkish, cloying sentiment. 'Mush' as a noun means sentimental nonsense, undemanding romance, and the adjective 'mushy' ('mushy talk', 'mushy love letters') is the standard dismissal of the tender and the gushing.

  • Semantic bridge: the same softness that lets food dissolve without chewing becomes the emotional softness that dissolves judgment — the word moves from matter to feeling by the image of a shapeless yielding mass.


SECTION 2: RICKETY — THE ADJECTIVE

1. Primary modern sense: shaky, loosely jointed, liable to collapse. A rickety thing — a chair, a table, a staircase, a body, a bridge, a government — is weakly constructed or worn until it trembles and threatens to give way.

  • Furniture and structures: a rickety old wooden chair, a rickety scaffold, a rickety boat; the word always implies audible creaking and visible wobble, and an imminent, comic or dreadful collapse.
  • Bodies: a rickety child, rickety legs — frail, bowed, unsteady; the bodily use is the historical origin of the word (see below).
  • Institutions and abstract things: 'a rickety peace', 'a rickety economy' — the metaphor of a structure held together by rust and habit rather than sound timber.


2. Secondary medical sense: affected with rickets. Historically, and still in medical writing, rickety means suffering from rickets — the children's disease in which vitamin D deficiency softens the bones, causing bowing of the legs, deformity of the chest and pelvis, and weakness.

  • Disease history: 'rickets' (1630s) is a corruption of Greek rhachitis 'inflammation of the spine', from rhachis 'spine, ridge'; the English form was perhaps influenced by the word 'wrick' (to twist) and by the old dialect 'rickets' for convulsive twitching.
  • Epidemiology: rickets was a scourge of the Industrial Revolution's sooty cities, where sunlight was scarce; 'rickety' children — bowed-legged, pigeon-chested — filled the slums. The adjective transferred its bony weakness to any wobbly object from the 19th century onward.


3. Semantic bridge between rickety's two senses. The medical sense is the source: a rickety frame is a frame like that of a rickets sufferer — soft-boned, deformed, unsteady. The word's literal 'diseased bones' meaning gave way, by metaphor, to 'wobbly structure', and the Vance image — mush trickling down glazed skin to fall on the rickety frame — makes the homunculus a body made of the two words: soft pulp that cannot hold its shape, on a skeleton too weak to bear it.

SECTION 3: THE WORDS TOGETHER IN CONTEXT

In Turjan of Miir, the creature refuses food; the mush falls, wasted, on its rickety frame. The pairing is a complete physiological diagnosis in two words: the body cannot take in nourishment (mush) and cannot hold itself up (rickety). Both words are words of failure — of matter that cannot solidify and structure that cannot stand.
