Tussocky is the adjective of tussock, meaning covered with, full of, or shaped into tussocks: dense clumps or tufts of coarse grass, sedge, or rushes that rise above the surrounding sward on little hillocks of matted roots, so that the ground becomes lumpy, springy, and treacherous underfoot.

In the source sentence the grass was thick and tussocky, and that single word explains why the hobbits are hindered: they are no longer striding over lawn but labouring through ankle-catching bunches where each step must lift, choose, and balance, while the uneven ground between the clumps hides holes, roots, and wet, and Tolkien, the walking philologist, makes one texture-word carry the whole fatigue of cross-country travel.

Core meaning in detail: a tussock is not merely long grass but grass grown in architecture: each clump binds soil and dead stems into a low dome, often crowned with bleached seed-heads, and tussocky ground alternates these domes with boggy hollows, rabbit runs, or bare earth, so walkers zigzag, stumble, and slow, while grazing animals crop the sweet shoots between and birds nest deep inside the thatch, and the suffix -y works as it does in boggy or rocky, turning the noun of the obstacle into the adjective of the terrain.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Covered with grass tussocks (primary, from the 1700s): tussocky pasture, a tussocky meadow, tussocky moorland, and the bridge is compositional, since tussock plus adjectival -y literally means abounding in tussocks, the same formation that gives tufty and hummocky.
  • Growing in clumps, tufted in habit (botanical): tussocky sedges and tussocky fescues describe plants that tiller outward into stools rather than spreading by runners, and the bridge runs from terrain to plant, since the ground looks tussocky because its grasses grow tussockily, clump by clump.
  • Rough, lumpy, and difficult to cross (transferred): a tussocky football pitch or tussocky track, and the bridge is the walker’s memory, since any surface that trips and jolts like tussock grass borrows the word even where no tussocks grow.
  • Shaggy or unkempt in outline (figurative, rarer): tussocky hair or a tussocky beard in comic description, and the bridge is visual analogy, since uncombed locks stand up in clumps exactly like grass after rain, coarse and separate rather than smooth.
  • Ecological shorthand for a habitat type (technical): tussocky grassland as a conservation category, home to voles, skylarks, and overwintering insects, and the bridge is scientific reuse, since ecologists needed one word for sward kept rough and uncut so small mammals can tunnel beneath the thatch.

Register and connotation: tussocky is a walker’s and naturalist’s word, plain, tactile, faintly comic, smelling of wet boots and seed-heads, and it never merely decorates but always warns: where prose turns tussocky, ankles are about to turn too, and Tolkien deploys it as landscape-as-plot, since the rough ground forces the hobbits out of the road’s ease into the thickets’ danger, one springy clump at a time.
