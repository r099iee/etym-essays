The adjective perfunctory looks like a word about performance, and historically it is — but with a twist that turned "doing the job fully" into "barely doing the job." Its story runs from a reconstructed Indo-European root of enjoyment and use, through a Latin verb of discharging duties, to a Classical Latin adjective of superficial compliance, and finally into English, where it became a quietly damning word for checkbox performance.

The Indo-European root: enjoyment and use
At the deepest recoverable level stands the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰruHg- (also written *bʰrewg-), reconstructed with the sense "to enjoy, to use, to make use of, to have the benefit of." This root is part of a broad family of "use/enjoy/benefit" vocabulary. On the Latin side it underlies fungor "to perform, discharge, busy oneself with," and also the noun frux "fruit, produce" and frūctus "fruit, enjoyment," from which English derives fruit (via Old French), frugal (thrifty in the use of resources, from frūx), and the scientific fructify and fructose. The semantic intuition linking "enjoy" and "use" to "perform a function" is that a role or office is something one "uses" or "works through" in the course of duty. The exact formal equation between the "enjoy" words and fungor is generally accepted but rests on reconstruction; this is a nuance to flag rather than a certainty to assert.

Latin: fungor and the function family
Latin fungor, fungī, functus is a deponent verb meaning "to perform, to execute, to discharge," especially of duties, offices, and obligations: one "functions" by carrying out what a role requires. From this verb Latin built an extraordinarily productive family: functio "performance, execution" (English function, functional), fungibilis "that may be performed or replaced in kind" (English fungible), and the compounds dēfungī "to finish, discharge; to die (having finished life)" (English defunct) and perfungī "to get all the way through, to discharge completely." The Roman sense of duty runs through the whole group: an office is something you work through, and when you have finished it you are "funct" — done with it.

The decisive formation: perfungī and perfūnctōrius
The decisive formation for our word is the compound verb perfungī: the prefix per- ("through, thoroughly, to the end") plus fungī. To perfungī something was to get all the way through it — to discharge it completely. In principle that could be neutral or even conscientious completion. But from the past participle perfūnctus, Latin formed the adjective perfūnctōrius with the suffix -ōrius ("pertaining to"), and the derived adjective specialized toward performance done merely for the sake of getting through: the letter of the duty without the spirit. This is the crucial semantic turn. Classical Latin authors use perfūnctorius of actions done as a matter of form — a cursory, careless, or merely formal performance. The positive "completion" sense had shaded into the pejorative "just to get it over with."

English adoption in the late sixteenth century
English borrowed perfunctory directly from Latin in the late 16th century, already carrying that slightly sour specialization: an action carried out with minimal care, reflection, or zeal, sufficient to claim that the obligation was met. The word arrived as a learned loan — an educated writer's term — and it has remained in that register ever since. In the 17th and 18th centuries it settled firmly as the adjective for duty gone through at minimum intensity: obligation discharged without heart. By the 19th century it had acquired its full critical force in English, describing the perfunctory nod, glance, or task that reveals the actor's indifference; and in the 20th and 21st centuries it became the standard word for institutional checkbox compliance — the opposite of absorbed, thorough work.

Sister words and the semantic field
The "enjoy/use" side of the root family illuminates the word by contrast. Fruit is the enjoyable yield of use; frugality is careful, thrifty use of resources; a function is the proper working or role of a thing; defunct is what is done with, finished, dead. A perfunctory act is performance stripped of fruitful engagement — the duty discharged so as to be free of it, not so as to benefit from it. The word thus stands in permanent contrast to conscientious, thorough, and heartfelt. When Gibbon insists that his ancient history "was never part of the perfunctory duties of an academic post," he mobilizes this whole history in a single adjective: his work was not a function discharged to get it over with, but absorbed labour pursued for its own sake — the very opposite of the Latin adjective English inherited.
