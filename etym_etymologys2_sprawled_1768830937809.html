Derivation chain for SPRAWLED (single root word): Stage 1 — PIE *(s)per- "to scatter, strew, sprinkle, cast outward" (reconstructed; pre-4th millennium BCE). The deepest plausible source connecting the Germanic family to the wider Indo-European scattering-roots; the link is probable but not universally proven. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *sprawlōn "to thrash, to move the limbs convulsively, to spread the limbs about" (reconstructed; before c. 500 CE). The Germanic verbal base in which the scattering root was specialised toward violent limb motion. Stage 3 — Old English sprēawlian "to move convulsively, writhe, kick about; to sprawl the limbs" (attested in the Old English period, 9th–11th centuries). Stage 4 — Middle English spraulen / sprowlen "to sprawl, thrash, spread the limbs carelessly" (attested 14th–15th centuries). Stage 5 — Early Modern English sprawl "to lie or sit with the limbs spread out carelessly; to extend loosely" (16th–17th centuries), when the violent sense relaxed into the modern posture sense. Stage 6 — Modern English sprawled, past tense and past participle of the verb sprawl ◄ TARGET. Note on the debate: The connection between PIE *(s)per- and Proto-Germanic *sprawlōn rests on an irregular consonant frame and is disputed; some authorities treat the Germanic family as independent or of imitative/sound-symbolic origin. The Germanic-to-English chain (stages 2–6) is itself fully secure and attested at every stage.
