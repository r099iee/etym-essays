DERIVATION CHAIN FOR "MARTYRED":
Stage 1 — PIE root: *smer- "to remember, be mindful of, recall" (reconstructed root of memory and mindful attention).
Stage 2 — Greek: mártys (μάρτυς) "witness," literally "one who remembers and can testify" (attested in classical Greek, from an *smer-tu- formation); the witness is etymologically the one who remembers what happened and can speak to it in court.
Stage 3 — Christian Greek: mártys (μάρτυς) "one who bears witness to Christ; one who dies for the faith" (1st–2nd centuries AD); the sense shift from "courtroom witness" to "witness sealed in blood" took place in the persecutions of the early Church.
Stage 4 — Latin (ecclesiastical): martyr "a martyr" (2nd century AD; used by Tertullian, d. c. 225).
Stage 5 — Old English: martyr / martir (10th century) "a martyr," borrowed from ecclesiastical Latin.
Stage 6 — English: martyr (noun), the form stable from Old English onward; reinforced in the 14th century by a second borrowing through Old French martir (via Anglo-Norman).
Stage 7 — English verb: to martyr "to put to death for one's faith or cause."
Stage 8 — English: MARTYRED — past participle / adjective of "to martyr," "killed because of one's beliefs."

DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY (explicit notes):
The derivation of Greek mártys from PIE *smer- is the traditional scholarly view (Pokorny, Chantraine): the witness as "the one who remembers." Some modern linguists, notably Beekes, are more cautious, pointing out that the Greek formation is phonologically odd and raising the possibility of a pre-Greek substrate origin; the *smer- connection therefore remains the standard account but is not universally accepted. Note also that the related Greek mérimna "care, anxiety" is a genuine *smer- derivative, but the -mn- words such as mnḗma and mnḗmē belong to the separate root *men- "to think," and English "mourn" descends from yet another root, *mer- "to die"; neither belongs under *smer-.
