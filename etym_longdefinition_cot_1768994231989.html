cot — a light, narrow bedstead, usually a wooden or metal frame strung with canvas or woven webbing, light enough to be folded, carried, or moved from room to room. In the literary context of this passage — a child recovering from a wound, tended for two weeks in an upstairs room by the tavernkeeper's wife — the word is chosen for its bare, provisional quality. It is not a bed of comfort or permanence but an object of pure utility: a minimal platform upon which a body is laid to heal before being returned to the road. The noun arrives with almost no descriptive support; nothing about its material, its age, or its softness is recorded, and that withholding is the point. The cot is the furniture of a life that owns almost nothing, in which rest itself is a temporary necessity borrowed from someone else's hospitality rather than a settled domestic right. Its single clipped syllable — /kɒt/ — falls flat and quickly, an auditory echo of something insubstantial and easily folded away; the child will lie on it for two weeks and then leave in the night, and the cot will remain behind, unremarked, as anonymous as the room. This is nuance the word carries that "bed" never could: "bed" promises comfort, privacy, and belonging, whereas "cot" promises only support, and even that support is lent.

Primary senses: (1) a light portable bed, especially a folding camp bed or hospital bed; (2) in British English, a baby's crib (a small bed with high sides for an infant); (3) historically, a hammock-like string bed or a narrow bedstead. All three centre on smallness, lightness, and simplicity.

  • Camp/army cot: the standard 20th-century American sense — a folding field bed of canvas or webbing strung on a collapsible frame; the bed of barracks, camps, and emergencies.
  • Baby cot / crib (British): a small bed with raised sides for an infant; the source of the compound "cot death" (SIDS). The British sense is a 19th-century transferred extension of the portable-bed idea to the baby's small enclosed bed.
  • Hospital/institutional cot: the narrow, practical bed of wards and charity rooms — a bed lent to a body, not owned by it.
  • Historical Indian sense: the word was borrowed from Hindi khaṭ (खाट), the light woven frame-bed of the subcontinent strung with rope or cotton webbing — the very "charpoy" of India, portable and collapsible.
  • Separate homonym: an unrelated native English word cot/cote means "a small shelter, hut, or shed" (as in sheepcot, dovecote, and the second element of cottage); it shares the spelling but not the etymology, and it is not the word on this card.

Boundary words map the precise corner the word occupies. A bed is the general domestic term, a piece of permanent furniture for sleeping, carrying associations of comfort, intimacy, and a settled household; a bunk is a narrow bed built into a wall, a ship's cabin, or a railway carriage — permanent but cramped, designed for shared quarters; a berth is specifically a sleeping place on a ship or train, often with an institutional or travelling overtone; a pallet is a makeshift bed laid directly on the floor, of straw or blankets, suggesting poverty or improvisation; a crib is the baby's bed, and in American English also a rough translation of the cot's small enclosed function; a charpoy is the Indian string-bed from which the English cot descends, still portable but culturally specific; a hammock is a swinging bed of canvas or netting suspended at both ends, portable in a different way. Against all of these, cot is the free-standing, light, easily moved frame-bed — the furniture of transience, exactly right for a wounded child healing in borrowed upstairs space before vanishing into the night.
