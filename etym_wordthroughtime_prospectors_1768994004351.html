- 15th c. the noun prospect - English takes "prospect" from Latin prospectus, "a looking forward": a landscape seen from a height, an expectation of what lies ahead.
- 18th c. mineral turn - The word shifts into mining: a "prospect" becomes a place showing signs of ore, and "to prospect" means to search ground for mineral wealth.
- 19th c. gold rush birth - "Prospector" enters American English with the California gold rush - the lone frontier gold-seeker, reading the horizon for color and outcrop.
- 19th-20th c. expansion - Spreads to the Alaskan and Australian rushes (the sourdoughs) and on to oil and other minerals, yet keeps the romantic image of the man who stakes a life on a forward gaze.
- Contemporary sense - At once heroic and ominous: the professional seeker of wealth whose own record can be written in disappearance.
