Note on the debated points. (1) The PIE stage is contested. Latin adagium is usually connected with a PIE verb of saying of the shape *h₂ed-, on the strength of the Latin verb aiō ‘I say’, ait ‘he says’, aiunt ‘they say’ and of the consonant shape -dɣ- inside the word. Etymonline instead points to a root *ad- ‘to say’ and adds a comparison with Greek aei ‘always’, which many specialists regard as weak or erroneous; the Greek adverb belongs with a different root and the semantics do not match. (2) The older derivation from agere. A long-standing alternative, found in nineteenth-century English dictionaries and still repeated, analyses adagium as ad + agere ‘to do’, so that it would literally mean ‘a deed, a thing done’, and thence a motto or a saying attached to an act. Latin agere would give an -ag- rather than an -adag-, so the analysis does not account for the shape of the word, and it is rejected by modern lexicography. (3) The -ium ending.adagium is formed on the neuter noun-suffix -ium (as in imperium, consortium), which is a Latin word-formational device rather than a diminutive proper; describing it as a diminutive of a hypothetical adaga is a convenient shorthand, not a fact. (4) No separate PIE form for the prefix. The ad- element in Latin generally expresses direction or purpose, not speech, and should not be confused with the ablaut-free root consonant d- carried over from *h₂ed-.
