- 1560s - plod (verb) to walk heavily or laboriously - imitative of the sound of heavy footsteps; purely physical
- Late 16th c. - plodding slow, heavy, laborious - the adjective describes gait, and plodder one who plods describes a drudge
- 17th c. - plodder: a person of dull but patient industry - the word begins to attach to character, not just movement
- 18th c. - plodding comes to describe mental and scholarly work: patient, slow, unglamorous study as opposed to brilliance
- 19th-20th c. - plodding develops a mildly dismissive tone (plodding bureaucracy), while keeping the admiring sense of steady perseverance
- 20th-21st c. - the Plod: a jocular British nickname for the police (from walking the beat on foot); in literary prose, plodding gloom names a heavy, trudging sadness
- Modern - plodding retains both the physical trudge and the figurative slow-but-dogged quality across walking, work, criticism, and atmosphere
