- [c. 1300–1450 Middle English] — Borrowed via Anglo-Norman from Old French conjurur / Latin coniūrātor, originally denoting one who binds others by a solemn mutual oath (a conspirator), but quickly specializing in theological and legal discourse for one who invokes or exorcises spirits by holy or unholy names — [perception: terrifying, ambiguous, and legally hazardous figure suspected of demonic traffic or solemn theurgic power].
- [c. 1500–1650 Tudor & Jacobean Eras] — Codified in English witchcraft statutes (such as the Witchcraft Act of 1542 and 1604) that outlawed "conjuration of evil and wicked spirits"; concurrently, skeptical writers like Reginald Scot (The Discoverie of Witchcraft, 1584) argued that "conjurors" were merely clever hucksters using optics, false bottoms, and sleight of hand — [perception: dual perception: a dangerous criminal heretic to religious zealots, but a clever stage deceiver to skeptics].
- [c. 1680–1800 18th Century & Augustan Enlightenment] — Solidified into the dominant colloquial word for stage magicians, parlor entertainers, and itinerant tricksters who dazzled crowds at Bartholomew Fair; generated the famous ironic idiom "he is no conjuror" (meaning he is a dullard of mediocre intellect) — [perception: respectable theatrical amusement; secularized symbol of harmless dexterity and comic wonder].
- [19th c. Victorian & Romantic Era] — Retained for theatrical performers while simultaneously preserved in rural folk culture to describe "cunning men" or white wizards who found lost livestock and cured bewitched butter; celebrated in literature as a mysterious wanderer — [perception: blend of nostalgic rustic folklore and popular middle-class music hall entertainment].
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary English] — Largely superseded in commercial entertainment by magician or illusionist, but retained in fantasy literature (notably Tolkien's Shire hobbits calling Gandalf an "old wandering conjuror"), British legal history, and classic literary idiom — [perception: flavorful, evocative archaism rich with both mysterious folkloric wonder and wry secular skepticism].
