- Old English "lēaf" (7th-11th c.) - the word already means both foliage and a parchment sheet; perception: flat, thin, layered things share one name.
- Glossing gold (14th-16th c.) - thin metal sheets become "leaves"; gilded statues, altars and books; perception: preciousness and craft.
- The page of reform (16th c.) - "turning over a new leaf" moralises the page-metaphor; perception: renewal, repentance, fresh starts.
- The verb collision (Old English onward) - the unrelated verb lǣfan "to go away, leave behind" produces the identically spelled and pronounced form leaves; perception: grammar-land homograph, eternal learner confusion.
- Mechanical and domestic extension (17th-20th c.) - table leaves, leaf springs, loose-leaf binders; perception: useful flatness applied everywhere.
- Digital abstraction (20th-21st c.) - leaf nodes in trees and spreadsheets, leaf-peeping and leaf blowers in the environmental age; perception: clean logic and green symbolism in one word.
