- 15th century - English takes 'venerable' from Latin 'venerabilis' (worthy of reverence), the word of the church: it is the honorific of age and sanctity, passing into English as 'the Venerable Bede,' a title for those of hallowed age and learning.
- 16th-18th century - the word broadens from the sacred to the civic: venerable men of state, venerable institutions and customs; it still fuses age with worth, and the ecclesiastical title lives on in Anglican usage.
- 19th century - as reverence itself thins in public life, 'venerable' becomes the word for anything impressively old and dignified — trees, universities, traditions — keeping a liturgical dignity and a hint of antiquity.
- Contemporary - 'venerable' remains solemn and hierarchical, fusing age, moral worth, and hallowed association; it attaches to persons, customs, and institutions alike ('a venerable tradition'), and its church-title aura still gives it a faint, reverent glow.
