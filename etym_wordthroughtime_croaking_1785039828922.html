- 15th century - Enters English as a direct imitation of the raven's and frog's harsh, grating call; the sound is immediately felt as ominous, for the raven's croak was read as a portent of evil.
- 17th-19th centuries - The word goes figurative: a 'croaker' is a prophet of doom who croaks complaints and forebodings, and in 19th-century slang to 'croak' means to die, as the body's last rough breath leaves the throat.
- Contemporary sense - Croaking still describes the low, hoarse, guttural noise of birds, frogs, and failing voices; it carries the same animal, ill-omened undertone it has had for five centuries.
