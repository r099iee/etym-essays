The history of loath is the history of hatred turning inward. It begins in the Germanic north with a reconstructed adjective *laiþaz, meaning hateful, hostile, grievous, a word aimed outward at enemies, wounds, and hateful deeds. Its Old English heir lāð fills Beowulf's world with exactly this outward hatred: Grendel is lāð, hostile to Heorot; injuries are lāð, grievous to bear; exile is lāð, hateful to the heart. The related verb lāðian, to hate, and the noun lāðð, hatred, show a whole family built on hostility felt toward things.

Around 1200 something quiet but profound happens: the hatred migrates from the object to the subject. Middle English writers begin to call not the dreaded thing but the dreading person loth: he is loth to go means he hates the thought of going. Linguists call this a reversal of orientation, and it mirrors a wider medieval softening, as the stark warrior vocabulary of enmity is reworked into the vocabulary of feeling, courtesy, and conscience. Reluctance is born from repulsion: I will not do it because I hate it.

Stages of the change:

  • Germanic hostility: *laiþaz and Old English lāð name what is hateful, from foes to afflictions, with cognates across the Germanic languages such as Old Saxon lēð, Old High German leid, and modern German Leid (sorrow) and leider (unfortunately), all remembering ancient grievance.
  • Middle English inward turn (c. 1200): the adjective reattaches to the feeler, giving loth to speak and loth to depart, the ancestors of every modern loath to admit.
  • The verb splits off: Middle English lothen and modern loathe carry the active hatred (I loathe cruelty), while the bare adjective keeps the passive reluctance (I am loath to accuse), identical in speech but divided in spelling by the verb's silent -e.
  • Early modern idiom: nothing loath, willing and glad, flourishes in chronicles, Shakespeare's England, and the King James Bible's world (he was nothing loath), its litotes lending willingness the grace of understatement.
  • Modern spelling wobble: because adjective and verb sound alike, loathe to for loath to spreads in print, now so common that some dictionaries list it as a variant, though style guides still defend the -e-less adjective.

Socially the word has climbed while narrowing. Where Old English lāð belonged to battlefields and blood-feuds, modern loath belongs to committee rooms and confession scenes: ministers are loath to intervene, witnesses loath to testify, lovers in translations nothing loath to stay. Its German cousins kept the sorrow (Leid) while English kept the shrinking, and the fairy-tale prince of the source sentence, dismounting nothing loath at Maria Morevna's side, shows the word's final courtesy: reluctance denied becomes devotion declared.
