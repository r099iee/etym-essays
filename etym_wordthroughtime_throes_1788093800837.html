- [c. 1200: "anguish, agony"] - throws enters Middle English as a word of intense pain and distress, applied to physical suffering and to the torments of sin in religious writing; the word is about pain itself, nothing else.
- [14th–16th centuries: birth and death] - The word attaches to the two great bodily struggles: "childbed throes" and the "throes of death"; the plural becomes dominant, because agony comes in waves and spasms, not as a single moment.
- [16th–17th centuries: the -oe- spelling] - Respelled throe(s) on the model of "throw" — the writhing, heaving body is imagined as throwing itself about; the word gains a dramatic, almost theatrical register and the fixed collocations death-throes and birth-throes.
- [18th–19th centuries: the figurative leap] - "The throes of revolution", "the throes of war": nations, causes and ideas undergo throes; pain shifts from the body to the body politic; "in the throes of" becomes the standard idiom for being helplessly caught in a painful process.
- [20th–21st centuries: creative upheaval] - People speak of the throes of creativity, of a takeover, of adolescence, of transformation; the word retains its convulsive physical image but now names any violent, inescapable middle-phase — including Tolkien's dragon, whose last throes splintered wood to sparks and glowing embers.
