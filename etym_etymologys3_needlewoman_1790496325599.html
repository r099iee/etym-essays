needlewoman is a young compound of two very old words, and its evolution must be told as three strands braided late: the five-thousand-year career of the needle, the thousand-year career of woman, and the three-hundred-year career of their union.

Needle begins in PIE *(s)neh-, to sew, to spin, to twist thread, with the famous s-mobile — an s- that comes and goes between daughters. With the s- it gives Latin nere (to spin), Greek nein (to spin), Old Irish snaithe (thread, needle); without it, Germanic *neþlo-, the sewing implement itself, formed with the instrumental suffix *-thlo- (as in spindle). Cognates crowd the sewing basket: Old High German nadala (modern Nadel), Old Norse nal, Dutch naald, Gothic nethla, Old English nædl. The consonant þ/d shows Verner's-law voicing inside the paradigm, and the long vowel æ/eː raises through the Great Vowel Shift to modern /niːdəl/. For millennia the object barely changes — a sliver of bone, bronze, iron, steel with an eye — because fingers do not change; archaeologists find eyed needles 20,000 years old that a Victorian needlewoman could thread today.

  • Woman from wifmann: Old English did not originally say woman but wif (female, wife) and mann (human being, person of either sex). A female human was a wifmann — wife-person — plural wifmenn, whose f voiced to m and whose vowel rounded under the influence of the w-: wifmann → wimman → woman (/ˈwʊmən/), plural women (/ˈwɪmɪn/). Mann itself continues PIE *man-/*mon- (person, thinker; Sanskrit manu, German Mann/Mensch), while wif has no secure PIE pedigree — proposals link it to weave or to a root for shame/veiled, but none commands consensus, and honest etymology leaves it marked unknown. By 1300 woman is the ordinary word, and compounds like gentlewoman, charwoman, needlewoman form freely on it as class-markers.
  • Seamstress before needlewoman: medieval England called a sewing woman a seamestre — seam plus feminine agent -estre (as in spinster, songstress). Needlewoman appears later, in the 1600s–1700s, on the analogy of gentlewoman: it sounds politer than seamstress because it names the tool rather than the seam, and the person rather than the piece-rate. Eighteenth-century advertisements seek a sober needlewoman for a household; parish registers list widows by the occupation needlewoman where men are tailor — the gendered division of cutting (male, prestigious) versus sewing (female, humble) written into lexis.
  • School prize and sweated trade (1800s): Regency boarding schools like Mrs Rice's at Peckham examined needlework as character: hemming, seaming, marking linen, working a Scripture sampler in cross-stitch. To be best needlewoman was to display the evangelical virtues — industry, neatness, submission — in cloth. Within a generation the same word darkens: the slop-shops of the East End employ thousands of needlewomen at starvation rates, and Hood, Dickens, Gaskell, and Mayhew turn her into the era's conscience-figure. Legislation, emigration societies, and co-operative shirt-factories all organise for the needlewoman; the compound that began as a compliment becomes an appeal.

The sewing machine (Singer, 1850s) and the ready-made industry remove the economic base, and garment worker replaces needlewoman in statistics; but schools, convents, and craft revivals keep the older word for the skilled hand. Ruskin's sentence catches it at its most innocent — before the Song of the Shirt, when a mother's best stitching still promised a godly daughter rather than an empty stomach.
