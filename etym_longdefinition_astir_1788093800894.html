Astir (adjective/adverb, used predicatively) describes a state of being roused into motion or activity: up and about, out of bed and busy, or thrown into excited commotion. In the sentence “In the camp all was now astir, as if for battle,” the word paints an entire company suddenly moving — arming, assembling, exchanging grim news — a collective stirring the narrator senses without listing a single action.

Grammar and position. Astir behaves like the other a- + noun/adjective compounds of English (asleep, afoot, abed, aglow, alive, astride): it stands only after a linking verb (the camp was astir; the town lay astir), never in attributive position before a noun (*an astir camp), and it takes no comparative or superlative degree. Its a- is the old English preposition on (“on, in, at”) worn down to a prefix, so astir is literally “in a stir” — resting inside the state named by the noun stir.

Primary senses — physical commotion.

  • Up and about: the first and still central use is of people (or creatures) awake, out of bed, and moving: “the household was astir before dawn.”
  • In motion collectively: camps, armies, cities, and streets are astir when everyone within them is bustling — preparing, marching, carrying loads, calling orders.
  • Roused by an event: a place is “astir with” news, rumour, or alarm; the motion is provoked from outside, and the word carries an undertone of urgency or expectancy (the camp astir “as if for battle”).

Secondary senses and figurative shifts.

  • Agitation and excitement: by a natural bridge from physical bustle to inner stir, astir also means emotionally or socially agitated: “the whole kingdom was astir at the news.” The sensory core (bodies moving) is transferred to minds and moods; the stir is felt rather than seen, but the intuitive bridge is obvious — a crowd that physically bustles is also emotionally stirred.
  • Awakening from rest: set against sleeping, the word stresses emergence: “astir early,” “still astir at midnight” — activity measured against a baseline of stillness or night.
  • Abstract forces astir: in elevated prose, winds, seas, rumours, and even fate can be astir, personifying nature or history as a stirring agent: “a strange wind was astir among the trees.” The word becomes a stylistic device for invisible but felt force.

Domain uses.

  • Military and maritime prose: dispatches and logs of the 19th–20th centuries use astir for crews turning out at an alarm and fleets preparing to sail.
  • Journalism of the 19th century: standard phrases like “the city was astir” summarised crowd excitement without naming events.
  • Fantasy and historical fiction: Tolkien and later fantasy writers favour astir for camp and battlefield tableaux (as in this context), where one word supplies period flavour and brisk collective motion.

Why the word means all this. The semantic history is a chain of transfers around one physical image: things stirring — moving, mixing, turning — came to name the movement itself (stir), then the state of being moved (the noun stir also means “fuss, excitement”, as in “a stir in the market”), and finally the adjectival condition of being inside that state (a-stir). The prefix compounds of English (afoot, aglow, abed) form a closed, archaic-feeling set, and their very formality lends astir its mildly literary tone today: perfectly clear, but with a whiff of the campfire and the chronicle rather than the daily news bulletin. It is not a technical word in any field; its power is purely atmospheric — announcing that many people are awake, moving, and expectant, on the edge of some event.
