ROOT WORDS: dampness = damp (noun/adjective) + -ness (suffix). Two lexical components require two chains, each in isolation.
CHAIN 1 — damp:
Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *dʰewh₂- / *dʰeu- “to rise in a cloud, to smoke, to be misty or vaporous” (reconstructed; among its reflexes are Sanskrit dhūmá “smoke,” Greek thūmós “breath, spirit,” Latin fūmus “smoke,” Old Church Slavonic dymъ “smoke”): the great Indo-European root of vapour and smoke.
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *dampaz “vapour, steam, exhalation” (reconstructed): an extended formation on the same *dʰeu- family, with a nasal-and-labial extension *-m-bʰ-; lying behind Old Norse dampi, Middle Dutch damp, Middle Low German damp, and modern German Dampf “steam.”
Stage 3 — Middle Dutch damp / Middle Low German damp “vapour, steam, smoke, fog” (attested medieval): the continental form borrowed into English.
Stage 4 — Middle English damp “vapour, smoke; noxious gas (as of a mine)” (borrowed 14th c.): the miner’s word, dread of the galleries.
Stage 5 — Modern English damp (noun) “moisture, humidity; noxious vapour in a mine” and (adjective) “slightly wet, moist” (adjective sense 17th c.): the sense drifts from gas toward wetness; the adjective follows the noun.
CHAIN 2 — the suffix -ness:
Stage 1 — PIE (uncertain, debated): the ultimate origin of the Germanic nominal suffix is not securely established; the second element *-assu- has been compared with the PIE root *h₂es- “to be” (a suffix of “state of being X”), but this is speculative.
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *-inassus “state, quality, condition” (reconstructed; reflected by Gothic -inassus): a compound suffix *-in- + *-assu- forming abstract nouns.
Stage 3 — Old English -nes(s), -nis(se) “state, quality, condition” (attested; highly productive, e.g. hālnes “health,” gōdnes “goodness”): the direct Old English continuation.
Stage 4 — Middle English -ness(e) (attested 12th–15th c.): unchanged in function.
Stage 5 — Modern English -ness; combined with damp to form dampness “the state of being damp” (first recorded 16th c.) = DAMPNESS (the form on this card): the abstract noun of the condition.
Notes on debate and nuance: The connection of Proto-Germanic *dampaz to PIE *dʰeu- is widely accepted, but the exact morphological bridge (the *-m-bʰ- extension) is not reconstructed with certainty in every handbook, and some scholars prefer to treat *dampaz as an imitative Germanic formation without secure PIE ancestry. The *-assu- element of the -ness suffix has no universally accepted PIE etymology; the “to be” hypothesis is speculative. The English noun and adjective damp developed from the same borrowed word: the noun (“noxious vapour, moisture”) is older, and the adjective “slightly wet” is a seventeenth-century transfer.”
