Stumped (past tense and past participle of stump) in its primary sense here means walked heavily, stiffly, and noisily — a gait that suggests wooden legs, irritated purpose, or the thud of someone marching on residual stumps rather than gliding on soft soles. In Tolkien's scene Bilbo does not merely leave the room; he stumped off to the pantries after Gandalf's call, a verb that freezes the hobbit mid-protest: body and will both balking. The sound-image is domestic comedy — Bilbo's usual quiet, carpeted dignity replaced by clumping indignation as he is driven to fetch yet more food for uninvited guests. The very phonetics enact the footfall: the plosive onset /st-/ and the snapped final /-mp/ make each step land as a distinct, faintly offended thump. The gait is not fast — Bilbo is not storming out — but it is emphatic; every footfall announces a small inner declaration that this errand is beneath him. The walk is the hobbit's body broadcasting what politeness will not yet allow him to say aloud: irritation made audible, hospitality performed under protest.

The word's polysemy is unusually rich, and each sense must be kept distinct. First and oldest, the noun stump: the short remaining base of a cut tree, and by extension any truncated leftover — a wooden leg, an amputated limb, a broken tooth, a candle end, a pencil stub, or the three vertical posts of a cricket wicket. Second, the verb of motion built on that noun: to walk heavily and clumsily as if on a wooden leg or on the short base of a cut-off limb (the sense Tolkien uses). Third, the verbal sense “to baffle, nonplus, or bring to a halt”: stumped as “completely at a loss”, as if one had run hard against a tree-stump and stopped short — the overwhelmingly dominant sense of stumped in modern everyday English (an examiner stumps a candidate; a question leaves one stumped). Fourth, in cricket, stumped names a specific mode of dismissal: the batter is out when, having left the crease to play the ball, the wicketkeeper breaks the wicket (knocks off the bails with the ball in hand) before the batter regains the crease — a technical sporting sense drawn from the same wooden stumps. Fifth, in American political idiom, to stump or to be on the stump means to campaign, speaking from a tree-stump platform to rural crowds (early 19th century): hence stump speech, stumping for a candidate. Sixth, the British idiom to stump up means to pay up reluctantly or cough up money. Seventh, the verb to stub (one's toe) is the closely related word for striking against a stump-like obstacle. The semantic core unifying all of them is the image of the blunt, truncated remainder: a thing cut short, and a person stopped short — in motion, in argument, or at the wicket.

The word must be set against its walking-neighbours, which an attentive reader should not flatten together. Trudged (weary, heavy-footed resignation) drags its feet in exhaustion and shows no anger; stumped clumps, stiff and percussive, charged with indignation. Strode is the confident, purposeful stride of someone at ease with their errand, the exact opposite of stumped's grudging compliance. Plodded is slow, flat-footed, and monotonous, heavy with weariness rather than stiffness. Clumped is the nearest sound-cousin, but it emphasises heavy boots and mud, whereas stumped draws on the image of a cut-off base — a wooden leg, a residual stump — so the walker is not muddy but shortened, moving on stubs with a rigid, faintly absurd dignity. Shuffled, by contrast, is soft, reluctant, and dragging, with no anger in it at all; limped implies injury and pain. The distinguishing core is the fused wooden-truncated quality: stumping is a march of protest performed on what remains.

Register, collocation, and usage. In its walking sense the verb is informal and mildly comic, at home in narrative fiction, children's books, and period comedy, and faintly archaic or rustic in British English. It is overwhelmingly intransitive, typically with an adverbial of destination: stump off, stump away, stump up the stairs, stump out of the room. The separate idiomatic senses must not be imported into Tolkien's line: stumped as “baffled” is the very next emotion Bilbo will feel, on a different word; here the verb is literal — he walks, and the sound of his walking speaks his protest.

  • Sense 1 — walk heavily: to move with heavy, stiff, noisy steps, as on a wooden leg or on residual stumps (Tolkien's sense; verb attested c. 1600–1610).
  • Sense 2 — baffle: to bring to a stop, nonplus, reduce to silence; stumped = utterly at a loss (extension, 19th c.).
  • Sense 3 — cricket: dismissal when the wicketkeeper breaks the wicket while the batter is out of the crease (18th–19th c.).
  • Sense 4 — politics: on the stump, stump speech, stumping for a candidate — campaigning from a tree-stump platform (American, early 19th c.).
  • Sense 5 — idiom: stump up = pay reluctantly (British).
  • Related noun: the stump itself — truncated base of tree, limb, tooth, candle, or cricket wicket.
