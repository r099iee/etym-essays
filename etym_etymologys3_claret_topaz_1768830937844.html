I. CLARET: from the "clear little wine" to the deep red of the sky

Claret is a wine-word that became a colour-word, and both halves of its history are visible in its form. The story begins not in France but in the oldest strata of Indo-European vocabulary. The reconstructed PIE root *kelh- / *kle- meant "to call, shout, resound," and it produced Latin clamare "to shout," calare "to proclaim," and the adjective clarus, "clear." The semantic development of clarus is a beautiful example of a metaphor crossing from one sense to another: what is resounding and distinct to the ear becomes clear and distinct to the eye, and from "clear, bright" it extends to "famous" — the clarus vir is the man whose name rings out, the man who is loudly known. So the stem that eventually yields claret begins as an idea of sound: the wine's name is built, at a distance of thousands of years, on a root that meant "to call."

In the Romance languages clarus became Old French cler (later clair), "clear, bright." From cler, Old French built a diminutive with the suffix -et: claret, literally "the clear little one." The word was first applied to a wine — a light, clear red wine, paler and more transparent than the deep, dark reds — and by the thirteenth century claret was an established French name for such a wine. English borrowed it in the fourteenth century (Middle English claret), and for the next few centuries it meant simply "a light, clear red wine," the word's own meaning of "clear" built into its name. The great narrowing came in the fifteenth through eighteenth centuries, when the English trade fixed the word on the red wines of Bordeaux. Claret became the gentleman's wine, a byword for richness and civilization, and the word entered the rituals of English drinking culture — a claret jug, a claret glass, the claret of the club and the dinner table.

The colour-sense is a later, natural extension. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the deep translucent ruby of Bordeaux red was being used as a colour-name — claret of cloth, of glass, of ink — and then of the sky itself. The card's context gives the word its fullest modern life: "beams of claret" sweep over the watcher among the "rich violet, radiant green" of an enchanted sky. The word imports into fantasy the bottle, the table, the civilization of the wine trade — an oddly homely richness among the jewel-hues. And its etymology continues to work: because claret meant "clear," the colour claret is a paradox the passage quietly exploits — at once the deepest red and the most limpid, a red that light passes through rather than one that absorbs all light. The pronunciation has settled as /klAret/ in English (with the characteristic English treatment of the French a), and the word now names both a wine and a colour, the deep red that light travels through, the clear red the word has been since the thirteenth century.

  • Sound and form: The English pronunciation /klAret/ reflects a medieval borrowing; the spelling claret has been fixed since the 14th c. The word is both noun (the wine, the colour) and, occasionally, attributive adjective.
  • Register: A gentlemanly, cultured word — wine, table, club, and palette; in the colour-sense it is literary and ornamental.
  • Literary attestations: Claret appears throughout English literature as the wine of conviviality — in Restoration comedy, in Georgian and Victorian drinking scenes, in the colour-language of poets describing deep translucent red.
  • In the card's context: "beams of claret, rich violet, radiant green" — the word lends the enchanted sky the clarity and richness of a fine red wine, a red you can see through, at once the deepest and the most limpid of the jewel-hues.

II. TOPAZ: a golden stone from an uncertain east

The story of topaz is the story of a gemstone whose name has drifted, changed colour, and ultimately been fixed by science — and of an etymology that remains, unusually for such a famous word, honestly uncertain. The word enters European history in Greek as topazos (TOPAZOS), the name of a golden gemstone, recorded in the first century BCE by Strabo and in the first century CE by Pliny the Elder. Pliny, with his fondness for a good story, reported that the stone took its name from Topazos, an island in the Red Sea (today Zabargad, or St. John's Island, off Egypt), where the stone was said to be found; but scholars have long suspected that the island-name is a back-formation from the gem-name rather than its source. The true ultimate source of topazos is unknown. A connection with Sanskrit tapas, "heat, fire, glow" — reflecting the stone's golden, sun-warmed colour — has been proposed more than once, and it is a seductive idea, but it is speculative and cannot be established; the word may well come from an older South Arabian or other Afro-Asiatic term now lost. This is one of the few famous gem-names whose origin scholarship must honestly mark as uncertain.

From Greek the word passed into Latin as topazus and into the languages of medieval Europe. English had topas and topaz by the thirteenth century, borrowed through Old French topace and topaze. For centuries the name was applied loosely to any golden or yellow gem, since colour — not mineralogy — governed the ancient and medieval naming. The fixing of the stone is a modern, scientific event: in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries mineralogy pinned topaz to a single translucent crystal of aluminium fluorosilicate, and the colour-word followed, meaning a golden yellow warmed toward orange — as if sunlight were trapped in the stone. In modern English topaz names both the gem and the colour: a clear, brilliant yellow that poets and fashion alike love for its rarity and polish. The card's context uses exactly this double life, making "beams of claret, topaz, rich violet, radiant green" a sequence of gem-light hues sweeping over the watcher — the wine-red, the golden-yellow, the violet, the green of an enchanted sky refracted like jewels. The etymology, uncertain at its root, has left the word free to carry the pure golden idea: a stone that seems to hold sunlight, golden heat made solid.

  • Sound and form: The modern pronunciation /tEUpaz/ shows the Great Vowel Shift's effect on the medieval /to-pas/; the spelling was regularized as topaz in the early modern period.
  • Register: A jewel-word of the palette — gemmology, poetry, fashion, and decorative description.
  • Literary attestations: Topaz has been a colour-word of light and treasure from medieval lapidaries through Renaissance poetry to modern fantasy, where it names a golden glow among the jewel-hues of enchanted skies.
  • In the card's context: "beams of claret, topaz, rich violet, radiant green" — the topaz beam is the golden-yellow ray, sunlight made stone, among the refracted colours of the sky's great loom.
