- 11th to 12th Century - Emerges in Old French epic poetry (such as the *Chanson de Roland*: "Roland est preut e Olivier est sage"); denotes chivalric prudence, tactical moderation, and sound counsel contrasted with reckless bravado.
- 13th to 14th Century - Deepens in medieval scholastic theology and vernacular biblical translations; designates divine wisdom (*Sapientia*), the personified wisdom of God, and the supreme theological virtue guiding the Christian soul.
- 16th Century (The Renaissance) - Transformed by Michel de Montaigne's *Essais* into a secular humanist philosophy of life; becomes the art of living well, maintaining skepticism toward dogmatism, and achieving peace amidst mortal human frailty.
- 17th to 18th Century (Classical and Enlightenment Era) - Cultivated by French classical moralists (La Rochefoucauld, La Fontaine, Voltaire); signifies rational moderation, social poise, philosophical restraint, and clarity of judgment.
- 19th Century - Bifurcates between high poetic and religious redemption (Paul Verlaine's 1881 masterpiece collection *Sagesse*) and domestic nursery parlance describing obedient, well-behaved children ("la sagesse d'un enfant").
- 20th to 21st Century - Canonized in Proustian and continental philosophical criticism in both French and English; characterizes the profound, melancholic, yet redemptive existential insight into time, memory, and human mortality.
