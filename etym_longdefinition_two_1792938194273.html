Two is the cardinal numeral denoting one more than one: the number 2, the smallest plurality, the first number that makes a pair, a couple, a duality, a contest, or a choice. To count one, two is to leave solitude behind; everything that needs exactly two hands, two eyes, two sides, or two minds belongs to two.

In the source sentence the numeral does lamplight arithmetic: white bollards by the water glimmer in the light of ______ lamps on high posts. Not one lamp, which would leave half the quay in shadow, and not a blaze of many, but a measured pair — twin pools of light answering each other across the dark, enough for a working waterfront at night. The smallness of the number matters: two lamps are countable at a glance, intimate and sufficient, the harbour’s modest constellation.

Core meaning in detail: Mathematically two is the first prime and the only even prime, the base of binary reckoning, the number of points that determine a line and of parents every child has. Linguistically it is suppletive and ancient, with distinct forms for masculine, feminine, and neuter in Old English, and it governs special grammar — both, either, neither, between — that larger numbers never command. Culturally it carries duality everywhere: two sides of a quarrel, two faces of a coin, two halves of a whole.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Cardinal number 2 (primary, timeless): one plus one, written 2, II, or two. The bridge is counting itself: the second step of every tally ever made.
  • A pair or couple of things (as in the source): two lamps, two hands, two friends. The bridge is grouping: the numeral slides from abstract count to the concrete brace it counts.
  • A small indefinite few (colloquial): a minute or two, a thing or two. The bridge runs through understatement: two is the smallest plural, so it politely minimises — just two seconds rarely means exactly two.
  • Duality, opposition, and choice (figurative): the two sides, torn in two, two minds, two camps. The bridge is the world’s habit of splitting: day and night, self and other, for and against — two becomes the number of dilemma itself.
  • Playing cards, dice, dominoes, and time (technical): a two of hearts, a two on the die, two o’clock. The bridge is naming: the pip-count or hour-count hardens into a noun — lay down your twos.
  • Binary and the deuce in sport (modern): base-two digits, tennis deuce from two points tied, the dreaded number two. The bridge carries twoness into systems that need exactly two states: on and off, in and out, win and lose.

Register and connotation: two is the most sociable of numerals — never lonely like one, never crowd-like many. It promises company, symmetry, and manageable size: two lamps, two friends, two pints. In idiom it can divide (two camps) or unite (two hearts beating as one), but it always keeps things human-scale. No other number has spawned so much grammar and so much philosophy from so small a count.
