TWO SEPARATE CHAINS — APOLOGETICALLY is built on the Greek compound apologia (apo- "off, away, back" + logos "speech, account, reason"), borrowed through Latin and French into English. The two elements have independent Indo-European histories. CHAIN 1 — LOGOS ("speech, reason"), the second element of apologia: → PIE *leg- "to collect, gather; to speak" (reconstructed root; the sense "to speak" grows from "to gather words," i.e., to pick out and arrange one's speech; the root also yields Latin legere "to read, gather" and English lexical, lecture) → Greek legein (v.) "to say, speak, tell" (Homeric and classical Greek, 8th–5th c. BCE), with the middle legesthai "to gather, count" preserving the older sense → Greek logos (n.) "word, speech, discourse, account, reason" (classical Greek, 5th–4th c. BCE) → Greek apologia (n.) "a speech in defense; a defense, justification" (apo- "back, off" + logos; classical Greek, 4th c. BCE — Plato's Apology of Socrates is the canonical instance) → Latin apologia (n.) "defense, justification" (borrowed into Latin; Christian Latin from the 2nd century CE, e.g. Justin Martyr's Apologiae) → French apologie (n.) "defense, vindication" (learned, from Latin) → English apology (n.) "a formal defense or vindication" (16th c.), then "an expression of regret or excuse for a fault" (from the late 17th–18th c. — the famous semantic shift; see note) → English apologetic (adj.) "of the nature of an apology; defensive; regretful" (from Greek apologētikos, via Latin apologeticus and French apologétique, late 16th–early 17th c.) → English APOLOGETICALLY (adverb, apologetic + -ly) — the card's target form. CHAIN 2 — APO- ("off, away, back"), the first element of apologia: → PIE *apo- (preposition/adverb) "off, away, back" (reconstructed; the everyday source of English of, off, and after through its Germanic reflexes) → Greek apo (preposition) "away, off, from, back" (classical Greek), used as the prefix in apologia → The prefix traveled inside apologia → Latin apologia → French apologie → English apology / apologetic, where it survives as the initial a- of apologetically. NOTE on the debate: the famous English shift of apology from "formal defense" to "expression of regret" is an English-internal semantic change of the 17th–18th centuries — in Renaissance English an "apology" was a vindication, never a "sorry" — and it is the single most important nuance for reading this word's family correctly. The Greek apologētikos (defensive) and Latin apologeticus are the direct sources of English apologetic; the adverb is transparently the -ly derivative of the adjective. The PIE root *leg- is well established (Greek legein, Latin legere, English lexical), and *apo- is one of the most securely reconstructed of all Indo-European prepositions.
