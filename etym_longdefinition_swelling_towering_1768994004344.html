swelling: Expanding outward and upward as from internal pressure — the present participle of swell, which in the passage describes the cloud as it rises, "swelling and towering swiftly," with genie-like suddenness on the planet's rim. The governing sense of the verb is growth in volume from within, a distension driven by pressure contained under the surface: a wound swells, dough swells, a river swells in flood, a sail swells with wind. Applied to the cloud, the word endows weather with a living, organic physics — matter fattening itself against the sky, gaining bulk and height at supernatural speed, like the smoke of Solomon's released genie. The nuance is not gradual accumulation but abrupt dilation: the cloud does not build up in layers but inflates from inside, as if breathing. In its figurative life swelling has long served pride, rage, sound, and emotion — a swelling heart, swelling anger, swelling music — the same pressure transferred from matter to feeling. Primary and secondary senses of swell. The core verb means "to become greater in size, volume, or quantity, especially by internal pressure." Secondary senses radiate naturally:
  • Medical: to swell is the body's tissue distending through injury or inflammation — a sprained ankle swells (Hindi sūjan, सूजन); the noun swelling is the standard medical term for a localized protuberance or oedematous enlargement.
  • Of water: rivers and tides swell, rising and spreading with added volume; the sea swells into waves; a flood swells over its banks.
  • Of sound: music and noise swell, growing louder and fuller; the crescendo is a swelling of sound.
  • Of emotion: the heart swells with pride, joy, or grief — the old physiology of the breast filling with feeling; swelling pride and swelling rage are the moralized forms of the same pressure.
  • Slang (19th–early 20th c.): "swell" as an adjective meaning "excellent, elegant" (a swell fellow, a swell party) drained the pressure out of the word into pure praise; the sense survives in period style and in "swell" as a mild exclamation.
  • Statistics and economics: numbers, populations, and costs swell — grow in magnitude — a modern, neutral extension of the same increase from within.
towering: Rising to a height that dwarfs everything around it — the present participle of tower, describing the cloud's upward thrust so that it stands above the horizon like a colossal structure. Where swelling describes the growth of bulk, towering describes the assertion of vertical dominance: the cloud rears, overreaches, and looms over the planet's rim. The tone is awestruck and faintly menacing — the cloud is treated not as mere weather but as an architectural presence, a monolith, a fortress, a genie grown vast. The participle is marked by its stillness: to tower is not to fly or soar but to hold height, static and monumental, dwarfing whatever lies below. In figurative use towering has become a standard intensifier of magnitude in a vertical or moral sense:
  • Of natural features and structures: towering cliffs, towering pines, a towering building — height that overtops the world around it.
  • Of passion and quality: a towering rage, towering ambition, towering intellect — emotion or excellence so great it seems to stand above the ordinary scale, converting height into magnitude.
  • Of persons: a towering figure — someone so eminent that others stand in his shadow, the vertical image transferred to rank and reputation.
Comparison of the pair. The two participles choreograph a double motion: outward inflation and upward supremacy — the cloud swelling like a body and towering like a tower. Against their near-synonyms they are exact: expanding is neutral and gradual; distending suggests abnormal, strained stretching from within; inflating implies hollow air, often deceptively; bloating is unpleasant and decay-tinged — only swelling carries organic, liquid energy. Against looming (a threatening, half-seen presence), soaring (graceful upward flight), rising (simple upward motion), and ascending (formal, measured movement), towering is uniquely static and monumental — height already achieved and held, like a genie grown vast above the rim of the planet.
