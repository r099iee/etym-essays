Etymology of English tribulations (plural of tribulation): a borrowing from Latin via Old French.

1. PIE *terh₁- / *tere- 'to rub, turn, bore' [reconstructed] — a root of friction and circular motion.
2. Latin terere (past participle tritus) 'to rub, wear away, thresh' [attested from the earliest Latin] — from *terh₁-; the family includes tritum and the derivatives trite, contrite, detriment.
3. Latin tribulum 'a threshing sledge' [attested in Roman agricultural writers, e.g. Cato and Varro] — a heavy board set with stones or teeth, dragged over grain to rub the husks free; formed on the same root as terere.
4. Latin tribulare 'to press, oppress, afflict' [attested from the 1st c. CE] — literally 'to crush as with a tribulum'.
5. Late Latin / Ecclesiastical Latin tribulatio 'distress, affliction, oppression' [attested in Christian Latin, e.g. the Vulgate] — the abstract noun of the state of being crushed.
6. Old French tribulation 'affliction, distress' [attested].
7. Middle English tribulacioun / tribulation 'affliction, trouble' [attested, 14th c.].
8. Modern English tribulation, plural tribulations [this form].

INSTRUMENT IMAGE
  • tribulum (tool) → tribulare (verb of crushing pressure) → tribulatio (state of being so crushed) → English tribulation(s).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS
  • The exact derivation of tribulum from terere involves an ancient instrumental suffix -bulum; the phonetic detail of the b is sometimes discussed, but the connection with 'rub / thresh' is accepted in the handbooks.
  • English tribulations is the ordinary plural of the mass / count noun; the -s form often stresses a series of afflictions rather than one generalized condition.
