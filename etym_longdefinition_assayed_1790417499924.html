Assayed is the past tense and past participle of the verb assay, meaning to have subjected a substance, person, claim, or enterprise to rigorous chemical, physical, intellectual, or moral testing in order to determine its precise composition, purity, strength, or intrinsic worth. In literary, figurative, and archaic registers, it also means to have attempted, ventured, or undertaken a difficult or perilous endeavor.

Primary Senses, Technical Domains, and Polysemy:

  • Metallurgical and Chemical Assay (Primary Concrete Sense): In metallurgy, chemistry, and mining, to have assayed an ore, alloy, or precious metal sample means to have performed a standardized quantitative or qualitative analysis (such as fire assaying, cupellation, or spectroscopy) to ascertain the exact percentage of noble metals (gold, silver, platinum) or specific chemical constituents present within the specimen.
  • Biomedical and Pharmacological Assay: In biochemistry, biotechnology, and modern clinical pharmacology, an assay is a procedure used to detect or quantify the biological activity, binding affinity, or concentration of a drug, hormone, enzyme, or pathogen (e.g., ELISA, immunoassay, bioassay). To say a pharmaceutical compound was assayed indicates that its therapeutic potency, receptor selectivity, or cytotoxic profile was empirically evaluated under laboratory controls.
  • Metaphorical and Literary Evaluation (Character, Sentiment, Quality): By metaphorical extension, assayed describes assessing the genuine worth, emotional depth, moral fiber, or psychological disposition of a person or expression. As demonstrated in Wodehouse's comedic context—where a letter "assayed a markedly larger percentage of the milk of human kindness"—the word playfully applies metallurgical rigor to quantify human feelings, evaluating their authentic benevolence against counterfeit politeness.
  • Archaic and Literary Sense (Attempted, Endeavored, Ventured): In Middle English, Early Modern English, and classical literature (e.g., Shakespeare, Milton, Spenser), assayed was frequently used in the sense of having attempted, ventured, or dared a task: "He assayed to speak, but grief choked his voice." This meaning stems directly from the shared origin of assay and essay (both originating from Latin exagium, "a weighing").
  • Forensic and Legal Scrutiny: In jurisprudence and rhetorical debate, arguments, witness credibility, and documentary evidence are said to be assayed when subjected to cross-examination, severe dialectical scrutiny, or evidentiary tests of veracity.

Semantic Bridges and Historical Convergence:

The semantic evolution of assayed centers on the unifying concept of "putting something on the scale." The Latin root exagium meant a literal balance-weighing. When one places a metal on a scale to measure its specific density or subjects it to fire, one determines its true worth. From this physical weighing of precious gold, the concept naturally branched into two parallel paths: (1) evaluating intrinsic quality through test and analysis (metallurgy, science, literary critique), and (2) weighing one's strength against an obstacle, hence making an attempt or trial (the literary/archaic sense of attempting a feat).
