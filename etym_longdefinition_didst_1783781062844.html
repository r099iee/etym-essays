DIDST (verb, archaic). The second-person singular past tense of the verb DO, used with the pronoun thou: "thou didst," equivalent to modern "you did." It is the past-tense counterpart of dost ("thou dost") and part of the full archaic thou-conjugation of do, which also includes the present dost/doest, the third-person doth/doeth, and the infinitive and participle forms. Because DIDST is an inflected form of one of the most polysemous verbs in English, its definition must register both its own grammatical meaning and the entire semantic range of the verb it conjugates. Grammatical function and uses:
  • Lexical main verb ("to perform, carry out, act"). "Thou didst write a beautiful poem" — the ordinary transitive and intransitive sense of do (perform an action, make, produce, work at) in the thou-past form.
  • Periphrastic / auxiliary past (early modern and biblical). "Thou didst inspire That Shepherd" (Milton, Paradise Lost I.8) — didst + bare infinitive forms the emphatic and verse-filling past construction typical of early modern poetry and scripture, where the plain past would be too short or too flat for the line.
  • Emphatic affirmation. "Thou didst indeed go" — the stress-carrying do-past that insists on the reality of the action, exactly as modern "you DID go" insists.
  • Pro-verb (substitute verb). "Thou didst as I bade thee" — didst standing for the whole predicate, the verb that avoids repeating the main verb, as modern do still does.
  • Archaic register marker. In modern English didst has no grammatical function at all; its entire meaning is stylistic — it instantly signals biblical, liturgical, Shakespearean, or Miltonic diction, the intimate and reverent address of thou.
The verb DO it inflects is itself radically polysemous, and every sense is available to didst:
  • Action and performance: to act, perform, execute, carry out, accomplish ("thou didst the work").
  • Creation and production: to make, produce, create, cause to exist ("thou didst a great work").
  • Cause and effect: to cause, effect, bring about ("this didst thou do unto me").
  • State and condition ("how do you do") and the whole auxiliary system of modern English ("did you go?"), all of which converge on the did-forms.
  • Phrasal and idiomatic do: the range of do in collocations (do good, do harm, do right, do one's duty, do away with, do for, do without, do up, do over) — in the thou-form "thou didst well," "thou didst harm."
  • Euphemistic "do" in archaic idiom ("thou didst as thou wert told"), the general "behave, fare, get on."
Historical bridge: didst descends from Old English dydest, the second-person singular past of dōn, built on the reduplicated past stem dyde ("did") plus the second-person ending -est, which by Middle English contracted to didst. Its grammatical meaning (past, second person singular, addressed to thou) is unchanged; its entire semantic breadth is that of the verb do. Meaning today: archaic, confined to scripture, liturgy, prayer, and archaizing poetry, where it functions as a solemn, reverent, intimate form of address.
