Tidings is the plural form of the archaic noun tiding (“a piece of news, a report”), and in practice it is the only form in living use: English speakers say “tidings” and almost never “a tiding”. The word denotes news, intelligence or reports concerning events, especially events of importance or of personal concern — the arrival of an army, the death of a king, the outcome of a battle, the coming of a child. In the source context (“Far over Mirkwood tidings spread: ‘Smaug is dead!’”) it carries its full traditional weight: momentous news travelling outward across a land, passed from mouth to mouth.

The semantic core of the word is reported event, and from that core several uses radiate:

  • Neutral news sense: tidings are simply reports — “tidings of war”, “no tidings of the missing men”, “the messenger brought tidings”. Here the word is interchangeable in meaning with “news” but differs in register: it is literary, archaic or solemn, and it is nearly always plural in construction (“the tidings were good”), though older writers also treated it as singular (“the tidings was...”).
  • Good and evil tidings: the word is strongly associated with evaluative adjectives — glad, good, joyful, heavy, evil, ill. “Glad tidings” and “good tidings” are the canonical collocations, and they have a specifically religious colouring: in Luke 2:10 the angel tells the shepherds “I bring you good tidings of great joy” (King James Version, 1611), and the phrase has echoed through Christmas liturgy, carols and sermons ever since. The traditional carol “God Rest Ye Merry, Gentlemen” sings of “tidings of comfort and joy”.
  • Literary and fantasy register: precisely because the word has receded from everyday speech, it has become a marker of elevated, archaic or genre prose — the language of chronicles, epics, fantasy and historical fiction. Tolkien's use in The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings (“tidings spread”, “evil tidings”) is a deliberate archaism that lends gravity to his narratorial voice.
  • Rare singular and derived uses: the singular tiding survives only in antiquarian or jocular use; the verb to tiding (to bring tidings) is obsolete. The word is never used of trivial chatter: it implies information worth travelling for.

The semantic bridge that built the word is visible in its history: Old English tīd meant “time” (as in “eventide”, “tide” in its old sense of “season, hour”). From tīd came the verb tīdian, “to happen, to occur” — literally “to come to pass at a time” — and from that verb the noun tīdung, “that which has happened, an occurrence, a report of an occurrence”. So tidings are, etymologically, “happenings”: the word names news precisely because news is a report of what has befallen, of what the tide of time has brought in. The plural form reflects the older habit of treating reports as a collection of events rather than a single item.

Grammatically, tidings behaves like “news” (plural in form) but, unlike modern “news” (which takes a singular verb), it is flexible: “the tidings were spread” and, in older usage, “the tidings was spread” both occur. In modern prose it appears almost exclusively in set phrases — “good tidings”, “glad tidings”, “tidings of comfort and joy”, “to bring tidings” — or in deliberately archaic narrative. Its register is the key to its use: choose it when you want the dignity of old chronicles; choose “news” for ordinary modern communication.
