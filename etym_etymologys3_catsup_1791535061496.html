I. The Maritime Asian Origins: Hokkien Fish Brine to Malay Trade Ports

The genealogical lineage of the word catsup represents one of the most remarkable linguistic and culinary journeys in global history, tracing back to the maritime trading networks of early modern East and Southeast Asia. The ultimate ancestor of the term is found in the Southern Min (Hokkien or Amoy) dialect of Chinese, where the compound 鮭汁 (pronounced in Hokkien as kê-tsiap or kôe-chiap) literally signified 'fermented fish sauce' or 'fish brine'. In this compound, 鮭 (kê) originally referred to a preserved, salted, or fermented fish or marine animal, while 汁 (tsiap) denoted juice, gravy, sap, or brine. This pungent, amber-colored liquor was widely produced along the coastal provinces of Fujian and Guangdong and throughout maritime Southeast Asia, where Chinese merchants traded extensively.

During the seventeenth century, Chinese seafaring traders brought this salted condiment to the bustling trading ports of the Malay Archipelago, including Malacca, Bantam, and the Riau Islands. In the Malay language, the foreign loanword was naturalized phonetically as kicap (modern Indonesian kecap), broadening semantically to designate any fermented, seasoned culinary sauce, particularly soy-based and fish-based sauces. It was in these Southeast Asian emporia that English and Dutch maritime merchants of the East India companies first encountered the savory condiment. Sailors prized the dark liquor for its extraordinary ability to withstand long tropical voyages without spoiling, using it to impart deep umami flavor to otherwise rancid shipboard rations of salt pork, hardtack, and dried peas.

II. English Transcriptions and the Georgian Kitchen Transformation

When British sailors brought samples of this Southeast Asian condiment back to the British Isles in the late seventeenth century, English scribes and merchants struggled to transcribe the unfamiliar Malay and Chinese phonology. The earliest known English attestation appeared in 1690 in B. E.'s A New Dictionary of the Canting Crew, where it was recorded as catchup and defined as 'a high East-India Sauce'. Shortly thereafter, in 1696, the travel writer John Ovington published A Voyage to Suratt, noting that sailors returned with 'ketchup, a kind of sauce, which they bring from China'.

The specific variant spelling catsup emerged in the early eighteenth century, likely arising through folk-etymological attraction to familiar English phonetic patterns or through alternative dialectal transcriptions. One of the earliest prominent literary appearances of the spelling occurred in 1730 in Jonathan Swift's satirical poem A Panegyric on the Dean, wherein he wrote:

  • 'And, for our home-bred British cheer, / Botargo, catsup, and caveer.'

Because true Asian fish sauce was prohibitively expensive to import in large quantities, eighteenth-century British housewives and domestic cookbook authors sought to synthesize native equivalents. Cookbook authors such as Hannah Glasse in The Art of Cookery Made Plain and Easy (1747) and later Isabella Beeton published elaborate household recipes for domestic catsups. However, these British recipes contained no tomatoes; instead, they relied upon native fungi, nuts, and shellfish that naturally possessed high concentrations of free glutamates:

  • Mushroom Catsup: Freshly gathered field mushrooms were layered with coarse salt in earthenware crocks, allowed to sit for days until their cell walls collapsed into a dark, viscous liquid, then strained and simmered with allspice, cloves, nutmeg, and black pepper. The resulting thin, savory extract was bottled and stored for years.
  • Walnut Catsup: Prepared by crushing tender green, unripe English walnuts before their shells hardened, soaking them in seasoned vinegar or brine, and boiling down the spiced liquor.
  • Oyster and Anchovy Catsup: Boiled reductions of shellfish liquor, white wine, shallots, and pounded anchovies, providing a briny, savory foundation for roasted poultry and fish.

III. The American Tomato Revolution and Commercial Standardization

The decisive semantic and physical transformation of catsup occurred in North America during the early nineteenth century. Although the tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) was native to western South America and Central America, it was long regarded with intense suspicion in Britain and the northern American colonies due to its botanical affiliation with the toxic nightshade family (Solanaceae). By the turn of the nineteenth century, horticulturalists and botanists began dispelling these fears, highlighting the fruit's culinary virtues and purported medicinal benefits.

The earliest documented recipe for tomato catsup was published in Philadelphia in 1812 by the esteemed physician and horticulturist Dr. James Mease. Mease referred to tomatoes as 'love apples' and praised tomato catsup as a superb, shelf-stable seasoning that imparted a vibrant, rich savor to gravies. Early American tomato catsups were relatively thin and heavily spiced, frequently seasoned with brandy, nutmeg, mace, and cloves, and fermented naturally. However, early commercial bottlers faced a catastrophic challenge: tomatoes ferment rapidly and spoil easily once bottled, frequently causing glass containers to explode on grocery shelves. To combat this, unscrupulous nineteenth-century processors added dangerous chemical preservatives such as sodium benzoate, coal-tar food dyes, and boric acid, while utilizing rotting tomato scraps.

The pivotal turning point arrived in the late 1870s through the pioneering efforts of industrialist Henry John Heinz and the crusading food chemist Dr. Harvey Washington Wiley, which culminated in the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906. Heinz revolutionized industrial condiment manufacturing by developing a recipe that used ripe, whole tomatoes packed with natural pectin, substantially increasing the concentration of distilled vinegar and sugar. The elevated acidity of the vinegar and the natural preservative qualities of sugar and salt allowed tomato condiment to be bottled safely without artificial chemical preservatives. This high-pectin, high-vinegar formulation created the thick, sweet-and-tangy, viscous sauce recognized universally today.

IV. Lexical Divergence: Catsup versus Ketchup in Modern Culture

Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the rival spellings catsup and ketchup competed fiercely in the English lexicon, particularly in the United States. While Henry J. Heinz deliberately adopted and trademarked the spelling 'Ketchup' in 1876 to distinguish his premium product from the flood of inferior 'catsups' flooding the market, many of his largest competitors championed catsup. For nearly a century, the Del Monte Corporation, Hunt's, and numerous regional Midwestern and Southern canning enterprises proudly labeled their glass bottles 'Tomato Catsup'. In 1949, when the United States Food and Drug Administration established official Standards of Identity for the food supply, it acknowledged the complete equivalence of the names under 21 CFR § 155.194, codifying catsup, ketchup, and catchup as identical legal articles.

By the late twentieth century, commercial consolidation and nationwide television advertising swung consumer preference decisively in favor of 'ketchup'. Recognizing Heinz's overwhelming market dominance, Del Monte officially phased out the spelling 'Tomato Catsup' in 1988 in favor of 'Tomato Ketchup'. Today, while catsup remains fully understood throughout the English-speaking world and persists in certain regional pockets, vintage packaging, and classic literature, it is widely viewed as a quaint, retro, or regional spelling of a beloved global condiment whose linguistic roots span three continents and four centuries of culinary evolution.
