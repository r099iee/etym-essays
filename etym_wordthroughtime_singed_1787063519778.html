- Old English sengan (attested c. 9th–10th c.): to scorch or burn the surface — already with the practical sense of burning the bristles or feathers off a carcass.
- The traditional explanation: the verb is a causative of “sing” — the burning hair “sings” (hisses) as it burns; the idea is the noise of scorching.
- Middle English sengen (14th c.): continued literal sense; also used of trimming and scorching cloth and candle-wicks.
- 16th–17th c.: Icarus's “singed wings” becomes a literary figure for ruined ambition; the word enters figurative use for minor, painful scorchings of pride or reputation.
- 18th–19th c.: settled practical vocabulary of cookery (singeing fowl), tailoring, and accident reports.
- Modern: the mild-end burn word — singed eyebrows, singed hair, singed reputations — always damage short of destruction; the Tolkien passage (1937) uses it with exact, homely physicality.
