Sighed is the past tense and past participle of the verb sigh, and to understand the form one must understand the verb from which it comes. To sigh is to draw in and release a long, deep, audible breath, most commonly as an involuntary expression of weariness, sorrow, longing, relief, or resignation. The word also names the sound so produced — the soft, whispering outflow of air itself — and by extension any gentle, breath-like noise: the sighing of the wind through leaves, the sighing of a fire settling, the sighing of distant seas. The distinctive feature of a sigh is that it is carried on the breath rather than on the voice; it is the sound of exhalation, and precisely for that reason it is the most intimate and least theatrical of the expressions of emotion, a sound one makes to oneself. Because sighed is a past form, it reports the completed act: somebody has drawn that long breath, has let it out, and the emotion carried with it has been, however briefly, released.

Polysemy: the full semantic range. The word operates across a spectrum of related senses that must be enumerated separately if the definition is to be exhaustive:

  • Literal, human sense: to emit a long, audible exhalation as a response to grief, weariness, relief, longing, regret, or resignation — "he sighed heavily", "she sighed with relief".
  • Transferred, natural-world sense: of the wind, of trees, of leaves, of waves, of a settling fire — to make a long, soft, mournful or rustling sound as if breathing out; the classic poetic anthropomorphism by which the landscape is granted breath and emotion.
  • Sense of yearning (intransitive, with for or after): to long for something with the breath of desire — "to sigh for lost youth", "sighing after the unattainable"; here the sigh is not a symptom but the expression of desire itself.
  • Nominal sense: the noun sigh — a single act of sighing, or the sound so made — "he heaved a sigh", "a sigh of relief", "the last sigh".
  • Sense of settling: a sigh can register the resolution of suspense — relief after anxiety, resignation after defeat — so that one physical act carries a whole emotional spectrum from pain to pleasure.

The sense in the context. In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the word is used in its transferred sense, but Tolkien chooses it precisely because the transfer is still felt: "Wind got up, and willows along its banks bent and sighed." The willows do not merely rustle; they sigh, and in that verb the natural world performs an act of weary emotion that mirrors the company's own exhaustion. The storm has broken at last, the grey clouds are being torn apart, and the wandering moon appears above the hills; but the willows, still bending and sighing under the gusty wind, keep the scene pitched in melancholy. The sigh is the sound of the wind's breath moving through supple wood, and Tolkien, by giving it to the trees, binds weather, landscape, and mood into a single exhalation — the whole world seems to breathe out its long-held wet weariness. The verb is intransitive; nothing is sighed, the trees themselves sigh, and that intransitivity lets the natural world act alone.

Grammar and register. Sigh is a common, neutral word of everyday speech, equally at home in prose and verse. Sighed is its regular weak preterite and past participle, formed by adding the past ending -ed to the modern verb — though in older English the preterite had a different, strong shape (see the etymology field for the story). Related formations include the noun sigh, the participial adjective sighing ("the sighing of the wind"), and idioms such as heave a sigh and breathe a sigh of relief. Collocations cluster around the verbs of breath and release: sigh deeply, sigh heavily, sigh with relief, sigh in resignation, sigh over lost chances, a sigh of content.

Distinguish the near-synonyms.

  • Groaned: a deeper, harsher sound of protest or pain, made with the voice; a sigh is breath alone.
  • Moaned: a low, drawn-out lament, more vocalised and prolonged than a sigh.
  • Sobbed: convulsive weeping broken by short breaths, far more violent than a quiet sigh.
  • Rustled: a soft dry sound of wind in leaves, making no emotional claim; to say trees "sighed" grants them weariness.
