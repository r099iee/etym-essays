Milch is an archaic and specialized agricultural adjective designating female domestic animals—most notably cattle, but also goats, sheep, camels, or asses—that are actively lactating or kept specifically for the commercial and domestic production of milk (as in a milch cow). Derived from the ancient Proto-Indo-European verb for stroking or milking, milch functions in modern English alongside dairy, though it preserves a distinctive rural, historical, and economic resonance. Figuratively, the compound milch cow has served for centuries as a metaphor for an enterprise, institution, customer, or individual that is systematically and effortlessly exploited as a continuous source of money, revenue, or favors (the direct conceptual predecessor of the modern corporate idiom cash cow).

In the source text—Oscar Wilde’s allegorical fairy tale The Devoted Friend—the word establishes the grotesque economic disparity between the wealthy, sanctimonious Miller and his impoverished victim: “Sometimes, indeed, the neighbours thought it strange that the rich Miller never gave little Hans anything in return, though he had a hundred sacks of flour stored away in his mill, and six milch cows, and a large flock of woolly sheep; but Hans never troubled his head about these things...” In the rural economy of nineteenth-century Europe, owning six healthy milch cows represented substantial agrarian wealth and ongoing liquidity, providing daily yields of fresh milk, cream, butter, and cheese. That the Miller refuses to share even a single bowl of warm milk or a scrap of bread with starving Hans during the freezing winter exposes the Miller’s flowery speeches on friendship as monstrous hypocrisy.

The term encompasses broad agricultural, figurative, dramatic, and culinary applications:

  • Agricultural Animal Husbandry and Dairy Terminology: In traditional livestock breeding and agrarian records, a milch cow (or milch beast) was explicitly distinguished from a dry cow (a non-lactating cow), a beef cow (raised solely for meat), or a draught ox (used for plowing). Milch animals required specialized feed, shelter, and daily care, representing prime capital investments that sustained rural families throughout the lactation cycle.
  • The Economic and Political Metaphor ("Milch Cow"): Since the early seventeenth century, the term has been employed figuratively in political and financial discourse. A monarch who heavily taxed the merchant class, a corrupt official who embezzled public revenue, or a corporation that squeezed profits from a captive consumer base was denounced for treating that entity as a milch cow. In twentieth-century corporate finance, this exact idiom was modernized by the Boston Consulting Group as the famous cash cow in the growth-share matrix.
  • Archaic Literary and Dramatic Usage (Tears and Compassion): In Elizabethan and Jacobean drama, milch was occasionally employed by poetic extension to describe eyes weeping profusely with compassion, as though crying tears of milky moisture. In Shakespeare's Hamlet (Act II, Scene 2), the First Player recites a speech describing the slaughter of King Priam and the grief of Queen Hecuba, declaring that her agony "Would have made milch the burning eyes of heaven, / And passion in the gods." Here, *milch* evokes a sky weeping moisture out of profound divine pity.
  • Veterinary Medicine and Dairy Pathology: In veterinary history, milch appeared in compound terms describing dairy ailments, most famously milch fever (more commonly *milk fever*, or bovine parturient hypocalcemia), an acute, life-threatening metabolic disorder occurring in high-producing dairy cows shortly after calving due to a rapid drop in blood calcium levels.
  • Culinary and Religious Parallels (Yiddish Milchig): The Germanic root of milch is directly cognate with Yiddish milchig (or milchige), the foundational term in Jewish kosher dietary laws designating dairy foods and dairy utensils, which must be kept strictly segregated from meat products (fleishig).
  • Semantic Bridge: The bridge connecting the physical act of squeezing milk from a cow to the modern financial exploitation of a "cash cow" lies in the biological constancy of dairy lactation. Unlike crops that are harvested only once a year, a healthy milch cow provides valuable, nourishing food every single day with minimal additional processing. Thus, to treat someone as a "milch cow" is to draw perpetual, effortless wealth from their unceasing generosity or labor.
