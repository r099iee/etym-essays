- Late Latin (4th–6th c.) — Coined as segregātiō from segregāre ("to separate from the flock"), used in Christian theological and monastic treatises to denote spiritual isolation from worldly corruption or the division of heretics from the faithful.
- 16th–17th c. — Entered Early Modern English through scholastic and theological works, referring broadly to the separation of bodies, chemical division of elements, or physical quarantine during epidemic plagues.
- Mid-19th c. — Adopted into natural sciences, describing the physical separation of mineral veins in geology, crystalline alloys in metallurgy, and subsequently codified in genetics through Gregor Mendel's 1866 paper establishing the Law of Segregation.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c. — Acquired its intense sociopolitical meaning in the American South following the collapse of Reconstruction, codifying the legal apparatus of racial "separate but equal" under Jim Crow statutes and the 1896 Plessy v. Ferguson ruling.
- Mid-20th c. to Present — Globally repudiated and legally dismantled through the American Civil Rights Movement and the anti-apartheid struggle; expanded in contemporary sociology to analyze de facto urban residential segregation, economic redlining, and digital filter bubbles.
