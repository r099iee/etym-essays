The uncertain PIE foundation and the West Germanic verb: The deepest recoverable ancestor of strode is a West Germanic strong verb reconstructed as *strīdaną, 'to step widely, to stride', with the secondary meaning 'to strive, contend'. Its Indo-European origin is not securely established: philologists most often connect the root with the PIE base *ster- 'to spread, stretch, extend' — the same family that yields English strew, street (a spread-out paved way), and ultimately straddle — or with an extended 'press, stretch' base, but no attested PIE intermediate survives, and some authorities treat the Germanic verb as an expressive, possibly imitative formation. What is certain is the Germanic picture: the root produced Old Saxon strīdan 'to strive, contend' and Old High German strītan 'to contend, fight, argue', the ancestor of modern German streiten 'to quarrel, fight'. This is a striking prehistoric split: on the Continent the root took on the meaning of striving, contending, and fighting; in English it became the word for confident long-step walking. The two branches drifted apart before the English verb is even recorded, and the Continental 'strive/fight' sense has left almost no trace in English.

Old English — strīdan and the astride sense: English inherits the verb as Old English strīdan, attested around the year 1000 in Ælfric's prose and in Old English glosses. The Old English sense is broader than the modern one: it means both 'to walk with long steps' and 'to straddle, to stand or move with legs apart'. The verb was a class-I strong verb, and its past was strād — the direct ancestor of modern strode. The 'straddle' strand of meaning is important because it shows that the original image was of spreading the legs wide: from 'to step widely' it was a natural step to 'to stand astride', and this astride sense survived into early modern English in stride and, especially, in the prefixed bestride, the verb of sitting astride a horse or standing astride something so as to dominate it.

Middle English and the rise of the modern gait sense: In Middle English the verb appears as striden, attested from the early 13th century (in texts such as the Ancrene Wisse), with the same two senses — long-step walking and straddling. Over the later Middle English and early modern periods the straddle sense retreated, surviving chiefly in the derived verbs straddle (a nasalized frequentative of the same stem, first recorded in the 16th century) and bestride, while plain stride concentrated on the confident long-step gait. The past tense remained strong: Middle English strod from Old English strād, settling in the modern period as strode. The vowel history follows the Great Vowel Shift: Old English strīdan (/striː.dan/) became Middle English strīden and then modern stride (/straɪd/) as the long /iː/ shifted to /aɪ/ in the 15th–17th centuries, while the past strād (/strɑːd/) became strode (/strəʊd/), a development that left the modern past with a quite different vowel from the infinitive — the familiar, fossilized irregularity stride / strode / stridden.

Early modern literature — the Colossus: The confident-gait sense of stride and its astride relative bestride found their most famous expression in Shakespeare's Julius Caesar (c. 1599), where Cassius says of Caesar: "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus". The image fuses both senses of the family: Caesar stands astride the world, dominating it with spread legs — a perfect emblem of power, and exactly the body-language that modern strode still carries in miniature whenever a figure "strides" into a room. The play confirms that in Shakespeare's English both the astride sense and the long-step sense were alive and could be played against each other.

Modern idioms — from gait to achievement: In modern English the physical image of the stride has been thoroughly transferred to the world of achievement and progress. The idioms to make (great) strides, to hit one's stride, to get into one's stride, and to take something in one's stride all take the long, confident, regular step of a person at their most efficient and recast it as competence and forward momentum in work or life. The verb is used figuratively in the same way ("the company has stridden ahead of its rivals"), and the noun has specialized technical senses in computing (the memory stride between array elements) and in horse-riding (the length of a canter or gallop beat). None of these departures breaks with the core: they all extend the image of a regular, confident, covering step.

The literary contrast — strode against scuttled: In J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the word is used with precise physical sympathy. Gandalf "strode away" from Bag End just as Bilbo, finishing his second cake, congratulates himself on escaping adventures; and later, when the adventure is offered again, "the hobbit turned and scuttled inside his round green door". The pairing is deliberate: strode and scuttled are opposite poles of the English gait-vocabulary — long, confident, adult steps against short, hasty, faintly comic ones — and the novel sets wizard and hobbit apart by nothing more than the way they walk. That the strong past strode carries this authority so cleanly is a tribute to a verb that has meant decisive walking in English for over a thousand years.

  • PIE (reconstructed, uncertain): probable link to *ster- 'spread, stretch'; no secure etymon.
  • Proto-Germanic: *strīdaną 'to stride, step widely; to strive' (Continental branch → Old Saxon strīdan, Old High German strītan 'strive/fight').
  • Old English (c. 1000): strīdan 'stride, straddle'; class-I strong past strād.
  • Middle English (13th c.): striden; straddle sense retreats.
  • Great Vowel Shift: strīden → stride (/straɪd/); strād → strode (/strəʊd/).
  • Early modern (c. 1599): Shakespeare's "bestride the narrow world".
  • Modern: idioms of progress (make strides, hit one's stride); technical senses (computing, equestrian).
