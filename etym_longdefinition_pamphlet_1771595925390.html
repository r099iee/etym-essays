pamphlet: a small, unbound printed work — a few pages in a paper cover, stitched or stapled — devoted to a single subject, most often an argument, a polemic, or a piece of topical information or advocacy; a short treatise or essay in booklet form. In the literary context the word marks a striking modesty of intention. Ruskin says that what became Modern Painters — one of the monuments of nineteenth-century art criticism, eventually running to five volumes over seventeen years — was originally conceived as "a short pamphlet, addressing the specific grievances against the contemporary art critics." A pamphlet in Ruskin's day was the standard weapon of controversy: cheap to print, quick to circulate, meant to strike while the argument was hot and to reach readers who would never buy a book. The word therefore signals immediacy, pugnacity, and lowliness of station: Ruskin began as a pamphleteer, an angry voice in a pamphlet war, and the sentence quietly contrasts the humble intended form with the vast treatise it unexpectedly became. The tone is self-aware and slightly rueful — the polemicist's shrug at the monster his squib turned into. The word is richer in senses than its modest size suggests.
  • Sense 1 — primary, modern: a small unbound or lightly bound printed booklet. A work of a few pages, usually in a paper cover, devoted to a single topic, most often argumentative or persuasive rather than neutral. This is the standard sense since the sixteenth century and the sense in which Ruskin uses the word.
  • Sense 2 — historical: the cheap medium of controversy. From the Reformation through the eighteenth century the pamphlet was the engine of religious, political, and literary warfare — the vehicle of the pamphlet war, in which rival controversialists exchanged printed attacks. It was the poor man's forum and the dissenter's pulpit.
  • Sense 3 — bibliographic and technical. In book-trade and library usage, a pamphlet is a work of a small number of pages (commonly defined as under 48 or 80 pages) issued without a hard binding; the term marks the boundary between a leaflet, a pamphlet, and a book.
  • Sense 4 — metonymic extension. By extension the word names anything brief and pointed on a single subject: a short speech, a compact argument, a small gathering of poems, a set of papers on one question.
  • Sense 5 — derived and figurative. A pamphleteer is a controversialist who writes such works; to pamphlet, though rare, is to attack or defend a cause in print. The word carries an inbuilt connotation of attack and partisanship — the pamphlet is the classic weapon of the opposition.
  • Sense 6 — legal and political history. Because pamphlets were the cheapest means of reaching the public, the licensing, censorship, and suppression of pamphlets was a recurring political issue from the Tudor licensing laws through the debates on press freedom; the word is thus inseparable from the history of the free press.
The semantic bridge is metonymy working twice over. The word began as a proper name — Pamphilus, the hero of a medieval schoolroom poem — and slid from the title of the poem to the cheap little unbound copy in which the poem circulated, and then from that particular object to the whole class of small unbound treatises. A name became a genre. The second bridge is social: because unbound booklets were cheap, they were the medium of ordinary controversialists, so the word acquired its democratic, pugnacious colouring and kept it for five centuries. In usage the word is collocated with a small constellation of companions: a short pamphlet, a political pamphlet, a religious pamphlet, a pamphlet war, a pamphleteer, to publish a pamphlet, to circulate a pamphlet. It is distinctively the printed object of a single burning cause — "one argument in a handful of pages" — and it remains the honest word for the little book that means to change a reader's mind quickly and cheaply. Modern substitutes such as booklet, brochure, and leaflet are blunter instruments that drop the polemical charge; pamphlet alone carries the whiff of the street-corner controversialist and the printed battle.
