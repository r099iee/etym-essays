The compound overall is built from two root words, over and all, each with an independent derivation chain; the two chains are given in full below, followed by the fusion that produced the modern word.

CHAIN 1 — the component “over”:
Stage 1 — PIE *upér / *upó, “up, over, above” (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE).
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *uber, “over, above” (c. 500 BCE–200 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English ofer, “over, above, across, beyond” (attested from c. 7th century CE).
Stage 4 — Middle English over / ouer, “over, above, across” (12th–15th century).
Stage 5 — Modern English over → compound with all: overall.

CHAIN 2 — the component “all”:
Stage 1 — PIE *al-, “beyond, other” (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE): the root of “beyondness” whose completion sense yields totality.
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *allaz, “whole, entire, all” (c. 500 BCE–200 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English eall, “all, every, whole, entire” (attested from c. 8th century CE).
Stage 4 — Middle English al / all (12th–15th century).
Stage 5 — Modern English all → compound with over: overall.

FUSION — the compound:
Stage 1 — Old English phrase ofer eall, “over all, across the whole extent” (attested before the Norman Conquest).
Stage 2 — Middle English overal, adverb “everywhere, altogether, over the whole extent” (14th century).
Stage 3 — adjective “total, comprehensive” (14th–16th centuries).
Stage 4 — American noun over-alls, bib-and-brace protective trousers (1759).
Stage 5 — British singular noun overall, the one-piece protective working suit (20th century).

Note on debate/nuance: The reconstruction of the PIE base (*upér vs *upó) is debated in detail but the vertical adverbial family is secure. The noun sense is an 18th-century English-internal reanalysis of the adjective/adverb — the garment worn “over all” the other clothes — not a direct continuation of the phrase's original meaning. Sanskrit sarvá, “all”, belongs to the unrelated root *solh₂-wó-, so the Indo-Aryan branch does not contribute to English all, whose cognates are confined to Germanic (with the “other” senses of *al- surviving in Latin alius and Greek állos).
