Grunts belongs to language’s basement: the echoic words that imitate the body rather than describe it. Its history cannot be told as a neat chain of sound-laws, because grunting is what humans and pigs have always done, and every language independently reinvents a gr- plus nasal formula for it. Latin had grunnire (“to grunt”), Greek had gryzein, Sanskrit ghurghura; none is the ancestor of the English word, all are cousins by imitation. Reconstructed imitative PIE *gru- or *ghreu- is therefore a convenience label for “the noise throats make,” not a normal etymon with regular daughters. Within Germanic, however, the lineage is documentary: Proto-Germanic *grunnitjaną / *grunnōną “to grunt” (cf. Old High German grunnizōn, Old Norse grynja “to howl”) yields Old English grunnettan (attested in glosses for the grunting of swine) and grunian (“to mutter”).

Middle English grunten widens the subject from pig to person without changing the sound:

  • 1300s barnyard and sickbed: Chaucer’s contemporaries use grunten of swine and of groaning men; the Reeve’s horse “grunteth,” the sick “grunt” in pain. No metaphor yet — just shared physiology.
  • 1500s–1600s contempt: with humanist eloquence as the ideal, to say a man “grunts” is to call him beast or rustic. Shakespeare’s Hamlet sneers at those who “grunt and sweat under a weary life” (the verb here hovers between groan and complain); pamphleteers dismiss Catholic or Puritan opponents as grunting herds.
  • 1800s evolutionary romance: Darwin (Descent of Man) and his popularizers make grunting the ancestor of speech — the “grunt-theory” (bow-wow vs pooh-pooh debates) holds that interjections and cries became words. Nursery tales and Kipling cement the cartoon: cavemen converse in grunts.
  • 1900s–2000s sociolinguistic rehabilitation: interactional linguists show that modern uh-huh, mm, huh — textbook grunts — do exquisite relational work: aligning, conceding, flirting, refusing. Vietnam slang’s grunt for the foot-soldier (c. 1965, from the sound of hauling packs, or acronymic folk-etymology “Ground Replacement Unit”) spreads the noun to persons, and gym culture normalizes the effort-grunt as honest signal.

Phonetically the word has barely moved in a millennium: initial /ɡr/, stressed /ʌ/, nasal + stop coda /nt/, plural adding /s/ to make the famously clustered /ɡrʌnts/. That stability is typical of onomatopoeia, which resists the sound-changes that reshape arbitrary signs because any drift would break the imitation. The source sentence’s “simple gestures and monosyllabic grunts” leans on the whole Victorian cartoon only to reject it: real talk, the author insists, is never mere grunting, because even grunts — as modern pragmatics shows — already carry attitude, and full speech carries the whole dancing negotiation of selves.

Clinical and acoustic detail confirms the continuity. Spectrograms of pig grunts, human effort-grunts, and conversational uh-huh show the same low first formant and abrupt onset: short duration (100–300 ms), fundamental frequency in the lower speaking range, and heavy nasal coupling. Infants grunt before they babble; stroke patients who lose syntax often retain apt grunting; weightlifters’ exhaled grunt measurably increases force output by bracing the trunk. Language, in other words, never left grunting behind but built upon it — prosody, timing, and breath remain the carriers of stance long after words arrive, which is why even the most articulate among us still answer a surprising bill with an eloquent huh.
