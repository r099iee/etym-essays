The word skirted begins, surprisingly, in the wardrobe. Its ancestor is not a verb of walking but a noun of clothing: Old Norse skyrta, a shirt-like tunic or kirtle, borrowed into Middle English around 1300 as skirt for the lower hanging part of a coat or dress. The garment sense is itself built on an old Germanic adjective for shortness, since tunics were cut short against cloaks and gowns, and the same cut-short root gives English both shirt, the native southern cousin, and short itself. To wear a skirt was, etymologically, to wear the short thing.

From clothing the noun spread rapidly to landscape, because hems make irresistible metaphors. By the 15th century writers speak of the skirts of a wood, a town, or a cloud, meaning its ragged trailing edges where the mass thins and flares exactly like cloth. The compound outskirts fixes this sense permanently: the fringes where the town dissolves into fields are the garment's hem where the weave loosens. Once an area could wear a skirt of trees, verbs were only a matter of time.

The verb skirt appears in the late 16th century in two linked uses: to furnish with a border and to move along a border. Tailors skirted cloaks with lace, coasts were skirted with rocks, and travellers skirted forests by keeping to their margins. Shakespeare's age loved verbs made from nouns of dress in this way, and the new verb carried a distinctly visual grace: it did not merely bound or limit, it draped. Phonetics stayed placid throughout, the sharp sk- onset surviving from Old Norse untouched while southern English softened nothing around it.

The 17th and 18th centuries turn the picturesque verb into a practical one. Military memoirs skirt redoubts, tourists skirt lakes, and road-books skirt bogs, each use converting the static hem into a chosen route. The traveller's logic is always the same: the centre is wet, hostile, or toll-gated, while the edge offers passage with a view. Cobbett, riding the chalk downs and clay vales of southern England, uses the verb exactly so, and his vales skirted by woodlands and cornfields show the static and the moving senses fused, since the woods that rim the vale are also the line along which the eye travels.

Semantic and social landmarks:

  • Medieval wardrobe: skyrta and early skirt clothe bodies in tunics and kirtles, the everyday wear of Norse settlers and their English neighbours.
  • Tudor tailoring: skirts of gowns grow wide and verbally active, and to skirt a garment means to trim or furnish its edge with fur or lace.
  • Elizabethan landscape: surveyors and poets extend the hem to coasts, woods, and storms, and the verb of borders enters literary prose.
  • Georgian travel: coaches, armies, and picturesque tourists skirt towns, marshes, and battlefields, making the word the standard term for the judicious detour.
  • Victorian evasion: debaters and novelists begin to skirt questions, scandals, and laws, and the honest hem-word learns to be sly.
  • Modern statistics: economists and journalists let figures skirt thresholds and targets, the cliff-edge metaphor now applied to pure number.

Literary attestations trace this arc cleanly. Early modern drama uses skirt for trims and borders; 18th-century travelogues use it for coasts fringed with foam; Romantic poets use it for clouds skirted with gold; and 19th-century parliamentary reporting gives us the first speakers accused of skirting awkward questions. Each era keeps the older senses alive beneath the new one, so that a modern reader meeting a vale skirted by woodlands hears, faintly, both the tailor and the tactician in the word.

Today skirted as a past form serves every layer at once. Walkers still skirt reservoirs, headline writers still catch ministers skirting inquiries, and geographers still map suburbs skirting motorways. The garment at the root has changed beyond recognition, from Norse tunic to modern fashion item, but the hem logic endures: wherever a mass meets its margin, something can be said to skirt it, and wherever a difficulty blocks the road, someone will skirt round rather than march through.
