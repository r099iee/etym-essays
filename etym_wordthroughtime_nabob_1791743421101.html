- 16th to 17th Century (Mughal Empire) - Borrowed directly into European traveler accounts as a dignified titular designation for provincial viceroys, regional governors, and tributary rulers (*nawwāb*) under the Great Mughal.
- Mid-18th Century (Post-Plassey British Empire, c. 1757–1780s) - Transformed into a biting, satiric social label for rapacious British East India Company servants who returned home with colossal, ill-gotten fortunes, flaunting their wealth through country manors and purchased parliamentary seats.
- Late 18th to Early 19th Century - Embodied acute national anxiety over the moral and political corruption of Britain, culminating in literary satires (such as Samuel Foote's play *The Nabob*) and the historic parliamentary impeachment of Warren Hastings led by Edmund Burke.
- Late 19th to Mid-20th Century - Generalized into American and global English as a colorful, slightly mocking colloquialism for any fabulously wealthy industrial baron, railroad boss, or flamboyant capitalist.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Re-popularized in American political rhetoric through speechwriter William Safire's famous 1970 phrase 'nattering nabobs of negativism' (delivered by Vice President Spiro Agnew), cementing its modern figurative use as an emblem of haughty, out-of-touch elites.
