Derivation chain for MEADOWS (plural of meadow); the card's form is the plural, so the final node is bolded as meadows, with the singular meadow given as the base of the chain. [1] PIE root *meh1- (also written *me-) "to mow, cut (grass, crops)" — attested across the family in the vocabulary of harvesting: Latin metere "to reap, harvest, mow," Greek amao "to mow, reap," and others. The root is part of the shared agrarian vocabulary of the Indo-European languages. [2] Proto-Germanic *medwo (also *medu-) "meadow, mowing-land, grassland set aside for the mow" — a feminine noun built from the mow-verb stem with the feminine noun-forming suffix; the same base yields the Germanic verb for "to mow" (English mow) and the noun for the cutting itself (German Mahd "mowing"). [3] Old English maed (oblique stem maedwe) "meadow, grassland, mowing-ground" (attested from the 7th c. onward, frequent in Anglo-Saxon land charters among the enumerated resources of an estate). [4] Middle English mede, medwe, medewe, medowe, medow "meadow" (12th–15th c.; the spelling crystallized gradually from the oblique form). [5] Modern English meadow "area of low-lying grassland, esp. for hay or pasture" (16th c. onward; the standard spelling). [6] Modern English plural meadows (regular plural; the form on the card). Note on the chain: the short poetic form mead (Old English maed) is the same noun without the -we extension of the oblique stem, not a separate root. The chain is fully attested and requires no conjecture between stages [1] and [6].
