From fitting out to dressing up. The deep root of adornments is a verb about arrangement and joining. The Indo-European base *h₂er- carried the sense "to fit together, join, arrange," and Latin's ordo "row, series, order" grew from it (with the -dh- suffix visible in ordin-). From the same family, Latin formed ornare, and here the semantic adventure begins: ornare at first meant not "to decorate" but "to fit out, equip, furnish" — supplying a thing with what it needs to function, exactly as order carries the idea of putting things in their proper places. A Roman general ornāret his army with supplies; a home was ornāta with its furniture. Beauty was still an implication, not yet the whole meaning.

Christian and Late Latin: adornare. As Latin turned to Late Latin, the prefix ad- ("to, toward") attached to form adornare, "to furnish, equip, decorate." In Christian and liturgical Latin the word took on spiritual colour: souls, altars, and churches are adorned with virtue, light, and ornament; the language of the Vulgate uses adornare for the dressing and beautifying that belongs to worship. The step from equipping to decorating was complete, and the religious register would echo for centuries in English devotional prose.

Old French and the courtly loan. Old French inherited the verb as adorner — "to adorn, to deck out" — and this is the form English borrowed in the 14th century, in the great wave of French vocabulary that followed the Norman Conquest. English adorn (Middle English adornen) arrived already wearing courtly, literary, and religious associations: to adorn a church, a bride, a text, or a monarch was to deck it with dignity and beauty. It was a high-register word from the start, more refined than plain native deck or trim.

Adornment and the -ment boom. The noun adornment formed in the 16th–17th centuries with the suffix -ment (from Old French -ment, Latin -mentum), which makes nouns of result or act. From the beginning it meant both the act (the adornment of the chapel took years) and the concrete result (the carvings, gems, and hangings were the adornments themselves). It shared the field with the older ornament (from Latin ornamentum), and the two have lived side by side ever since, adornment the more verbal and personal, ornament the more object-like.

Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: buildings, bodies, and books. In the neoclassical period, when architecture, furniture, and dress were judged by their ornament, adornments became a standard word for the decorative apparatus of a great building — its statuary, gilding, and mouldings — and of a person's attire and jewels. Fiction and travel writing delight in inventories of adornments, and the word also served rhetoric: prose "adorned" with figures was a classical ideal, its adornments the tropes themselves. Tolkien's sentence in the source passage, mourning the old adornments of a dwarven kingdom, speaks directly from this long tradition of architectural splendour and its ruin.

  • Sound history. Latin adōrnāre → Old French adorner → English adorn /əˈdɔːn/ shows the standard loss of the final -are ending and the shift of the Latin short o in the stressed syllable to the modern /ɔː/; adornment /əˈdɔːnmənt/ adds -ment unchanged.
  • Attestations. The Vulgate; Chaucer and Middle English devotional writers for adorn; the 1611 Authorized Version uses "adorn" for the beautifying of souls and churches; eighteenth-century art and travel writing for architectural adornments; Victorian fiction and fantasy for ornate interiors.
  • Cultural note. Because the word tracks a journey from practical equipping to luxurious beauty, it is unusually well suited to descriptions of loss — when adornments are destroyed, what perishes is not function but the whole accumulated dignity of a place.
