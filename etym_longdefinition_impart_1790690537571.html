Impart is a formal transitive verb meaning to give, convey, or communicate something — especially knowledge, qualities, or effects — to another person or thing. In the source sentence analysis can “impart to us” an intuitive grasp of motive: it can transmit or bestow that understanding, giving us a share in it.

Core senses in contemporary use:

  • To communicate information or knowledge: the most frequent sense. Teachers impart knowledge, wisdom, skills; witnesses impart information; briefings impart news. “She imparted the results to the team.” More formal and weighty than tell or pass on; implies valuable content worth sharing and a responsible giver. Takes to + recipient (“impart X to Y”) or double object in older style (“impart us knowledge” is now rare; “impart knowledge to us” is standard).
  • To bestow or give a quality, feeling, or impetus: parents impart values, courage imparts strength, experience imparts confidence. “Travel imparted a new urgency to his work.” The object is abstract and the transfer is causal rather than verbal: X comes to have more of Y because of contact with Z.
  • To give a physical property or effect (technical): materials and processes impart colour, flavour, motion, heat, spin. “The barrel imparts oak notes.” “Friction imparts heat.” Standard in food science, chemistry, engineering, and sport (“the bowler imparts spin”). No human intention need be involved; it is purely causal transmission.
  • Archaic/legal — to disclose, reveal, or grant formally: “imparting state secrets,” “powers imparted by statute.” Still found in contracts, minutes, and journalism for solemn disclosure. The construction impart information is Euro-English legalese for what plain style would call provide.

Grammar and collocation:

  • Transitivity: almost always transitive with expressed object; intransitive use (“he imparted at length”) is obsolete. Passive is common (“wisdom imparted by elders”). Cannot be used reflexively in modern prose.
  • Typical objects: knowledge, wisdom, information, news, skills, values, confidence, courage, meaning, flavour, colour, warmth, motion, spin, momentum. Concrete countable objects (“impart a book”) sound odd unless the book is conceived as content; one imparts what the book contains, not the binding.
  • Impart vs. import: frequently confused by learners. Impart = give out (share with others); import = bring in (from abroad). Mnemonic: impart parts with something; import ports in something.
  • Impart vs. convey / communicate / transmit: communicate is neutral and bidirectional; convey stresses carrying meaning (often non-verbally); transmit is technical-mechanical (signals, disease); impart stresses generous bestowal of something valuable, with a human giver and a human gain. Analysis “imparts” grasp because grasp is figured as a gift, not a signal.

In short, impart dignifies giving as sharing: the giver retains while the receiver gains a part. Whether the thing shared is insight, confidence, vanilla, or spin, the verb frames transfer as enrichment rather than loss.
