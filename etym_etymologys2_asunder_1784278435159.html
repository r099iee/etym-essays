Proto-Indo-European root *sen(i)- / *sane- "apart, separate, without" — reconstructed, no attested date. The root names separation and apartness; it underlies Latin sine "without" (as in sine qua non), Sanskrit sanutár "away, far off, apart," and, via Proto-Germanic, the entire Germanic family of "separate, apart, alone" words. Proto-Germanic *sundraz "separate, distinct" and *sundrō "apart, singly, asunder" — reconstructed (not attested in writing); inferred from Gothic sundrō "apart," Old Norse sundr "asunder, in pieces," Old English sundor "apart, separately," Old High German suntar "apart." The -tr-/-dr- suffix marks the adverbial formation ("in a state of apartness"). Old English sundor "apart, separately, singly" — attested in Old English texts (8th–11th century). From the same stem Old English also formed the adjective syndrig "separate, distinct" (source of modern sundry "various") and the verb sundrian "to separate, to divide" (source of modern sunder). Old English on sundran "in a separate state, in two, apart" — the preposition on "in, into" governing the dative of sundor; attested in the Old English period. This is the immediate ancestor of the modern word. Middle English asunder "into pieces, apart, in two" — fused from on + sundran, with the unstressed preposition on reduced to a- (the same erosion that produced a-bed, a-foot, a-shore); attested from the 13th century onward. English ASUNDER — the modern form, used in the liturgical formula "put asunder" (Matthew 19:6; Book of Common Prayer) and in the literary pairs torn/rent/split/burst asunder. ◄ TARGET Note on debated/nuanced stages: the PIE root is sometimes cited as *seni- and sometimes as *sane-, reflecting uncertainty about the original ablaut grade of the etymon; both forms are given in the literature (Pokorny gives *sen(i)- / *sane-). The precise phonetic path from PIE *sen(i)- to Proto-Germanic *sundraz involves an unexplained nasal assimilation (*sen- → *sun- before -dr-/-tr-), which is reconstructed but not directly attested. The Germanic stages themselves (Gothic sundrō, Old English sundor) are securely attested, and the fusion on sundran → asunder is documented in Middle English from the 13th century.
