c. 1600 Renaissance Coinage - Aonian enters English as a learned Latin poetic epithet, meaning "of Aonia, of the Muses, of Helicon," used in the epic diction of the Renaissance to invoke classical poetic heights.
17th-Century Epic Usage - Milton and his contemporaries use Aonian to name the pagan summit of poetry (the "Aonian Mount") in contrast to biblical inspiration, giving the word a confident, competitive, learned register.
18th-Century Augustan Fashion - The Augustan poets keep Aonian in stock classical collocations (Aonian spring, Aonian maids), but it is already an ornament of high verse rather than a living geographic term.
Contemporary Sense - Today Aonian is essentially archaic, surviving only in literary histories and archaizing verse as a synonym for "poetic" or "Muse-inspired," rarely used outside scholarship.
