PIE *ser- ‘to line up, bind, put in order, join’ (reconstructed; also the root of Latin serere ‘to join, arrange, weave’ and of English series) — the standard textbook hypothesis, but debated (see note) → Latin sors, sortis (gen.) ‘a lot as drawn or thrown, share, portion, chance, fate, oracular response’ (classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) → Vulgar Latin *sortiarius ‘one who works by lots; fortune-teller, diviner, sorcerer’ (late Latin, reconstructed; -arius marks the agent, ‘one concerned with’) → Old French sorcier ‘sorcerer, wizard, one who casts lots and deals in spells’ (11th–12th c.) → Middle English sorcer / sorcerer ‘sorcerer’ (late 14th c.; the second -er is English reinforcement of an agent already marked) → English sorcerer + adjectival suffix -ous → sorcerous ‘of, like, or characteristic of a sorcerer’ (late 16th c.) => sorcerous (modern English adjective). DEBATE / NUANCE: The PIE *ser- derivation is the common textbook etymology but is not beyond dispute; some linguists prefer to keep sors etymologically isolated beyond Latin. The semantic bridge within Latin, however, is secure: casting lots (sortes) to learn or assign fate → divination → magic in general.
