- c. 900–1150: Old English becweþan — Originally meant "to say about, declare, affirm, utter," but rapidly developed a specialized legal sense in Anglo-Saxon customary law referring to the formal oral declaration of one's dying wishes or verbal transfer of property (since wills were predominantly spoken before witnesses).
- c. 1150–1450: Middle English biquethe / bequethen — Retained the legal meaning of leaving goods by will, but lost its general sense of "speaking" or "saying" (as the base verb cwethan died out, surviving only in the fossilized past-tense quoth).
- c. 1450–1600: Common Law Formalization — Consolidated into precise English jurisprudence alongside Anglo-French legal vocabulary, establishing the strict formula: "I give, devise, and bequeath" (where devise applied to real estate and bequeath to personal property/bequests).
- c. 1600–1850: Poetic & Cultural Broadening — Widely adopted in English literature (Shakespeare, Milton, Wordsworth) beyond monetary estates to denote the transmission of intangible heritage, wisdom, liberty, and historical burdens to posterity.
- c. 1850–Present: Modern Legal & Figurative Standard — Maintained as an indispensable formal verb in estate planning and probate law, while functioning in serious intellectual, sociological, and ethical prose to describe the generational stewardship of civilizational and ecological inheritances.
