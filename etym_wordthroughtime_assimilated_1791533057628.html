- 15th c. — Borrowed from French medical and alchemical writing: food or humours are assimilated when the body turns them into its own substance. The register is learned and physiological; the word belongs to physicians and translators of Latin.
- 16th–17th c. — The sense widens to any substance taken into another and made like it. Alchemists speak of assimilating metals; divines speak of assimilating truth. The tone is technical but hopeful: assimilation is a natural, healthy process.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment writers extend the term to knowledge and ideas, which the mind is said to assimilate as the body assimilates food. The metaphor is now fully mental, and the word acquires a mildly intellectual, self-improving flavour.
- 19th c. — Two expansions occur at once. In biology and chemistry, assimilation becomes a technical term for nutrition and photosynthesis; in the United States, it is applied to immigrants, and “assimilation” becomes a policy goal. The connotation is progressive and confident: the melting pot is thought of as a success.
- Early 20th c. — Official Americanisation programmes, citizenship classes and English-only campaigns make “assimilated” a compliment in mainstream discourse and a demand in official discourse.
- Mid 20th c. — Sociology formalises the concept (Robert Park, Milton Gordon), measuring assimilation by intermarriage, residential mixing and language shift. The word sounds neutral and scientific.
- 1960s–1980s — Minority and post-colonial critics attack assimilation as cultural erasure; “assimilationist” becomes a term of reproach, and “integration” is preferred by policymakers. The word’s emotional temperature drops sharply.
- 1990s–2000s — Star Trek gives “You will be assimilated” global currency, so that the word now carries a ready-made image of forced absorption into a collective. In computing and meteorology, “data assimilation” becomes an everyday technical phrase.
- 2010s–present — In migration debates, “assimilated” is contested: used approvingly of successful newcomers, and suspiciously of those accused of abandoning their roots. In security discourse it describes absorbed intelligence; in popular culture it still evokes the Borg.
