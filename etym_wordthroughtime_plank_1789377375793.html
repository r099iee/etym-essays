- Late 13th Century (Middle English) - Borrowed from Old Northern French *planke* (derived from Late Latin *planca*), strictly designating a heavy, broad, thick cut of sawn timber used for bridges, flooring, scaffolding, and ship hulls.
- 14th to 15th Century - Becomes ubiquitous in naval architecture and river crossings; records document town councils purchasing "plankis" for repairing wharf docks and city gates.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - Solidified in maritime trade during the Age of Discovery; also develops colloquial commercial verbs like *to plank down* (to plunk down coin or pay cash on the table).
- Late 18th to 19th Century - Gained dramatic notoriety through nautical folklore and melodramatic maritime literature in the phrase *to walk the plank*, depicting Caribbean pirates executing captives; also entered British dialectal slang in *thick as two short planks*.
- Mid-19th Century (American Politics) - Metaphorically adopted into American political rhetoric (c. 1848–1856); an individual resolution or policy item within a party's *platform* became universally known as a *plank*.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Revolutionized fitness culture and athletic training as the global name for an isometric abdominal core exercise, while briefly surfacing in the 2011 internet phenomenon of "planking".
