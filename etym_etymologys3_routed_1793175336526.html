The ancestor of routed is a breaking. PIE *reup-, to snatch, break, tear, is a root of violence, surviving in words for robbery and rupture across the family, and in Latin it became rumpere, to break or burst — the verb inside eruption, rupture, abrupt, and bankrupt. Its past participle ruptus, broken, in the feminine rupta, supplied two Roman nouns at once: rupta (via), a broken or forced road through obstacles, and rupta alone, a broken-off piece, a detachment of soldiers split from the main body.

Old French inherited both as route: a company of men travelling or fighting together, and the path they travel. English borrowed it in the fourteenth century in the company-sense — a route of men could be a retinue, a gang, or a tumultuous crowd — and for two hundred years the word wobbled between neutral and noisy. The sixteenth century fixed the military meaning: on the gunpowder battlefield, where formations either held or dissolved, a rout became specifically the dissolution — the moment a beaten army ceases to be an army and becomes a fleeing crowd — and to rout became the victor's verb, to inflict that dissolution.

English then doubled the word without quite duplicating it. The road-sense, respelt route under French influence and now pronounced to rhyme with out or root according to taste and continent, kept the path; the defeat-sense, spelt rout and always rhyming with out, kept the breaking. They remain doublets — one breaking forced a way, the other forced men — and poets have never tired of the pun: the rout takes to its route, the broken army streaming down the broken road. Meanwhile an unrelated rout, from root, to dig and grub like a hog, gave carpenters their router and sleepers their snoring, homonyms by accident living under the same roof.

  • The breaking root: PIE *reup- gives Latin a whole family of fractures — erupt, disrupt, abrupt — and warfare borrows the physics of snapping sticks for the snapping of armies.
  • The detached band: Roman rupta, a broken-off detachment, becomes French route, any company of armed men, the seed of both road and rout.
  • The English wobble: Chaucer's rout is any loud crowd, festive or fierce; only Tudor warfare narrows it to defeated flight.
  • The doublet split: spelling and pronunciation part the ways — route the path, rout the ruin — while the pun keeps them neighbours.
  • The false friends: the carpenter's and the snorer's rout arrive from digging and noise, proving that one spelling can house three bloodlines.

So who hath routed this great host asks, in the tale's high diction, who performed the ancient physics of breaking: who snapped the stick of the army, and sent its splinters streaming down every road.
