An obscure beginning (c. 1500). English toss suddenly appears in the written record around 1510, meaning "to throw about, to fling, to jerk upward", with no clear ancestry in Old or Middle English and no cognates in the older Germanic languages. Its origin is, in the words of the standard etymological authorities, obscure. The leading hypothesis is a Scandinavian borrowing: Norwegian dialectal tossa / tos(s)e, "to strew, to toss, to throw carelessly", which would have entered English through the North Sea contacts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when hundreds of Scandinavian words were passing into northern dialects. An older rival suggestion linked the word to Latin toxāre, "to poison", via the idea of "to jerk about as if poisoned" — a piece of free association now universally rejected. A third line of thought sees an expressive, onomatopoeic formation — the short, abrupt syllable toss imitating the quick flick of a throw — an etymology of last resort that many scholars find plausible precisely because the word behaves like the "fancy" coinages of early modern English slang. Whatever its origin, the word's late, sudden, colloquial character is itself historical evidence: it entered the language exactly when English prose was expanding explosively, borrowing from every quarter and coining freely, in the two centuries between Caxton and Shakespeare.


Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: the word in Shakespeare's England. By the 1590s toss and tossing were fully at home in English. Shakespeare uses the verb repeatedly — literally, of tossing enemies, tossing the waves, and tossing the head, and figuratively, of the agitation of fortune and of the mind. The King James Bible of 1611 stamped the figurative sense forever in Ephesians 4:14, "that we henceforth be no more children, tossed to and fro, and carried about with every wind of doctrine", and in James 1:6, where the doubter is "like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed". These biblical collocations carried the word's meteorological and psychological senses through four centuries of literacy: "storm-tossed", "tossed by fate", "tossed on the seas of doubt" are Jacobean rhythms still audible in modern prose. The century also fixed the gesture sense — the disdainful toss of the head appears in Restoration comedy as a stock sign of pride — and the chance sense, as the coin toss became the standard way of deciding any matter to which no other rule applied.


Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: the word goes domestic and sporting. The eighteenth century domesticated the verb: cookery books describe tossing salads and tossing pancakes; the "toss" that throws a rider from a horse entered sporting vocabulary; and the Scottish Highland Games' "tossing the caber", once a rural labourers' feat, became a formal spectacle. The phrase "toss-up", meaning an even chance or a decision by lot, is recorded from the early nineteenth century and quickly became political journalists' favourite word for an undecided contest. The novelists of the period stretched the word's metaphorical reach — characters are "tossed" between hope and fear, their sleep "tossing" with anxiety — preparing the word's modern clinical career.


Twentieth and twenty-first centuries: insomnia, sport, and slang. The twentieth century gave the word its most famous fixed collocation: "tossing and turning", the standard language of insomnia in medical writing, self-help books, and film — and Oscar Wilde's "tossing feverishly", written in 1888, stands near the beginning of that idiom's journey. Sport adopted "toss" wholesale (the pre-match coin toss of cricket and football), and slang kept the word busy: "toss one's cookies" (to vomit), "toss off" (to do quickly), "toss" as a mild insult. The era of probability theory meanwhile made the coin toss a figure for radical uncertainty — "a toss-up" — used in economics and physics alike. Today tossing remains what it has been for five hundred years: the most kinetic verb of English restlessness, equally at home in the fever ward, on the open sea, in the salad bowl, and in the referee's palm. Its unknown origins have never hindered a career built entirely on motion.
