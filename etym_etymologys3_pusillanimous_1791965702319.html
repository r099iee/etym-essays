Pusillanimous is one of the most transparent Latin compounds in English, provided one knows the two pieces. The first is pusillus, a Latin adjective meaning "very small, tiny" and, by extension, "petty, insignificant". It is a diminutive of pūsus, a rare and mostly early Latin word for "boy, lad", which belongs to the nursery stratum of the vocabulary alongside pūpa "girl, doll". The origin of pūsus is not known; the old suggestion that it is related to puer "boy" cannot be demonstrated, and the word may simply be a childish formation. What matters for the compound is that pusillus carried both the physical sense "tiny" and the evaluative sense "petty" — a double meaning that English preserves in its own uses of small.

The second element. Animus is one of the central words of Latin psychology. It means "mind, soul, spirit", and also "courage, will, purpose"; it is related to anima "breath, soul" and descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂enh₁- "to breathe", which also yields Greek anemos "wind" and Sanskrit aniti "breathes". For the Romans, courage was a property of the animus, and the size of a man's spirit was a moral fact about him. Hence the two compounds: magnanimus, "great-souled", and pusillanimus, "small-souled". The second is the exact morphological negation of the first, and the pair survives in English as magnanimous and pusillanimous.

A Christian coinage? Pusillanimis is not a classical Latin word; it belongs to Late Latin, and it appears chiefly in Christian writing of the fourth century and later. This has suggested to many scholars that it was coined as a calque on the Greek ὀλιγόψυχος (literally "little-souled"), an adjective used in Jewish and Christian Greek for the faint-hearted and the discouraged. The Latin word may therefore be a translation-artefact rather than an organic formation, which would explain why it is not found in Cicero or Livy. The suggestion is plausible but not proven; what is certain is that the word's early life was religious, and that it entered English through that channel.

  • Latin pūsus: "boy", a rare nursery word of unknown origin.
  • pusillus: diminutive, "very small" → "petty, insignificant".
  • Late Latin pusillanimis: "small-souled", probably modelled on Greek ὀλιγόψυχος.
  • English 1580s: pusillanimous borrowed as a term of moral theology.

Into English. The adjective is first recorded in English in the 1580s, one of the wave of Latin moral vocabulary that entered the language during the sixteenth century. It was at home immediately in sermons, treatises on the virtues and translations of the classics, where it named the vice opposed to fortitude. Its companion noun pusillanimity followed, and the pair settled into the vocabulary of moral philosophy alongside magnanimity.

Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. As political prose developed, pusillanimous migrated from the pulpit to the dispatch box. It was used of councillors who advised surrender, of commanders who avoided battle, and of ministers who shrank from unpopular decisions. The word's length and Latinity made it useful in debate: it allowed a speaker to accuse an opponent of cowardice while appearing to offer a considered moral judgment rather than a bare insult. This is still its rhetorical function.

Nineteenth-century confidence. In the Victorian period the word flourished in leader columns, biographies and histories, often in the phrase pusillanimous policy. It belonged to the same moral vocabulary as manly, duty and courage, and it carried the period's confidence that public conduct could be judged by private virtues. The twentieth century inherited both the word and its tone.

Modern use. Pusillanimous is now uncommon but far from dead. It appears regularly in political commentary, usually of leaders or institutions accused of failing to confront aggression or of sacrificing principle to convenience. Because it is rare in speech, it retains a distinctly literary and slightly archaic force; a journalist who calls a policy pusillanimous is reaching for the moral vocabulary of the Victorians, and expects the reader to notice.
