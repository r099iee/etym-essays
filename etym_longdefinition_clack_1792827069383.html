Clack names in its primary modern sense a short, sharp, hard sound — the noise of two flat surfaces striking: shears snapping shut, heels on stone, a typewriter's keys, the source sentence's garden shears heard faintly through the window as Sam works nearer and nearer.

Tolkien's use is a small masterclass in acoustic storytelling. The room is dark and silent, so the clack carries the whole garden into the chamber: industry against stillness, day-labour against night-counsel, Sam's honest steel punctuating grave talk. The word does not describe the sound so much as reproduce it, two hard consonants shutting like the blades themselves.

Core meaning in detail: A clack is briefer and harder than a click, drier than a clatter, thinner than a clang. Prototypes are shears and scissors snapping, billiard balls meeting, knitting needles crossing, a lock engaging, heels striking pavement. The verb to clack covers both making the noise (shears clack, teeth clack with cold) and producing it idly (to clack one's tongue), while the noun names each single report and, collectively, the chatter it resembles.

From noise to talk — the bridge: The oldest English sense of clack, around 1200, was to chatter, prattle, gossip — the tongue clacking like a mill. Sound-words easily become talk-words because talk is sound with meaning added, and empty talk sinks back toward mere noise. So clack travelled from tongues to machines and back: gossip clacks, mills clack, tongues clack disapproval, and every rapid rattle of speech or steel answers to the same syllable.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A sharp striking sound (primary modern sense): the snap of shears, heels, keys, balls. The bridge is pure imitation: the word's own cl- onset and -ack stop enact the impact they name.
  • Rapid mechanical rattling: the clack of the mill, the press, the rails — repeated clacks blurring into industry. The bridge is iteration: one clack is an event, a run of clacks is a machine, and mills were clacking in English verse centuries before engines.
  • Idle chatter, gossip (earliest sense, c. 1200, still lively): the clack of tongues, mere clack. The bridge runs through the clack-mill, the rattling gossip-mill of proverb: tongues that never stop are machinery that never stops.
  • The sound of disapproval: to clack one's tongue against the teeth. The bridge narrows chatter to a single scolding snap, the mouth's own shears closing on folly.
  • Animal and bodily clacks: storks clack their bills, teeth clack with cold, joints clack with age. The bridge is anatomy as mechanism: every hinged thing, beak or jaw or knee, reports its motion in the same syllable.
  • A clack valve or clacker (technical): the non-return valve of pumps and the toy or signalling clacker. The bridge carries the noise into the device: the thing named for the sound it cannot help making.
  • To chatter with cold or fear (verb of the body): teeth clacking, knees clacking. The bridge is involuntary percussion: the body playing its own bones when will surrenders to shivering.

Register and connotation: clack is brisk, workmanlike, faintly comic — honest labour and busy tongues rather than tragedy. Against Tolkien's dark silent room it sounds almost comforting: while powers debate, Sam clips, and the small steel music of the garden promises that ordinary work goes on. Even as gossip the word amuses more than it wounds; a clacking tongue is a nuisance, never a serpent.
