Linear derivation chains for DOUBLY (two morphemes: base 'double' + suffix '-ly'). CHAIN 1 — base 'double': 1. PIE *dwóh₁ 'two' (the great Indo-European cardinal numeral, c. 4500-2500 BC; ancestor of Sanskrit dvā́u, Greek δύο, Latin duo, Lithuanian du, Proto-Germanic *twai). 2. Latin duo 'two' (Classical Latin; Romance daughters Italian due, French deux, Spanish dos). 3. Latin duplus 'twofold' — duo + -plus 'fold, times'; the element -plus from PIE *pel- 'to fold, plait' (attested 1st c. BC; cf. simplex 'single-fold', multiplex 'manifold'). 4. Vulgar Latin *duplum (spoken form, through the Empire and early Middle Ages). 5. Old French doble / double 'double' (12th c.; form influenced in learned uses by Latin duplex 'of twofold nature', duo + *plek- 'to plait'). 6. Anglo-Norman double → Middle English double 'double' (adjective, then verb, c. 1200). 7. English double (adjective, verb, adverb). 8. double + -ly → DOUBLY (adverb, formed in Middle English; earliest secure evidence from the 14th c.). CHAIN 2 — suffix '-ly': 1. Proto-Germanic *līką 'body, form' (PIE origin not securely reconstructable; cf. Old English līc 'body, corpse', modern English lich and like) — ultimate ancestry unknown, see note. 2. Proto-Germanic *-līkō / *-līkaz 'having the form of' (adverbial/adjectival suffix). 3. Old English -līce (adverbial suffix: cwīclīce 'quickly', 'in a quick manner', built on the noun līc 'body, form'). 4. Middle English -li / -ly (eroded form of -līce). 5. English -ly (universal adverbial suffix). 6. Combined in DOUBLY 'in a twofold manner' (14th c.). Note: the adverbial suffix -ly descends from the Germanic noun *līką 'body, form' — to act x-līce is to act 'in the body-form of x'. The PIE ancestry of that noun is debated and not securely established, so the chain for the suffix is honestly stated as Germanic onward; the base chain for 'double' is fully Indo-European.
