Note on historical phonology and etymological doublet: Historical linguists (including the Oxford English Dictionary and Walther von Wartburg's FEW) emphasize that guise is an etymological doublet of native English wise ("manner, fashion", as in clockwise, likewise, crosswise, in any wise). Both derive from Proto-Germanic *wīsō. The phonetic difference illustrates the classic Gallo-Romance consonant adaptation: when Germanic-speaking Franks entered Roman Gaul in the 5th century, Romance speakers lacked a native /w/ sound in their phonological inventory and systematically reinforced Germanic initial /w/ with a voiced velar stop /ɡ/, producing /ɡw/, which subsequently simplified in Old French to /ɡ/ (spelled gu-). Thus Germanic *wīsa became Old French guise, just as Frankish *wardōn became garder (giving English guard vs. native ward), *werra became guerre (giving war), and *willehelm became Guillaume (giving William).
