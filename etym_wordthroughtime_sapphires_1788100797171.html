- c. 1300–1500 (Middle English 'saphires') - the plural occurs chiefly in church and royal inventories; perception: an unearthly heavenly blue, fit for relics, shrines and episcopal rings.
- 16th–17th c. - goldsmiths and lapidaries treat sapphires as the prince of blue stones; 'sapphire' begins to name a pure colour ('sapphire blue'), and poets use it for the sky and for virtuous eyes — the stone of chastity and heaven.
- 18th c. - mineralogists identify the sapphire as corundum; the mysterious 'eastern gem' gains a chemical identity, yet jewellers still charge by colour and origin, and colonial trade from Ceylon and Burma feeds a romantic prestige.
- 19th c. - Victorian gem mania: sapphires appear beside diamonds in engagement jewellery, and in fiction (Wilde's 'The Happy Prince', 1888) paired 'bright sapphires' measure the beauty — and later the sacrifice — of eyes.
- 1902 onward - synthetic sapphires pour from Verneuil furnaces; the word now also names industrial watch crystals; the gem sense stays prestigious while the material sense spreads into technology.
- 20th–21st c. - September's birthstone, the 'sapphire wedding' (45th anniversary) and the 2017 Sapphire Jubilee; in modern speech 'sapphires' means blue gemstones almost exclusively, but the colour-adjective use ('sapphire eyes') keeps the old romantic halo alive.
