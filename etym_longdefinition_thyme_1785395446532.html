Thyme is a low-growing, woody-stemmed aromatic herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae), genus Thymus, bearing tiny oval leaves and small lilac, pink, or white flowers in whorls or small heads, with a strong, warm, resinous, slightly dry fragrance and flavour. It is used in cooking as a seasoning of roasts, stews, soups, and stuffings; in herbal medicine as an antiseptic, expectorant, and preservative (the essential oil thymol is a standard pharmaceutical agent); and, in antiquity and medieval practice, in incense, temple fumigation, ritual purification, and embalming. In the passage the fragrant flora of the vanished high country is inventoried — "the thyme and the sage and the marjoram" — and the word carries the sensory signature of the dry, sunny upland: thyme is the scent of chalk downs and Mediterranean turf, its perfume released by sun, heat, and the brushing feet of walkers and animals. The nuance is strongly evocative and synaesthetic: Tolkien names the herbs of the hillside to make the place vividly real before it dissolves, and thyme is the most pungently fragrant item of the list — a plant you smell before you see, whose very mention perfumes the sentence. In English tradition thyme is also the bee-plant and the herb of courage (medieval knights embroidered sprigs of it on their scarves), and the honey of Mount Hymettus in Attica, famous since antiquity, was credited to the bees that fed on its wild thyme. Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Thymus vulgaris (common or garden thyme): The primary culinary sense — the low, shrubby Mediterranean species with grey-green leaves, the sovereign seasoning of roasts and stews; the plant-name par excellence.
  • The genus Thymus generally: Botanically, some hundreds of species of aromatic herbs and sub-shrubs of the mint family, including lemon thyme (T. citriodorus), wild thyme (T. serpyllum), and creeping thyme, all sharing the warm, resinous character.
  • Wild thyme / mother of thyme (Thymus serpyllum, T. praecox): The low, creeping, intensely fragrant thyme of chalk downs, heaths, and mountain turf — the sense most relevant to Tolkien's high, sun-baked upland; the plant of sheep-walks and downs, its scent the smell of summer turf.
  • Culinary and kitchen sense: Dried or fresh thyme as a flavouring — bouquet garni, poultry seasoning, thyme butter, thyme honey — the everyday, domestic sense in modern English.
  • Medicinal and pharmaceutical (modern jargon): Thyme oil and thymol (a phenol first isolated from thyme oil in the 18th c.) are standard antiseptics, mouthwash and cough remedy components, and preservatives; "thymol" is the technical derivative in pharmacology and chemistry.
  • Ritual and fumigation (historical-religious): In ancient Greece thyme was burned as incense in temples and used in purification and embalming; the very name means "the fumigating herb" (see etymology), so the ritual sense is the etymological one — thyme is the plant that fills the air with sacred smoke.
  • Symbolic and folkloric sense: The herb of courage and fidelity (knights' scarves, wedding posies), the bee-plant whose honey was the glory of Mount Hymettus, and in folk belief a bringer of good luck, strength, and protection against nightmares.
  • Homophonic play: Because thyme is pronounced identically with time, the word participates in puns and wordplay ("thyme flies", "a sprig of thyme for a pinch of time"), a purely phonetic secondary sense exploited in literature and advertising.
  • Horticultural sense: Creeping and woolly thymes are garden ground-covers and rock-garden plants, valued for fragrance, drought-tolerance, and their role as bee-forage — the modern ornamental sense of the word.
Historical bridge: the semantic career runs from smoke to taste. Thyme's name is fixed by its earliest attested function — burning in temples (Greek thýein "to burn sacrifice") — then the word travelled into the kitchen, the still-room, and the pharmacy, and finally into gardens and wordplay. Every sense, from incense to thymol to time-puns, hangs on the plant's piercing, released fragrance: the herb you smell, and burn, and season with, all at once.
