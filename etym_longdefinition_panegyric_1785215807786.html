panegyric is a formal public speech or elaborate written composition delivered or published in lavish, deliberately elevated praise of a person, institution, achievement, or cause. It is a set-piece of rhetorical celebration: grand, ceremonial, and presumptively sincere, distinct both from the intimate warmth of ordinary compliments and from the crude flattery of self-serving sycophancy. In the present context the word labels the famous dedication of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859) to Harriet Taylor: Mill's inscription hails her as “the inspirer, and in part the author, of all that is best in my writings,” and the biographer writes that it “contains the most extraordinary panegyric on a woman that any philosopher has ever penned.” The nuance is one of public, almost worshipful tribute — not private affection but a formal encomium intended for the world to read.

  • Sense 1 (rhetoric / oratory): an oration or written discourse in high praise of a person, nation, ruler, or city, belonging to the ancient genre of epideictic (display) oratory — the speech of praise and blame in which praise was a civic and ceremonial duty.
  • Sense 2 (literary / critical): any elaborate composition of praise, especially a biography, dedication, or review so admiring as to be suspected of losing objectivity — “a one-sided panegyric rather than an objective analysis.”
  • Sense 3 (figurative / ironic): by extension, excessive or uncritical praise, a piece of flattering overstatement — “the speech was a panegyric to the chairman's every whim.” In this ironic use the word keeps its formal grandeur while suggesting insincerity.
  • Historical bridge: Greek panēgyrikós (lógos) means literally “a festival-assembly speech” — the oration delivered at a great national gathering (panēgyris) such as the Olympic or Panathenaic festivals, where orators displayed their art and celebrated their city and its heroes. The word therefore always carried the idea of public, ceremonial, full-dress praise performed before the assembled people; English preserves that public, formal character.
  • Distinction from near-synonyms: eulogy is specifically praise at a funeral, while a panegyric may praise the living or institutions and is festival, not funeral, in origin. Encomium (Greek enkṓmion, “song of praise at a victory celebration”) is nearly synonymous but tends to be used of formal literary tributes; laudation is a plainer Latin-derived equivalent; tribute is broader and warmer, usable of a small gesture of gratitude; testimonial implies a practical public honouring of a retiring official; flattery is insincere, self-serving praise — the corrupt shadow of the panegyric.
  • Register and connotation: learned, formal, slightly archaic-flavoured; rare in speech but standard in literary criticism and historical writing. Its connotation is grand and public: it is at once a compliment to the subject and a display of the speaker's eloquence.

Antonyms and contrasts: vituperation, invective, philippic, diatribe, denunciation, criticism, satire, lampoon. Where the panegyric raises the subject to the heavens, the invective and the lampoon drag the target through the mud; the two are the twin poles of epideictic rhetoric. A full account of panegyric therefore ranges from the ancient festival speech, through the imperial panegyrics of Rome and the courts of the Renaissance, to the Victorian dedication that moved a biographer to call it the most extraordinary tribute ever penned by a philosopher to a woman.
