Ancient Germanic Drink - Ale descends from the Old English ealu, the everyday fermented malt drink of the Anglo-Saxon world — the banquet-hall beverage of Beowulf, poured by the mead-bench queens, hearty and social.
Medieval Ale vs. Beer Distinction - In medieval England ale (unhopped, brewed from malt) is contrasted with beer (hopped, imported technique), so the two words name socially and technically different drinks, with ale the older, native, ordinary one.
Early Modern Continuity - Ale remains the plain English word for the everyday malt drink through the early modern period — the drink of toasts, tankards, and taverns, the heart of English feasting vocabulary.
Contemporary Sense - Today ale is the top-fermented style of beer (pale ale, brown ale, real ale), the word revived by the CAMRA real-ale movement as the badge of traditional British brewing, still hearth-side and unpretentious.
