Core meaning. DESPATCH — spelled despatch and dispatch, both standard, despatch the older British preference — denotes brisk promptness and decisive speed in getting business done: rapid, efficient settlement and sending off of matters, things finished and put out of the way without dallying. In the abstract sense used here it is a personal quality: the editor's habit of moving work to completion swiftly and economically. The word carries not mere rapidity but rapidity with finality — the business is not just done quickly; it is done, closed, and dispatched.

In this passage. The sentence comes from a warm, graceful tribute to the editor Randolph Quirk: "Nor can I list all the qualities Randolph Quirk has shown as an editor; despatch, meticulousness, and humour will have to suffice." The three nouns are chosen as three representative virtues, and their pairing is pointed. Despatch is the speed and decisiveness; meticulousness is the exactness that balances it — the editor is both quick and careful; humour is the humanity that keeps the two from hardening into mere efficiency. The tone is affectionate and slightly apologetic, since the author cannot list everything and must let three qualities stand for the whole.

Polysemy: the senses of DESPATCH/DISPATCH today. The word family is unusually wide, all senses radiating from the core idea of "sending off" and "finishing up":

  • Promptness, expedition (the sense here). Speed and decisiveness in settling business: "he acted with dispatch". The oldest abstract sense, from the 16th century.
  • Sending off of messages, troops, cargo. To dispatch a letter, a messenger, a courier, troops, or goods — the concrete act of causing things to be sent. From this: dispatcher (a person who controls the sending of vehicles or messages).
  • An official report. In journalism and diplomacy, a dispatch is a formal report sent by a correspondent or an ambassador ("a news dispatch", "a diplomatic dispatch"). Military despatches are reports of operations, often bearing the names of the brave ("mentioned in despatches").
  • Execution, killing. To dispatch a person or animal is to kill or put out of the way, usually quickly and efficiently — a euphemism with an old literary pedigree ("he dispatched the messenger").
  • Dispatch box / dispatch case. A box or case for carrying official papers, as in the British House of Commons "Despatch Box".
  • Software and technical jargon (modern). In computing, dispatch means routing an event, message, or request to the code that handles it ("event dispatch", "dynamic dispatch"); a dispatcher is the component that assigns tasks to workers. This modern use is a straight extension of the old "sending off" sense into new machinery.

Semantic bridge. The whole family descends from the idea of releasing the feet. Latin pedica was a fetter for the foot; to unshackle — *dispedicāre — was to set the feet free, and a freed man can move, run, and carry a message away at once. "Sending off" and "finishing quickly" both follow from this: the unshackled messenger departs instantly, and the business he is charged with is settled without delay. The abstract "promptness" sense is therefore the oldest image grown abstract — speed as the release of movement. The "kill" sense is the grim tail of the same idea: first to put a matter out of the way, then to put a person out of the way.

Spelling. Both dispatch and despatch are correct; the OED notes that despatch is the commoner British form, especially in the 19th century, and dispatch the commoner in American and technical use. In computing the spelling is always dispatch. Here the author's despatch suits the book's English setting and period flavour.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. Speed is a neutral measure of rate, with no connotation of competence or completion. Promptness means being on time, punctuality, rather than speed of execution. Alacrity adds cheerful eagerness to swiftness. Efficiency concerns the best use of resources, not necessarily speed. Expedition, a close cousin, means speed in doing business and is near-synonymous but slightly grander and less everyday. Haste implies urgency and often carelessness. Dispatch alone combines rapidity with decisiveness, finality, and a quiet sense of control — the business is closed and sent on its way.

Register and collocation. As an abstract noun, dispatch is formal and literary: "with great dispatch", "acted with dispatch". Common collocations: "news dispatch", "military dispatch", "to dispatch a message", "to dispatch troops", "mentioned in despatches", "dispatch rider". Register: neutral-to-formal, comfortable in journalism, official writing, and literary prose — exactly the register of the tribute in which it appears.
