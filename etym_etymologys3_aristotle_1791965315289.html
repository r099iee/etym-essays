Aristotle is a transliteration before it is a biography. The Greek Ἀριστοτέλης is a compound of two ordinary words, and the history of the name is the history of those two words being welded into an aristocratic label and then carried across languages.

The elements.

  • ἄριστος (aristos), “best”: the superlative of ἀγαθός (agathos), “good”, and the source of English aristocracy (“rule of the best”) and aristocrat. Its own etymology is not securely established; Beekes treats it as having no agreed Indo-European source, and a pre-Greek origin is possible. The word belongs to the vocabulary of praise and rank, not of philosophy.
  • τέλος (telos), “end, purpose, completion”: a noun of wide range — the finish of a race, the fulfilment of a nature, a tax or duty, a military detachment. It gives English teleology, television (far-off end) and the philosophical vocabulary of ends. It is often derived from PIE *kʷel-, “to turn”, with the sense of a turning point or completion, though the semantic step is debated.

The compound. Greek personal names of this shape — a qualifying first element plus a noun — are extremely common: Ἀριστόβουλος (“best counsel”), Ἀριστόδημος (“best of the people”), Ἀριστοφάνης (“best appearance”). Read literally, Ἀριστοτέλης means “best end” or “best purpose”; it may have been intended to express something like “of the best purpose” or “best at achieving the end”. The name was borne by other Greeks, including an Athenian politician of the late fifth century, so it carried no prophecy. The accent falls on the e of the second element (Ἀριστοτέλης), and Latin reproduces the word as Aristoteles, with the genitive Aristotelis.

From Greek to English. Latin Aristoteles passed into medieval learned use, and the vernaculars shortened it: Old French Aristote, Middle English Aristotle (Chaucer uses the form). English kept the Latin-derived spelling with -tle, while the Arabic tradition rendered the name as Arisṭūṭālīs, and the Hebrew and Syriac traditions produced their own forms. The name therefore travelled with the corpus, through Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Hebrew and Latin, before it settled into the modern European spellings.

The man behind the name. Aristotle was born at Stagira in 384 BCE, studied under Plato for two decades, and then pursued independent research in Asia Minor and Lesbos. His years as tutor to Alexander the Great (343–335) gave him a unique vantage on politics and power; his foundation of the Lyceum in 335 gave him a research school with a library, a collection of specimens and a programme of collaborative inquiry. The surviving works are lecture materials, edited and arranged centuries later — the traditional story of their burial and rediscovery, ending with Andronicus of Rhodes’s edition in the first century BCE, is now treated with caution, but the corpus that emerged became the foundation of Western philosophy and science.

The Rhetoric. Aristotle’s treatise is not a manual for courtroom victory but an inquiry into a human capacity. He defines rhetoric as the ability to see the available means of persuasion in any case, distinguishes the artistic proofs of ethos, pathos and logos from mere evidence, classifies speeches as deliberative, judicial and epideictic, and analyses the enthymeme as the form of argument proper to popular audiences. He also treats style, arrangement and the emotions in detail. The work was little read in the early Middle Ages but returned to prominence through Arabic philosophy and, in the Latin West, through translations and commentaries from the thirteenth century onward; George of Trebizond’s Latin version in the 1440s made it a humanist school text.

The Poetics. The short treatise on poetry, surviving in a mutilated form, supplied the Renaissance with its vocabulary of mimesis, catharsis, plot, character and probability. Lorenzo Valla’s Latin translation was printed in 1498, and sixteenth-century critics built on it the rules of dramatic construction. The name Aristotle thus became attached not only to logic and metaphysics but to the theory of literature.

Reputation. In the medieval universities Aristotle was “the Philosopher”, the master of those who know; in the Renaissance he was alternately the authority to be recovered and the schoolman to be mocked; in the seventeenth century his physics was displaced by Galileo and Newton, while his ethics and poetics survived. Modern philosophy has returned to him repeatedly — for virtue ethics, for the theory of action, for biology and for rhetoric — and the name still stands for systematic, observation-based inquiry across the whole range of knowledge. In Bryant’s sentence he is the philosophical foundation on which Cicero’s practice and Quintilian’s teaching rest.
