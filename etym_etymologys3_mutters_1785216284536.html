MUTTERS: the sound that became a word, and a word that became many A SOUND, NOT A MEANING The lineage of mutters reaches back to a sound, not a meaning: the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European imitative base *mut-, "to grunt, mutter," a sound-symbolic formation made with compressed lips — the very posture of mumbling. The root does not mean anything abstract; it imitates a universal human noise, the low, closed-mouth grumble that every human throat produces when it wants to be heard but not understood. Because the sound is physically given to every mouth, it reappears independently across the language families of Europe: Old Norse muðla, "to murmur," Old High German mutilon, "to murmur, mutter," and Latin muttire, "to mutter, mumble," are all parallel creations from the same expressive base rather than direct descendants of a single spoken form. This is the signature of onomatopoeia: the word is coined anew wherever people hear the same noise. ENGLAND AND THE FREQUENTATIVE In English the story begins in the early fourteenth century, when the verb moteren appears meaning "to mumble, to utter words in a low tone with compressed lips." It is a frequentative formation: the suffix -er gives the sense of repeated, habitual action — as in stammer and wander — so that moteren is literally "to go on muttering," a verb shaped for a habit rather than a single act. The modern spelling mutter is fixed by the end of the Middle English period, and the noun mutter, "a murmur," follows much later, in 1630. From the start the word carried a private, self-directed colour: to mutter is to speak without really speaking to anyone. ROME AND THE MOTTO Meanwhile the Latin branch of the same imitative base ran its own course, and it produced one of the stranger journeys in this family. Latin muttire, "to mutter, mumble," produced Late Latin muttum, "a grunt, a word" — the merest particle of speech, the minimum utterance a mouth can make. From muttum came Italian motto, "a word, a saying," and by way of Old French the English word motto (1580s) — the pithy saying that a family or a nation adopts as its watchword. The etymological chain is a perfect arc: from the closed-mouth grunt of a muttering man to the polished epigram engraved beneath a coat of arms, the word travelled from mumbling to eloquence. SUSPICION, SOLITUDE, AND THE MODERN SOUND The word's social history is as telling as its etymology. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries muttering gained a dark, political register: the mutterer was the grumbler and the potential plotter, the man whose half-heard words were suspected of sedition, and mutterings of discontent were the prelude to rebellion — a sense the political vocabulary still uses today when it speaks of "mutterings" in a party or a population. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word settled as the speech of solitude — old men and malcontents grumbling to themselves — and the low, self-directed mutter became a staple of the novelists' characterisation of eccentric or isolated figures. The sound itself, so far as English phonology reveals it, was always built on the labial m and the relaxed medial vowel and dental of the frequentative suffix: Middle English moteren, Modern English mutter, with the third-person mutters adding the -s that makes it the verb of habit and ongoing state. THE RIDDLE OF THE WIND In Gollum's riddle of the wind — "Voiceless it cries, Wingless flutters, Toothless bites, Mouthless mutters" — Tolkien uses the third-person present form mutters in its most ancient sense: a low, closed-mouth sound that is almost speech but not quite, exactly the sound a mouthless wind might make as it stirs through cracks and hollows, and exactly the sound the root *mut- was coined to imitate millennia ago. The word that began as a grunt made with compressed lips has come, through the mumble of malcontents and the murmur of the wind, to name the very edge of language — speech that is felt but not heard, the muttering of a creature, or a wind, that will not open its mouth. The derivation chain, from the earliest stage: PIE imitative base *mut- "to grunt, mutter" (sound-symbolic, compressed-lips; no date, prehistoric) → Middle English moteren "to mumble, utter words in a low tone" (early 14th c., frequentative -er) → English mutter (spelling fixed by end of Middle English; noun mutter 1630) → English mutters (third-person singular present). Parallel independent line: Latin muttire → Late Latin muttum → Italian motto → English motto (1580s).
