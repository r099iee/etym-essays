- Old English — ditch and bank in one: dic names both the dug trench and the bank thrown up beside it, for drains, boundaries, and fortifications alike.
- Viking Age — the northern ditch: Old Norse diki reinforces the word across the Danelaw, where field drains are still called dikes today.
- Medieval — draining the Fens: monastic and Dutch engineers cut dikes across the marshes, and the word becomes the signature of reclaimed lowland.
- 17th–18th c. — the Dutch mirror: visitors marvel at Holland, a country visibly kept dry by dikes, and the word takes on its heroic engineering flavour.
- Modern — flood defence: from the Fens to New Orleans, dike means the long earthen wall between water and dry land, breached at peril.
- Today — bank first, ditch second: standard English means the embankment, northern dialect keeps the ditch, and the lane-and-causeway landscape of the source sentence preserves both in one walk.
