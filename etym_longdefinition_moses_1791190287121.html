Moses is the central prophet, liberator, and lawgiver of the Hebrew Bible, traditionally held to have led the Israelites from slavery in Egypt through the Red Sea and desert wanderings to the edge of Canaan, receiving the Torah including the Ten Commandments on Mount Sinai. As a proper name it denotes that individual — Moshe in Hebrew, Mouses in Greek, Musa in Arabic — and by extension anyPP deliverer-like leader, as in the Moses of her people.

The rod of Moses, invoked in the source passage among wands, staves, and maces as symbols of power, refers to the wooden staff that becomes a serpent before Pharaoh, parts the sea, strikes rock for water, and wins battles when raised. The passage places it in a comparative anthropology of authority objects from fairy wand to consular cane, presenting Moses's rod as the Hebrew instance of a universal staff-as-power type.

  • Biblical core sense — prophet and mediator: son of Amram and Jochebed, adopted by Pharaoh's daughter, spokesman with brother Aaron, mediator of plagues, Passover, Sinai covenant, and forty years of guidance; Pentateuch traditionally called the Five Books of Moses.
  • Religious senses across traditions: in Judaism Moshe Rabbeinu, our teacher Moses, greatest prophet; in Christianity prefigurer of Christ and giver of Law versus Grace; in Islam Musa, messenger and interlocutor of God, honoured with Aaron/Harun; in Bahá'í a Manifestation; each retains liberator-lawgiver core with different theology.
  • Emblematic object sense — rod/staff of Moses: shepherd's staff turned wonder-working rod; symbol of delegated divine authority, not magic wand; paired with Aaron's rod that buds; art shows horned Moses after Vulgate mistranslation of radiant face.
  • Figurative and idiomatic senses: Mosaic law, Mosaic authorship, Mosaic distinction; to lead like Moses through wilderness; Holy Moses! and Oh Moses! as mild 19th-century exclamations; Moses basket for infants evoking the Nile ark of bulrushes.
  • Onomastic sense — given name: Moses, Moshe, Moïse, Moisés, Musa, Movses used in Jewish, Christian, and Muslim communities; connotes deliverance, humility, and law; borne by figures from Moses Mendelssohn to Moses Malone.
  • Literary and artistic reception: Philo's Life of Moses, Josephus, Dante, Milton, Schoenberg's Moses und Aron, DeMille's Ten Commandments, Freud's Moses and Monotheism positing Egyptian origin; each reframes him as philosopher-king, revolutionary, or repressed memory.
  • Historical-critical nuance: modern scholarship debates historicity, Egyptian setting in New Kingdom, and name's Egyptian versus Hebrew origin; none erases cultural meaning as founding narrative of law and liberation.

Whether taken as history, epic, or myth, Moses fuses liberation with legislation: he both draws out of water and draws out of bondage, then binds a people with law. Secondary exclamatory and basket senses derive by euphemism and by relic of infancy, not by separate roots. The rod belongs inseparably to the name — staff, serpent, sea-parting — which is why the passage lists rod of Moses or Aaron as the Hebrew entry in a world catalogue of power sticks.
