The biography of slighted is the story of a word that travelled from physical smoothness to moral smallness — from describing level ground to describing the wound of social neglect. It is one of the clearest case studies in the English language of semantic pejoration, the process by which a neutral word for something plain and flat gradually darkened into a word for something contemptible.

I. The PIE root: *sleyǵ- and the smooth-and-glide family

The story begins with the Proto-Indo-European root reconstructed as *sleyǵ-, "to be smooth, slick, slippery, to glide". Its daughters everywhere describe polished and gliding things: the level ground, the sleek hide, the slippery path. In the Germanic branch the root produced a family whose members English still uses daily: sleek (smooth and glossy, as of a cat's coat or an oily surface), slick (slippery, and then glibly smooth, a "slick salesman"), and — with a slightly different root grade — slip and slide, the verbs of gliding. Scholars differ slightly on the root's exact shape: some reconstruct *sleyǵ- with a final velar, others the simpler *slei-; the velar-extended form is the one generally credited to English slight, and the difference matters only to specialists, but it is worth noting because the whole family is a continuum of "smoothness" and "gliding".

II. Germanic *slihtaz and its three daughters

The Germanic branch turned the root into the adjective *slihtaz, "even, smooth, level, plain". From this single adjective three daughter-trajectories unfolded across the Germanic world. In Old Norse the word was sléttr, "smooth, even, level"; in Old English it was the native sliht, "smooth, level, plain"; and in Old High German it was sleht, "straight, even, plain". The Old Norse form is the one that matters for English: during the Viking Age and the Scandinavian settlement of northern England, Old Norse words flooded into English, and sléttr was among the borrowings. The German line is famous in its own right, because sleht — through a chain of pejoration from "straight, even" to "plain, ordinary" to "worthless" — became modern German schlecht, the ordinary word for "bad". The English word walked the same road but stopped one station short.

III. Middle English: from flat to small

In Middle English the borrowed word slight first meant "smooth, flat, level" — a purely physical word about surfaces and even ground. But between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries the adjective began to widen and to devalue. From "smooth, level, plain" it slipped to "plain, ordinary, unremarkable", and from "plain, ordinary" it slipped again to "small, slender, thin, of little weight or account". These steps are all visible in the record: a "slight" figure is a slender one; a "slight" reason is a weak one; a "slight" degree is a small one. The decisive step is the last: from "of little account" in size to "of little account" in worth. The metaphor is the ancient one of scale — weight and size as measures of importance — and the word moved steadily down the scale.

IV. The social turn: noun, verb, and participle in the sixteenth century

The sixteenth century made the step from physical smallness to social belittlement explicit. From the adjective's "of little account" sense, English created the noun a slight, "an act of neglect or disdain", and the verb to slight, "to treat with studied indifference, to treat as of small account". The new forms are exactly contemporaneous with the age of the great court dramas of intrigue, where honour and reputation were the highest stakes, and where the vocabulary of social estimation — slight, slighting, slighted — found a ready audience. From the verb came the past participle slighted, "treated as slight, rendered small": the state of a person who has been weighed and found beneath notice. It is a subtle and modern injury — an insult delivered not by blows or even by words but by the withholding of the attention that acknowledges worth.

V. The military detour: to slight a fortress

Alongside the social sense, the word kept a strikingly physical career in military engineering of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. To slight a fortress or a town meant to demolish or level it — to raze its walls so that it could no longer be defended, making it flat. The usage preserves the word's oldest stratum of meaning, "smooth, flat, level", transferred from describing even ground to creating it. The two senses — military and social — illuminate each other: to slight a fortress is to make it flat; to slight a person is, in the same image, to flatten them, to reduce them to ground level. The pejorative human sense is the physical one turned inward on society.

VI. Austen's world and the modern register

  • In Jane Austen's Regency England, where social estimation was the currency of worth, the word was in its element. In Pride and Prejudice the entire plot turns on the perception of slights — Elizabeth's feeling "slighted by his lack of attention", the studied neglects and withholdings by which rank measured rank.
  • Modern usage keeps the participle alive in literary and reflective registers: "she felt slighted", "slighted pride", "a slighted guest". The noun "a slight" and the verb "to slight" are its living siblings.
  • The word still carries its etymology on its sleeve: to be slighted is to be made slight — thin, weightless, small — which is exactly why the injury stings so quietly and so long.

VII. Sound changes and form

  • Old Norse sléttr (from *slihtaz) entered English in the Viking Age settlement period, giving Middle English slight with initial /sl-/.
  • The Middle English "gh" spelling reflects a fricative pronunciation now silent: slight lost its medial fricative in early modern pronunciation, rhyming with "light".
  • The past participle suffix "-ed" is regular; the pronunciation /ˈslaɪtɪd/ preserves the syllabic -ed of the historical verb inflection.
  • Cognates in the modern world: German schlecht "bad" (pejorated from "plain"), and the smooth-and-glide family sleek, slick, slip, slide.
