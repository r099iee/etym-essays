- [Middle English 'enemy-ship'] - In its current form since Middle English (from Old French enemi + -ty), 'enmity' named the state of being an enemy - mutual, settled hostility between persons or peoples.
- [Biblical and religious register] - In the Bible and religious prose the word carried heavy moral weight - the enmity between man and God, between seed and serpent - a word of lasting, even cosmic, opposition.
- [Political and historical use] - In 18th and 19th century historical prose 'enmity' compressed long national or factional hatreds into a single dignified abstraction - 'after ages of enmity' - grave and impersonal.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'enmity' is formal and literary for durable, mutual hostility, its everyday ground taken over by 'hostility', 'rivalry' and 'hatred', while its solemn gravity remains.
