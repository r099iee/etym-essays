Fair is in the source sentence a noun: a periodic public market for buying and selling — at Chertsey, in Cobbett’s ride, a Fair for horses, cattle and pigs, the Surrey livestock market filling the town with beasts, farmers, and dealers for the day. The capital letter is the eighteenth-century printer’s courtesy to a substantial noun, but the meaning is the ordinary English fair: trade made festive.

Core meaning in detail: A traditional fair is chartered or customary, recurrent (yearly, on a saint’s day or season), and mixed: business first — livestock judged, horses trotted out, pigs squealing in pens — with pleasure riding alongside in booths, shows, food, and drink. The Chertsey fair belonged to the farming calendar as surely as harvest: the day Surrey graziers met, prices were set by bellowing auction, and news travelled faster than any newspaper. Cobbett notes it in passing because such fairs were the economic pulse of every country town he rode through.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Periodic market for goods and livestock (primary noun sense, from 13th c., Cobbett’s sense): charter fairs, horse fairs, hiring fairs where labourers sought masters. The bridge is the Roman calendar: markets held on feast-days (feriae) when country people were free to gather.
  • Funfair — travelling amusement fair (secondary, 19th–20th c.): roundabouts, coconut shies, candy floss. The bridge is the sideshow swallowing the show: entertainments once attached to markets outlived the livestock and became the whole event.
  • Exhibition or exposition (modern): the world’s fair, the book fair, the science fair. The bridge carries the gathering idea indoors and upward: commerce and curiosity displayed under one roof, the prize-stock become prize-books.
  • Beautiful, pleasing to see (adjective, from Old English): a fair maiden, fair hair. This is a wholly different word — Germanic fæger, fitting and lovely — whose bridge runs from suitable to beautiful: what fits the eye pleases it.
  • Just, equitable, according to rules (adjective): a fair trial, fair play, fair game. The bridge extends fittingness to conduct: what fits the moral measure is just, and the sporting sense (fair vs foul) spread it worldwide.
  • Moderate, middling, clear (adjective): fair weather, a fair amount, fair copy. The bridge branches three ways: clear skies fit for travel, middling quality that fits modest praise, and a clean copy fit to keep.

Register and connotation: The noun fair smells of straw, dung, and fried onions — hearty, noisy, countrified — while the adjective is among the noblest in English, carrying beauty, justice, and clarity in one syllable. Cobbett’s Fair sits between: a working market he respects as honest rural commerce, unlike the tax-eaters’ boxes he despises. Modern usage keeps both worlds apart effortlessly: nobody confuses the cattle fair with the fair maiden, though history insists they share three letters by pure accident of convergence.
