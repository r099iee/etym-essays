- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French *damas* and Medieval Latin *damascus*, naming the sumptuous, self-patterned silk fabrics imported into Europe from the artisan workshops of Damascus.
- 15th to Early 16th Century - Spreads across Western Europe as the premier luxury fabric for aristocratic banquet napery (*damask linen*) and ecclesiastical vestments.
- Mid-16th Century (Tudor Era) - The *Damask rose* (*Rosa × damascena*) is formally introduced and catalogued in England; English poets (notably Shakespeare) adopt *damask* as a poetic color term for a blushing rose-pink complexion ("damask cheek").
- 17th to 18th Century - The term expands into metallurgy, identifying the wavy, watered pattern of high-carbon crucible steel (*Damascus steel*) and the artisan metalwork of inlaying gold in iron (*damascening*).
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Joseph Marie Jacquard's automated punch-card loom industrializes damask textile production, making damask tablecloths a universal staple of Victorian domestic respectability; featured prominently in Oscar Wilde's romantic garden descriptions.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as an international design standard in luxury home furnishings, fine dining linens, niche culinary confectionery, and high-end botanical perfumery.
