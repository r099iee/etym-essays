- 1400s: bulrish appears in English — the great coarse rush of the fens, companion of floors, thatch and basket-weaving.
- 1530s: Tyndale's Bible needs a word for the reeds of the Nile — his "ark of bulrushes" carries the infant Moses into English scripture.
- 1611: the King James Bible fixes the word forever: "an ark of bulrushes" (Exodus 2:3), "bow down his head as a bulrush" (Isaiah 58:5); the plural grows solemn and scriptural.
- 1700s–1800s: naturalists try to pin the name to one plant — club-rush or reedmace? — while poets and painters choose "Moses in the bulrushes" instead.
- 1900s: America renames it the cattail; Britain argues for reedmace; literature keeps "bulrushes" for Nile scenes — Wilde's riverhorse still couches among them.
- Today: the word is biblical, literary and ecological — restored wetlands are proudly replanted with bulrushes.
