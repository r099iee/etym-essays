The English word sock is really two words that happen to live in the same body, and their two biographies could hardly be more different: one is an ancient Mediterranean slipper two and a half thousand years old, the other is a piece of rough street slang born in the brawls of seventeenth-century England. The two must be told separately, because they share no origin at all. Chain A — the garment
The garment-sock begins in classical antiquity. The Greek word sýkkhos (σύκχος) named a light, soft shoe or slipper worn indoors, and it is already a word of unknown ancestry: Greek itself seems to have borrowed it, and no Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it — the most likely source is an eastern Mediterranean or Phoenician word of the kind that circulated among the trading cities of the ancient world. When Rome absorbed the Greek world, Latin borrowed the item and its name as soccus, a light, low-heeled slipper. In the Roman theatre the soccus was the distinctive footwear of the comic actor — the low, humble shoe of comedy — set against the high boot, the cothurnus, worn by actors of tragedy. From that theatrical pairing Latin literature built a permanent symbolism: "the sock" meant comedy, "the buskin" meant tragedy, and Roman writers spoke of putting on the sock or the buskin for writing or acting in each mode. The symbolism survived the centuries and still survives in the phrase "the sock and the buskin" for comedy and tragedy. From Latin the word travelled north with the Roman trade routes and the Christian missions. Old English socc (attested by the 8th–9th century) meant a slipper or light shoe, a clear early loan from Latin soccus. For the next five hundred years the word meant a light indoor shoe of leather or cloth. The great change came with the slow spread of knitting from the medieval world: as knitted foot-coverings replaced cut-and-sewn slippers, sock (Middle English sokke, socke) shifted its meaning from "slipper" to the modern knitted garment worn inside a shoe — the sense in "a pair of socks." This is a classic case of a word following its object through a technology change, keeping its name while its referent was transformed. By the 16th century the modern garment-sense was firmly in place, and today "sock" is one of the most ordinary words of daily life. Chain B — the verb "to hit"
The verb sock, "to hit or punch hard," has no connection to the garment. It appears in the record in the late 17th century — slang dictionaries of the 1690s record it as a word for striking hard — and its origin is genuinely unknown. Etymologists offer three theories. The first and most widely accepted is that the word is imitative: it was coined to sound like the heavy, dull thud of a fist landing on flesh, joining the English echoic family of strike-words (thump, whack, smack, bash, clout, wallop), each of which reproduces the sound of the blow it names. The second theory is a literal weapon: a stocking stuffed with coins or a stone — a "sock" in the old sense of a bag — was swung as an improvised blackjack in 17th-century street-fighting and maritime brawling, and the verb could have been born from that practice. The third is a vague appeal to some dialectal inheritance of obscure source. No theory is proven; the honest etymology is "origin unknown, probably imitative." What is certain is the semantic core: heavy, blunt, decisive impact, born in the rough speech of the English underworld and the boxing ring. How the verb grew
  • Late 17th c.: "to sock" = to punch hard, slang of the street; recorded in late-17th-century cant and slang collections.
  • 18th–19th c.: the verb and noun ("a sock" = a hard blow) become staple working-class and prizefighting vocabulary; "to sock it to (someone)" — to hit hard — becomes a fixed phrase.
  • 20th c.: the phrase "sock it to (someone)" is catapulted into global fame by Aretha Franklin's 1967 "Respect" (with its demand to "sock it to me") and the television show Rowan & Martin's Laugh-In; the phrase softens from a threat of violence into a vigorous exhortation to give one's all.
  • Metaphor: "to be socked by the news" — struck hard emotionally; "sock it away" — to save money, as if stashing a heavy bundle away.
  • Modern sense: "to sock" still means to punch forcefully, and "a sock" is a hard blow; the word remains informal, blunt, and heavy, with a thudding register perfectly matched to its sound.
The meeting of the two words
The garment and the verb now share a spelling and a pronunciation, and English speakers use both without noticing their separate histories: we put on a sock and we sock someone in the jaw, and the two actions could hardly be more different. The garment is a two-and-a-half-thousand-year-old Mediterranean inheritance whose origin is lost in the ancient east; the verb is a four-hundred-year-old English coinage born of the sound of a punch. In John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, George's threat — "do I have to sock you?" — belongs wholly to the second word, the blunt, heavy, colloquial blow of the common speech of the American West, a world away from the comic slipper of the Roman stage.
