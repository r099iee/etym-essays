- 1620s: borrowed into English as a learned Latinism — “to make furious, to enrage” — used in 17th-century sermons, histories and tragedies; the register is formal and dramatic, the anger is god-like and terrifying.
- 1660s: the adjective “infuriate” (= enraged) appears; both forms coexist in literary prose; public perception: a word for extraordinary, almost supernatural rage.
- 18th–19th centuries: moves out of the pulpit and into journalism and fiction; begins to be used hyperbolically for any serious annoyance; perception shifts from sacred/dramatic to secular and rhetorical.
- 20th–21st century: fully everyday word; “infuriating” and “infuriated” dominate; the adjective “infuriate” fades; perception now: a strong but ordinary verb for maximum anger — the Furies have faded into the background.
