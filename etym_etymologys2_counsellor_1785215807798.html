One chain leads from the PIE root for calling to the English noun; a debated link at the Latin stage is noted at the end. Chain — from “to call together” to COUNSELLOR: - PIE *kel(h₁)- “to call, to cry out, to summon” (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE; also the source of Greek kalein “to call”) → Latin calāre “to call together, to proclaim, to summon” (Classical Latin) → Latin concilium “an assembly, a calling-together of the people” (com- “together” + calāre; Classical Latin) → Latin consilium “deliberation, plan, advice; the body gathered to deliberate” (derivation debated, see note; Classical Latin) → Latin consiliārius “adviser, one who gives counsel” (Late Latin) → Old French / Anglo-Norman conseiller “adviser” (noun; also the verb “to advise”; 11th–13th c.) → Middle English counseiler / counsellour (13th–14th c.) → Modern English COUNSELLOR “a person who gives advice or guidance; a trusted adviser.” Note on debate: The traditional derivation treats consilium as a derivative of the same stem as concilium, “an assembly called together,” so that counsel is literally “the deliberation of those summoned.” Alternative scholarship derives consilium from the verb consulere “to consult, to take counsel,” which would give the word a different (though closely related) base. Both proposals cluster around the root sense of calling or summoning, and neither affects the attested Latin–French–English chain. The PIE root *kel(h₁)- is well attested in Greek (kalein) and Latin, though the exact laryngeal and vowel grades are reconstructed; no Germanic inheritance of the “counsel” sense is claimed (Old English ceallian “to call” is itself a late borrowing from Latin).
