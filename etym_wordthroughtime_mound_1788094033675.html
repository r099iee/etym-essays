- (c. 1500) - First recorded (Scots): "mound" = a hedge or fence — a boundary enclosure that protects land; a word of property and protection, not yet of hills.
- (1500s–1600s) - Defensive sense grows: a mound is an earthwork, a rampart raised around a fort or castle — the protective hedge becomes a protective bank of earth.
- (1600s–1700s) - Generalisation: any raised bank or heap of earth is a mound; burial mounds (barrows, tumuli) take the name, and the word begins to carry the grave's undertone.
- (1800s) - America: "Mound Builders" — 19th-century archaeology of the great Ohio and Mississippi earthworks makes the mound famous as a monument of vanished peoples; ant mounds and molehills join the everyday sense; "to mound" (the verb, to heap up) is recorded.
- (1900s) - Baseball fixes "pitcher's mound" (early 20th century) — the word gains its most familiar sporting home; "a mound of paperwork/work" extends the heap-image to abstract accumulations.
- (today) - Mound = hillock, heap, burial mound, earthwork, baseball mound; in fantasy (as in Tolkien's dragon-hoard) the treasure-mound keeps the barrow's chill — gold heaped like earth over ancient dead.
