- [17th century: drill-ground command] - enters English as the sharp command of German drill manuals; 'Halt!' rings with military authority and demands an instant, obedient, absolute stop, not a gradual slowing.
- [18th-19th century: march and machine] - the verb widens from ranks of soldiers to any ordered movement — an army halts, then a railway train halts — retaining the note of procedural, commanded cessation.
- [20th century: official and figurative uses] - 'halt' becomes the word of boards and bulletins: halting production, halting a campaign, halting the spread of disease; a clean, bureaucratic, slightly stern form of 'stop.'
- [Contemporary: abrupt deliberate stillness] - 'halted' today fuses suddenness with self-command: the body snaps from motion to stillness in a single instant, by choice rather than by paralysis — which is why it suits a fugitive freezing deliberately against a tunnel-wall.
