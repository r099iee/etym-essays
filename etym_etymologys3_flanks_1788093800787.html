1. The Germanic root. "Flank" enters English from French, but its taproot is Germanic. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic *hlankō "side, flank", with the adjective *hlankaz "bent, pliant", belongs to the root *kleng- / *kel-, "to bend, turn" — the side of the body is literally the bending part, the place where the trunk flexes. The same family gives Old English hlanc "loose, slender" → modern "lank", and Old Norse hlekkr "ring, link" → English "link" (a bent ring): three common English words — flank, lank, link — from one picture of curving. Handbooks differ on the exact PIE shape of the root (Pokorny gives *kel- "to bend" with extensions), so the top stage is presented here with that caution stated rather than with false precision.

2. Through Frankish into French. The immediate donor is Frankish *hlanka, the speech of the Franks in Gaul, mirrored by Old High German hlanca "hip, flank, loin" and Middle Dutch lanke. The Frankish word passed into Old French as flanc (recorded from the 11th century), with the Germanic initial cluster hl- adapted as fl-; the same donor produced Italian fianco (through the Langobards), a useful comparative — French flanc and Italian fianco are siblings from one Germanic word, each restructuring the cluster in its own way. Because no Frankish documents survive, the Frankish form is reconstructed, but the Germanic cognates make the reconstruction secure; the honest account says so plainly rather than pretending to a manuscript.

3. Anatomical Middle English. English takes flanc / flanke in the later 14th century, at first purely anatomical: the fleshy side of man or beast, the horse's heaving side, the hunter's aim-point. The word arrived as a courtly-French loan, at home in romance and in the natural-history writing of the age; 15th-century prose uses "flankes" for horses and knights alike, and Middle English spelling wobbles (flank, flanc, flaunke) before "flank" settles. The plural "flanks" — the pair of sides — is already the dominant form in these early body-texts, exactly as in our context sentence six centuries later.

4. The military turn. The 16th century converts the word. As gunpowder armies and drill manuals reshape war, the flank of a formation — its side — becomes a tactical obsession: flank attacks, flanking marches, flanking fire, flanking bastions whose guns enfilade the walls. Elizabethan military prose is full of the word; the verb "to flank" (to guard a side, or to pass round and strike the side) is in use by the 1590s; "flanker" follows, for the soldier or the protruding fortification posted on a flank. From the 17th century the flank march is the standard grammar of European battle — the oblique order of Marlborough and Frederick, Napoleon's turning movements all live in this vocabulary, and the word "flank" is their hinge: the side that must be protected, and sought out, at all costs.

5. Generalisation and metaphor. From the side of an army, "flank" spreads to every lateral surface: the flank of a mountain, a glacier, a building, a column of men (18th–19th centuries). The butcher's "flank steak" and the zoologist's "flank" (a standard measurement point) keep the body sense alive; rugby's "flanker" (1880s) brings the military word into sport as the forward who guards the scrum's sides; the US Navy's "flank speed" (20th century) pushes the word to its extreme — the last, highest notch of the engine-order telegraph. Meanwhile the figurative uses flourish: "the flanks of an argument", "covering one's flanks", "flanked by" (with something on either side) — and in fantasy and heroic narrative the word keeps its military edge, as in Tolkien's "His flanks were unguarded": the exposed sides of a great beast, at once anatomy and tactics, waiting to be turned.
