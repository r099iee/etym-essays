crumpled: (1) crushed, pressed, or squeezed into an irregular mass of creases, folds, and wrinkles, said especially of paper, cloth, clothing, and other flexible materials; (2) figurative, collapsed, crushed, or giving way under pressure, said of a person's spirits, composure, or body. Sense in this literary context. In the passage "There was a handkerchief in the pocket, all crumpled and bundled up, so that Rover was not very deep down..." the word describes a pocket handkerchief that has been squashed and stuffed into the pocket in a loose, messy, creased mass rather than folded flat and neat. The word teams naturally with "bundled up": the handkerchief has lost its smooth flatness and now bulks irregularly, full of deep creases and overlapping folds — and this very crumpledness is what keeps the hidden dog near the surface, where he can poke out his nose and sniff. The tone is comic and practical: a crumpled handkerchief is everyday, unglamorous, and delightfully useful to the story's plot. Primary sense — crushed into creases and wrinkles. At its core, crumpled describes the state of a flexible material that has been compressed, squeezed, or crushed so that its surface is no longer flat or smooth but covered with random creases, wrinkles, and folds. It applies to paper thrown in a bin, a shirt worn and slept in, a letter crushed in a fist, wrapping paper bundled into a ball. The key idea is loss of smoothness through crushing: a crumpled thing has been disarranged by force, usually careless or hasty, and it shows the disorder of that force in its surface. Secondary figurative sense — to collapse or give way. By metaphor, crumple (the verb) means to collapse or give way suddenly under pressure: "her face crumpled with tears," "he crumpled to the floor," "his confidence crumpled." Here the image is of a rigid, upright thing suddenly folding in on itself, exactly as a sheet of paper loses its stiffness and buckles when crushed. The adjective crumpled in this figurative register describes a person or expression that has lost its composure and folded into distress or defeat. Technical and jargon senses:
  • Crumple zone (automotive engineering, from the 1950s): the deliberately engineered sections at the front and rear of a car designed to collapse and absorb impact energy in a crash, protecting the passenger cell. The noun phrase is the most famous technical descendant of the verb.
  • Crumple / buckling (engineering and materials science): the local folding or wrinkling of a structure under compression before it fails; crumpling is a mode of failure in thin shells, sheets, and tubes.
  • Crumple in textiles: the deliberate creasing of fabric, and the "wrinkle-resistance" treatments designed to prevent crumpling.
  • Crumple in the paper arts: the intentional crushing of paper for texture or collage.
Full polysemy map (all senses):
  • Crushed, pressed, or squeezed into creases and wrinkles (paper, cloth, clothing) — primary modern sense.
  • Verb: to crush or press into such a state ("crumple the paper").
  • Verb figurative: to collapse or give way suddenly ("he crumpled under the blow"; "her face crumpled").
  • Adjective figurative: (of composure, hopes, spirits) broken down, crushed, defeated.
  • Noun (rare): a crease or fold made by crushing ("the crumples in the paper").
  • Automotive: crumple zone — engineered collapse of a car body to absorb crash energy.
  • Historical: bent, crooked, or curled (the old sense of the base word, surviving in dialect).
The semantic bridge. The whole family descends from the Old English idea of "crooked, bent, curled" — crump meant bent, crooked, or twisted. The verb crumplen added the frequentative suffix to make "to bend and press into folds," and from "bent into folds" came "wrinkled and crushed," and from that the figurative "bent and broken by pressure." The modern meaning "crushed into creases" is thus a specialization of the ancient idea of bending, and the figurative "collapse under pressure" is simply the same bending applied to a person instead of a sheet of paper. Register, collocation, and confusables.
  • "Crumpled paper / a crumpled shirt / crumpled sheets" — physical, standard, neutral.
  • "He crumpled to the ground / her face crumpled" — figurative, dramatic.
  • "Crumple zone" — automotive jargon, from the 1950s safety era.
  • Cognates and confusables: crump (dialect, bent; also a heavy thud), crimp (to pinch into parallel ridges), crinkle (to form many small wavy folds), rumple (to crease, especially clothing).
In the test sentence crumpled is used in its primary physical sense, and the accidental disorder of the handkerchief is made to drive the comedy of the plot.
