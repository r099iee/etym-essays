Bated means "beaten down, lessened, restrained," and in modern English it survives almost exclusively in the frozen phrase bated breath — a state of intense anticipation, fear, or reverence in which one's breathing is literally held down and reduced. The literary context is David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone: "'I've missed the arrival of our guest.' 'Everyone else around here seems to go about with bated breath,' thought Dai-yu." Dai-yu's private observation is quiet and inward, and the idiom does subtle work. The household is not gasping with excitement but moving watchfully, breath restrained by nervous formality — everyone fears to offend the elders, everyone steps carefully, and the "beaten-down" breath becomes the physiological signature of a world in which every impulse is policed. The irony is pointed: the residents "go about" — move freely through the house — yet do so with their breathing subdued, as if even air must be rationed under the family's discipline.

Linguistically, bated is the aphetic (initial-clipping) form of abated — the past participle of the verb abate ("to lessen, diminish, hold in"), which descends from Old French abatre ("to beat down, fell"). The word is therefore a fossil: the a- was dropped in ordinary speech (in the same way that mend was clipped from amend and sport from disport), leaving bated as a shortened adjective that has lost its productive connection to the parent verb. The senses and uses to be recorded are these:

  • The frozen idiom "bated breath" (almost always "with bated breath"): breath held, reduced, or subdued through suspense, fear, reverence, or eager waiting — "we waited with bated breath for the announcement." This is the sense in which the word lives today, and its fixed pair with "breath" is so strong that the word is rarely used with any other noun.
  • Standalone adjectival / participial sense (rare, archaic): "abated, lessened, restrained, shortened" — a thing that has been beaten down or reduced; when used at all apart from "breath," the word is consciously archaic or poetic.
  • The historical verb "bate" (16th–17th c., now archaic): "to lessen, hold in, abate," the clipped form of "abate," as in "bate me some" or "bated (i.e. reduced) in price"; this is the verb whose participle the modern adjective is.
  • Homographic hazard — falconry "bate": a hawk "bates" when it beats or flutters its wings against the leash; this is a different word, from Old French batre "to beat" (same ultimate root), and is not the source of "bated breath."
  • Spelling caution: "bated" is frequently misspelled "baited" (as though the breath were baited like a hook); the correct form keeps the sense of "abated" — beaten down — not of baiting.
  • Dialectal "bate" (a mood): in British dialect, "in a bate" means in a rage or temper; the origin is uncertain (possibly connected with baiting or with abate), and it is a separate word that merely shares the spelling.

The synonym boundaries are delicate. Restrained is the general adjective of control, but it lacks bated's violent hidden image of something struck down. Held (as in "held one's breath") describes a voluntary action; bated describes a state of being beaten down, not chosen but suffered. Suppressed is clinical and psychological, where bated is poetic and nearly idiom-bound. Subdued belongs to spirits, colours, and sounds, not to breath. Hushed applies to voices and rooms — a place is hushed — while bated applies to the breath itself. Abated, the un-clipped source word, means "lessened" (a storm abates) and is the living verb, whereas bated is the shortened, fossilized remnant used only before "breath." And stilled implies cessation, while bated implies reduction without stopping: the breathing continues, but subdued.
