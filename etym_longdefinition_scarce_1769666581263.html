scarce (adjective; also adverb, archaic): insufficient for the demand; present in such small numbers or amounts as to be hard to find, rare, limited, or in short supply.

Primary Sense

The core sense of scarce is quantitative deficit: a resource, commodity, or phenomenon exists in smaller quantity than is needed, wanted, or expected. When goods are scarce, they are difficult or impossible to obtain because supply falls short of demand. This is the sense in "scarce food," "scarce water in a drought," or "scarce jobs in a depression." The word always measures a thing against a demand, need, or expectation — scarcity is relational, not merely statistical.

Secondary & Extended Senses

  • Adverbial "scarce" (archaic/literary) = scarcely, barely, hardly: In older and poetic English, scarce functions as an adverb meaning "barely, only just," as in "I scarce believed my eyes." This survives in modern scarcely, and the two forms long overlapped; the sense developed from "in a stinted, short measure" to "by a narrow margin."
  • Idiom — "make oneself scarce": To leave quickly or go into hiding, literally to make oneself "absent" or "in short supply." The phrase (recorded from the 19th century) converts the quality of things into a deliberate act of a person; in the C.S. Lewis passage in this card, the speaker removes himself from sight to avoid interrogation in Miraz’s torture chamber.
  • Idiom — "as scarce as hen’s teeth": A folksy intensifier meaning extremely rare or nonexistent, since hens have no teeth; it reinforces the rarity pole of the word.
  • Economics — the scarcity principle: In modern economics, scarcity is the fundamental problem that wants are unlimited while resources are limited; scarce goods command a price, and economics is sometimes defined as the science of allocating scarce resources. The related "scarcity mindset" (psychology/business jargon) describes a state of mind obsessed with lack, opposed to an "abundance mindset."
  • Ecology & conservation: "Scarce species," "scarce water resources," and "scarce habitat" name organisms or resources under threat of depletion — a technical register that has grown enormously since the 20th century.
  • Obsolete/archaic — frugal or stingy (of persons): Old French escars meant both "rare" and "stingy, parsimonious," applied to people who doled out sparingly. This personal sense faded in English but survives in the related noun "scarcity" applied to sparingness.

Semantic Reasoning & Historical Bridge

The word descends from the Latin verb carpere, "to pluck, pick, crop, gather" — the gesture of harvesting fruit or shearing a field. With the prefix ex- ("out"), Latin formed excerpere, "to pick out," whose Vulgar Latin participle *excarpsus meant "plucked out, stripped bare." A field that has been picked over yields only scattered remnants: the mental image of a plucked-bare, stripped field underlies every later meaning. Old French escars applied this to "rare, scanty, frugal," and Middle English scars generalized it to any supply falling short of demand. The adverbial "barely" is the same image applied to measure itself: a quantity so thin it barely suffices. The 19th-century idiom "make oneself scarce" completes the arc by applying the scarcity of objects to the disappearance of a person.

Register & Usage Notes

  • Predicative and attributive: "supplies are scarce"; "scarce resources."
  • Generally more formal/literary than plain "rare" when applied to objects; extremely common in economic and environmental writing.
  • Almost always carries the meaning of insufficiency relative to need; do not confuse with scared ("afraid"), a frequent spelling confusion (the card’s mnemonic plays on the near-homophony).
