ascesis (noun, plural asceses; pronounced ah-SEE-sis) — Deliberate self-discipline, abstention, and training of the self, undertaken as an exercise: the systematic practice by which body, soul, or artistic skill is hardened and refined. The word is the noun behind the more familiar asceticism: while asceticism names the doctrine or way of life, ascesis names the actual discipline — the regimen itself.

Primary sense — training and exercise (Greek inheritance). Greek áskēsis meant literally "exercise, training", in the gymnasium above all: diet, exertion, and endurance by which athletes formed their bodies and competitors their skill. From athletic training the sense broadened to any methodical self-formation: the orator’s practice, the philosopher’s habituation in virtue, the soldier’s drill. The core idea is never mere denial for its own sake but formation through discipline — the point is the strength built, not the pain.

Sense 2 — ascetic self-denial (Stoic, Cynic, then Christian). The Stoics and Cynics turned askēsis toward the soul: exercises in endurance, poverty, and self-command that free the mind from craving. Christian writers inherited the term: the desert fathers’ fasting, vigil, and celibacy were an ascesis — spiritual athleticism conquering the passions. In the reading context Pater invokes exactly this colour: the "charm of ascesis, of the austere and serious girding of the loins in youth" — the bracing moral athlete’s harness of early effort, set against the sweetness of late decadence.

Ascesis and the critique of culture. Modern philosophy has found the word indispensable. Nietzsche’s "ascetic ideal" describes how morality itself, as a discipline of denial, became the West’s strangest exercise of power; Weber’s Protestant ethic analyses worldly ascesis — methodical work and thrift — as the engine of capitalism; Foucault recovers askēsis as "care of the self", the ancient technology by which a person constitutes freedom rather than obedience. In each case the term’s athletic root does the work: ascesis is not punishment imposed but strength built, a training in which the agent is both athlete and coach.

Distinctions within the family.Asceticism names the system or world-view; ascesis names the practice itself; austerity names the flavour of that practice in style or life; self-denial stresses refusal rather than goal. In criticism, the "ascesis of the medium" denotes the artist’s deliberate refusal of easy means — the discipline by which material is mastered into form, the very discipline Pater hears in the earnestness of early Renaissance art.

  • In philosophy — Foucault’s late work rehabilitates askēsis as the ancient "practice of the self": freedom achieved by self-formation rather than repressive rule.
  • In literary and art criticism — ascesis describes the artist’s rigorous self-restraint: discipline of medium, renunciation of easy effects (Pater, Wilde, later the aesthetics of the moderns).
  • In religious studies — the technical term for the regimen of ascetics (fasts, vigils, vows), as distinct from the theology of asceticism.
  • In rhetoric — the classical askēsis of speech: the set exercises of the oratorical schools.

Register and scale.Ascesis is a learned borrowing, confined to philosophy, theology, and criticism; in general prose asceticism or self-discipline replaces it. It is prized precisely for precision: one word that distinguishes the practised regimen from the doctrine, and that carries the Greek gymnasium inside Christian austerity.
