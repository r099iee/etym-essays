In the little novice’s backstory, the rigours of monastic life are the harsh, unyielding demands—strict rule, hard labour, cold comforts, and spiritual discipline—that have exhausted his patience. The plural is important: not a single severity but a whole regime of hardnesses that accumulate until escape into a “gentlemanly” yamen post seems irresistible. The tone is physical and moral at once: rigours suggests both bodily strain (deprivation, fatigue) and the inflexible character of the Rule. Within the narrative the word justifies the novice’s worldly career-change without quite endorsing it; it invites sympathy for youth worn thin by discipline, while still naming monastic life as something objectively severe. Core meaning. Rigour is the quality of being extremely thorough, exhaustive, strict, or severe—intellectual exactness in methods and reasoning, or harshness and inflexibility in conditions and demands. The plural rigours is a specialized and very idiomatic form: it means the harsh, demanding aspects of a situation, way of life, or season, considered as a collection of hardships to be endured (the rigours of winter, of a campaign, of a monastery). Etymological and conceptual bridge. The word descends from Latin rigor “stiffness, numbness, hardness,” from rigēre “to be stiff,” ultimately from a PIE root *reig- / *reik- meaning “to stretch, be stiff.” The bridge runs from physical stiffness to moral and intellectual unbendingness: what is stiff will not bend, so a rigid rule is one that will not yield, and a rigorous method is one that does not cut corners. The plural rigours keeps the oldest physical flavour—the “hardness” of conditions—while the singular rigour has specialized toward exactness and thoroughness (scientific rigour, academic rigour). Polysemy and secondary senses (exhaustive):
  • Severity / harshness sense: extreme strictness, harshness, or severity of a law, discipline, climate, or situation; “the rigour of the law.”
  • Exactness / thoroughness sense (modern dominant in the singular): strict accuracy, exhaustiveness, and methodological discipline; “scientific rigour,” “academic rigour.”
  • Plural “rigours” sense: the harsh, demanding aspects of a way of life, season, or ordeal, endured collectively; “the rigours of monastic life,” “the rigours of winter.”
  • Physical / medical sense: stiffness, numbness, or rigidity of the body; Latin rigor mortis “stiffness of death” survives untranslated in English; rigor (uncountable, chiefly US spelling) names the chill and shivering that precede a fever.
  • Historical sense of austerity: hard, severe discipline, especially religious or military; close to austerity but imposed rather than chosen.
  • Technical / scholarly sense: in logic and mathematics, the demand for complete, exception-free proof and reasoning; “mathematical rigour.”
  • Spelling split: rigor (no -u) is the American spelling of the singular, while rigour / rigours is British; rigor mortis is spelled identically everywhere.
Register and collocations. Learned, formal, and literary. Common collocations: the rigours of winter / of the climate / of a campaign / of monastic life / of a journey; scientific / methodological / intellectual rigour; the full rigour of the law; rigor mortis (fixed phrase). In this passage the plural does the work of a compound noun—a pile-up of hardnesses—and the similarWords list (hardships, severities, austerities, strictness, stringency, rigidity) maps the boundaries of that force.
