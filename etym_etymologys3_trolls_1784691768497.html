The biography of trolls runs from the pre-Christian wilds of Scandinavia to the darkest corners of the modern internet, and it involves two separate words that have collided and cross-pollinated. The noun that names the folkloric being and the verb that names online provocation are not one lineage, but they have grown so entangled in the last thirty years that no honest account of the word can keep them entirely apart.

The folkloric being. The noun enters English from Old Norse troll, a word already old when the sagas were written down in the thirteenth century. In the Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson, and in the saga literature of Iceland, troll names a being of the wild — sometimes a giant (the jǫtunn and the troll are near neighbours in the sources), sometimes a magical, often female being who can ride the winds, work spells, and eat men. The word's deeper origin is debated. The most common reconstruction is a Proto-Germanic *trullan-, which would make it a native Germanic word; some scholars connect it to a Germanic root for "to walk, tread, wander" (the troll as the loping, roaming being of the mountains), others to the sense "to charm, bewitch" (Swedish trolla, "to do magic"), and still others to a root meaning "to rage or devour". No single PIE etymon commands assent, and the OED frankly labels the origin uncertain — an honest etymology must stop at the reconstructed Germanic stage and say so rather than inventing a neat Indo-European ancestor.

From saga to English. English met the troll twice. In the early seventeenth century, as antiquarians and translators brought Norse and Danish learning into English — translations of Saxo Grammaticus, then of the sagas — the word was borrowed as the name of the Scandinavian giant; the first recorded uses, around 1616, are learned and exotic, describing the beings of the north. The nineteenth-century folklore revival did the rest: Asbjørnsen and Moe's collection of Norwegian folktales, translated into English, fixed the folkloric troll — the hill-dweller, the bridge-warden, the being that cannot abide the sun and turns to stone at dawn — in the English-speaking imagination. It was this Victorian troll, half giant and half goblin, that Tolkien found when he wrote The Hobbit (1937) and set three bickering, Cockney-voiced specimens round their campfire. Tolkien's trolls are comic-menacing: they threaten to eat the dwarves, but they squabble over cooking, speak in rustic slang, and are outwitted into the sunrise by Gandalf — "Dawn take you all", and stone they become. In doing so Tolkien did for the troll what he did for the goblin and the dragon: he took a creature of folklore and gave it a recognisable, quotable personality.

  • Sound shifts. Within Germanic the form is stable: Old Norse troll (with the cluster -rl-) → English troll, with only the vowel adjusting to English norms; the plural trolls is a regular English inflection. There is no dramatic sound-change history because the word was borrowed, not inherited, in the seventeenth century — and that itself is the point: troll is a loanword, and its shape in English is the shape it had when it crossed the North Sea in print.
  • Semantic drift 1 — folklore to insult. From the folkloric being came a transferred human sense: a "troll" is a clumsy, stupid, lumpish person, recorded in the 19th century — the creature's slowness and ugliness projected onto people. This is the step that made the word portable: once a human could be a troll, the noun was free to move into any context where something big, ugly, and annoying lurked.
  • Semantic drift 2 — the internet. Around 1990, on early bulletin boards and Usenet, "troll" acquired its second life. The verb "to troll" already existed in English as a fishing term — to draw a baited line or lure slowly through the water, from the unrelated Old French troller, "to hunt in a wandering fashion" — and the image of trailing bait in hope of a strike is exactly what online provocation does: the troll casts an inflammatory post and waits for the bites of the angry. But the folkloric noun is also part of the picture: the troll is the thing lurking under the bridge of the discussion, waiting for travellers. Both images fused in the 1990s, and "troll" and "trolling" became the standard terms for posting to provoke, outrage, or derail.
  • Register today. In ordinary English, "troll" now most often means the online provocateur; the folkloric giant survives in fantasy, gaming, and mythology, where it has also hardened into a familiar type — the regenerating brute of role-playing games. The two senses coexist uneasily: the same word names a mountain giant and a keyboard coward, and the troll-bridge of the folktale has become the troll-thread of the internet.
  • Literary attestations. Key datapoints of the word's journey: the thirteenth-century Eddas and sagas; the 1616-plus English borrowings in antiquarian writing; the Victorian translations of Asbjørnsen and Moe that fixed the sun-stone folk belief in English; Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), whose trolls "But they were trolls" became one of the most quoted sentences in modern fantasy; the stone-trolls of The Lord of the Rings (1954–55); and the Usenet and BBS culture of the 1990s that produced the modern verb. Each of these is a distinct layer of the word, and the modern word carries all of them at once.
