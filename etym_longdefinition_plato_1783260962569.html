Plato (proper noun) — The classical Athenian philosopher Plato (c. 428–348 BC), pupil of Socrates, teacher of Aristotle, and founder of the Academy, whose surviving dialogues laid the foundations of Western metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, and political philosophy. The name is at once a historical person, a philosophical movement, and a pervasive cultural reference. Primary historical senses:
  • The philosopher: Plato, born Aristocles son of Ariston, of an aristocratic Athenian family claiming descent from the early kings of Athens, was Socrates' most famous student and the author of some thirty philosophical dialogues, including the Republic, the Symposium, the Phaedo, the Timaeus, and the Apology. After Socrates' execution in 399 BC he travelled, then founded the Academy in Athens around 387 BC, the first institution of higher learning in the Western world, which operated for nearly nine hundred years until closed by Justinian in 529 AD.
  • The founder of a school: Plato's Academy and his doctrine of the Forms (the eternal, immaterial archetypes of which the visible world is an imperfect copy) defined the Platonist tradition; his followers are Platonists, and the systematic body of thought is Platonism.
  • The name itself: Plato is a nickname. According to the ancient tradition preserved by Diogenes Laërtius, he was called Platon ("broad") by his wrestling coach Ariston of Argos because of his broad build — usually said to be his broad shoulders or chest, sometimes his broad forehead. His birth name Aristocles was eclipsed, and posterity knows him only by the gymnasium nickname.
Secondary and transferred meanings:
  • "Platonic" love: The adjective platonic has become a common word for a non-sexual, spiritual, or purely intellectual affection between persons, from Plato's account in the Symposium of love (eros) rising from the particular beloved toward the Form of Beauty. The modern phrase "just platonic" is a late development, but it is the most frequent everyday use of the philosopher's name.
  • Platonism as a doctrine: In philosophy, Platonism names the view that abstract objects, including the Forms, exist independently of the mind and of the material world; the mathematician's Platonism (numbers exist "out there") is a modern technical borrowing of the term.
  • Metonym for idealism: In general intellectual history, "Plato" stands as the archetype of idealist, transcendental, world-transcending philosophy, in permanent contrast with the empiricism of his pupil Aristotle — a pairing summarised in Whitehead's famous remark that the European philosophical tradition "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato."
  • Eponyms and institutions: The name survives in Platonist and Neoplatonist, in the Platonists of Renaissance Florence, in the adjective Platonic of ideal love and ideal forms, and in countless institutional names (Plato societies, the "Plato" of educational theory), where it simply means philosophy's founding teacher.
Semantic bridge: From the Greek adjective platýs "broad, flat" (from the Indo-European root meaning "to spread"), the nickname Platon "the Broad" — a physical joke about a wrestler's build — was borne by one philosopher and became the foundation stone of Western metaphysics; the same root reappears in English flat, in the plate of "plateau," and in the plateia (broad street) that became the town square of Europe. In the source text: Macaulay names Plato as the third of his triad of Greek masterpieces — with Sophocles and Demosthenes — still being read and interpreted in the decaying Byzantine world by "laborious pedants, themselves destitute of taste, sense, and spirit." The name here stands for the summit of Greek prose philosophy, preserved but no longer understood, in the eastern empire whose court still exhibited the splendour of Diocletian and Constantine. Register and collocation: Plato is a formal proper noun of philosophy and history, invariable in the singular. It collocates with the dialogues of Plato, the Republic, Platonic love, the Platonic tradition, and the phrase "the student of Socrates"; the adjective Platonic (capitalised in strict usage) has long since become an ordinary English adjective, while Platonism and Platonist denote the school and its followers.
