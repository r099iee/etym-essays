Derivation chain for DRATTING (with the verb DRAT): Stage 1 — Old English rotian 'to decay, putrefy, rot' (recorded from the 10th century), from Proto-Germanic *rutēn / *rutjan 'to rot, decay' (reconstructed; cognates: Old Saxon roton, Old Frisian rotia, Middle Dutch roten, Old Norse rotna). No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon has been established for the Germanic root; the deeper connection is unknown, and this is stated explicitly rather than guessed. Stage 2 — Modern English rot (verb) 'to decompose, decay' — the continuing form of rotian. Stage 3 — The genuine imprecation "God rot (him / it / them)!" — a curse wishing decay and ruin upon its object, current in the 17th century. Stage 4 — The euphemistic, minced-oath reduction "'d rot" (with the divine name masked, sometimes written od rot), then to the monosyllable drat (interjection and verb) 'a mild exclamation of annoyance or vexation', first recorded c. 1815. Stage 5 — The derived participial adjective dratted 'confounded, annoying' (19th century). Stage 6 — Modern English dratting — present participle and gerund of drat (recorded in Tolkien's The Hobbit, 1937). Final node: DRATTING. Note on debate: the derivation from "God rot" is the standard and accepted account, but the intermediate shape is not directly attested; some handbooks prefer an intermediate "od rot" (od being a masked form of God), while others see the elision as proceeding through "'d rot". Either way the ultimate source is the same imprecation, reduced to avoid blasphemy.
