lances is the plural noun and third-person verb form of lance. As a noun it most centrally means a long thrusting spear: a wooden shaft 2–4 metres long tipped with a sharp steel head, used couched under the arm by mounted soldiers in charging, or on foot for thrusting, fishing, and whaling. In the source sentence African villages make boats or lances — i.e. manufacture spears as a specialized craft alongside arrow-heads, pottery and smithing.

The noun family is wide and historically layered:

  • Cavalry lance: The knightly and later uhlan weapon for shock action, distinct from javelin (thrown) and pike (longer infantry). Example: Napoleon's Polish lancers. Connotes chivalry, heraldry, and parade: lances with pennons.
  • Generic spear / fishing spear: Any sharp thrusting implement: whaling lance, salmon lance, African assegai glossed as lance in travel writing. This is the ethnographic sense in the prompt.
  • Surgical lancet / lance: A small double-edged knife for opening boils, veins, and abscesses. To lance a boil is to incise and drain. The instrument is properly a lancet, but lance is used elliptically. Medical register, neutral-technical.
  • Industrial / military extensions: Oxygen lance (consumable pipe jetting oxygen to cut steel), sand-lance (small eel-like fish named by shape resemblance), and Lance missile (U.S. tactical rocket). These are shape or name transfers, capitalized when proper.

As a verb, lances means he/she/it pierces with a lance or lancet: she lances the boil; the pain lances through him (shooting pain metaphor), or he lances forward (darts). Past lanced, participle lancing (lancing pain = stabbing).

Polysemy bridges: weapon → tool by shape similarity (spearhead to blade); tool → action (to lance = to cut open); action → sensation (lancing pain). The cavalry-to-surgery bridge passes through Old French diminutive lancette: a little lance for the body. The chivalric idiom break a lance (to contend, to joust verbally) and free-lance (originally a mercenary knight offering his lance for hire, now an independent worker) show how deeply the weapon permeates metaphor: freelancers sell their spear-skills without a lord.

Register contrasts with synonyms: spear is generic and prehistoric; lance is medieval, mounted, and honour-laden; pike is infantry mass; javelin is thrown; harpoon is barbed for sea mammals. Choosing lances over spears evokes horsemen, tournaments, and heraldic romance even when describing African ironwork.

In contemporary usage the choice between lances and spears is stylistic and historical: museum labels reserve lance for the long couched cavalry arm with vamplate and pennon, while field reports gloss any thrusting spear as a lance for English readers. Collectors distinguish jousting lances (blunt coronels), war lances (sharp), and boar lances with crossbars. Fencing and equestrian sports preserve the lineage in foil play and tent-pegging, where riders still lance rings and pegs at speed.
