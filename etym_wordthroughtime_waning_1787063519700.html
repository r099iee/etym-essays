- OE (8th–11th c.) — waniende, participle of wanian "to diminish"; used of the moon's phases in monastic calendars and of tides and light in prose and verse; wholly neutral and descriptive
- ME (12th–15th c.) — "waning" becomes the standard term for the moon between full and new; appears in almanacs, church computus, and popular lore as a time of receding and withdrawal
- 16th–17th c. — the participle is pressed into figurative service: waning fortunes, waning years, waning love; literary and rhetorical, often melancholy, used by Shakespeare and the Elizabethan poets
- 18th–19th c. — formal and scientific: astronomy fixes waxing/waning crescent and gibbous as technical phases; geology and medicine adopt the participle for retreating glaciers and failing strength
- 20th–21st c. — idiomatic "on the wane"; modern collocations of decline (waning influence, waning interest, waning powers); epidemiology coins "waning immunity" during the COVID-19 pandemic, returning the word to a scientific register
- Present — a durable word of decline, literary and formal in figurative use, still literal and necessary for the moon's phases
