"Rattled" is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb "rattle": to make, or to cause something to make, a rapid succession of short, sharp, clattering sounds, as when hard objects strike one another again and again. In The Hobbit (J. R. R. Tolkien, 1937, Chapter 2, "Roast Mutton") the word appears in a sentence whose whole purpose is acoustic bedlam: "Whistles blew, armour clashed, swords rattled, goblins cursed and swore and ran hither and thither, falling over one another and getting very angry." Here "rattled" is purely aural. It paints the metallic, percussive chatter of blades knocked together in the chaos of scrambling goblins — the thin, dry, rapid clatter of steel on steel that is the sound-language of confusion and hurry. The tone is staccato, disorderly, and faintly comic in its accumulation, because Tolkien stacks noise on noise — whistles, clashing armour, rattling swords, cursing goblins — until the reader hears bedlam itself. "Rattled" names a quick, irregular, repetitive noise, not a single crash: the impact is broken into many small, sharp percussions, each too fast and too light to be heard separately. It is the sound of something loose, hollow, or hard-colliding: loose window panes in a gale, sabers knocking together as cavalry move, dry leaves skittering in an autumn wind (all of which figure in the model sentences). Near-synonyms, carefully separated:
  • clashed — a single heavy metallic collision, a loud crash of solid masses.
  • clattered — a continuous rattling of harder, duller objects (dishes, hoofs, planks), less sharp than "rattled".
  • jingled — the light, musical tinkle of small metal objects such as coins and spurs.
  • clanked — the heavy, hollow ring of chains or machinery, slower and deeper.
  • rang — a sustained musical resonance.
  • chattered — a rapid, aimless tapping, often of teeth, machinery, or birds.
Only "rattled" captures the rapid, disorderly, brittle chatter of weapons meeting haphazardly — the swords skittering and bouncing off one another as the goblins run every which way. Polysemy & secondary modern senses. The verb "rattle" has branched into several distinct, coexisting meanings, each with its own historical bridge:
  • The noise sense (c. 1300 to the present): the literal rapid clatter described above, unchanged in nearly seven hundred years.
  • The mechanical and motion sense (from c. 1500): to move with a rattling noise, of carts, chariots, looms, trains, and machinery; hence the adjective "rattling" = brisk and energetic ("a rattling pace", 18th–19th c.).
  • The death-rattle (from the 16th c.): the final rattling sound in the throat of a dying person — a grim specialization of the throat-noise sense.
  • The psychological sense (from 1869): "to rattle someone" = to disconcert, fluster, unnerve, throw off balance; the noise is transferred from objects to the nerves. "Don't let it rattle you." Hence the participial "rattled" = unnerved, flustered, agitated — the sense that survives today in psychology and sports journalism for a competitor shaken by pressure.
  • Idiomatic extensions: "to rattle off" = to recite or produce rapidly (1823); "to rattle through" = to do hurriedly; "saber-rattling" = aggressive display of military power (1876, a calque of German Säbelgerassel); "rattle-cage", "rattlebox".
  • The noun senses built on the same sound: a baby's rattle (a toy that rattles); a snake's rattle (the warning organ of the rattlesnake, so named from the 18th c.); the rattle of a machine; "the rattle" of a horn or alarm.
The historical bridge connecting all these senses is the sound itself: "rattle" began as an imitation of a rapid repeated percussion, and every later meaning either names the same noise, names the object that makes it, or — in the psychological sense — transfers the abrupt, disordering shock of that noise onto a person's mental state. To say that someone is "rattled" is to say that their composure has been struck by a quick succession of disordering impacts, exactly as a loose window pane is rattled by the gale. In register the word is thoroughly naturalized: it does ordinary onomatopoeic work in prose, verse, journalism, and conversation alike, and its long polysemous career — from the clatter of steel to the flutter of nerves — makes it one of English's most vivid sound-verbs.
