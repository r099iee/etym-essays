- 13th c. Anglo-Norman religious importation - enters Middle English from Old French piteus / petous; originally meant both "pious, devout, full of Christian charity" (active) and "evoking compassionate pity" (passive); reverent and devotional.
- 14th–15th c. Chaucerian romance and chivalric tragedy - widely used in medieval narrative poetry (Chaucer's Canterbury Tales) to describe the tears of forsaken maidens, dying knights, and suffering martyrs; high emotional gravitas.
- 16th–17th c. Elizabethan tragic consolidation - Shakespeare and Jacobean dramatists establish piteous as the quintessential term for dramatic pathos ("piteous spectacle", "piteous predicament"); the active sense ("compassionate") becomes obsolete.
- 18th–19th c. Divergence from "pitiful" - while pitiful increasingly degenerated into an expression of contempt and scorn ("a pitiful liar"), piteous remained elevated, noble, and untainted by disdain.
- 20th–21st c. Literary styling and satirical adaptation - preserved in serious literature for moving accounts of trauma and war; simultaneously adopted by comedic writers (such as P. G. Wodehouse) for mock-heroic portrayals of overdramatic characters.
