Perth is a city in central Scotland on the west bank of the River Tay, at the lowest bridging point and head of tide, about 20 miles south-west of Dundee. Known as The Fair City since medieval times, it was for centuries the effective capital of Scotland, site of coronations at nearby Scone, of parliaments, and of the parish kirk of St John that gave it the alias St John's Toun. In the source sentence — she lived at Bridge-end, in the town of Perth, and had a garden sloping down to the Tay — Ruskin recalls his aunt's house in this Perth: a provincial, pious, gooseberry-gardened town where a child could gaze into the clear-brown Tay over pebbles, three or four feet deep, swift-eddying.

As a proper noun Perth is always capitalised and has no common-noun meaning, but its uses fan widely. Scots mean by Perth the county town of Perthshire, a market, garrison, and administrative centre with castle, bridge, Inches (water-meadows), and railway junction. Historians mean a sequence of Perths: Pictish woodland ford, Roman Bertha fort three miles north, medieval walled burgh founded by David I about 1125, Reformation flashpoint where Knox preached in 1559, Gowrie-conspiracy stage, Jacobite-occupied town in 1715 and 1745. Australians mean a second Perth — the capital of Western Australia founded in 1829 and named after the Scottish town at the request of Sir George Murray, born in Perth.

  • Scottish city sense (head sense): the Fair City on the Tay; e.g. to live in Perth, Perth Bridge, Perth railway station. The bridge is direct identity: Perth is the place where Tay can be crossed, hence market, court, and kirk gather. Adjectival form is Perthite for a native, Perthshire for the county.
  • Historical-capital sense: medieval seat of government until 1482; e.g. Perth as capital, the Perth parliament, Scone by Perth where kings were crowned on the Stone. The semantic bridge is association: coronations at Scone needed lodging, worship, and defence at Perth, making the town the court's shadow.
  • Ruskin's domestic sense: aunts, gardens, and Tay: Bridge-end suburb, cold mutton on Sundays, gooseberry-bushes sloping to swift water; e.g. Ruskin's Perth aunt. Here Perth stands for strict Lowland Presbyterian kinship — thrifty, godly, unsentimental — against which the boy measures art and nature. The bridge is metonymy: town for family atmosphere.
  • Ecclesiastical alias St John's Toun: after the medieval parish church of St John the Baptist; e.g. St Johnstoun, St John's Kirk in Perth. Still used in football (St Johnstone FC) and pride. The bridge is dedication: saint gives town a second name.
  • Global namesake sense: Perth, Australia: the sunlit capital on the Swan River, population over two million; e.g. Perth mint, Perth glory (sunset phenomenon), to fly to Perth. Named deliberately to honour Scottish Perth, it now dwarfs its parent in size. The bridge is commemorative colonisation: emigrants carry home names abroad.
  • Derived institutional senses: ships, titles, and events; e.g. HMS Perth, HMAS Perth (cruisers sunk in World War II), Earl of Perth, Perth Agreement (2011 succession rules), Perthshire amber. Each borrows civic dignity: to call a warship Perth is to claim fair-city steadfastness.

Etymologically Perth is celebrated as a Pictish survival meaning wood, copse, or thicket — a grove-town by a ford. Whether Roman Bertha is the same word latinised or a separate fort-name is debated, but both point to trees by water. To a child looking down into the Tay, Perth meant gooseberries, pebbles, and eddies; to a historian, it means oaks cleared for a burgh that became a kingdom's heart.

In modern usage Perth needs disambiguation only by context: Britons hear Scotland, Australians hear Western Australia. Both hear fairness — the Fair City and the fair-weather city — a coincidence of epithets that keeps the name luminous.
