jutted — the past tense and past participle of the verb jut: to project, protrude, or extend sharply outward, upward, or forward from a surrounding surface, typically with an abrupt, angular, thrusting, shelf-like or chin-like character. It is a word about a broken line and a mass asserting itself beyond that line; it is rarely neutral. A thing that juts does not merely stand out — it breaks the plane with a hard, unsoftened angle, as if it had been thrown out from the surface and frozen in the instant of its emergence.

Primary senses (physical and descriptive)

  • Of land and rock (topography, geology): a cliff, crag, headland, spur, or promontory juts from a coast, mountainside, or plain; a 'jut of rock' is a sudden, angular projection — typically the resistant remnant of a harder stratum left standing after softer stone has worn away. The passage exploits this sense exactly: the great stones of the Outer Wall 'jutted forward in the form of a massive shelf stretching from east to west for about two hundred to three hundred feet.'
  • Of architecture and structures: a shelf, balcony, cornice, buttress, turret, or tower juts from a wall or a building's principal line. Here the word often carries menace or monumentality, as though the structure lunged outward rather than merely extending itself.
  • Of the human and animal body: a jutting chin, jaw, brow, cheekbone, or shoulder-blade projects beyond the normal plane of the face or figure. The word is a stock tool of character description: a jutting jaw signals stubbornness, defiance, or brute force, and a 'jut' of the chin is a gesture of aggression.
  • Of objects generally: anything that abruptly breaks a plane — a spar from a wreck, a root from a bank, a crag from a ridge, an antler from a skull — may be said to jut.

Secondary senses (figurative and metaphorical)

  • Assertion and defiance: because the word preserves the image of throwing or thrusting (English jut descends from Old French jeter, 'to throw'), its figurative uses are never wholly passive. A chin 'jutted out in defiance' does not simply display itself; it is hurled forward as a challenge. In the passage the wall's stones jut 'forward,' and the forwardness is everything: an emergence with attitude, stark, abrupt, unsoftened by curve.
  • Literary register: jut and jutted remain firmly literary and physical. They are favourite verbs of descriptive prose for abrupt, angular emergence — jutting rocks, jutting roofs, jutting silhouettes — and they carry a faintly ominous, almost hostile energy that neutral synonyms lack.

Grammar and derivation

  • Intransitive verb: the stones jutted forward — the subject moves out from its own surface; an adverb of direction (forward, out, upward) is typical and often essential to the sense.
  • Transitive use (rare, obsolete-leaning): to cause to protrude, to thrust out ('he jutted his jaw').
  • Noun: 'a jut' = a protrusion or projection, especially of land or bone ('the jut of his jaw').
  • Participle and adjective: jutting ('a jutting shelf'), and the past form jutted used here as the finite preterite of the verb.

Semantic boundaries with near-synonyms

  • Protrude and project are the neutral, technical terms: anything standing out from a surface may protrude, with no implication of force, angle, or menace; jut insists on an abrupt, angular, thrusting quality.
  • Overhang specifies projection above a void, usually horizontal and often threatening (a cliff overhangs a road); jut may break the plane in any direction.
  • Bulge is a rounded, swelling protuberance — soft where a jut is sharp, full where a jut is angular.
  • Extend merely adds length or reach in any direction and carries no sense of rupture, angle, or attitude.

Modern status and technical uses

  • The word has never been fully absorbed into neutral scientific speech. Geology and engineering prefer projection, outcrop, spur, or protuberance; anatomy prefers prognathous or prominent. Jut survives as a distinctly visual, physical, literary word.
  • Its polysemy is modest but real: an intransitive verb of breaking the plane, a noun for the thing that breaks it, and participial/adjectival forms for the breaking thing itself — all united by the image of a thrown or thrust mass arrested mid-flight.
  • In the present passage jutted does double work: it names the physical fact precisely (a shelf two to three hundred feet long, painted white, ranged each year for judgement) and it supplies the scene's ominous, muscular energy — the wall does not merely have a feature, it lunges, and the annual judgement happens on the very ledge of that lunge.
