- Old English — sweltan rules: the Angles say sweltan for dying while dead and death already exist beside it, a split vocabulary of doom.
- c. 1200 — the Vikings lend a verb: Old Norse deyja enters northern English as dien, blunter and handier than the native word, and begins its march south.
- 14th c. — Chaucer's choice: dien is now the standard literary word, and the gaming dee — the cube — arrives from French to share its spelling.
- 16th–17th c. — Shakespeare's gamut: men die nobly, flowers die sweetly, and lovers are dying daily in sonnets — the hyperbole is born beside the tragedy.
- 18th–19th c. — Cobbett's page: black-letter habits print stranger shapes, and die can stand where the stands, a reminder that spelling itself was still settling.
- Victorian age — the euphemism bloom: pass away, fall asleep, and lose multiply in parlours precisely because die will not flinch.
- Today — the honest monosyllable: medicine, headlines, and comedy all reach for die first — dying of laughter, dying phones, the die is cast — while the noun survives in casinos and mints.
