The participial adjective incapacitated describes any person, organism, machine, organization, or system that has been stripped of the strength, power, legal authority, or operational capacity needed to function normally. To be incapacitated is to be rendered, temporarily or permanently, unable to act, think clearly, perform duties, defend oneself, or carry out the tasks that one's role, health, or circumstances would ordinarily require. The word is a past-participial adjective formed from the verb incapacitate, and it therefore carries both a strong sense of result (the state now obtains) and an implied agent or cause (something has done the incapacitating — an illness, a wound, an anesthetic, a legal ruling, a mechanical failure).

Core sense and morphology. Morphologically, incapacitated decomposes into the negative prefix in- ("not"), the noun capacity (ultimately from Latin capacitas, "ability to hold or contain"), and the verbal and past-participial suffix -ate / -ated. Literally, then, it means "made to have no capacity" — made unable to contain, hold, or receive the power or function that a healthy being or working device should have. The root image is one of containment and grasp: the Latin capere ("to take, seize, hold") underlies the whole family, so that incapacity is imagined as the loss of the very ability to take hold of one's own strength, judgment, or function.

Sense 1 — Physical and medical disablement (primary modern sense). In medicine, emergency services, and ordinary speech, incapacitated denotes a body that can no longer perform its normal functions because of injury, disease, anesthesia, intoxication, severe exhaustion, or poisoning. A driver knocked unconscious in a crash is incapacitated; a patient under general anesthesia is incapacitated; a person so drunk or drugged that they cannot make rational decisions or control their movements is incapacitated. This is the broadest and most neutral use: it says nothing about permanence, only that normal functioning has been interrupted.

Sense 2 — Legal disqualification (jurisprudential sense). In law, incapacitated refers to a person who has been deprived of legal capacity — the ability to make binding contracts, manage property, hold office, consent to treatment, or stand trial. A chief executive who becomes incapacitated triggers constitutional succession; an incapacitated adult may be placed under a guardianship or conservatorship; an incapacitated witness cannot give valid testimony. This sense preserves the word's early modern legal core, when incapacitate meant specifically "to disqualify" — to remove a person's legal standing because of mental infirmity, treason, or moral unfitness.

Sense 3 — Mechanical and operational failure (technical and engineering sense). Extending the same logic to non-human systems, incapacitated describes machines, vehicles, weapons, power grids, computer systems, or organizations that have been made inoperable by damage, fuel depletion, software failure, or systemic breakdown. A truck frozen by frost is incapacitated; a fighter aircraft with a damaged engine is incapacitated; a server under attack may be effectively incapacitated. This sense transfers the human bodily metaphor of helplessness onto technology.

Sense 4 — Military and tactical usage. In military and policing language, to incapacitate is to neutralize a threat without necessarily killing it — incapacitating agents (certain chemical agents), incapacitating blows, incapacitating fire. An incapacitated combatant or suspect is one who has been rendered unable to fight, flee, or resist, which is often the preferred tactical outcome.

Sense 5 — Insurance, medicine, and bureaucratic jargon. In disability insurance and workers' compensation, incapacitated (or totally / partially incapacitated) is a term of art denoting a person whose earning capacity or bodily function is diminished by injury or illness, entitling them to benefits. Temporarily incapacitated and permanently incapacitated are distinct legal-medical statuses.

Secondary nuances. (a) Temporary vs. permanent: the word is agnostic about duration, and the temporal qualifier usually does the work ("temporarily incapacitated by the flu"). (b) Mental incapacity: an incapacitated mind cannot give informed consent — a central concept in medical ethics and guardianship law. (c) Register: the word is formal, objective, and clinical; unlike crippled or helpless it does not carry emotional color, which is why law, medicine, and the military prefer it.

Historical bridge. The modern breadth is a widening from the word's 17th-century legal beginning. Incapacitate was coined in the 1650s to mean "to disqualify, to deprive of legal competence," applied to persons who lost the right to hold office or inherit because of treason, heresy, or mental defect. During the 18th and 19th centuries, as medicine and military administration grew more systematic, the term was pulled into physical contexts — military surgeons catalogued soldiers "incapacitated" by wounds or disease, and by the 20th century it had become standard in emergency medicine, insurance law, and everyday description of any functional breakdown.

Collocations and usage. severely / utterly / totally incapacitated, permanently / temporarily incapacitated, medically incapacitated, mentally incapacitated, to render someone incapacitated, incapacitated driver / passenger / pilot, incapacitating injury, incapacitating agent. Because the word is so broad it is rarely used of trivial setbacks; it implies a real threshold of loss — the person or thing can no longer do what it is supposed to do.

Antonyms and near-opposites. capable, able-bodied, competent, operational, functional, in working order, unimpaired.
