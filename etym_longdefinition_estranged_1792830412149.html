Estranged, the past participle of estrange, describes a bond that has become foreign: people once close — spouses, friends, families, allies, peoples — now separated by distance, offence, or coldness, living as strangers to each other. The word always implies a was beneath the is: nobody is estranged from a stranger; estrangement is closeness remembered across a gap.

In the source sentence the gap is measured across ages and races: not all Men were estranged from them — from the Elves. Long ago, the strength of the Elves to resist Sauron was greater, and some Men stood in fellowship with them; others drifted, through fear, pride, or distance, into foreignness. Tolkien's estranged is exact: the separation is not mere geography but alienation of kind — Mortals grown strange to the Immortal, fellowship thinned into legend, until only a few (the Faithful, the Rangers' ancestors) remain unestranged. The participle mourns what lapsed.

Core meaning in detail: At its centre estranged names affection withdrawn or fellowship broken. Prototypical subjects are intimates: estranged spouses living apart, an estranged father and son exchanging silence, estranged friends passing in the street. The cause may be quarrel, betrayal, neglect, ideology, or simple drift — the word itself does not adjudicate blame, only records the resulting foreignness. Two grammatical frames divide the uses: estranged from (separated from someone: estranged from his family) and attributive estranged (standing in that state: his estranged wife). Both keep the Latin root visible: ex-, out of, plus strangeness — made an outsider, put outside.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Alienated in affection, no longer close or friendly (central modern sense, from the 1500s): estranged from her parents; the estranged couple met only through lawyers. The bridge runs straight from Old French estrangier, to treat as a stranger: to make foreign what was familiar. Divorce courts and family sagas keep this sense dominant.
  • Separated politically or socially, divided into hostile camps (collective sense): two nations estranged by decades of war; voters estranged from the party. The bridge scales the household quarrel to history: peoples, classes, and electorates become strangers exactly as spouses do, through injury remembered and fellowship unpractised.
  • Withdrawn from support or allegiance, disaffected (political and religious register): subjects estranged from their king; believers estranged from the church. The bridge carries alienation into loyalty: the subject still lives under the crown but feels it as foreign power. Histories of civil war and reformation use it constantly.
  • Made strange in character, rendered unfamiliar (literary and psychological shade): grief had estranged him from himself; success estranged her from old friends. The bridge turns the stranger-relationship inward or sideways: a person can grow foreign even to his own habits, and fortune can foreignise its favourite. Novelists prize this reflexive use.
  • Kept apart physically over time until closeness lapses (older and Tolkien's shade): colonies estranged by an ocean; Men estranged from Elves across the ages. The bridge is distance-as-alienator: no quarrel need occur, only long separation, for fellowship to cool into legend. Epic and history share this slow-motion estrangement.
  • Averted or diverted, turned away (rare, of property and attention): funds estranged from their purpose; minds estranged by spectacle. The bridge preserves the verb's old legal sense of alienating — transferring away — applied to money and minds instead of land. Legal history keeps this fossil alive.

Register and connotation: Estranged is formal, sorrowful, and dignified — the word of obituaries, court orders, and epic histories, never slangy or light. It dignifies separation by implying the lost closeness mattered: to say a husband is estranged grants the marriage weight even in wreckage. Its emotional temperature is cool rather than hot — not hatred but foreignness, the chill where warmth was — which makes it sadder than hostile and quieter than alienated. Tolkien's use shows its full power: whole peoples grown strange to one another across centuries, with no single quarrel to blame, only the slow tide of time.
