altar is a raised structure on which sacrifice is offered or on which the central act of worship is performed. In English it is chiefly a Christian word, naming the consecrated table at which the Eucharist is celebrated, but it reaches back to the stone altars of Rome and forward to the secular altars of modern rhetoric, where people are said to sacrifice themselves on the altar of ambition, money or the nation.

Primary sense: the sacrificial structure

An altar is defined by three things: elevation, consecration and the offering of something precious. In the religions of the ancient Mediterranean the altar was the fixed point of the sacred precinct, the place where the victim was killed and burned and where the god was believed to receive the offering. The Latin word ara named the ordinary altar; altare, from altus "high", named the raised or high altar, and it is altare that English borrowed.

  • Ancient and biblical altars — the altars of the Hebrew Bible (mizbeaḥ), the Greek bōmos, and the Roman ara, all places of blood sacrifice and divine contact.
  • Christian altar — the table of the Eucharist, consecrated by a bishop, usually of stone, and often containing relics.
  • High altar — the principal altar of a church, at the east end, where the main Mass is celebrated.
  • Side altar — a secondary altar in a chapel or aisle, dedicated to a saint or mystery.
  • Altar of repose — the place where the consecrated host is reserved on Maundy Thursday.

Parts and furniture

Because the altar is the ritual centre of a church, it has generated a precise vocabulary of furniture, textiles and ceremony.

  • Altar stone — a consecrated slab, often containing relics, set into the top of a stone altar.
  • Altar frontal — the decorated cloth or panel covering the front of the altar, changing with the liturgical season.
  • Altar rail — the communion rail separating the sanctuary from the nave, where communicants kneel.
  • Altar cloth and linen — the white cloths laid on the altar for the Eucharist.
  • Altar candle — a candle placed on or beside the altar, signifying the presence of Christ.
  • Altar cross — the cross standing on or behind the altar.

Ritual and devotional extensions

Beyond Christianity the word covers the raised shrines of many traditions, and in English it is used ecumenically for any constructed focus of worship.

  • Domestic altar — a shelf or table with images, candles and offerings in a home.
  • Buddhist altar — a table with a Buddha image, incense, flowers and offerings.
  • Hindu altar — a shrine or puja table in the home or temple.
  • Altar call — the evangelical invitation to come forward to the front of the church, often to a railing or altar, to receive Christ.
  • Altar boy, altar girl, altar server — a lay assistant who helps the priest at the altar.
  • Altar guild — the volunteer body that cares for the altar linens and vessels.

Figurative and secular senses

The altar has long been a metaphor for total commitment or sacrifice, because it is the place where something valuable is given up. The metaphor survives vigorously in secular English.

  • "To lead to the altar" — to marry someone, from the wedding service performed before the altar.
  • "On the altar of..." — sacrificing something to a cause: "sacrificed on the altar of profit", "placed on the altar of national security".
  • "Altar of Mammon" — the worship of money, a phrase used in sermons and social critique.
  • "Altar of fame" — the pursuit of celebrity as a false religion.
  • "Altar ego" — a modern pun, playing on "alter ego" and the altar as a place of self-offering.

Technical and extended uses

  • Masonic altar — the central table in a lodge, holding the Volume of the Sacred Law, square and compasses.
  • The constellation Ara — Latin for "altar", one of the southern constellations catalogued by Ptolemy.
  • Altar in place names — churches dedicated to a holy cross or altar often generated field and street names.
  • Altar in geology and archaeology — a raised stone platform interpreted as a ritual focus, as in the "altar" of a megalithic tomb.

Semantic bridge

The bridge from Latin altus "high" to the English word is architectural: the god was approached at a raised place, the victim was lifted onto a stone, and the table itself was elevated above the floor of the sanctuary. Height signalled separateness, and separateness signalled the sacred. From that physical elevation came the moral elevation of the thing offered, and from that came the modern metaphor of sacrifice. altar thus carries a whole theology of height, offering and loss inside six letters.
