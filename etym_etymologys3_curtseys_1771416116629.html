The biography of curtseys begins in the concrete act of enclosing. Its ultimate ancestor is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *gher-, "to grasp, enclose, surround," a base that splits into two faces: the grasping hand that seizes, and the encircling wall that holds a space in. From the second face flows the entire story of this card. In the Indo-Iranian world the root may name the enclosed home itself — Sanskrit griha- (गृह) "house, home," descending into the everyday Hindi ghar (घर) and Odia ghara — though scholars hedge, because the "house" sense may also belong to a wider web of related roots. In Greek the same enclosure appears as khortos (χόρτος), "enclosed pasture, farmyard," in Homer — the walled space where the stock is kept — while the sibling khorde gives English cord, the twisted thing that binds.

It is in Latin that the root builds the house of power. Latin hortus means "garden, enclosed plot," and its derivative cohors, cohortis, from com- "together" plus the *hort- enclosure, first meant a farmer's enclosed yard, then the company of men gathered in it — a retinue, a troop, and so the Roman military cohort. The great semantic leap happens in the Latin of the provinces and in Old French: the enclosed yard of a great house comes to mean the cour, the court — the place where the lord dwells, keeps his household, and dispenses justice. Sound change accompanies the leap: the aspirated PIE *gh- loses its aspiration in Latin (hortus), the initial cluster is reduced in Proto-Romance (*curtis), and Gallo-Romance drops final unstressed syllables to give Old French cort, with the cluster later simplified to cour in modern spelling.

With the court comes courtliness. Old French cortois, "courtly," and its noun cortoisie name the whole code of refined behaviour proper to courtiers — chivalry, elegance, and the courteous treatment of others — the ideal celebrated in the 12th-century romances of Chrétien de Troyes and by the Provençal troubadours. Anglo-Norman carried curteisie into England, where Middle English courtesy (13th century, used by Chaucer among others) long meant general civil virtue. Then, in the 16th century, came the decisive narrowing: courtesy, soon respelled curtsey and curtsy, became specifically the woman's gesture of greeting — the bending of the knees and the dip of the body that a female performed to a superior. The grand abstraction shrank into a single graceful motion, and the whole vocabulary of courtly virtue was folded into the angle of a knee. Socially, the gesture belonged to the great houses and the royal presence, where the etiquette of the court measured rank in the exact depth of the bend; in the 19th century dancing-masters and governesses drilled it into young ladies until a girl's curtsey publicly attested her breeding.

In the literary context of Dream of the Red Chamber, the plural does its cultural work. Dai-yu, the newcomer, "exchanged curtseys and introductions" with her cousins — the plural marks the reciprocity of the ritual, each gesture acknowledging the other's place in the Rong-guo hierarchy. The translator naturalizes an aristocratic Qing-dynasty greeting as a Western gesture of rank, and the word lands with aptness because both cultures used the body to map the family's web of seniority. Key stations in the word's journey:

  • PIE *gher- "enclose" → Latin hortus "garden" → cohors "enclosed yard; troop" — the enclosure becomes the band of men within it.
  • Vulgar Latin *curtis "manorial court" → Old French cort "court" — the enclosure becomes the seat of lordship and justice.
  • Old French cortoisie "courtliness" → Middle English courtesy — the place-name becomes a code of conduct.
  • 16th-century courtesy → curtsey — the code becomes a gesture; the abstraction becomes a bend of the knee.
  • The plural curtseys in the novel — the gesture becomes a reciprocal ritual of rank between the two parties.
