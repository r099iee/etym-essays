Chain for REINS: - PIE *ten- 'to stretch' — the oldest reconstructable source of the notion of holding via stretching out; the same root gives Latin tendere 'to stretch' and English thin, tender, tense. - Latin tenēre 'to hold, keep, grasp' (1st c. BCE onward) — from the stretch-sense: to hold something out and keep hold of it. - Latin retinēre 'to hold back, retain, restrain' — compound of re- 'back' + tenēre 'to hold'; the root that also gives English retain, retention, retinue. - Latin retina 'a band, cord, leash, bridle-rein' — a noun derived from retinēre, literally 'that which holds back.' - Old French resne / reine (later also reigne) 'rein' (11th-12th c.) — the direct source of the English word. - Middle English rene / reyne / reyn 'rein' (14th c.), plural reynes. - Modern English reins (plural) — the form in which the word is almost always used. Note on nuance/debate: The rein-sense of Latin retina is attested in late Latin and is uncontroversial; however, some authorities note a parallel influence of Old French reigne/reigne 'kingdom, rule' (from Latin regnum) and of the verb regere 'to rule' on the medieval spelling of the word, a folk-etymological interference that reinforced the 'rule' senses. The modern plural 'reins' is idiomatic, the matched pair being conceived as a single instrument; the -eins spelling settles in the 16th c. under French influence. Chain for FLOURISHED: - PIE *bʰleh₃- 'to bloom, flower, thrive' — the source also of Latin flōs and English bloom, blossom. - Latin flōs (genitive flōris) 'flower, blossom, bloom' — also 'the flower of something,' its finest part or prime. - Latin flōrēre 'to bloom, blossom, be in flower' (1st c. BCE) and, by extension, 'to flourish, prosper, be in one's prime' — a denominative verb built on flōs, flōris. - Vulgar Latin *flōrīre 'to bloom, flourish' — the verb shifts from the 2nd-conjugation flōrēre to a 4th-conjugation -īre type, producing the stem flōrī- that feeds the Old French forms; the inchoative form *flōrīscere 'to begin to bloom' yields the Old French stem floriss-. - Old French florir 'to blossom, flower, flourish, prosper' (10th-11th c.) — with the present stem floriss- that English borrows. - Middle English florisshen / florysshen 'to blossom, bloom, flourish, thrive' (13th-14th c.). - Modern English flourish (flourished) — the spellings settle in the 16th c., by which time the ornamental and brandishing senses are established. Note: The modern 'waving showily' sense is not inherited from Latin directly but developed in English (16th c.) out of the ornamental 'flourish' (an embellishing stroke in writing or music), itself an extension of 'blooming.' The -ish spelling reflects the Old French inchoative stem.
