Chain — the root of “wield” (past form “wielded”): - PIE *h₂welh₁- “to be strong, to rule” (also reconstructed as *wal- in older scholarship; the root yields words for power and might across the family, e.g. Latin valere “to be strong”) → Proto-Germanic *waldaną “to rule, to have power over, to govern” (reconstructed, c. 1st millennium BCE) → Old English wealdan (strong verb of class VII) “to rule, govern, direct, control; to wield” (attested from c. 8th century, including in Beowulf) → Old English wielde / wieldan (i-mutated variant forms) → Middle English welden / wolden “to govern, rule; to handle and use a weapon or tool” (12th–15th centuries) → Early Modern English wield (16th century; the modern vowel established by the regularisation of the Middle English form) “to hold and handle (a weapon or tool) with skill; to exercise power or influence” → Modern English wield + -ed (past-tense and past-participle suffix) = WIELDED “handled, exercised, or exerted effectively” (past forms attested from Old English onward; the figurative sense “to exercise influence, power, authority” develops from the 16th–17th centuries). Chain — the inflectional suffix -ed: - PIE *-tó- (verbal-adjective / participle suffix, “having been …-ed”) → Proto-Germanic *-þaz / *-daz (past-participle ending) → Old English -ede / -ode (weak past and past-participle ending) → Middle English -ed(e) → Modern English -ed, giving wielded. Note on debate: the modern spelling wield, with the vowel -ie-, continues the Old English i-mutated alternant (wieldan) alongside the base form wealdan; the OED records that the i-form becomes fixed in the 16th century. The ultimate PIE root is sometimes grouped with the family of *h₂welh₁- “to be strong” (compare Latin valere), but some scholars prefer a root *wal- of disputed laryngeal shape; the Germanic line *waldaną → Old English wealdan is secure and undisputed, and there is no attestation gap between Old English and Middle English for this verb.
