The compound overall brings two ancient roots to a single English word, and its evolution is best told as a descent from the vertical space above the head to the folded cloth of a custodian's uniform — a journey that moves through bureaucracy, cloth, and dust.

Movement 1 — the biography of over: The first element descends from the PIE root *upér, an adverbial formation upon the base *upó, “up from under”, which marked position in vertical space — above, over, beyond. The root is among the most productive in Indo-European, and its branches demonstrate how a single spatial notion diversifies across a continent. In Greek it gave hupér, “over, above, beyond”, the source of the prefix hyper- and of hyperbole, the “over-throwing” of language; in Sanskrit it gave upári, “above, over”, from which Hindi ūpar and Odia uparē still name the space above the speaker; in Latin it gave super — with the s-mobile, the wandering initial consonant that distinguishes super from Greek hupér — the ancestor of super, supreme, and insuperable. In Germanic it gave Proto-Germanic *uber, Old English ofer, and modern English over, together with the great family of over- compounds (overcoat, overdo, overlord, overrule) in which the prefix oscillates between physical covering and moral excess. That oscillation matters here: the “over” of “overall” means both “above” — a layer laid over the clothes — and “beyond measure”, the totality that exceeds particulars. When Old English joined ofer with eall, the phrase described what lay across the whole extent of a thing — a net cast over a field, an eye passing over a page — and this phrase, once fused, carried the spatial sense of totality into the language of administration and summary.

Movement 2 — the biography of all: The second element descends from PIE *al-, an adverbial root of “beyondness” whose most famous semantic leap made the word for “other” into the word for “every”. In Greek, *al- gave állos, “other” — the source of allegory, “speaking other”, and allopathy; in Latin it gave alius, “other”, the ancestor of alias, alibi (“elsewhere”), and alien. In Germanic it produced the adjective *allaz, “whole, entire, all”: Old English eall, German all, and English all, also (“all so”), already, and albeit (“all be it”). The semantic shift from “beyond” to “the whole” is a classic instance of completion: what lies beyond every particular is the totality, so the word that meant “other” came to mean “the whole”. The compound “over-all” therefore means not merely “above everything” but “embracing everything”: the “all” already carries totality, and “over” adds the gesture of the encompassing sweep. This is the deep grammar of every “overall winner”, “overall impression”, and “overall responsibility” spoken today.

Movement 3 — the fusion, and the descent into cloth: When English fused ofer and eall, the compound moved from phrase to word. In the 14th century overal was an adverb, “everywhere, altogether”; by the 16th century it was an adjective of summary, “total, comprehensive”, the bureaucratic instrument by which estates, courts, and merchants held a whole in a single glance. Then in 1759 American usage coined the noun over-alls for heavy protective trousers with a bib and braces: the abstract word of totality became the workingman's cloth. In the 20th century the British singular overall named the one-piece suit covering trunk and limbs — the livery of factory, garage, and museum. The semantic bridge is exact: a garment worn over all the others is literally the “over-all” garment, the last layer enclosing the whole person, just as the adjective encloses a whole subject. The social history is written in the word's registers: born of medieval totality phrases, generalised by Renaissance accountancy, democratised into frontier labour in 1759 America, and worn by the Victorian and twentieth-century working class as a badge of honest toil.

Movement 4 — the word in Gormenghast: In Titus Groan, the overall appears not on Rottcodd's body but in his ante-room, kept “ready for use” beside the stock of white candles, the dust-white visitors' book, and the stepladder. Peake thus captures the word's whole history in a single inventory: the abstract word of totality re-materialised as cloth, hanging ready to transform the sleeping man in his hammock into the ritual custodian of the carvings. The garment carries “over-all” completeness in its very name — the one man in whom the Hall of the Bright Carvings is summed up, keeper of the candles, of the book, of the ladder, of everything the wing contains. Every shift in the word's long line — spatial phrase, fused adverb, adjective of totality, American bib-and-brace trousers, British one-piece suit — is documented, and each is a re-materialisation or re-abstraction of the same core idea: the sweeping gesture that takes in everything above and beyond the particular, from the PIE sky of *upér to the grey cloth of a custodian's uniform.
