creek
- 13th century - Middle English creke means a fissure, a crack, a narrow opening in the coast; its likeliest sources are Old Norse kriki ("nook, corner") and Old French crique ("bay, cove"), both built on the image of a bend or hook in the land that lets the sea reach inward.
- 14th-17th centuries - the coastal sense hardens: a creek is a narrow, sheltered inlet of the sea, a natural harbour hidden from the open water; in Britain this remains the working seaman's word for a snug place of refuge, the very sense Lewis uses.
- 17th-century Atlantic shift - American settlers apply creek to the inland streams that ran down to those inlets, then to any small river or tributary; in America the word becomes a freshwater stream (dialectal "crick"), while British English keeps the tidal inlet - one word, two kinds of water - and the name even hardens into an ethnonym, the Creek (Muscogee) people.
- 20th century onward - the idiom "up the creek (without a paddle)" fixes the stranded, in-trouble image (c. 1930s); today a British creek remains a tidal inlet and an American creek a small stream, the same "bend" image ruling both sides of the Atlantic.
