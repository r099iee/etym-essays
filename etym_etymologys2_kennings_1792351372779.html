Note on historical phonology and sister cognates: The noun kenning is an erudite 19th-century borrowing into English literary scholarship directly from Old Norse kenning. However, the root itself is deeply native to the Germanic stock of English. The Proto-Germanic causative verb *kannijaną directly produced Old English cennan ("to bring forth, declare, make known"). While Old English possessed the nominal form cenning ("birth, declaration, producing"), it did not apply that noun technically to the poetic device of compounding. Meanwhile, the base preterite-present verb *kunnaną ("to know, be acquainted with") gave rise to Modern English can, cunning, uncouth (literally "unknown"), and the Scottish/Northern English dialect verb ken ("to know, sight, perceive"). Thus, when Victorian philologists adopted the Old Norse term kenning to describe Anglo-Saxon poetic compounds (like whale-road), they were reuniting a Scandinavian technical term with its exact Old English cognate lineage.
