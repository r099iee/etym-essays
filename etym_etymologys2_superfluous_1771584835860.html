[Root core] PIE *bʰlew- "to swell, to well up, to gush, to overflow" (c. 4000 BC) — de Vaan's base for Latin fluere; Pokorny groups it within the extended family *pleu-/plū- "to flow, to float," the bʰ/p alternation being well attested (Gk. φλύω vs. πλέω) [Stage 2] Proto-Italic *flēwe- "to flow" (reconstructed) — the Italic reflex of the root [Stage 3] Latin fluere "to flow" (attested from the earliest Latin literature) — the base verb of continuous movement, applied to water, time, and speech [Stage 4] Latin superfluus "running over, overflowing; superfluous, more than enough" (1st c. BC) — compound of super- "over, above, beyond" + fluere "to flow" [Stage 5] Old French superflu "superfluous, excessive" (12th c.) [Stage 6] Middle English superfluous "more than is necessary; excessive" (15th c., c. 1450) — borrowed from Latin superfluus / French superflu [Stage 7] English superfluous "unnecessary, especially through being more than enough" — this card's word Same-root companions: Latin flumen "a river" (> English flume), fluere + in- "into" → influere "to flow in" → Latin influentia (astrology: "the flowing in of the stars' power") → English influence; Italian influenza "an epidemic (viewed as an inflow)" → English influenza; Greek φλύω phlúō "to gush forth, to overflow"; Greek πλέω pléō "to sail, to float" (from the twin variant *plew-); Sanskrit plu- "to swim, to float, to flow"; Proto-Germanic *flōan- "to flow" → Old English flōwan → English flow; Gothic flōdus "a flood, a river." Note on the debate: The relationship between the variants *bʰlew- and *plew- is a standard point of discussion among Indo-Europeanists; de Vaan reconstructs *bʰlew- as the base of Latin fluere, while Pokorny folds it into the *pleu-/plū- family. The attested Latin formation superfluus (Cicero, 1st c. BC) is not in dispute.
