- Old Norse roots — firmness: traust names help, confidence, and the firm ground of reliance, from timber-strong traustr.
- c. 1200 — English adopts trust: trust enters northern English for confidence and obligation, and trusty follows as its adjective of proven worth.
- Medieval — retainers and vassals: chronicles praise trusty knights and trusty commons, loyalty as the currency of feudal survival.
- 16th–17th c. — crowns and covenants: proclamations address trusty and well-beloved subjects, and the prayer book makes the word liturgical.
- 18th–19th c. — ballads and blades: minstrels sing of trusty steeds while every hero draws a trusty sword, romance burnishing the everyday virtue.
- 20th c. — Tolkien's fellowship: Elrond's counsel to take trusty friends crowns the word with questing dignity, loyalty as the first equipment.
- Today — fond archaism: trustworthy handles duty, but trusty survives for companions, tools, and steeds we love, a medal-word for the proven.
