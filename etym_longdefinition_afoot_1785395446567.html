The adverb afoot means "in preparation, in progress, or beginning to happen", said of an event, plan, or undertaking that is actively developing and usually still hidden. Its literal sense is "on foot, walking" — a person on the move — and from the sixteenth century the figurative meaning developed: what is on foot is moving, and so what is afoot is stirring into action.
  • Literal sense (historical): "on foot, walking", said of a person or creature travelling by walking rather than by horse, cart, or ship. In early texts a messenger arrives "afoot", a pilgrim makes a journey afoot; the sense is purely physical and describes mode of travel.
  • Figurative core sense (modern): "in progress, in preparation, happening or beginning to happen", said of plans, plots, undertakings, changes, and schemes. The image behind the shift is movement: what is afoot is stirring, astir, moving towards its issue. The word is typically impersonal — something is afoot, with no agent named — and that impersonality is what makes it so useful for suspense and foreboding.
  • In the present literary context: the Lord of the Eagles, filled with curiosity to know what was afoot in the world below, summons many other eagles; together they fly away from the mountains and, slowly circling ever round and round, come down towards the ring of wolves and the meeting-place of the goblins. The word here carries a rich sense of ominous, hidden activity: something significant, dangerous, and not yet fully known is stirring beneath the surface of events — a plot is in motion whose authors are invisible. The word is slightly archaic and literary, belonging to the storyteller's register of foreboding: plots do not merely happen, they are afoot. It is also impersonal, keeping the agent deliberately vague: what is afoot is a mystery known only to its secret instigators, and the eagles' curiosity is the reader's own.
  • Register and distribution: in modern English afoot is chiefly literary or journalistic, almost always used of developments that are mysterious, worrying, or at least noteworthy. One says "something is afoot" in a whodunnit, a fantasy, or a news report on unexplained activity; one rarely says it of a cheerful plan being announced openly. It has a faintly archaic flavour even in contemporary prose, and that archaism is part of its power in Tolkien's narrative voice.
  • Related adverbial and adjectival functions: afoot is traditionally an adverb (used with be and with verbs of motion), but it also functions as a predicative adjective ("the plan is afoot"), and in attributive use it appears as an adverb modifying a past participle ("work afoot", "negotiations afoot"). It takes no comparative or superlative and has no plural; it is invariable.
  • Metaphoric extensions: because what is afoot is moving but not yet visible, the word extends naturally to anything in an early, hidden, or incubating stage: "great changes are afoot", "a conspiracy is afoot", "something strange is afoot". The metaphor is one of a journey begun but not completed — the destination unknown, the walkers unseen.
  • Modern idiom: the phrase "what's afoot?" — used literally ("what is happening?") — is a bookish way of asking for news, and it preserves the word's detective-story associations. In journalism "moves are afoot to..." reports quietly developing initiatives; in political writing "plots afoot" keeps the conspiratorial colour.
The word is formally a compound of the preposition on (reduced to a-) and the noun foot, and its history is the history of that reduction and of the figurative leap. On the literal side its near-relations are on foot, walking, travelling by foot; on the figurative side it brushes against in progress, underway, in motion, stirring, brewing, in the works, and astir. The distinctive feature of afoot among these is its deliberate vagueness combined with its sense of secret agency: an event that is underway is officially and openly progressing; a plan that is in the works is known to be prepared; but something that is afoot is moving without being announced, its instigators unnamed and its outcome uncertain. That is why the word belongs so naturally to the language of detection and of heroic fantasy alike: the reader, like the Lord of the Eagles, feels that the world is stirring and wants to know what is afoot. Grammatically it is a closed, invariable form, and it illustrates the general English pattern by which a prepositional phrase (on foot) contracts into a single adverb (afoot) — the same pattern seen in abed, aboard, afloat, aground, asleep, and astir.
