A NAME GUARDING ITS WEALTH Odoacer is an East Germanic personal name, conventionally analysed as a two-element compound meaning "one who guards his wealth" or "wealth-watchful." Recorded in the ancient sources in a rich spray of spellings — Odoacer, Odovacar, Odovaker, Audovacar — the name belongs to the same class of dithermatic (two-element) Germanic hero-names as Theodoric, "ruler of the people," and Alaric, "all-ruler," and its two roots carry it back into the deepest layers of the Indo-European lexicon. THE FIRST ELEMENT: WEALTH AND FORTUNE The first stem, *auda-, "wealth, fortune, property," descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h2ew-, "to enjoy, consume," via an extended form *h2ewd-. The family it produced is one of the richest in Germanic:
  • Gothic auda- in compounds, e.g., audahafts "blessed";
  • Old Norse audr "riches, fortune" — a word that also carries the sense "fate, lot," joining wealth to destiny;
  • Old English ead "prosperity, happiness," the favourite first element of Anglo-Saxon royal names — Edward, Eadmund, Eadgar, Edith — the kings whose very names announced blessedness;
  • Old High German ot "possession, riches."
In the wider Indo-European frame the root connects with notions of plenty and enjoyment, and in the name Odoacer the element declares the man's wealth — the property and fortune a warrior-king was expected to guard. THE SECOND ELEMENT: WATCHFULNESS The second stem, *wakr-, "watchful, awake, alert," goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *weg-, "to be strong, to be lively, to be awake." Its Germanic reflexes form the great wake/watch family:
  • Gothic wakan "to watch, to be awake";
  • Old Norse vaka "to be awake" and vakr "alert, bold";
  • Old English wacian "to stay awake, watch" (modern watch), wacor/waeccer "watchful" (modern wakeful, and through related forms our verb wake);
  • Old High German wahhen "to watch", wackar "awake, alert" (modern German wacker, "valiant").
In the wider family the same root appears in Latin vegere, "to be lively," and vigere, "to thrive" (modern English vigor/vigorous), and in Sanskrit vaja-, "strength" — a root that fuses alertness, vigour, and power. A "wealth-watchful" man is thus not merely a rich man but a vigilant one: the watchman of his own fortune, awake to every threat. THE MAN AND HIS CENTURY The name's bearer, Odoacer (c. 433-493), belonged to the Scirian people, federate soldiers who served on the Danube frontier and in Italy. In 476, when the Western regime of Orestes refused his barbarian federates the Italian lands promised them, Odoacer led the mutiny: he marched on Ravenna, killed Orestes, and deposed the child-emperor Romulus Augustulus, whom he spared and pensioned. Then came the constitutional masterstroke — he sent the Western imperial regalia and insignia back to Constantinople, inviting the Eastern Emperor Zeno to rule a unified empire while Odoacer governed Italy as king (rex) in his name. He never took the purple; he declined the imperial title precisely because it was the emperor's to give. RULE, WAR, AND THE NAME IN THE SOURCES For seventeen years Odoacer ruled Italy from Ravenna, preserving the Roman senate, law, and administration, defending Sicily and Dalmatia, and fighting the Rugians and the Vandal fleet. His name appears across the fifth- and sixth-century sources in its variant spellings — the chronicle of Marcellinus comes, Jordanes' Getica, Eugippius' Vita Severini — and in Greek as Odoachos; the very multiplicity of forms testifies to a Germanic name heard and re-spelled by Latin- and Greek-writing clerks. It is a documented, datable historical name, and that is precisely why Macaulay, in the source context, sets "Odoacer and Totila, Euric and Thrasimund, Clovis, Fredegunda, and Brunechild" against the mythical Arthur, Hengist, and Vortigern: Odoacer belongs to verifiable history. THE END: THEODORIC AND THE AFTERLIFE Odoacer's reign ended at the hands of another Germanic king with a wealth-announcing name. Theodoric the Ostrogoth invaded Italy in 489 at Zeno's prompting, fought Odoacer for four years, and in 493 — after a negotiated truce — invited him to a banquet and cut him down. The assassination made Theodoric the second barbarian king of Italy and gave Odoacer's name its tragic finish: the watchman of his wealth was finally outwatched. THE SYMBOLIC AFTERLIFE Modern historiography, following Gibbon and the nineteenth-century masters, has made 476 and Odoacer the hinge between Antiquity and the Middle Ages. Historians now debate how real the rupture was — whether Odoacer "fell" Rome or quietly ended a succession that was already meaningless — but the symbolic value of the name is fixed. Odoacer is the last barbarian at the gates become the barbarian in the palace: the outsider who closed the book on the Western emperors and opened the Middle Ages. His name, born of Germanic "wealth" and "wakefulness," has become the byword for the moment a Germanic king replaced a Roman emperor at the head of Italy.
