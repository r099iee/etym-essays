Athens, 351 BCE

The word begins in Athenian politics. Philip II of Macedon, having reorganised his kingdom's army, began in the 350s to encroach on the Greek cities of the north. Demosthenes, an Athenian politician and the greatest orator of the age, delivered a series of speeches urging resistance. Ancient editors grouped four of them as Philippikoi — “the speeches about Philip” — a title that may be editorial rather than authorial. In them Demosthenes casts Philip as the enemy of Greek freedom and Athens as its natural champion; the tone is urgent, accusatory and often scornful.

Rome, 44–43 BCE

Cicero knew Demosthenes' speeches intimately and consciously imitated them. When Antony emerged as Caesar's political heir, Cicero answered with a series of speeches that his correspondents and later readers called Philippicae, the Roman sequel to the Athenian original. The parallel was both flattering and dangerous: it cast Antony as a new Philip, a king in all but name, and Cicero as the defender of the Republic. The identification stuck. It is why the two sets are taught together and why “philippic” entered the modern languages as the name of a political attack rather than of a particular occasion.

How the word reached English

  • Greek: Philippikos, “of Philip”, from Philippos, the king's name, literally “horse-lover”.
  • Latin: Philippicae, the title of Cicero's collection; Latin also has the adjective Philippicus.
  • French: philippique, recorded in the sixteenth century for a violent speech of denunciation.
  • English: philippic is recorded from the 1590s; the plural philippics follows within the same period.

Semantic drift

The shift from title to genre is a textbook case of antonomasia: a proper name for a set of speeches becomes a common noun for the kind of speech they represent. The crucial step was the loss of the specifically Demosthenic or Ciceronian reference. Once “a philippic” could be delivered against a tax official or a literary rival, the word had become a genre label. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries it was a favourite of parliamentary and pamphlet warfare; in the nineteenth it suited the long, moralising review essay. Modern usage tends to be shorter, more ironic, and often applied to a single outburst rather than a campaign.

Literary and political afterlife

  • Schoolroom pairing: Demosthenes and Cicero were the twin models of the deliberative orator in European education, so the Philippics entered curricula as a set text.
  • Pamphlet culture: seventeenth- and eighteenth-century polemicists borrowed the title for attacks on ministers and prelates.
  • Journalism: the nineteenth-century press used “philippic” for a leader or a letter of sustained abuse; the twentieth century preferred “diatribe” and “tirade”, leaving philippic with a slightly antique sheen.
  • Modern politics: the word still appears when a speech is felt to be classical in its fury — “a philippic against the new orthodoxy”.

The Greek components

Philippos is a compound of philos “dear, beloved” and hippos “horse”, hence “fond of horses” — an aristocratic name of the sort common in the Greek upper class. Hippos descends from Proto-Indo-European *h₁éḱwos, the same root that gives Latin equus and English equestrian. The first element, philos, has no generally accepted Indo-European etymology; Beekes lists it without one, and the proposals that connect it with a root meaning “to be dear” remain speculative. The compound is therefore only partly analysable, a fact worth remembering when the etymology of “philippic” is presented as if it were transparent.

Sound and spelling in English

English preserves the Greek ph in philippic, pronounced /f/, and the stress falls on the second syllable: /fɪˈlɪp.ɪks/. The word is not related to Philippine (from the Philippines, named for Philip II of Spain) or to Philippi, the Macedonian city near which Brutus and Cassius were defeated in 42 BCE — though all three ultimately contain the same Greek name. That cluster of lookalikes is a common source of spelling mistakes and of false etymologies.

Why the plural matters

The source uses the plural because Cicero's attack was a series, not a single speech. English keeps the distinction: a philippic is one denunciation, philippics is a campaign or a collection. The plural also carries the capitalised historical sense, the Philippics, which names the corpus itself. In a sentence such as “he delivered the philippics for which he is best remembered”, the plural signals both the number of speeches and their status as a single achievement.
