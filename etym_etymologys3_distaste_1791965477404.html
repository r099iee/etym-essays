Distaste is unusual among the words in this batch in being a native English formation rather than a direct Latin or French loan: it is dis- added to the inherited noun and verb taste in the sixteenth century. Its second element, however, carries a long Romance prehistory that reaches back through Old French to vulgar Latin and ultimately to the Latin verb for touching.

The Latin prehistory of “taste”

Classical Latin tangere means “to touch” and is attested from the earliest literature; its frequentative taxare means “to touch sharply, handle, assess,” and from the notion of handling came the sense “to appraise, to estimate,” which survives in English tax, taxable, and taxation. Vulgar Latin developed a further form, reconstructed as *tastare, usually explained as a frequentative of taxare that was influenced in meaning by gustare, “to taste.” The reconstruction is secure in outline but debated in detail: some scholars treat *tastare as a blending of the two verbs rather than a straightforward derivative, and no text preserves it.

  • tangere → “to touch” → English tangent, tactile, contact, intact.
  • taxare → “to handle, assess” → English tax, taxis, taxonomy (via Greek), or tax as a verb of censure.
  • *tastare → “to touch and taste” → the Romance verbs for tasting: French tâter, Italian tastare, Spanish tastar.
  • Semantic mechanism: the tongue is the organ that touches food, so a verb of handling slides into a verb of tasting, and from tasting into testing and judging.

Old French and Middle English

Old French taster, recorded from the twelfth century, means both “to touch, feel” and “to taste,” and English borrows it in the thirteenth century as tasten. Middle English uses it for physical touching as well as for the perception of flavour, and by the fourteenth century it has acquired the figurative sense “to have experience of” — to taste sorrow, to taste victory. The noun follows the verb: “taste” first denotes the act of tasting, then the flavour perceived, then a small portion taken as a sample, then a liking or inclination for something.

  • Chaucer’s usage: Middle English tasten covers touch, feel, and try, and the noun can mean a sample or a foretaste.
  • “A taste of” — a small quantity, a sample — is a fifteenth-century development that feeds the modern sense of a brief experience.
  • “To taste of” — to smack of, to have the flavour of — is already idiomatic in the sixteenth century.

The prefix “dis-”

Latin dis- originally meant “apart, in different directions,” as in dividere, “to divide,” and discedere, “to depart.” In later Latin and in French it developed a privative and reversive force — “not, the opposite of” — and English adopted that weakened negative prefix freely from the sixteenth century onwards, attaching it to native words as well as to loans: dislike, distrust, dishearten, disown, distaste. The formation therefore belongs to a specifically early modern English habit of negating native vocabulary with a Latin prefix, which is why distaste has no exact counterpart in French or Latin.

Sixteenth-century coinage and its first senses

The earliest English uses are concrete and gustatory: the verb means “to spoil the taste of, to make distasteful,” and the noun means “a disagreeable flavour or quality.” Only later in the same century does the modern emotional sense appear, first as a dislike of food, then as a dislike of persons, conduct, or ideas. By the seventeenth century distaste is a normal word of cultivated prose, and by the eighteenth it has settled into the polite register it still occupies.

  • Physical stage: a bad taste, an unpleasant flavour, the act of spoiling a flavour.
  • Psychological stage: a settled aversion, often restrained and often unspoken.
  • Social stage: a marker of refined sensibility — the word of a person whose taste is offended rather than whose stomach is turned.

The metaphor of the palate

The whole later history of the word is governed by the ancient equation of taste with judgement. English speaks of a taste for music, good taste in clothes, and a tasteless remark, all of which treat aesthetic discrimination as a kind of palate. Distaste is the negative pole of that metaphor: the mind’s palate rejecting what it finds unpalatable. This explains why the word can be applied to a style or a doctrine with perfect naturalness, and why it carries a faint suggestion of class and cultivation that dislike does not.

Modern usage and connotation

Today distaste is most often found in formal written English, especially biography, criticism, and political journalism. It is the preferred word when a writer wants to record opposition without raising the temperature: “he regarded the proposal with distaste” is cooler and more contemptuous than “he hated it.” Its typical objects are publicity, compromise, sentimentality, jargon, violence, and other people’s manners. In British usage especially, the word’s restraint is part of its force, since it implies a standard of taste that the speaker need not argue for.
