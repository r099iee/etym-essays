- Old English (c. 9th-11th c.) - Attested as clingan, originally meaning to shrink, shrivel, coagulate, or stick together tightly through cold, dryness, or heat.
- Middle English (c. 12th-15th c.) - The meaning shifted from internal shrinking to external grasping, with the participle clingand / clinginge describing people holding desperately onto physical objects or loved ones.
- Early Modern English (c. 16th-17th c.) - Extended in Renaissance drama and lyric poetry to depict amorous embraces, ivy wrapping around ancient oaks, and wet fabrics adhering to the body.
- 18th-19th c. - Broadened into moral, intellectual, and psychological realms, describing individuals clinging to fading traditions, political illusions, or lost hopes.
- 20th-21st c. - Adopted into developmental psychology (attachment theory and "clingy" behavior) while remaining standard in fashion, sports, and survival narratives.
