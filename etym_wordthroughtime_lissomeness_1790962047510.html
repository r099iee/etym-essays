- Old English (c. 8th–11th Century) - Ancestral root *līðe*: Meant gentle, soft, mild, calm, or physically pliable.
- Middle English (c. 1200–1450) - Sense narrowing to physical flexibility: Evolved as *lithe*, describing slender, bendable bodies, flexible willow twigs, and gentle demeanor.
- 18th Century (c. 1760s–1790s) - Suffixation and contraction: Formed as *lithesome* (*lithe* + *-some*), which phonetically contracted into *lissome* / *lissom* in poetic and dialectal English.
- 19th Century (c. 1820s–1880s) - Emergence of abstract noun *lissomeness*: Adopted enthusiastically by Romantic and Victorian poets (like Tennyson and Swinburne) and novelists to celebrate youthful physical grace, dancers, and gymnasts.
- Early 20th Century to Present - Comic and aesthetic literary fixture: Famously utilized by P. G. Wodehouse for comedic aristocratic self-praise; remains an elegant, highly expressive literary term for physical suppleness and poise.
