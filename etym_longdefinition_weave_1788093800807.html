Weave is a verb whose literal home is the loom: to weave is to make cloth, fabric, a web or a basket by interlacing two sets of threads — the longitudinal warp and the transverse weft — or by plaiting and interworking pliable materials. The noun weave names the pattern of such interlacing ('a tight weave', 'a herringbone weave'). From this ancient craft sense radiate several figurative extensions, plus an apparently separate motion sense, making 'weave' one of the richest small verbs of English.

Primary sense — textile manufacture. To weave cloth is to form it on a loom by passing the weft thread alternately over and under the warp threads; the craft is among the oldest of technologies, its tools (loom weights, woven impressions) dating to the Neolithic, and the verb has been in English continuously since Old English wefan. Domestic collocations: 'weave wool into blankets', 'woven silk', 'hand-woven'; the weaver is one of the oldest English occupational surnames.

Secondary sense — constructing a narrative or plan. From interlacing threads, weaving becomes the standard metaphor for composing anything complex out of separate strands: to weave a tale, a plot, a lie; to 'weave together' facts, voices or melodies. The bridge is intrinsic — both activities join distinct elements into an ordered, seamless whole, and both hide their joints in the finished surface. Where 'spin' (a yarn) emphasises the pulling out of a single thread, 'weave' emphasises the crossing and ordering of many; hence 'weave a web of deceit', 'woven into the story'.

Tertiary senses.

  • Magic and fate: weaving spells and enchantments — the weaver as maker of destiny — inherits the classical Fates, who spin, measure and cut the thread of life (Greek Moirai, Roman Parcae, Norse Norns), and the Indo-European image of the weaver-goddess. In fantasy fiction, as in Tolkien's 'and dreams let us weave him', weaving is a gentle, artful making of comfort and protection.
  • Motion: to weave is to move from side to side while advancing — a river weaving through a valley, a cyclist weaving through traffic. This sense is probably a different word: it surfaces in nautical and colloquial English from the 19th century and is linked by the OED to an old base seen in 'wave' and Old Norse veifa 'to swing, wave'; the visual resemblance of winding motion to crossing threads helps obscure the boundary, but the etymology is debated and the two 'weaves' may be distinct words that converged.
  • Hair and fashion: since the late 20th century, 'a weave' (noun) is a hairpiece woven into natural hair, and 'to weave' hair means to attach such extensions — a modern specialised sense built on literal interlacement, prominent in Black hair culture and now general fashion.
  • Technical textile language: 'plain weave', 'twill weave' and 'satin weave' name the three elementary interlacing structures; warp, weft, loom and shuttle are its companions; 'woven' doubled into the noun 'web' describes the spider's product and, by extension, the World Wide Web.

Usage notes.

  • Irregular verb: weave – wove – woven for the textile and figurative senses ('woven into the fabric of society'); the motion sense is commonly regularised — weave – weaved – weaved ('he weaved through the crowd'). 'Weaved' for textiles is non-standard.
  • Collocations: 'weave a web'; 'weave together'; 'woven into (the fabric of)'; 'closely woven'; 'weave in and out'.
  • Register: everyday in textile use; formal-literary and even consciously archaic in the narrative and magical senses ('weave a spell' sounds poetic); casual in the motion sense.

Cultural resonance. Weaving is a master metaphor of civilisation: the web, the fabric of society, the tapestry of history, and the text itself — Latin texere 'to weave' underlies 'text' and 'textile' alike. 'Weave' taps this ancient reservoir: to weave is to make, to unite, to create order out of strands — the verb of poets, plotters, spiders and fates alike.
