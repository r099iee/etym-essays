Arras is a biography of a trade route disguised as a noun. It does not begin, as most English words do, in the sound of some ancient action — a sitting, a striking, a carrying. It begins instead in a place, or rather in a people: the Atrebates, a Gaulish tribe of the Belgae whose name Celticists derive from *treb-, "dwelling," so that the Atrebates were in effect "the settlers," "the people of the homesteads." Julius Caesar encountered them in the Gallic Wars and recorded them among the most formidable of the Belgian peoples; their king Commius fought against Rome, submitted, and even crossed to Britain, where a second Atrebatic people had long held the chalk country around modern Silchester. It is one of the oddities of this word's history that the same tribal name lived on both sides of the Channel — in Artois and in Hampshire — long before either place made cloth. (The deeper step from Celtic *treb- to a reconstructed PIE *treb- "dwelling, building," supported by Latin trabs and Lithuanian tróba, is attractive but contested, and honest scholarship flags it rather than asserting it.)

The tribe gave its name to its chief town. The Roman civitas of the Atrebates was Nemetocenna, soon called Atrebatum, "the city of the Atrebates." Through the thousand-year drift of northern Gallo-Romance, Latin Atrebatum crumbled into Arteis and finally Arras, the city of the Scarpe. Medieval Arras became one of the great commercial towns of northern Europe: wool came up from England, dye from the Mediterranean, and the looms of Arras turned them into the finest and most expensive hangings in Europe. By the fourteenth century arras was already shifting from a place-name to a product-name, the way champagne or cashmere would shift in later centuries. English inventories and wills of the period list "arres," "arras work," and "hangings of Arras" as the most prized furnishings a household could own; Chaucer plants the word in the Knight's Tale to signal wealth and ceremony.

The decisive semantic turn is from commodity to concealment. Because these great woven walls were heavy, permanent, and opaque, they became the natural hiding-places of medieval interiors — doorways were masked by them, alcoves screened by them, eavesdroppers concealed behind them. The physics of the object quietly wrote itself into the meaning of the word. When Shakespeare placed Polonius "behind the arras" in Hamlet, he was drawing on a hundred years of stage practice and real-world practice alike; the scene of the old councillor stabbed through the hanging turned the phrase into a proverb of hidden observation and of the fatal danger of listening behind a veil of silk and wool. From the sixteenth century on, the word carried a double charge in English: sumptuous display on its face, concealed presence behind it — a decorative surface that is always, implicitly, a screen.

The centuries that followed eroded the trade and then the word. The Arras workshops declined after the sixteenth century, the French religious wars scattered their weavers, and ordinary English speech gradually retreated from arras to tapestry and wall hanging. The word survived in the fixed phrase "behind the arras," in historical fiction, in stage directions, and in the deliberate archaism of literary allusion. Its modern life is therefore almost wholly metaphorical: a modern writer uses arras not to name a textile but to summon a texture — thickness, craft, opacity, concealment. The present passage is the perfect instance: "the arras of cryptic silence." The silence of the cave is imagined as a heavy, woven, decorative curtain, beautiful and complete, but engineered to hide; behind it, "time and again," the listeners seem to hear a faint whisper, "the sigh of sunken seas far down at some hemispheric depth." Everything in the word's six-hundred-year English career converges in that image — the medieval wealth of the hangings, the Shakespearean treachery of the hidden listener, the thickness that converts an absence of sound into a present, palpable, concealing thing.

In miniature, the word's trajectory is a complete lesson in lexical change: a tribal ethnonym became a city name; the city name became a product name; the product name acquired a hiding connotation through the physical properties of the product and one immortal play; and the concrete noun receded from daily use precisely as it was freed for metaphor. Arras today is not a word about textiles at all, but a word about what textiles can do — conceal, insulate, dignify — and about what lies, wrapped in beauty, behind them.
