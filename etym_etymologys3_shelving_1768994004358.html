Shelving is a biography of the slice. Its distant ancestor is the PIE root *skel-, "to cut, to split, to cleave," a root whose entire family is governed by a single idea: that things are made by being split off from a larger mass. Greek skállein, "to hoe, to scrape," belongs to it; so, probably, does the mysterious Germanic word skull, the "shell" of the head; and so, certainly, does the common Germanic word for the husk or covering split away from the kernel — Proto-Germanic *skaljō, which became Old English sciell, our shell, and Old Norse skel and German Schale. The relevant branch for our word is the Germanic term for the object split off to serve as a flat board or ledge: Proto-Germanic *skelf-, whose Old English descendant scylfe named a board resting against a wall and, already in the earliest records, a ledge of rock. The semantic core is simple and ancient: a shelf is a slice — a flat piece cut or split away from a larger block, whether the block is a tree, a wall, or a cliff face. From that single image the English word shelf grew two lives. The first life is domestic. From the fourteenth century a shelf is the household board on which things are laid, and to shelve means to place things upon such boards — a sense that gives us shelving for the whole apparatus of shelves. And from the domestic verb came a subtle managerial metaphor, recorded from the seventeenth century: to shelve a plan or a bill is to lay it aside on a shelf, to postpone or abandon it. That metaphor is still alive in modern English: a proposal is "shelved for now," a bill is "shelved in committee," and shelving has become the standard bureaucratic word for indefinite postponement — a quiet, unviolent verb that stores a thing out of sight instead of killing it. The second life is geological, and it is the one the passage uses. A "shelf of rock" is a flat ledge in a cliff or shore, and by the late sixteenth century the verb to shelve had come to mean "to slope gradually," describing a shore or seabed that descends not in a cliff but in a series of ledges, each one a shelf left behind by the water. The geological sense was probably born at sea: sailors and hydrographers spoke of shelving shores and shelving bottoms — shores that shoal gently, that a boat can approach, that a man can walk up from the water. The sound of the word followed the grammar: Middle English schelf became modern shelf, and the verb shelve, with its voiced final consonant, split from the noun in the sixteenth century, so that the pair shelf/shelve now behaves like the older pairs of leaf/leaves and wolf/wolves — a fossilized reminder of the Old English sound system, in which a final /f/ became /v/ when a vowel followed. Burroughs's Martian shore "shelves" high above the dry riverbed: the word lets the reader see, without being told, the vanished sea that cut those terraces. The passage's "shelving shore" is a shore that rises in flat stages — each a ledge left by an older waterline — and the participle makes the landscape itself a staircase, the fossil record of waters that have slowly receded. The word's odd beauty is that the homely household shelf and the grand geological shelving shore are the same root: the land is furnished with shelves as a wall might be, each one a flat shelf cut out of the steep.
  • PIE *skel- "to cut, split" — the ancient idea that flat things are split off from a mass.
  • Proto-Germanic *skelf- — the flat board or ledge, the split-off slice.
  • Old English scylfe — already both a board and a ledge of rock; the double life is ancient.
  • 14th–16th c. — shelf the domestic board; shelve "to place on shelves".
  • 16th c. — shelve "to slope gradually" of shores, born among sailors and hydrographers.
  • 17th c. — shelve "to postpone, lay aside a project"; the bureaucratic metaphor.
  • 20th c. — shelving filter in audio engineering; the stepped curve echoes the stepped shore.
In modern usage shelving lives three lives at once: the noun for a set of storage shelves, the verb of postponement ("we're shelving the plan"), and the rare but vivid participle of a gradual, stepped incline, still at home in nautical and geological description. No modern word has quite its combination of the domestic and the geological — a slope built, ledge by ledge, by the slow withdrawal of water, furnished like a cupboard by a vanished sea. The word that began as a slice, a thing cut away from a tree or a cliff, has ended by naming the whole architecture of storage, the whole politics of delay, and the whole history of a dry Martian basin — and in each of its lives it is still, at bottom, a shelf: a flat place made by splitting, on which something is laid or from which something has receded.
