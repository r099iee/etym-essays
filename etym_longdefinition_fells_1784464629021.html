Fells (the plural of fell used as a landscape word) are high, barren hills, rocky uplands, or mountain moors — especially the treeless heights of northern England and related Scandinavian terrain. The word is regional and topographical rather than a generic poetic synonym for "hill": it names a particular kind of wild, elevated, often bleak landform.

Core sense. A fell is a high, bare, uncultivated hill or stretch of upland — the kind of windswept height above the treeline, covered in grass, heather, or rock, that dominates the English Lake District, the Pennines, and the hills of the Scottish Borders and northern England. The plural fells names a whole expanse or range of such heights: "the Lakeland fells," "the high fells."

Geography and register. Fell is the living local word of northern England and southern Scotland (and, through Norse settlement, of related Scandinavian countryside), and it has passed into standard English as a precise, mildly literary term. It carries rugged, northern colour: fells are walked, climbed, run, and grazed by sheep; the word belongs to fell-walkers, fell-runners, and the farmers of the uplands as much as to poets.

How it differs from its neighbours.

  • mountain — the general high peak mass; usually higher and more imposing than a fell.
  • hill — lower and broader in ordinary use, without the fell's specific bleak, northern barrenness.
  • moor — open uncultivated upland, often wet heath or bog; a fell can be a moor, but a moor need not be a fell (and many fells are dry, rocky heights).
  • ridge — a linear crest, one element of a fell skyline rather than the whole upland.
  • peak / summit — the topmost point of a height, not the hill itself.
  • tundra — treeless cold-region plain; broader and climatic rather than topographic, and not British.
  • downs / wolds — southern English rolling chalk or limestone uplands; softer, greener, and gentler than northern fells.

Polysemy: the many unrelated meanings of the spelling fell. Because fells is the plural of a word with several homonyms, it is worth separating the trees:

  • Landscape noun (this card): a high barren hill or upland — from Old Norse fjall.
  • Adjective fell "fierce, cruel, deadly" — from Old English felle and kin; a different history (e.g., "a fell blow").
  • Verb fell (past of fall) — "the tree fell"; and verb to fell "to cut down" — a causative built on fall; different histories again.
  • Noun fell (dialect) "a pelt, an animal's skin" — Old English fell "skin," a separate word unrelated to the landscape sense.

These are homonyms sharing only the spelling and pronunciation; only the landscape sense is this card.

Modern technical and cultural senses of the landscape word.

  • Fell-walking / fell-running: the English sports of walking or racing over high fell country; "the fells" is the standard name of the terrain itself.
  • Fell pony / fell sheep: breeds named for the fells they were bred on — the Fell pony of Cumbria, the Herdwick sheep of the Lakeland fells.
  • Place-names: fell appears in hundreds of northern place-names (Scafell, Seatallan, Whernside — often in the Norse -fell element) and as a common noun in Lake District guides.
  • Geology / geography: "fell" appears in scientific writing as a regional term for bare upland, alongside fell-field, rocky frost-shattered upland terrain.
  • Figurative use: rarer, but "the high fells" can stand metaphorically for remote, harsh, or exposed places — heights that are beautiful and dangerous at once.

Historical bridge of the senses. The landscape word entered English from Old Norse fjall (also fell), "mountain, high rocky ground," carried into northern England and Scotland by the Viking settlers of the ninth–eleventh centuries. It survived in the dialects of the Danelaw, in place-names, and in the language of upland farming while standard English used hill and mountain; the eighteenth-century Lake District poets and guides to the Lakes and, later, the fell-walking movement carried it into national literary and outdoor use.

In the card's context. In the dwarves' song — "In hollow halls beneath the fells" — Tolkien uses northern English and Norse landscape vocabulary so that the dwarf-homes sit under real-feeling high country: fells above, hollow halls below. The plural sketches a whole skyline of high, wild ground rather than a single named peak, making geography itself part of the mythic northern setting.
