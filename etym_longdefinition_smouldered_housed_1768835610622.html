smouldered (verb, past tense of 'smoulder'; American spelling 'smoldered'): burned slowly and smokily without flame; underwent slow, stifled combustion. In the Gormenghast passage the discarded carvings do not blaze — they 'smoulder to ashes in obedience to the law.' The fire is a muted, obedient fire, one that destroys without drama, exactly as the law commands: the burning is smothered, choked, kept below the level of flame. The word's tone is one of suppression — heat without light, decay without spectacle, a destruction that is almost a form of suffocation. The unselected carvings perish quietly, their artistic effort stifled into grey ash rather than celebrated in pyre-flame, and the adverb 'innumerable' makes the silent waste more terrible than any conflagration could be. Polysemy of 'smoulder' — the senses it carries today
  • Literal combustion sense (core): to burn slowly with smoke but no flame, of damp fuel, peat, embers, fires banked down or tightly packed — 'the fire smouldered all night'; also of charcoal, smouldering heaps, and buried combustibles.
  • Figurative emotion sense (from the 19th c., now dominant in everyday use): of suppressed feeling — anger, resentment, desire, jealousy, rebellion — burning inwardly without erupting: 'resentment smouldered in him for years'; the emotion is imagined as banked embers waiting to catch.
  • Figurative of conditions and conflicts (19th–21st c.): a dispute, feud, crisis or 'low-grade war' smoulders when it persists without breaking out into open conflict — 'the ethnic tensions smouldered beneath the surface'.
  • Of bodies and sensuality (literary): a person 'smoulders' with restrained passion or magnetism; a glance 'smoulders' — intensity held just below flame, often with erotic charge.
  • Register: the literal sense is technical and slightly archaic in everyday speech; the figurative sense carries the word's modern weight, pairing damp literal smoke with simmering emotion — heat held just below the level of light.
The semantic bridge is the physics of fire. A smouldering fire is one that burns incompletely, stifled by its own smoke or by lack of oxygen — and a stifled fire is the perfect image of a stifled feeling. The verb's buried sense of being 'smothered' (its Middle English ancestor smolderen originally meant 'to suffocate') is what connects combustion to emotion: what burns without vent is precisely what burns longest and most dangerously.
housed (verb, past tense and past participle of 'house'): sheltered, lodged, given a dwelling; provided with a permanent protective home; kept, stored or accommodated under cover. To say that the choicest carvings 'were still housed in the Hall of the Bright Carvings' is to say far more than that they were kept or stored: they are inhabitants, given a home within the castle, treated as honoured residents rather than objects. The passive form carries a ceremonial gravity — the chosen carvings have been taken into the keeping of stone walls and are safe from the smouldering fate of their rejected peers. Where the smouldered carvings vanish into ash, the housed ones persist as 'bright' presences, and the two verbs of the sentence form an implicit contrast between destruction and sanctuary. Polysemy of 'house' as a verb — the senses it carries today
  • To shelter / give dwelling to persons (core, Old English): 'the refugees were housed in temporary camps' — to provide a home or lodging; the oldest verbal sense, of putting people under a roof.
  • To contain / accommodate objects (extended): 'the museum houses the largest collection of carvings' — of buildings, institutions and containers giving a home to things; storage with care, permanence and honour.
  • To store or keep goods (practical): seed-corn, tools, grain and machinery are 'housed' under cover for the season — the agricultural and industrial sense of protection against weather.
  • Technical engineering sense: a component is 'housed' in a casing, socket or housing — to be enclosed and protected within a fitted structure; 'to house' a bearing, a cable, a mechanism.
  • Of a body accommodating something (figurative): 'she housed the secret for decades' — a person or institution containing something within, an extension of the containing sense to the abstract.
  • Register: as a verb, 'house' is formal and capable of gravity; unlike 'store' or 'keep', it implies belonging, protection and residence — a home, not a shelf.
The semantic bridge is the noun itself. The verb is simply the noun 'house' set to work: to house is to give something a house, and every later sense — containing, casing, storing, sheltering — preserves the root idea of a protective dwelling. In the passage the ceremonial contrast turns on exactly this: the smouldered carvings have no house; the 'choicest' have been given the Hall of the Bright Carvings as their home, and are 'housed' in the full dignity of the word.

Relation of the two words in the passage: the sentence is built on an antithesis of preservation. 'Smouldered' describes the obedient, stifled annihilation of the rejected carvings — heat without flame, destruction without honour; 'housed' describes the permanent shelter of the chosen ones — care, residence and safety. Between them the two verbs divide the fate of every carving: to become ash or to become an inhabitant of the Bright Hall.
