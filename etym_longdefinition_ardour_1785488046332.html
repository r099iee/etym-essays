“Ardour” means intense, burning enthusiasm, fervour, or zeal — and the author of the chapter’s survey reaches for it to name “this vast demonstration of scholarly and critical ardour” that fills the old prosodic handbooks, “ranging from the comically idiosyncratic to the laboriously obvious.” The word is chosen for its root sense of fire: ardour is the passion that burns, and it therefore does justice both to the genuine love these books display and to the slightly alarming heat of that love — the heavy capitals, the savage footnotes, the snippets of verse triumphantly displayed like newly-discovered zoological specimens. “Scholarly and critical ardour” suggests writers who burned with devotion to their subject even when the results were absurd, and the collocation “ranging from the comically idiosyncratic to the laboriously obvious” measures the width of the conflagration: from eccentric genius to plodding dullness, the flame is the same. The tone of the whole passage is affectionate mockery — ardour can be admirable and comic at once, and the author, a connoisseur of the absurd, honours the heat while smiling at the shapes it takes. The word is richer and older than its modern use suggests, with several distinguishable senses:
  • Core modern sense — intense, sustained enthusiasm or devotion: a glowing, consuming commitment to a cause, study, pursuit, or person (“the ardour of the collector”, “scholarly ardour”). Formal, literary, and slightly bookish.
  • Romantic sense — burning love and desire: ardour as the passion of the lover, the fire of desire; common in poetry and romantic prose (“the ardour of young love”).
  • Religious and devotional sense — zeal of worship: the burning love of God and the soul, the fervour of prayer and devotion; close to fervour and zeal but warmer and less doctrinal.
  • Historical physical sense — burning heat, flame (now obsolete except in poetry): the original meaning, borrowed from Latin ardor, “heat, flame”; a purely physical, almost meteorological heat, still felt as a poetic archaism.
  • Intellectual and critical sense — ardour in scholarship: the burning devotion of the researcher or critic to a subject; the exact sense of the passage, where the flame is applied to prosody and the study of metre.
The historical bridge is the metaphor of fire. Latin ardor meant “heat, flame” — the physical burning of a fire — and Latin authors, who had long used fire as the master-metaphor of emotion, pressed it into service for every passion that burns in the mind: love, courage, eagerness, rage. Cicero speaks of the ardor of the spirit, Virgil sets ardor in the breast of warriors, and Ovid makes the lover’s ardor the fire that will not be quenched. Old French took ardor as ardeur, “heat, passion, zeal”, and Middle English borrowed it around 1400, at first still meaning “burning heat” but already reaching for the figurative fire. The British spelling is ardour (from French), the American ardor (a Latinizing respelling); both derive from the same ardor. The boundaries against near-synonyms are sharp. “Enthusiasm” (from the Greek for “god-possessed”) is modern, social, and comparatively light — one is enthusiastic about a hobby, and the word has been drained of mystery since the eighteenth century. “Zeal” implies religious or political intensity, often partisan and combative, and carries a built-in hint of excess or fanaticism (zealot). “Passion” is far broader, covering love, anger, and suffering, and can be uncontrolled or turbulent. “Fervour” (from Latin fervere, “to boil”) suggests religious warmth and devotion, closer to prayer than to argument. “Eagerness” is restless, impatient wanting, with no sense of depth. “Ardour” is the most consistently fiery of them all: it means a sustained, glowing, consuming heat of feeling — which is precisely what the passage’s vast demonstration of scholarly and critical ardour supplies.
