PIE *rep- 'to snatch, seize' (reconstructed; attested through Latin rapere; note that this *rep- is homonymous with a separate PIE *rep- 'to crawl, slink' — the two are distinct roots, and the rapere family is the secure source here) → Latin rapere 'to seize, snatch, grab, carry off' (attested from the earliest Latin, 3rd c. BCE; source of rapidus 'swift, rapid', rapiō 'I seize') → Latin subripere, also assimilated surripere, 'to snatch away secretly, to steal' — sub- 'under, from below, secretly' + rapere; the b of sub- assimilates to the following r, giving surripere (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero) → Latin surreptus, past participle of surripere, 'stolen, snatched away secretly' → Latin surrepticius (also spelled surreptītius) 'stolen, furtive, secret, done by stealth' — an adjective in -icius/-itius built on the participle (Classical to Late Latin) → Medieval Latin surrepticius 'obtained by stealth or fraud' (the register of canon law and notarial documents, describing documents or gains obtained by concealment) → English surreptitious 'secret, stealthy, done or obtained by fraud or stealth' (borrowed c. 1430–1450, partly through Old French surreptice) → English surreptitiously 'in a stealthy, unauthorized manner' — surreptitious + the adverbial suffix -ly (first attested late 16th c., c. 1590) → SURREPTITIOUSLY (current form). Debated/nuanced stages: Classical orthography prefers subripere (with -b-), while the adjective surrepticius with the assimilated -rr- is the form English took directly; both spellings are attested, and the difference is spelling convention, not a different derivation. The PIE root *rep- 'to snatch' is homonymous with *rep- 'to crawl', which has confused older dictionaries; the Latin evidence (rapere, rapina 'robbery', rapin), keeps the two firmly apart. English borrowed the adjective from the Latin form, and the adverb was built in English with the native suffix -ly — no separate Latin or French adverb underlies it.
