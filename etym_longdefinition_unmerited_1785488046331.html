“Unmerited” means “not deserved, not earned” — and in the sentence that closes the survey of the old prosodic handbooks, the library books’ “undisturbed repose on the library shelves is not unmerited”: a double negative that, with studied courtesy, concedes that the books do, on the whole, deserve their rest. The passage has been cataloguing these volumes’ defects — their lists are “unwieldy”, their prose thick with scanned quotations, their claims “absolute”, their footnotes “savagely civil” — and now comes the qualification: “Very few fail to offer some illumination of a corner or two… but by and large their undisturbed repose on the library shelves is not unmerited.” The construction “not unmerited” is deliberate litotes — the classical figure of understatement used for politeness and irony. It is at once a compliment and a sentence of retirement: the books are honoured and shelved, praised and put to rest. “Unmerited” carries an ethical, almost legal weight: merit is what one has earned by desert, and to be unmerited is to be without just claim; the word sets the books before a scale of justice on which they have weighed in and earned their quiet corner. The word is morphologically transparent and semantically centred, but it carries several distinguishable senses and fields:
  • Core sense — not deserved or earned: applied to praise, blame, reward, punishment, treatment, or reputation (“unmerited praise”, “unmerited criticism”). The central, fully modern sense, formal and judicial in tone.
  • Theological sense — unmerited grace or favour: in Christian theology, God’s grace is unmerited favour — given freely, not earned by works; a key Reformation and liturgical term (gratia non merita).
  • Ethical/legal sense — without just claim or right: describing a benefit or burden that justice does not assign to the person who receives it; the word sets the matter before a scale of desert.
  • Litotes usage — “not unmerited”: the double negative meaning “well deserved”, used for courtesy, irony, or studied understatement; the exact construction in the passage, and a hallmark of measured, half-teasing critical prose.
  • Personal/emotional sense — unmerited suffering: misfortune or pain that falls on a person who did nothing to deserve it (an unmerited blow, unmerited grief); a common ethical and literary use.
The historical bridge is the noun merit, which developed from a share-allotment root into a word of earned worth. Originally meritum meant “what one has earned, desert, worth”, and also “a service done, a kindness”; in medieval theology it became the spiritual reward earned by good works, and from that religious economy of deserts modern “merit” grew — earned worth, the claim one has established by labour, virtue, or service. “Unmerited” is the simple negation of that claim: the negative prefix un- + the past participle merited. The boundaries against near-synonyms are worth drawing. “Undeserved” is the plain, everyday word (undeserved praise, undeserved punishment) and lacks the measured, quasi-legal tone of unmerited. “Unwarranted” implies lacking justification or reasonable grounds and is more at home with accusations, conclusions, or intrusions. “Unearned” emphasizes reward or payment without the corresponding labour, and has a faintly economic cast (unearned income). “Gratuitous” means given or done freely, often without provocation, and carries a distinctly negative charge (a gratuitous insult). “Unjustified” and “groundless” concern the absence of a reason or basis rather than the absence of desert. “Unmerited” alone combines desert, justice, and moral worth in a single dignified register, which is exactly what the passage needs for its measured, half-teasing tribute to the sleeping books.
