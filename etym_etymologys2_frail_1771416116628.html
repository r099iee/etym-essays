Derivation chain for FRAIL: 1. Proto-Indo-European *bhreg- — “to break” (reconstructed; one of the best-attested PIE breaking-roots, the source of Latin frangere and, by the Germanic path, of English break itself). 2. Latin frangere — “to break” (frango, frangere, fregi, fractum; attested from the 3rd century BCE; the stem frag- underlies fragilis). 3. Latin fragilis — “easily broken, brittle, fragile” (adjective in -ilis from the frag- stem; attested 1st century BCE, e.g., in Lucretius and Cicero). 4. Latin fragilitas — “frailty, fragility, weakness” (abstract noun from fragilis; classical). 5. Old French fraile — “weak, fragile, frail” (the regular Romance development of fragilis, with loss/syncope of the medial voiced consonant; attested from the 10th century). 6. Middle English frele / fraile — “morally weak; weak in body; easily broken” (borrowed from Old French in the 14th century). 7. Modern English frail — “weak, delicate, easily broken or damaged” (modern spelling settled by the 15th–16th century). Final node: FRAIL. Note on the nuance: English frail and fragile are doublets — both from Latin fragilis, frail through the natural Old French development and fragile borrowed directly from Latin in the 16th century — a divergence that produced the subtle modern distinction (weakness vs. brittleness). The chain is secure at every stage; the only point worth flagging is that *bhreg- has a very wide set of reflexes across Indo-European (English break itself is from the same root by the Germanic path), making it one of the best-attested PIE breaking-roots.
