INTRANSIGENT (/ɪnˈtræn.sɪ.dʒənt/ or /ɪnˈtræn.zɪ.dʒənt/) is an adjective (and occasionally a noun) describing a person, group, political faction, or philosophical position that refuses to compromise, moderate its demands, yield to persuasion, or come to an agreement.

The term belongs to an elevated, formal register, frequently appearing in political science, diplomacy, jurisprudence, and literary criticism. It denotes an attitude of unbending firmness that regards compromise not as a pragmatic necessity or a virtue of civil discourse, but as an unacceptable betrayal of core principles, ideological purity, or vital interests.

Primary & Secondary Senses:

  • Refusing Compromise in Negotiation (Diplomatic & Political Sense): Exhibiting absolute inflexibility during treaties, peace talks, collective bargaining, or legislative disputes. An intransigent party refuses to make concessions, resulting in diplomatic deadlock, government shutdowns, or prolonged strikes.
  • Dogmatic Adherence to Ideology (Ideological & Intellectual Sense): Characterized by an unyielding commitment to a theoretical, political, or theological dogma. It describes thinkers, activists, or movements that reject moderation or reformism, insisting on total revolution, complete purity, or nothing.
  • Unbending Personal Demeanor (Interpersonal Sense): Describing an individual whose temperament is utterly unyielding, intractable, and resistant to emotional appeal, reason, or friendly persuasion in personal relationships or corporate management.
  • Substantive Noun Use (An Intransigent / The Intransigents): Historically and politically, a person who adheres strictly to an extreme, uncompromising political program; an extremist or irreconcilable radical who rejects centrist coalitions.

Polysemy & Semantic Bridges:

  • The Legal/Transactional Bridge (From 'Transact' to 'Uncompromising'): The root of intransigent lies in the Latin verb transigere ("to drive through, settle a dispute, make a transaction"). In Roman law and civil jurisprudence, a transactio was an out-of-court settlement where both parties yielded part of their claims to avoid litigation. Adding the negative prefix in- created intransigent: literally, "one who refuses to settle or transact."
  • The 19th-Century Revolutionary Bridge: The modern political word entered English from 19th-century Spanish politics (los intransigentes), where it designated extreme left-wing federalist Republicans who refused to collaborate with monarchists or moderate republicans during the Cantonal Revolution of 1873. The term was quickly imported into French (les intransigeants) and applied to radical political factions and even avant-garde painters (the Impressionists) who refused to conform to academic salon standards.
  • Transition from Radical Pejorative to Formal Lexicon: Originally used by political moderates as a reproach against dangerous fanatics who were "impossible to work with," the term gradually shed its purely partisan sting in 20th-century English, becoming a standard analytical adjective for any severe, unyielding posture in diplomacy, labor relations, or moral philosophy.
