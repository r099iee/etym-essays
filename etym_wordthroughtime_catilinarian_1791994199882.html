- 1st c. BCE — Coined in Classical Latin as Catilinārius to designate the armed plot, letters, and partisans of Lucius Sergius Catilina, carrying an aura of terror, state sacrilege, and civic emergency.
- 1st–5th c. CE — Institutionalized in imperial Roman rhetorical schools as the supreme pedagogical model of forensic prosecution and dramatic political declamation.
- 16th–17th c. — Adopted into Early Modern English through Renaissance humanist translations of Cicero and Sallust; frequently applied to Elizabethan and Jacobean Catholic plots (such as the Gunpowder Plot) as the definitive term for treasonous domestic cabals.
- 18th–19th c. — Pervasive in British Parliamentary debates and American revolutionary literature (e.g., Hamilton and Madison in The Federalist) to condemn demagogues attempting to exploit public debt or factional divisions.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains high-register literary and political usage, characterizing both scathing, theatrical parliamentary oratory and elite-driven subversion of constitutional norms.
