purported is the past participle of the verb purport, used in the source as a passive verbal adjective: the Thesaurus Ciceronianus was purported to be a compendium, that is, it was claimed, held out or reputed to be one, without the historian endorsing the claim in his own voice. The word carries its characteristic modern evidential charge: the status of “compendium of all pure Ciceronian language” is attributed to the book by others, and the following clause — that some scholars refused any expression not found in it — shows that the claim possessed real authority even though the writer carefully declines to assert it.

Morphologically it is purport + -ed, and it occupies three grammatical slots: the past tense and past participle of the verb (“the letter purported to reveal the plot”); a participial adjective before a noun (“a purported expert”); and the passive frame be purported to + infinitive (“the manuscript is purported to date from the ninth century”). The adverb purportedly performs the same hedging work at sentence level.

Core senses of the verb

  • To claim or profess to be something, especially doubtfully: “he purports to be a doctor” implies that the claim may not survive scrutiny. This is the dominant modern sense and the one at work in the source.
  • To have as its meaning or intention; to signify: said of words, documents, statutes or symbols. “The purport of the clause” is its tenor or drift; this is the oldest English sense and remains current in legal and formal prose.
  • To intend or purpose (archaic): the verb was once a near-synonym of intend, a use fossilised in the noun sense “intention, design”.

The noun purport

  • Meaning, gist, tenor: “the purport of his remarks was that reform could wait.” Neutral, slightly formal, common in law and journalism.
  • Legal sense: the meaning and effect of an instrument as construed by a court — “the purport of the deed”, “the purport and effect of section 3”. Here the word is technical and non-pejorative, unlike the adjective.
  • Intention, object: an archaic or literary use, as in “to what purport?” meaning “to what end?”

The adjective purported and its evidential work

  • Alleged, ostensible, reputed: “a purported heir”, “a purported cure”, “the purported link between the two events”. The adjective marks a proposition as somebody else’s claim, not the writer’s assertion.
  • Legal usage: a “purported will” or “purported contract” is a document that claims to be one but may fail in law. The word signals contested validity rather than outright falsity: not proven false, simply not established.
  • Journalistic hedge: modern news style uses purported to report a claim without vouching for it, and partly to avoid libel. It is the written equivalent of raised eyebrows; repeated use can shade into irony or insinuation.

Register, connotation, collocations

The word is formal and bookish. It belongs to broadsheet journalism, legal drafting, intelligence reporting and academic prose rather than to conversation. Its emotional temperature is cool: it does not accuse anyone of lying, but it withholds belief. Typical partners are abstract nouns of identity, origin, purpose or causation — purported heir, expert, author, message, motive, benefit, sighting, coup — and the frame purported to be / to show / to prove. Because it is a hedge, it can be used honourably (genuine scholarly caution) or manipulatively (smuggling an allegation past the reader while retaining deniability).

Semantic bridge: the sense “claim” grew out of “carry”. Latin portāre “to carry” gave French purporter, “to carry forth”: a document carries a meaning, and a person carries a title or a claim before the world. From “what a text carries” it was a short step to “what a person puts forward”, and from there to the modern, sceptical “puts forward without warrant”.

Why the word matters here

Mario Nizzoli’s Thesaurus Ciceronianus (1535) was a landmark of Ciceronian lexicography: it gathered the vocabulary of Cicero for imitation by Renaissance schoolboys and orators, and it helped fix the sixteenth-century cult of purus Cicero. To say that it was “purported to be a compendium of all pure Ciceronian language” is to report a contemporary reputation while keeping the historian’s distance. The book claimed completeness; the Ciceronian purists policed the boundary of admissible words accordingly. The participle thus does double duty — it describes a claim about a dictionary, and it quietly warns the modern reader that the claim was a programme rather than a neutral fact.
