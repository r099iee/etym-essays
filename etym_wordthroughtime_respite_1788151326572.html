- [13th c.] - Entered English as a legal borrowing: Middle English respit names the time a court grants a debtor or defendant — postponement, reprieve; register technical, legal, administrative.
- [14th–15th c.] - Widens to everyday relief: rest from labour, pain, siege, or weather; “without respite” becomes the idiom for relentless pressure.
- [16th–17th c.] - Literary and devotional prose adopt it for mercy and mortal reprieve; the “day of judgment” respite enters homiletic English.
- [18th–19th c.] - Settles as a formal but common noun for any lull; in medicine, prisons, and debt law “respite” remains the sanctioned pause.
- [20th c.] - “Respite care” enters social-policy jargon as relief for family caregivers; pronunciation shifts toward /ˈrɛspaɪt/ in general usage.
- [21st c.] - Both meanings — precise legal postponement and general relief — coexist; the everyday “breather” sense dominates speech while the legal sense keeps the word in statute and judgment.
