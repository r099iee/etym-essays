MAIN CHAIN (traditional OED route): PIE *bʰeh₂- "to shine, to gleam, to appear" (reconstructed; no date) → Ancient Greek φαίνω (phaínō) "to bring to light, to show, to make appear" (attested 8th c. BCE; formed on the zero grade *bʰh₂-n-) → Ancient Greek φαντασία (phantasía) "the making visible; an apparition; an impression on the mind" (5th c. BCE) → Latin phantasia "fancy, imagination, apparition" (1st c. BCE; borrowed from Greek) → Vulgar Latin *phentasiāre "to be oppressed with visions; to gasp and be breathless, as one who faints" (late 3rd–7th c. CE; reconstructed, not directly attested) → Old French pantaisier "to be breathless, to be distressed, to pant" (12th c.; beside the adjective pantois "out of breath, panting") → Middle English panten "to breathe rapidly and spasmodically" (14th c.; via Anglo-Norman panta(i)sier) → English pant "to breathe short and quickly" (16th c.) → FINAL: English panting "breathing rapidly in short quick breaths" (present participle; the form used in the Narnia passage). DEBATED / NUANCED NOTES: (1) A rival account, defended in parts of Romance scholarship, derives the word from Latin pantex / panticem "paunch, belly," giving a verb *pantiāre "to breathe hard from the belly"; the Greek phantasia route is the traditional one favoured by the OED, but the belly etymology has modern defenders, and the two compete for the word's true origin. (2) The intermediate Vulgar Latin stage is reconstructed, not attested — its exact shape is inferred from the Old French outcome. (3) The Greek φαίνω cluster gave English its learned cousins by a separate route: phantom, phantasm, phantasmagoria, phenomenal, phenomenon, emphasis, diaphanous (and, via Latin phantasia and Old French fantasie, fantasy and fancy) — these are siblings of pant, not ancestors.
