Sloe is the small round blue-black fruit of the blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): a hedgerow plum no bigger than a marble, dusted with a pale bloom, brutally sour and astringent when raw, yet prized after frost or steeped in gin, and one of the oldest wild harvests of Britain and northern Europe.

In the source line the word closes a catalogue of hedgerow bounty — apple, thorn, and nut and sloe — where it stands for late-autumn wild food itself: the fruits a traveller can pick without garden, orchard, or permission, and Tolkien places it last for its music, since sloe rhymes and chimes against the longer words before it, a dark full stop of sound as well as sense.

Core meaning in detail: botanically the sloe is a drupe, a single-stoned fruit with green flesh under ink-dark skin, ripening from September into November and sweetening only after frost bursts its cells or the picker’s patience outlasts its tannins: birds, mice, and badgers eat it freely, humans mostly cook, jam, or infuse it, and the blackthorn’s savage spines guard every cluster, so gathering sloes means scratched hands for purple reward.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Blackthorn fruit (primary, from Old English): the berry-like plum of wayside thickets, and the bridge is direct inheritance, since Old English slā already named exactly this fruit on exactly this thorn.
  • Sloe gin and sloe wine (derived drink sense, from the 1700s): a ruby liqueur made by steeping sloes in gin with sugar, and the bridge is culinary transformation, since the inedible raw fruit becomes the celebrated winter cordial, served at Christmas and after shoots.
  • Sloe-eyed, sloe-black (colour adjective, poetic): eyes or hair dark as sloes, and the bridge is colour borrowing, since the fruit’s bloom-dusted midnight blue is darker and softer than mere black, prized by songwriters and romancers for exotic depth.
  • Blackthorn wood and winter (transferred landscape sense): sloe thickets, sloe time, the sloe season, and the bridge runs from fruit to habitat and calendar, since where sloes hang the thorn rules and their ripening dates the walker’s year.
  • Slang for dull or slow persons (rare, punning): wordplay on slow, since sloe and slow are homophones in most accents, exploited by headline writers for sloe justice or sloe progress, and the bridge is pure sound, not botany.

Register and connotation: sloe is countryside in a syllable: hedges, frosts, cold fingers, and patience, carrying none of the orchard’s pride and all of the thicket’s gift, and its flavour story — mouth-puckering sourness turned by frost, sugar, or gin into celebrated depth — makes it the emblem of wild things improved by waiting, a moral as well as a fruit, since no other hedgerow harvest teaches patience so sharply: the raw mouthful punishes haste while the frosted, sugared, or gin-steeped jar rewards those who waited for winter to finish its slow chemistry.
