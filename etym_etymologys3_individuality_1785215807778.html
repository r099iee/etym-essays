**The indivisible self: how INDIVIDUALITY came to mean the self that cannot be split off** The biography of INDIVIDUALITY is a story of a word that began in logic and mathematics, moved into metaphysics, and only late in its life became a moral and political ideal. Its literal, historical meaning is "indivisibility," and every later sense is an echo of that ancient claim: that the individual is that which cannot be divided or dissolved. **Two roots meet in Latin** The word is assembled from three Latin pieces, resting on two Indo-European roots. The first root is *dwis-, the Indo-European word for "two," which gave Latin the prefix dis-, "apart, asunder, in two" — the same element seen in Latin discumbere, English dismember, and the Greek dí- of dichotomy. The second is *weidh-, "to separate, divide," the root of Latin vidua, "widow," literally "a woman separated" from her husband, and of English widow through the Germanic branch. Out of these two roots Latin built the verb dividere, "to force apart" — to split something in two — whose past participle divisus gave English divide and division, and whose derivatives include dividuus, "divisible," and, with the negative in-, individuus, "indivisible." In the hands of Cicero and Lucretius, individuus was already being pressed into philosophical service: Lucretius uses individuum corpus for the "indivisible body," the atom of Epicurean physics, and it is no accident that the Greek word for "atom," á-tomos, literally means "that which cannot be cut." Latin Christianity and the medieval schools completed the transfer: the noun individuum was fixed as the technical rendering of the Greek term, so that the "individual" entered European thought as the ultimate, undivided member of a species — the smallest thing that still counts as a thing. **From indivisibility to personhood** When the word entered English in the fifteenth century, it arrived still wearing its logical clothing. The adjective individual, recorded around 1450, meant "indivisible, single, inseparable" — a word of mathematics and metaphysics, not of people. In the sixteenth century it extended to "one, particular, single" as opposed to general; in the early seventeenth century it crossed decisively into the human realm as a noun, "a single human being," one member of the human race considered apart from the species. This was the step that changed everything: from a term that described any indivisible object, individual came to designate a person, and from designating a person it began, in the eighteenth century, to dignify one — for the abstract noun individuality, coined in the early seventeenth century by adding the suffix -ity to the adjective, still meant "individual existence, the state of being a single being," but by the eighteenth century it was being pulled toward "the sum of qualities that make a person or thing distinct." **The Romantic and Victorian apotheosis** The decisive revolution was Romantic. Against the Enlightenment's faith in universal rules, the Romantics prized the unique, the organic, the unrepeatable: a poem, an organism, and a person were each a living whole that could not be reproduced by following rules, and the name for this quality was individuality. Goethe's insistence that every organism realizes its own principle, Coleridge's "esemplastic" imagination, Emerson's Self-Reliance (1841) with its warning that "envy is ignorance" and "imitation is suicide" — all are celebrations of individuality. It was John Stuart Mill, himself the victim of an education designed to stamp out individuality, who gave the word its definitive political force in On Liberty (1859). For Mill, individuality is the ground of all that is valuable in human life: "the free development of individuality is one of the leading essentials of well-being"; it is "not only a coordinate element with all that is designated by the terms civilization, instruction, education, culture, but is itself a necessary part and condition of all those things." Against the "despotism of custom" and the tyranny of majority opinion, Mill sets the individual who "chooses his plan for himself." It is precisely this doctrine that the Autobiography records being violated — the boy whose individuality was declared "unimportant" became the philosopher who made individuality the cornerstone of liberty, and the tension between the two is one of the most moving arcs in English prose. **Widening and softening in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries** In the twentieth century the word spread far beyond philosophy. Biology speaks of the individuality of organisms and colonies; statistics and law of individual cases and individual rights; aesthetics of an artist's individual style. In ordinary speech individuality has softened from a demanding moral ideal into a marker of mere distinctness — "she has a lot of individuality," "the apartment lacks individuality," "celebrate your individuality" in advertisements for everything from coffee to clothing. The ancient claim of indivisibility survives as a shadow: what must not be split off from a person is exactly what the word was invented to protect. From Lucretius's atom to Mill's self-expressing citizen to the boutique notion of a personal brand, the word has never quite lost its oldest insistence — that the individual is that which cannot be divided, and that to treat a person as disposable raw material is to commit the very division the word denies.
