Covenant begins with feet. Proto-Indo-European *gʷem- meant to go, come, step, stride, the most basic verb of motion, reflected in Sanskrit gam- to go, Greek bainein to walk (whence basis), Latin venire to come, English come via Germanic *kweman. Latin compounded com- together with venire to form convenire: to come together in one place, and by natural metaphor to come together in mind, to agree, to suit. Its present participle conveniens gave English convenient (things that come together well) and convene (to call together). Its perfect participle conventus gave convent (those come together in religious life) and convention (a coming-together or its agreement).

In Vulgar and Medieval Latin, convenire in the sense to agree produced a new noun for the agreement itself. Old French, which regularly reduced Latin -en- and reshaped endings, formed covenant, covenance from the present participle stem convenant meaning agreeing, as a noun meaning agreement, pact, promise. The form with -a- reflects Anglo-Norman phonetics: Old French convenant became Anglo-Norman covenant, with o for on- before consonant. Thirteenth-century Anglo-Norman legal deeds are full of covenant: par covenant by agreement, covenantens those bound. Middle English borrowed it directly around 1300 as covenaunt, covenant meaning a mutual promise, deed, or contract, especially one under seal. Early citations in Robert of Gloucester, the Cursor Mundi, and Wycliffe's Bible use it for both secular bargains and God's promises to Noah and Abraham.

  • Convenir to covenant: participle turned noun: Latin convenire (to agree) → present participle conveniens, convenant- (agreeing) → Old French noun covenant (an agreeing, agreement). English preserves the -ant ending fossilised as noun, parallel to servant, merchant, lieutenant.
  • Covenant vs. convent vs. convention: All three descend from convenire but diverged: convent kept the religious assembly sense, convention took political and customary senses, covenant kept solemn, binding, often sacred promise. Spelling with o versus on marks the Anglo-Norman branch.
  • Wycliffe to King James: Wycliffe (1382) uses couenaunt for Latin testamentum, foedus, pactum indifferently. Tyndale and the King James Version (1611) standardised covenant for Hebrew berith and Greek diatheke, fixing its biblical solemnity for all later English.
  • Deed and seal: In common law, a covenant became technically a promise under seal in a deed, actionable by writ of covenant, distinct from simple parol contract. This legal precision still lives in property covenants.

Reformation Scotland made covenant a political sacrament. The National Covenant (1638) and Solemn League and Covenant (1643), signed in blood by thousands in Greyfriars Kirkyard, bound Scots Presbyterians to resist Charles I's liturgy. Covenanters became martyrs and folk heroes; to be called a Covenanter still signals stern Presbyterian conscience. Parallel Puritan New England spoke of church covenants, town covenants, and the national covenant with God, making covenant the legal-theological foundation of American self-government. Hobbes, Locke, and later Rawls secularised the idea into social contract, but covenant retained a warmer, oath-bound flavour: not mere bargain but kinship sworn.

In modern law covenant survives as a solemn term for binding clauses: restrictive covenants on land (no fences above four feet), covenants in bond indentures (to maintain ratios), marriage covenants in religious discourse. In theology it denotes God's successive pacts: Noahic, Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic, New Covenant in Christ's blood — This cup is the new covenant in my blood (Luke 22:20). Durant's phrase as a rite or covenant invokes exactly this blood logic in primitive form: drinking blood together makes parties of one blood, bound as kin. The anthropology of blood-covenant (blood brotherhood by mingling or drinking blood) documented by Robertson Smith and Frazer treated it as the savage prototype of all later contracts. Phonologically Received Pronunciation keeps /ˈkʌvənənt/ with reduced middle vowel, sometimes /ˈkʌvnənt/ in rapid speech; the -ant is never stressed, preserving its French participial origin. To understand covenant is to hear feet coming together, hands joining, blood mingling — motion turned into mutual obligation.
