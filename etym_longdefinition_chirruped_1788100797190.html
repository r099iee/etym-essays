Chirruped is the past tense and past participle of chirrup, an English verb of onomatopoeic origin meaning "to make a series of short, high-pitched, vibrant sounds" — the characteristic utterance of small songbirds, and by extension of crickets, mice, and (affectionately) of chirping people. The word belongs to the family chirp → chirrup → chirruped: it is a lengthened, softened, gently rhythmic cousin of plain "chirp", and it names a sound, not a meaning — a word that is itself the thing it describes.

1. Core sense: small-bird song (primary sense). "Chirruped" describes the continuous, soft, trilling note-sequences of small birds — sparrows, finches, wrens — and above all the sociable twittering of a flock: sparrows chirrup in the eaves, chicks chirrup to the hen, a cage-bird chirrups at dawn. The verb differs from "chirp" in texture: a chirp is a single short sharp note or a sparse series, while a chirrup is a fuller, running, almost conversational stream of notes, often with a trilled or rolled quality. Etymologically the form is often explained as "chirp" + the adverbial particle "up" (compare "cheer up"), which would make a chirrup literally a "cheer-up" note — an etymology perfectly fitting the word's cheerful register, though the details (as with all echoic words) are necessarily conjectural.

2. Extension to insects and small animals (secondary sense). By natural extension the verb covers any similar small, vibrant sound: crickets and grasshoppers chirrup — the "chirruping" of the cricket on the hearth is a Victorian cliché of cosy domesticity — frogs in spring chorus may be said to chirrup, and mice in the wainscot chirrup and squeak. The common thread is smallness, softness, and cheerful persistence: a sound too gentle to be a call, too articulate to be a noise.

3. Of human voices (tertiary sense). "To chirrup" is also used of people: a woman chirrups encouragement to a child or a pet, a nurse chirrups cheerfully to a baby, and — in the word's most famous technical use — coachmen, post-boys, and riders "chirrup" to horses: a special sharp, trilling, clucking sound of tongue and lips used to urge a horse on. This horse-sense, well attested from the 17th century to the early 20th (it appears in the fiction and sporting memoirs of the coaching age), shows the word migrating from the sound itself to the act of producing it as a signal; it is now archaic except in historical fiction and equestrian writing. A person described as having "chirruped" an answer has spoken it brightly, quickly, and with a trilling lightness — a tone of cheerful inconsequence.

4. Grammar and morphology. "Chirruped" is a regular weak past form: chirrup + -ed → chirruped, pronounced with the clipped /t/ of the -ed-suffix after the voiceless stop (hence /ˈtʃɪrəpt/). The base verb is usually intransitive (the sparrows chirruped) but may take a quoted utterance as its object ("Come along!" she chirruped); it inflects through the full set — chirrups, chirruped, chirruping — with the double-p spelling chirrupping common in British usage.

5. Register and connotation. The word's connotations are unfailingly positive: chirruping is small, bright, friendly, innocuous — the sound of a world at peace with itself. Because it is gentle and diminutive, "chirruped" in literary prose is a favourite of children's literature and nature writing (the sparrows of the source context, charmed by their "distinguished stranger" and chirruping wherever he goes, are precisely the word's natural subjects), where it confers warmth and animation. In modern speech the verb is rare — most speakers simply say "chirped" or "twittered" — and "chirruped" carries a distinctly literary, slightly old-fashioned, almost whimsical flavour, like a word borrowed from a Victorian parlour or a Beatrix Potter watercolour. Its niche is exactly the cheerful smallness it describes: the past tense of the sound that asks nothing of the world.

6. Semantic summary and bridge. The sense-development is a straight line of widening application united by one acoustic image: the short, bright, repetitive note. From the sparrow (first sense, established by the 16th–17th centuries) the word spread to every creature that makes the same small music — insects, mice, children, horses, cheerful people — each step licensed by the same acoustic prototype, and each step carrying the same overtone of sociable cheer. To have "chirruped" is to have spoken, sounded, or sung in the key of the songbird.
