Core sense (dried up, shrivelled). Withered is the past participle of the verb wither and means "dried up, shrivelled, or decayed through the loss of moisture or vitality". The primary, literal application is to plants and vegetation: a "withered" leaf, flower, or stalk has lost its water and life, becoming brown, brittle, and shrunken. In horticulture and botany the word describes tissue that has dried out and died from drought, heat, frost, disease, or age. The image is of moisture withdrawn and firmness lost — the opposite of green, lush, and thriving. This botanical sense underlies the famous biblical image of grass that withers: "the grass withereth, the flower fadeth" (Isaiah 40:7, KJV), where the withering of grass and flowers figures the brevity and frailty of human life.

  • Of plants and vegetation: dried up, dead, brown, brittle ("withered leaves", "a withered tree", "withered grass") — the literal and oldest sense.
  • Of people and body parts: shrivelled, shrunken, wrinkled, and frail through age, disease, or disuse — "a withered hand", "withered limbs", "a withered old man". In the Bible, Jesus heals "a man with a withered hand" (Matthew 12, Mark 3, Luke 6), where "withered" denotes a hand paralysed, shrunken, and useless — the flesh dried and wasted.
  • Figurative sense (hopes, powers, reputations): "withered" describes anything that has lost vitality, vigour, or promise — "withered hopes", "a withered reputation", "withered ambitions". The metaphor transfers the drying-up of a plant to the decline and death of abstract things: what once flourished has been blighted and reduced to ruin.
  • "Wither on the vine" idiom: a plan or project that "withers on the vine" fails or dies before it is realised, by analogy with grapes that dry up unharvested on the vine — used of initiatives abandoned or neglected until they collapse.
  • Nuanced distinction from related words: "withered" implies irreversible loss of moisture and life (dried to the point of death); "wilted" implies drooping from loss of turgidity that may still be reversible by water; "shrivelled" emphasises the wrinkling and contracting of the surface; "decayed" stresses decomposition by rot, not mere drying. "Withered" sits between simple dryness and outright rot — a dry, shrunken, lifeless state.

Verb and derived forms. Withered is both a true past participle ("the flowers have withered") and a fully adjectival form ("a withered flower"). The verb wither means "to dry up and shrivel" (intransitive: "the leaves withered") and "to cause to shrivel" (transitive: "the drought withered the crops"), with figurative extensions to decline and decay ("his influence withered"). Related forms: withering (adjective, "a withering glance" — a glance that strips someone of confidence; literally one that "shrinks" its target), witheredness, and the set phrase "withered away". In biology and medicine the word is used of tissue atrophy and ischemia-related drying; in literary language it is a favourite for decay, mortality, and the transience of beauty and power.

Semantic bridge. The whole range of senses pivots on one physical fact: the withdrawal of moisture and life from living tissue. From the literal drying of plants, the word extends by analogy first to the human body (flesh that dries, shrinks, and wastes with age or disease) and then to the abstract (anything that loses force decays "as if" by drying). The modern figurative register is thus a metaphor built on the oldest literal sense, and the “weather connection” — this word shares its root with "weather", because a thing left to the weather dries up — makes the entire family feel both earthy and inevitable.
