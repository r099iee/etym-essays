The history of thine is the history of the English second person itself, from a fully inflected Germanic pronoun system to the strangely impoverished modern you that serves for singular and plural, subject and object, familiar and formal alike. To understand thine is to watch English lose its familiar singular and then preserve that loss as poetry.

The deepest root lies in the Proto-Indo-European second-person stem *te- / *tu-, meaning you singular, with a possessive adjective conventionally reconstructed as *teynos or *tewos, thy. Every Indo-European language preserves some reflex: Latin tuus, Greek teos, Sanskrit tava, Gothic theins. The Germanic branch formed its possessive with the adjectival suffix *-īnaz added to the thou-stem *þu-, yielding Proto-Germanic *þīnaz, thine. The -n is thus not decorative but the old possessive-adjective marker, the same -n seen in mine and German mein and dein.

  • Proto-Germanic *þīnaz: A strongly inflected possessive adjective agreeing with its noun in gender, case, and number. It meant belonging to thou, the single intimate addressee, as opposed to *izwaraz belonging to you plural. Already the thou / you distinction carried social closeness versus distance or plurality.
  • Old English þīn: Fully inflected: þīn, þīne, þīnes, þīnum, þīna according to case and gender. Used both attributively (þīn nama, thy name) and absolutely (þīn is, thine is). The Lord's Prayer, Beowulf, and charters use it as ordinary everyday possession with no archaism at all.
  • Middle English thin / thine: After the Norman Conquest, inflections eroded and spelling varied wildly: thin, thyn, thyne, thine. Final -e became silent but was retained in writing. The thou / ye distinction sharpened socially: thou for intimates, children, inferiors, and God in prayerful intimacy; ye / you for superiors, strangers, and plurals. Thine rode along as the faithful possessive of thou.
  • Early Modern thine / thy: The King James Bible of 1611 and Shakespeare fix the classic distribution for all later readers: thy before consonants, thine before vowels and in predicative position. Mine eyes, thine eyes, mine honour, thine honour. The rule was phonetic euphony, avoiding vowel clash, exactly parallel to an apple versus a book. At this stage thine is still fully living, though you is already encroaching in polite London speech.
  • Eighteenth-century retreat: Polite society abandoned thou as impolite or Quakerish. Grammarians taught you as correct for all address. Thine survived only in fixed liturgy, hymns, legal formulae, and poetry deliberately reaching for age. What had been neutral became marked: to say thine was now to quote, to pray, or to perform.

Phonetically the change is simple: Old English þīn was pronounced with a long ee vowel [θiːn]. The Great Vowel Shift raised and diphthongized it to modern [ðaɪn], rhyming with mine and wine. Spelling thine preserves the silent -e that once marked length. The initial th represents the voiced dental fricative [ð] in function words, contrasting with voiceless [θ] in thrift or think.

Culturally, thine owes its afterlife to two great accidents of prestige: Shakespeare and the King James Bible. Both used thou / thine at the very moment it was dying in speech, embalming it as the language of love, majesty, and God. Later Romantic poets, hymn-writers, and even modern fantasy imitate that aura whenever they want solemnity without inventing a new word. The Quaker inner light testimony kept thou alive as a lived egalitarian protest into the nineteenth century, refusing to give different pronouns to different ranks.

Today thine is a fossil with force. No child acquires it as a native pronoun, yet every English reader recognizes it instantly. Its evolution essay is therefore not about sound laws alone but about how loss creates style: a common possessive became rare, and rarity became reverence.
