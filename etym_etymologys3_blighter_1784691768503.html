The story of blighter is really two stories: the story of blight, a word of obscure origin that became the standard name for agricultural catastrophe, and the story of a slang suffix that softened the resulting insult into a term of affection.

1. The deep root. Most etymologists connect blight to one of the Indo-European roots for burning and shining, *bʰleg-, "to shine, burn, blaze," attested across the family in Greek phlégō, "to burn," Latin flagrāre, "to blaze," and Sanskrit bhrājate, "he gleams." In Germanic the root produced forms meaning both "pale/shining" and "scorched/blackened," the two poles of fire: Old Norse blika, "to gleam," blikna, "to become pale," Old English blǣc, "pale, shining" (the ancestor of English bleak), and the family that gives English black. The connection of blight to this family rests on the "scorched, blackened" pole of meaning: a field of blighted corn looks blasted, withered, and blackened, as though struck by fire or a burning breath — and indeed one old explanation of blight was that it was caused by a "blasting" wind. The word blast itself was long used alongside blight for such damage.

2. The uncertain birth of blight. The immediate origin of the English noun blight, first recorded in the sixteenth century, is genuinely uncertain. The OED marks it "of unknown origin," and scholars divide between two hypotheses: derivation from Old English blæce, "scab, rash, skin eruption" — a word for a blistered, blackened condition of the skin, easily transferred to the blotched condition of diseased plants — and borrowing from a Scandinavian or Low German form related to Old Norse blikna, "to become pale or blackened." No decisive evidence settles the question, and the honest account must present both possibilities. What is certain is that by the seventeenth century blight was the standard English term for the mysterious maladies that withered, blasted, and blackened whole fields of crops, and that it rapidly acquired a figurative life, so that by the eighteenth century anything that ruined or corrupted — the blight of poverty, the blight of war, the blight of ambition — could be called a blight.

3. The coinage of blighter. The leap to blighter came in the late nineteenth century, when British slang, with its fondness for agent nouns in -er, forged blight + -er. In its earliest recorded uses a blighter was a genuinely contemptible person, one who blighted the lives of others — a pest, a cad, a ruinous fellow. The word, however, almost immediately began to soften, following the same path that wretch and beggar had trodden before it. By the early twentieth century a blighter meant simply "a fellow or chap," especially one regarded with amused tolerance or pity, and "poor blighter" had become a stock phrase of affectionate sympathy for the unfortunate. The First World War entrenched this softened register in the speech of the trenches, where "old blighter" was a comradely term, and the word's journey from hard insult to affectionate mockery was complete.

4. Blighter in The Hobbit. It is precisely this softened, double-edged register that Tolkien exploits when the troll William surveys the frantic, pleading hobbit and calls him "Poor little blighter." On the surface the phrase expresses a kind of rough pity — the troll regards the hobbit as a small, unfortunate creature not worth serious animosity. But the word also carries patronising amusement: the terrified hobbit, pleading with eloquent desperation not to be cooked for supper, is reduced to the status of a blighted little creature, one whose luck has withered as by a blast. The diminutive "little" reinforces the comedy of scale between the frantic hobbit and his hulking captor, and the whole sentence shows Tolkien deploying the colloquial registers of English with philological exactness.

5. Social and linguistic summary.

  • Semantic drift: "to burn/blaze, to scorch black" > (uncertain) "blight, the disease that blackens crops" (16th c.) > "anything that ruins" (18th c.) > "one who blights" (late 19th c.) > "a fellow, a pitiable or annoying chap" (20th c.).
  • Socio-cultural factors: the softening tracks the Victorian-to-Edwardian fashion for jocular, self-deprecating understatement in British slang, and the trench speech of 1914–18 cemented its affectionate use.
  • Sound and form: blight shows the Germanic -gh- spelling of the early modern period; the suffix -er is the everyday agent suffix; no further sound changes affect the modern form.
  • Literary attestations: blight is attested in sixteenth-century agricultural writing and in Shakespeare's Hamlet ("blasting" and blighted corn imagery); "poor blighter" is common in twentieth-century British fiction; Tolkien's troll scene (1937) is the word's best-known literary appearance.
