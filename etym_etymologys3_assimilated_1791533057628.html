Assimilated has a double life. In physiology it is one of the oldest learned words in English; in sociology it is one of the most contested. The distance between “food turned into flesh” and “immigrants turned into nationals” is the whole story of the word, and it is a story about how easily a metaphor of digestion can be transferred from bodies to peoples.

Latin origins: likeness as a philosophical idea

The Latin adjective similis “like, similar” belongs to a family of words built on the root *sem- “one, together”: semel “once,” simul “at the same time,” simulāre “to make like, imitate.” The prefixed verb assimulāre (also assimilāre) meant “to make similar, to liken, to compare,” and was used in rhetoric for the drawing of comparisons and in philosophy for the process by which like is known by like. The past participle assimilātus is the direct formal ancestor of English assimilated, though English took the word through French rather than directly.

Medieval and early modern physiology

Medieval Latin kept the word chiefly in medicine and alchemy, where assimilatio named the conversion of nutriment into the substance of the body. English borrowed it in the fifteenth century, in the wake of French medical translation, and used it first of food, humours and medicines. The underlying assumption was Aristotelian: digestion is a process of making the foreign like the self, so that whatever is eaten becomes flesh. This physiological sense remained dominant until the eighteenth century and still survives in nutrient assimilation and carbon assimilation.

The Enlightenment extension to mind

Locke and his successors had already described the mind as taking in ideas; by the eighteenth century the language of digestion was applied to knowledge. To assimilate an idea was to make it one’s own, to convert it into mental substance. The metaphor was attractive because it implied activity rather than passive reception: a reader who merely remembers has not assimilated, just as a patient who merely swallows has not digested. This cognitive sense prepared the ground for Piaget’s technical use in the twentieth century.

Nineteenth-century biology and the birth of the social sense

Nineteenth-century physiology gave assimilation a precise metabolic meaning — the constructive processes by which living tissue is built up — and plant science added carbon assimilation for photosynthesis. Almost simultaneously, the word was applied to human groups. In the United States, where mass immigration from Europe was transforming the population, assimilation became the name of a policy and an ideal: newcomers should shed their old languages and loyalties and become Americans. The metaphor was explicitly digestive — the melting pot — and it was widely regarded as benevolent.

The high-water mark came in the early twentieth century, with Americanisation programmes, English-only instruction and citizenship classes. To call a family assimilated was to praise it; to call it unassimilated was to suggest a problem. The same vocabulary was used in colonial settings, where subject peoples were said to be assimilated to French or British civilisation — the French mission civilisatrice made assimilation an explicit doctrine of colonial policy.

Sociology and the measurement of belonging

From the 1920s the Chicago School turned assimilation into an object of empirical study. Robert Park described it as “a process of interpenetration and fusion”; Milton Gordon in 1964 distinguished cultural from structural assimilation and argued that the latter — intermarriage, friendship, club membership — was the decisive stage. The word became neutral and technical, but it also became a yardstick against which minorities were measured and found wanting.

The critical turn

From the 1960s, minority writers and post-colonial theorists attacked the concept. Assimilation, they argued, was not a natural process but a demand imposed by the powerful: it required the abandonment of heritage languages, religions and naming practices, and it rewarded those who performed the majority’s norms. Assimilationist became a pejorative, and “integration,” “multiculturalism” and “acculturation” were preferred in policy language. The word’s emotional register shifted from approval to suspicion, and in many academic contexts it is now used with visible unease.

Linguistics, computing and popular culture

Meanwhile the technical senses multiplied. Nineteenth-century philology made assimilation a core concept of sound change; twentieth-century meteorology developed data assimilation as a mathematical technique; and intelligence services speak of assimilated information. The most widely circulated modern use, however, is fictional: the Borg of Star Trek, who announce that “resistance is futile” and that the individual will be “assimilated.” The phrase has become a joke about committees, fandoms, chains of cafés and any institution that absorbs its members entirely.

Literary and public attestations

  • Mary Shelley and nineteenth-century scientific prose use assimilation in its physiological sense, often with Gothic overtones of the body converting foreign matter into itself.
  • Americanisation pamphlets of the 1910s use assimilated as a measurable civic achievement: the immigrant who has learned English and taken out papers.
  • Milton Gordon’s Assimilation in American Life (1964) fixed the academic vocabulary of the field.
  • Post-colonial writing — Fanon, Memmi, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o — treats assimilation as a strategy of colonial domination, and the word acquires a bitter irony.
  • Star Trek scripts from 1989 onward give the participle a global pop-culture afterlife that no dictionary of the 1970s could have predicted.

Why the word still matters

The history of assimilated shows how a metaphor can become a policy. Once the digestion of food was mapped onto the reception of strangers, the conclusion seemed natural: the stranger must be converted into the same substance, or remain undigested and troublesome. Modern debates about migration are still conducted in the shadow of that metaphor, even by those who reject it. To describe someone as assimilated is never a neutral act: it says something about what the speaker thinks a society is, and about how much difference it can tolerate.
