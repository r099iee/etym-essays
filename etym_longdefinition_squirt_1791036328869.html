squirt lives a double life, and both senses hinge on smallness discharged with force. As a common noun and verb it first means a thin, sudden jet of liquid expelled under pressure — to squirt water, ink, lemon juice, oil — and the jet itself or the instrument that makes it (a squirt gun, syringe). By a transparent metaphor of size and contempt, it secondly means a small, insignificant, cheeky person, especially a child or youngster presumed to overstep: the little squirt, as in the Wodehouse source where Angela is dismissed as an offensive little squirt. The insult works by miniaturising the target into something to be flicked away like a drop.

The physical sense is precise. A squirt is narrower and sharper than a spray, splash, gush, or stream: a focused, brief ejection through a small aperture, often with a hissing or spurting sound. Verbs of manner cluster around it — squirt, spurt, spout, spurt out, jet — all implying pressure behind a constriction. Nautically, horticulturally, medically, and domestically the word is technical yet everyday: squirt of oil, washing-up liquid, perfume, venom, ink from a fountain pen. The onomatopoeic squ- + -irt cluster itself feels like the sound of compression and release.

The personal sense, the one in context, is informal, mildly contemptuous, and often affectionately patronising rather than truly hateful. Calling a child a little squirt conveys: you are tiny, presumptuous, impertinent, and not to be taken seriously — an annoyed elder's put-down. It can be gendered toward boys (a young squirt) but Wodehouse applies it to Angela, a young woman, showing its general use for any junior who offends. Tone matters enormously: between friends it is teasing; from a superior to a subordinate it is belittling; shouted in anger it is dismissive abuse. It rarely denotes moral depravity (unlike stinker in the same novel); it denotes lack of stature.

  • Primary physical senses: verb — to eject liquid in a jet (she squirted detergent); noun — the jet itself (a squirt of lemon) or device (a plastic squirt). Extension to semi-liquids and powders in colloquial use: squirt of cream, mustard, sunscreen.
  • Secondary personal sense: an insignificant, impudent, or presumptuous youngster; a nobody, whippersnapper. E.g. obnoxious little squirt, arrogant squirt, the squirt in accounts. Bridge: small size + ease of dismissal + sudden impudence likened to an unexpected spurt.
  • Tertiary slang senses: in older slang, a syringe or the act of injection; in printing, a fresh apprentice; in American slang, a short, contemptible fellow. Vulgar extensions exist but are marginal in standard use.
  • Domain usages: everyday domestic (squirt of soap); horticulture and cleaning (squirt bottle, hose); medicine (squirt syringe, now usually syringe); marine biology (sea squirt — an ascidian that ejects water, though etymologically distinct in part); firearms/toys (squirt gun, water pistol).
  • Collocations and register: informal, vivid, slightly comic. Physical: squirt out, squirt at, squirt gun, squirt bottle. Personal: almost always with diminutive little — little squirt — and often with arrogant, cheeky, impertinent, obnoxious. More playful than brat, runt, punk; less vicious than worm, rat, stinker.
  • Grammar: countable noun and regular verb (squirt/squirted/squirting). Attributive uses: squirt gun, squirt bottle. Diminutive force often reinforced by little even when the referent is adult, to infantilise.

In short, squirt compresses physics and social hierarchy into one syllable: pressure through a narrow opening, and a person put through a narrowing estimation. To call Angela the little squirt is to perform the very dismissal the plot then avenges — a small word for a small (in the speaker's eyes) offender, uttered to seize a bit of one's own back.
