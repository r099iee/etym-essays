STOWAWAYS (plural noun; singular stowaway) — persons who conceal themselves aboard a ship, aircraft, train, or other vehicle in order to travel without paying, to escape danger or pursuit, or to enter a country illegally. The word belongs to the working vocabulary of seafaring, customs, and border control, yet it has long since spread beyond the docks into journalism, law, and everyday metaphor, and today it can name anything that travels hidden inside a larger structure or enterprise. Core senses and usages
  • Nautical and transport sense (the primary sense): a person who hides on a vessel — in the cargo hold, among crates, in a lifeboat, a ventilation shaft, an engine room, or a container — before departure, remaining concealed until the ship is at sea or has docked. By extension the same term is used of stowaways on aircraft (often hiding in wheel bays or cargo holds) and on freight and passenger trains.
  • Maritime-law and border-security sense: in international law the stowaway is a precisely defined legal category. The International Maritime Organization's FAL (Facilitation) Convention and the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea impose duties on shipmasters, port states, and flag states concerning the stowaway's surrender, detention, and repatriation; carriers face substantial "carrier liability" fines for transporting undocumented persons; and stowaways routinely claim asylum on arrival, so that refugee-protection rules under the 1951 Geneva Convention are triggered. The stowaway thus sits at the intersection of criminal, immigration, and human-rights law.
  • Extended sense (things, not people): by the twentieth century the word came to be used of anything hidden or inserted without authorization — a "stowaway" program or file on a computer, a "stowaway" clause slipped into a legislative bill, a "stowaway" guest at an event. The unifying image is of something carried along unnoticed inside a larger container or journey.
  • Figurative and literary sense: in prose and poetry the stowaway becomes a figure of the unnoticed rider, the uninvited presence, the secret burden — "the stowaway on the voyage of life". In fantasy and adventure fiction the word often supplies a whiff of romance and of nineteenth-century adventure, a trace of the great age of sail and steam when stowaways were a standing nuisance on every emigrant liner.
Why the word means what it does — the semantic bridge The modern noun is a compound, stow + away. To stow is to pack or store things away compactly and safely — the verb a mariner uses for securing cargo in a hold; away adds the sense of removal, of being put out of sight and out of hand. The phrasal verb to stow away, "to put cargo away in a ship's hold", is attested from the sixteenth century. In the mid-nineteenth century, with the explosion of steam shipping, mass emigration, and ever-stricter port formalities, the same phrase was transferred from cargo to people: someone who hid among the stores to steal a passage became, by a natural metonymy, a stowaway — the word for the act (hidden stowage) and the object (the person) fused into a single noun. The first recorded uses of the noun date to the 1850s, and the plural stowaways quickly became the standard legal and nautical term. Because the verb was already nautical slang, the noun carries a distinctly maritime tone to this day, even when applied to trains, aircraft, or metaphors. Register and connotation In modern English stowaways is a neutral-to-formal word of the news report and the legal document, but it retains a faint romantic undertow: the stowaway is at once a lawbreaker, a victim, and an adventurer. The word collocates with discover, detect, catch, hide, conceal, cargo hold, repatriate, and asylum. Its plural is the form most often met in headlines, shipping regulations, and fiction alike, since stowaways are rarely found singly.
