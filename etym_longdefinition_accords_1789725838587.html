ACCORDS is the third-person singular present of the verb accord, meaning in its central intransitive use to agree, correspond, or be consistent with something: this accords with my experience, his story accords with the evidence, his behaviour ill accords with his promises. The subject harmonises with an object of comparison, introduced almost always by with, and the judgement is one of fit rather than identity — two accounts need not be word-identical to accord, only mutually supportive without contradiction. The register is formal and slightly literary; everyday speech prefers agrees with, matches, fits, while reports, scholarship, and biography reach for accords with to dignify agreement as principled coherence.

In the immediate context, ACCORDS adjudicates memory against self-testimony: No-one could remember an interest in birds at school – which ______ with Baker’s own admission that ‘I came late to a love of birds’. The absence of schoolboy birdwatching might have seemed a gap in a naturalist’s legend, but Baker himself confessed lateness, so outer oblivion and inner admission harmonise perfectly. The dash sets up an explanatory gloss: the narrators anticipate objection (surely a great bird man always loved birds?) and dissolve it by showing the two witnesses sing in tune. To accord here is to corroborate without collusion.

  • Primary sense: to agree, correspond, be in harmony (with). His account accords with the documents; the painting accords well with the room; theory accords with observation. This preserves the heart metaphor of Old French: two hearts beat together. Semantic bridge to the present sentence is direct identity: lack of memory and late love are two hearts beating as one. The construction is stative and evaluative, often modified by fully, exactly, closely, poorly, ill, well.
  • Secondary sense: to grant, bestow, or concede (transitive, formal). To accord someone a warm welcome, to accord priority to safety, the rights accorded by treaty, the honour accorded him. Here the subject confers recognition or privilege. Bridge from agreement to granting is medieval courtesy: to be of one heart with someone is to give them what their heart desires, so concord becomes largesse. Modern use survives in diplomacy, ceremony, and academic prose, typically passive: was accorded full honours. The -s form appears as she accords him unusual respect.
  • Tertiary senses: noun accord and spontaneous-volition phrase. As a noun, accord means agreement, treaty, or harmony: peace accords, the Camp David Accords, to be in accord, with one accord. The phrase of one’s own accord means voluntarily, from one’s own heart without compulsion. Bridge: all three keep the image of hearts joined — treaty as joined national hearts, voluntary action as following one’s own heart. The diplomatic plural Accords capitalised is now the most visible noun use, while of one’s own accord preserves the oldest spontaneous sense.
  • Grammar, collocation, and nuance: intransitive accord with takes abstract subjects (facts, memories, theories) and abstract complements (experience, evidence, principles); transitive accord something (to someone) takes nouns of honour (welcome, respect, status). The verb is gradable only adverbially, not comparatively. Its antonyms split by sense: disagrees with, contradicts, clashes with for agreement; withholds, denies for granting. To choose accords with over agrees with is to claim principled harmony rather than mere coincidence.

As an inflected form ACCORDS marks present habitual truth seen from outside: it does not narrate a single handshake but states a standing relation of fit. Historians and biographers favour it for exactly this timeless verdict, as here where a decades-old absence of memory is ruled permanently consonant with a lifetime’s late conversion to birds.
