Imparting looks like a plain English word, but almost every element of it is Latin. Its history traces the way a verb of dividing and sharing became the standard formal word for communicating knowledge — and how a gerund can turn a process into a policy.

From pars to impartire

  • Latin pars (genitive partis) means "part, portion, share"; it is one of the basic words of Roman law, commerce and philosophy, and it underlies English part, partial, particle, portion and partner.
  • From it Latin formed the verb partire "to divide, distribute", and with the prefix in- the classical compound impertire "to give a share of, bestow." Late Latin reshaped the word as impartire after pars, and it is this form that passes into French and then English.
  • The prefix does not mean "not" here but "into, upon": to impart is to put a share into someone's hands. The same in- appears in impart, impartation and the legal phrase "imparted information".
  • The classical verb impertire is at home in comedy and prose from the Republic onward; its object can be money, land, a secret or a quality, and the recipient is regularly marked by the dative — a construction English reproduces with to.

Entry into English

  • English takes the word from Anglo-Norman and Middle French impartir in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; the earliest English uses, around the early fifteenth century, concern the granting or sharing of property.
  • The sense "to communicate, make known" develops in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. It is a natural extension: if knowledge is a possession, teaching is a division of that possession, and the person taught receives a part.
  • The Authorized Version of 1611 gives the word lasting religious prestige: "that I may impart unto you some spiritual gift" (Romans 1:11) and "we were willing to have imparted unto you, not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls" (1 Thessalonians 2:8).
  • From the seventeenth century the verb is at home in dedications, sermons, statutes and scientific prose, always with an air of gravity and deliberate generosity.

The semantic map of the modern word

  • Information: impart news, facts, a secret, a warning. The recipient is a person or an audience, and the implication is that the information is true and entrusted.
  • Qualities and effects: impart flavour, colour, warmth, stability, momentum. The object is a property transferred from one thing to another, as a sauce imparts flavour to meat or a teacher imparts enthusiasm to a class.
  • Instruction: impart knowledge, skills, wisdom. This is the sense activated by the source passage, where the phrase "the imparting of tricks" makes the transfer sound mechanical.
  • Law and confidentiality: information imparted in confidence; the phrase belongs to the law of confidence and to professional ethics, where the act of imparting creates an obligation.
  • Theology: "imparted righteousness" denotes righteousness actually conferred on the believer, as distinct from "imputed righteousness", which is reckoned or credited; the contrast is a fault line between Wesleyan and Reformed soteriology. "Impartation" in Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity names the conferring of spiritual gifts, often through the laying on of hands.

Morphology, doublets and traps

  • impartation: the nominal derivative, rare outside theology and law, and heavier than the gerund.
  • impartible is a trap: one word, two histories. "Impartible" meaning "capable of being imparted" derives from impart; "impartible" meaning "not divisible" derives from in- "not" plus partible, and appears in theology of the divine nature.
  • The gerund imparting itself is grammatically a noun but semantically a process, which is why it can be modified like a verb ("imparting knowledge to beginners") while still taking a definite article ("the imparting of knowledge").
  • Related English words from the same Latin base include part, portion, particle, partner and parse, the last from the schoolroom habit of analysing a sentence into its "parts" of speech.

Register and modern perception: impart is now a marked word. In conversation it sounds formal, even arch; in education, law, religion and ceremonial prose it remains the natural choice. Its dignified, slightly archaic air is precisely what makes "the imparting of tricks" so effective in the source: the phrase dresses up a debased conception of teaching in the language of generous gift, and then denies that this is what oratory is.
