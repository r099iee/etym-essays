The name Alboin is a classic two-element Germanic anthroponym — a compound of the sort that named most of the warrior-elite of early medieval Europe — and its two halves reach back separately into Proto-Indo-European. The first element, Alb-, is the Germanic word for "elf," Proto-Germanic *albiz, from an Indo-European root *albh-, "white, bright, shining." The ancient elves were not the small mischievous creatures of Victorian fairy-tale but the luminous, shining beings of Germanic paganism — "the white ones." The same root *albh- produced Latin albus "white," and through Latin our words albino, album, and the ancient name Albion for Britain. The root is widely though not perfectly distributed: Greek alphós, "whitish leprosy," shows it in Hellenic, and the Tocharian ālpe "white" has been adduced, though cautiously.

The second element, -oin (from -win), is Proto-Germanic *winiz, "friend, beloved," from the PIE root *wenh₁-, "to desire, strive for, win, love," whose cognates span Sanskrit vánati ("loves, wins"), Latin Venus and venia, and Old Irish fine "kindred." In Old English the same element is the poetic word wine, "friend" — the lord's intimate companion of the comitatus — and it is a striking fact that the Old English name Ælfwine, "elf-friend," is the exact structural twin of Alboin, which in Langobardic was Albwin. A Wessex man could bear the same name as the Lombard king; the two languages were building the same names out of the same inherited stock, each independently combining "elf" and "friend."

The name is thus a mobile piece of a common Germanic naming system, and its history as a personal name is inseparable from the history of the man who bore it. Alboin, king of the Lombards, reigned from about 560, and in 568 led his people in the great migration out of Pannonia across the Alps into Italy. Within a few years the Lombards had overrun the north and centre of the peninsula, seized Pavia (their capital from 572), and created a Germanic kingdom that would last two centuries. The Latin sources Latinized his name as Alboinus — the spelling later English and continental historiography took up — while the Old English heroic poem Widsith preserved the native English form Elfwine, showing how the name circulated in heroic verse across the Germanic world.

His modern fame owes as much to legend as to history. The story of the skull-cup — that Alboin forced his wife Rosamund, daughter of the Gepid king Cunimund he had slain, to drink from her father's skull — and her revenge through a bodyguard's assassination (572) turned the "elf-friend" into the archetype of the barbarian king brought down by domestic treachery, a tale retold from Paul the Deacon's History of the Lombards (8th century) down to the historians of the 18th and 19th centuries. On this card, however, he appears in the dignified roll-call of the Christianized Germanic dynasty-founders — "Alaric, Theodoric, Clovis, Alboin, were zealous Christians" — where the pagan "elf-friend" name now crowns a leader of a nation that had, at least formally, embraced the religion of the empire it had overrun. The name thus shows, in miniature, the whole transformation of the Germanic world: an old pagan compound of luminous spirits and lordly friendship, Latinized, chronicled, and finally made an emblem of Christian dynastic foundation.

  • Sound change: Langobardic Albwin → Latinized Alboinus (epenthetic -o- before the labial) → English historical spelling Alboin, which drops the Latin -us and restores the two-element shape.
  • Two roots: PIE *albh- "white, bright" (the elf as "white being") + PIE *wenh₁- "to desire, love" (the friend).
  • Cognate family in English: albino, album, Albion (first element); none of the second element survives as a separate English noun, but Latin Venus, Sanskrit vánati, and Old Irish fine are its kin.
  • Socio-cultural context: the name's career tracks the migration age — from pagan Germanic onomastics, through Latin Christian chronicle, to the romantic historiography of the 18th–19th centuries that made the barbarian founder-kings of Europe a literary roll-call.
