• cash: Similar because both mean money in hand; differs because cash is the neutral, standard word for banknotes and coin, while jack is slang with a working-class, American flavour — money earned by labour and saved as a stake.
  • dough: Similar because both are American slang for money; differs because dough is the more general slang term for spending money, while jack carries the sense of the accumulated fund or stake ("get the jack together").
  • bucks: Similar because both are colloquial American money-words; differs because bucks names units of dollars ("fifty bucks"), while jack is a mass noun for money in general, not a count of dollars.
  • bread: Similar because both are slang for money; differs because bread (from the 1930s–50s, revived in the 90s) is slang for money that sustains you, while jack is the working man's saved stake — both are plain and earthy, but jack is more specifically American laboring speech.
  • moolah: Similar because both are informal words for money; differs because moolah is jaunty and vaguely carnivalesque, without the working-class labour association of jack.
  • loot: Similar because both are slang for money; differs because loot literally means plunder or ill-gotten gains, carrying a hint of criminality, while jack is honestly earned wages and savings.
  • scratch: Similar because both are American slang for money; differs because scratch is slang for ready cash or spending money, while jack in this context is the saved fund — a nuance of thrift rather than pocket-money.
  • greenbacks: Similar because both are American money terms; differs because greenbacks is dated, patriotic slang for paper dollars, while jack is class slang for money in general.
  • wonga: Similar because both are informal slang for money; differs because wonga is British (from Romany), while jack is the American working-class word — two national voices saying the same thing.
  • fellow / bloke / guy: Similar because all can mean a common man, the generic "Jack"; differs because fellow and bloke are the neutral words for a man, while the specific "common fellow" sense of jack survives mainly in idioms like "every man jack."
  • hoist: Similar because both are lifting mechanisms; differs because a hoist raises loads by ropes and pulleys from above, while a jack pushes upward from below — the jack works underneath the load.
  • winch: Similar because both are mechanical lifting devices; differs because a winch winds a cable around a drum to pull or lift, while a jack is a screw, ratchet, or hydraulic device that lifts by pushing up from beneath.
  • lever: Similar because both give mechanical advantage in lifting; differs because a lever is a simple bar pivoting on a fulcrum, while a jack is the complete, self-contained lifting device built around a screw or piston.
