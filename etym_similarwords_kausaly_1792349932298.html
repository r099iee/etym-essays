• Kaikeyī: Similar because both are principal queen consorts of King Daśaratha and mothers of royal princes (Rāma and Bharata respectively); differs because Kaikeyī is the passionate, strong-willed junior queen who yields to Mantharā's instigation and demands Rāma's exile, whereas Kausalyā is the patient, dignified senior queen who suffers the tragedy of her son's departure.
  • Sumitrā: Similar because both are co-queens of Daśaratha who support Rāma unconditionally during the succession crisis; differs because Sumitrā is the mother of the twins Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna, celebrated for her philosophical detachment and selflessness in urging Lakṣmaṇa to accompany Rāma into the forest, whereas Kausalyā is the primary Queen Mother (*Rājamātā*) and central mourning figure.
  • Sītā: Similar because both are foremost heroines of the Rāmāyaṇa celebrated for virtue, fidelity, and enduring profound trials with spiritual grace; differs because Sītā is Rāma's consort and daughter of King Janaka representing marital fidelity and self-sacrifice, whereas Kausalyā represents maternal love (*mātṛ-tva*) and the grief of a bereaved mother.
  • Yashodā: Similar because both are iconic maternal figures in Hindu tradition deeply revered for their profound love and tender care toward an avatar of Viṣṇu; differs because Yashodā is the foster mother of Krishna in the pastoral, playful rural setting of Gokula experiencing joyous *vātsalya*, whereas Kausalyā is the biological mother of Rāma set within the formal, tragic courtly environment of Ayodhyā.
  • Devakī: Similar because both are royal biological mothers of Viṣṇu's avatars who endure agonizing separation and sorrow due to tyrannical rulers or fate; differs because Devakī was imprisoned in a dungeon by the tyrant Kaṃsa and had her infants slaughtered before Krishna was spirited away, while Kausalyā loses her adult son to forest exile following court intrigue.
  • Kuntī: Similar because both are senior epic matriarchs (in the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata respectively) who guide noble princes through profound exile and dynastic warfare; differs because Kuntī is a more politically active, assertive matriarch grappling with complex secrets (such as Karṇa's parentage), while Kausalyā is characterized primarily by maternal pathos and quiet spiritual grace.
  • Gāndhārī: Similar because both are tragic royal queen mothers who witness the devastating consequences of palace rivalry and unbending oaths; differs because Gāndhārī is the queen of the Kurus who blindfolded herself and watched all one hundred of her sons perish in the Kurukṣetra war, whereas Kausalyā ultimately welcomes Rāma back in triumph after fourteen years.
  • Mandodarī: Similar because both are virtuous, noble royal queens caught in tragic circumstances caused by royal obstinacy; differs because Mandodarī is the chief queen of the demon king Rāvaṇa in Laṅkā who repeatedly warns her husband against fighting Rāma, whereas Kausalyā belongs to the righteous Solar dynasty of Ayodhyā.
  • Anasūyā: Similar because both are revered ancient sage-like women in the Rāmāyaṇa who bless and instruct the protagonists; differs because Anasūyā is the wife of the sage Atri dwelling in the forest hermitage, bestowing divine cosmetics and garments upon Sītā, while Kausalyā is a queen residing in the imperial palace of Ayodhyā.
  • Mātṛkā: Similar because both embody the archetypal maternal principle and divine protective female power in Hindu tradition; differs because Mātṛkā refers to a collective group of fierce, martial mother-goddesses in Tantric and Puranic worship, whereas Kausalyā is a human-divine epic queen celebrated for maternal tenderness and quiet endurance.
