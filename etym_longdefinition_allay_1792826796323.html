Allay is a formal, literary verb meaning to calm, quiet, pacify, or relieve — to make fear, grief, pain, suspicion, or thirst grow less, as when the narrator of the source sentence despairs that no words of Saruman could allay the dread Gandalf's deeds had planted.

The sentence captures the word's characteristic gravity: one allays terrors, not tickles, and the failure to allay is itself terrible. Fear that cannot be allayed does not merely persist; it proves itself beyond words, stronger than the most honeyed voice in Middle-earth, and the verb measures exactly that contest between dread and reassurance.

Core meaning in detail: To allay is to lay something down to rest: fears allayed subside, suspicions allayed dissolve, pains allayed ease, thirst allayed is quenched. The grammar is transitive and gentle — to allay fears, to allay concerns, to allay hunger — and the subject is usually comfort itself: words, presence, remedies, time. Dictionaries mark it formal; everyday speech prefers calm down, ease, relieve, while allay belongs to speeches, sermons, novels, and news reports.

Why laying down means calming — the bridge: Old English alecgan meant literally to lay down, to put down what is raised: a burden set down, a weapon lowered, anger laid to rest. Raised feeling is figured as a rising thing — swelling fear, mounting anger, climbing fever — and calming is laying it flat again, as one lays a restless limb along the bed. Every modern use keeps this posture: the storm laid, the ghost laid, the fear allayed.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To calm fear, anxiety, or grief (primary modern sense): to allay fears, to allay public concern. The bridge is the laying-down image: dread raised like dust settles when words lay it to rest.
  • To relieve pain, hunger, or thirst: to allay pain with medicine, to allay thirst with water. The bridge runs through quenching: craving figured as a rising fire that remedy lays flat, older than aspirin and still at home in hospitals and hymns.
  • To dispel suspicion or doubt: to allay suspicions that the accounts were cooked. The bridge is exorcism by explanation: suspicion haunts like a ghost until evidence lays it, and newspapermen reach for allay whenever denials are meant to bury a story.
  • To diminish force or intensity generally (formal): to allay the storm, to allay criticism. The bridge generalises the calming from feelings to forces: whatever rages can be laid, from tempests to markets to tempers.
  • To lay to rest, to bury (archaic overlap): older writers allay bodies and lay ghosts with the same verb family. The bridge is literal laying in earth: the corpse laid down, the spirit laid quiet, the fear laid with them.
  • To mix metals, to alloy (homograph, distinct origin): gold allayed with copper is gold tempered by mixture, from Old French alaiier, to combine. The bridge is false kinship worth naming only to refuse: same spelling, different blood, the one laying down and the other laying together.
  • To abate legally or administratively (rare formal): nuisances allayed, prices allayed in older statutes. The bridge carries private calming into public ordering: the magistrate lays the disturbance as the nurse lays the fever.

Register and connotation: allay is dignified, slow, and trustworthy — the verb of making peace with language. It promises mastery without violence: nothing is crushed or denied, everything is gently laid to rest. Its failure, as in the source sentence, therefore measures the depth of the wound: fear that no words could allay has passed beyond rhetoric into knowledge, and Saruman's eloquence breaks against it like water on stone.
