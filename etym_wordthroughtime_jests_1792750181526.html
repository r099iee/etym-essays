- [13th–14th c. Middle English] — Attested as gest or geste (borrowed from Old French geste and Latin gesta), designating noble deeds, heroic exploits, or metrical chivalric romances chanting the valiant deeds of knights (as in The Gest of Robyn Hode or Gesta Romanorum) — [perception: elevated, serious, and revered genre of epic history and martial valor]
- [15th c. Late Middle English] — Underwent gradual minstrel dilution, shifting from solemn heroic chronicles to popular amusing anecdotes, merry narrative tales, and light tavern entertainment — [perception: lighthearted popular entertainment; beginning to lose its aristocratic martial solemnity]
- [16th c. Tudor & Elizabethan Era] — Standardized in spelling as jest, narrowing strictly to witty verbal jokes, sharp quips, practical pranks, and published compilations of humor (jestbooks) — [perception: clever, sharp-witted humor; associated with theatrical fools, court jesters, and urban wit]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment Era] — Firmly established in moral and legal antithesis to seriousness ("in jest" versus "in earnest"), frequently warned against by moralists when bordering on cruel mockery or blasphemous levity — [perception: recognized as delightful social amusement, but viewed with moral caution when lacking gentlemanly restraint]
- [19th c. Victorian & Romantic Era] — Adopted into historical novels (Walter Scott) and nostalgic rural prose as a slightly archaic, picturesque term for traditional merrymaking, tavern banter, and harmless rustic laughter — [perception: charmingly antique, warm-hearted, and evocative of Merry England]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Embraced in classic literature and fantasy (such as Tolkien's depiction of hearty hobbit feasts) as a rich, timeless synonym for good-natured jokes and playful teasing — [perception: warm, literary, and evocative marker of wholesome laughter and convivial fellowship]
