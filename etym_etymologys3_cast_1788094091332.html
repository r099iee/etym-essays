I. The Scandinavian loan (9th–13th c.). Cast is one of the great Scandinavian loanwords of English — words like sky, skin, egg, window, and die that arrived with the Viking settlers of the Danelaw. Old Norse kasta meant "to throw, cast, fling". The verb’s ultimate origin is uncertain: it has cognates in the other Scandinavian languages (Old Swedish kasta, Danish kaste, Norwegian kaste) and in Middle Low German kasten "to throw", which point to a common North Germanic formation, but no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon has been established — the OED marks the derivation as obscure. Some scholars speculate about a connection with Old Norse kǫst- "heap, pile" (to throw things into a heap), an appealing but unproven idea. The honest statement is that the word surfaces fully formed in North Germanic and no older ancestry can be traced — an unusually interesting fate for a word that would become one of the most productive in the language.

II. Middle English: the throw becomes a reckoning (12th–15th c.). English borrowed casten around 1200, and for several centuries it competed with — and gradually displaced — native weorpan "to throw" (cognate with German werfen and English warp). The Middle English verb was astonishingly productive: to cast stones, dice, nets, and spears; to cast lots (deciding by throw); to cast accounts (reckoning, from throwing counters on a board); to cast a horoscope (calculating); to cast the eye; to cast down (depress); to cast out (expel — giving outcast, attested from the 14th century). The noun cast "a throw" appeared by the mid-13th century. Already by 1400 the word had the shape of its modern polysemy: a single verb for throwing, allotting, reckoning, and directing. Phonologically the form stayed stable — Scandinavian /ˈkas.ta/ → Middle English /ˈkas.tən/ → modern /kɑːst/ (RP; /kæst/ in American English) — one of the few major words whose vowels were shaped by later changes but whose consonants remain exactly the Viking originals.

III. The mould and the metal (14th–17th c.). The industrial sense — "to shape by pouring molten metal into a mould" — is attested from the 14th century (casting bells, cannon, and coins), and "cast" as the moulded object from the same period. The semantic logic is the throw: the molten metal is "thrown" or poured into the form. The 17th century added cast-iron (the iron is cast, not wrought), and the 16th–17th centuries extended the mould-sense to plaster, wax, and glass. The noun "a cast" as an exact copy made in a mould (a cast of a statue) dates from the 17th century, and the medical plaster cast for fractures follows in the 19th century: the broken limb is held in a mould of plaster. The punning continuity — the throw, the mould, the copy — made "cast" a natural word for reproduction of every kind.

IV. The theatre and the allotment of parts (16th–19th c.). In the 16th century "to cast" acquired the sense "to allot, distribute, assign" — casting parts to actors as one casts lots or counts counters. By the 1630s "to cast a play" meant to assign the roles, and "the cast" — the company of actors so allotted — became standard theatrical jargon. The phrase "cast of characters" (a list of the dramatis personae) follows in the 19th century. The bridge is the ancient image of the lot: the throw that decides who plays whom. Shakespeare’s theatre was full of the word in both senses — playwrights cast plays, and destiny cast fortunes.

V. The eye, the colour, and the worm (16th–19th c.). Meanwhile the noun proliferated along the throw-image: "a cast of the eye" (16th c.) — a squint, the gaze thrown sideways; "a cast of colour" (by the 18th c.) — a tinge or shade, the surface throw of appearance; "the cast of one’s features" — the thrown shape of the face; "worm casts" — the soil thrown out by earthworms; "a hawk’s cast" — the indigestible pellet thrown up. Fishing "to cast a line" and "a cast" as the throw of the line are firmly established by the 18th century; "to cast anchor" and "to cast off" (nautical and, in knitting, the binding off of stitches) are older still.

VI. The twentieth century and computing (20th–21st c.). In the 20th century the word gained two new domains. Cinema and television made "the cast" a word of daily life — "the cast of thousands", "the ensemble cast". And computing adopted "cast" for the conversion of a value from one data type to another (a value is "thrown" from, say, an integer to a string); "type casting" is standard in C, Java, Python, and most typed languages, and the metaphor is exact: the data keeps its identity but takes a new form, as metal keeps its substance in a new mould. The old verb of the Viking forecastle now runs silently in every compiler on earth.

VII. Summary. The history of cast is a case study in polysemy from a single concrete verb: one Norse word for "throw" generated the mould, the reckoning, the lot, the squint, the tint, the worm-cast, the plaster, the troupe of actors, and the compiler’s type conversion. Each new sense is a new application of the same gesture — a decisive, directed fling. When Tolkien writes that the elves "cast" the barrels through the trapdoors, he is using the oldest sense of the word, unchanged since the Vikings: to throw.

Key literary attestations

  • Middle English chronicles and romances (13th–14th c.): casten of stones, dice, and lots.
  • Chaucer (late 14th c.): "cast" of reckoning and of the eye.
  • Shakespeare (1590s–1610s): "cast" in throwing, reckoning, and allotting senses throughout the plays.
  • The Authorized Version (1611): "cast lots", "cast out", "cast down".
  • 19th-century industrial and medical writing: cast iron, plaster casts.
  • Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937): the elves cast the barrels through the trapdoors.
