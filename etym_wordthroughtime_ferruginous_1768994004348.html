- 17th c. technical debut (c. 1661) - Entered English through geological and medical writers meaning "containing or yielding iron" - iron-bearing earths, springs, and sandstone.
- 18th-19th c. scientific spread - Mineralogy keeps it for iron-stained strata; medicine finds "ferruginous bodies" lodged in the lungs, a breath that deposits rust inside a man.
- 19th-20th c. color detached from composition - Biology adopts it for plumage and pelage (the Ferruginous Hawk, ferruginous ducks), where it now names the dusky reddish-brown of iron oxide rather than actual iron content.
- Contemporary sense - Formal and scientific, the word fuses hue with corrosion: reddish-brown rust understood as the visible product of chemical decay - age, aridity, and a planet's slow oxidation.
