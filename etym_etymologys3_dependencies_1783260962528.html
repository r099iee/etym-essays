At the root of dependencies stands the PIE base *(s)pend-, glossed "to pull, stretch, draw out, spin". Its concrete world is that of the spinner's thread, the stretched cord, and the snare pulled tight — things drawn out and suspended. The cognates are spread wide across the family. Germanic gives Proto-Germanic *spinnan "to spin", source of Old English spinnan (our spin), spannan "to stretch, fasten" (our span), and spīdra "spider", literally "the spinner"; German preserves the pair in spinnen and spannen. Baltic shows Lithuanian spę̃sti "to set a snare, stretch" with spąstai "trap", a stretched thing. Greek yields pénomai "to toil" and pónos "labor, hardship" — the strain of work itself, an abstract extension of the physical pulling.

In Italic the same root gives Latin a celebrated pair of verbs: pendēre "to hang, be suspended" and pendere "to weigh, pay". They are a transitive and an intransitive doublet, both rooted in the image of a scale whose pan hangs and stretches downward as it is weighted. From "weigh" flows the money of pendere — pensiō "payment" (our pension), expendere "to weigh out" (our expend, expense, and spend via Old French despendre), dispendere "to weigh out, distribute" (our dispense) — because ancient payment was literally weighed out in metal. From "hang" flow pendulus "hanging down" (our pendant, pendulum), appendere "to hang on" (our append, appendix), suspendere "to hang up" (our suspend, suspense), impendēre "to hang over" (our impend, impending), and pondus "weight" (our ponder, ponderous). The whole family shows how a single image of stretching and hanging became the vocabulary of money, law, and time.

The decisive step for our word is Latin dēpendēre, "to hang down from" — dē- "down from" + pendēre "to hang". What hangs down from a support is supported by it and cannot stand without it, so dēpendēre shades from the physical (a branch hanging from a tree) to the logical and moral ("to rest upon, be conditioned by, be subject to"). From its present participle dēpendēns, Late and Medieval Latin formed the abstract noun dēpendentia, "the state of hanging down from; dependence". Old French took it as dependance, and Middle English adopted dependence in the fifteenth century and dependency in the sixteenth.

The word's political career began as its grammatical and logical ones developed. In feudal and early modern usage, to "depend on" the crown meant to be held by and answerable to it — a relation of homage and protection. As European states acquired overseas possessions, the noun dependency hardened into an administrative term for exactly that kind of subordinate territory: a land that hung from the crown, protected by its navy, ruled by its governors, yet never quite a part of the home realm. By the eighteenth century the plural dependencies was the standard word of imperial administration, and Macaulay uses it with full awareness of that weight.

  • Sound change. Dependence is a loanword; the Latin dēpendēre → dēpendentia → Old French dependance → English dependence shows ordinary Romance vowel weakening and the loss of Latin endings, with no Germanic sound laws involved.
  • Sibling words. Depend, dependent, independent, independence, pendulum, pendant, append, appendix, suspend, suspense, spend, expense, dispense, pension, pensive, ponder, compensate — all from the same hanging/weighing root.
  • Semantic drift in the singular. The modern everyday sense of dependency — addiction (drug dependency) — is a twentieth-century clinical specialisation of the same image: the addict "hangs on" a substance and cannot stand without it.
  • Literary attestations. Macaulay's History of England; the administrative prose of the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century empires; modern constitutional law (Crown Dependencies); and the medical literature of the twentieth century for the addiction sense.
  • Contemporary note. The imperial plural dependencies is now chiefly historical and legal, while the singular dependency is alive in computing, medicine, economics, and psychology — all still obedient to the ancient image of hanging from a support.
