Ambiguity enters English in the late fourteenth century as ambiguite, borrowed from Old French ambiguite which itself continues Latin ambiguitatem, nominative ambiguitas. The Latin noun is built on the adjective ambiguus, doubtful, going here and there, from the verb ambigere, to drive around, to dispute, to waver. That verb joins the prefix ambi-, around, on both sides, from PIE *h2mbhi, also seen in Greek amphi and Sanskrit abhi, with agere, to drive, do, act, from PIE *h2eg-, to drive. The image is of cattle or argument driven both ways, unable to settle on one path.

In Classical Latin ambiguitas already spans the modern range from linguistic double meaning to general uncertainty. Cicero uses it for oracular obscurity and for disputed points of law; Quintilian warns orators against harmful ambiguitas while teaching them to exploit it. Phonologically the borrowing is conservative: Latin -tas, -tatis becomes French -te and then English -ty by the regular adaptation of abstract nouns, as in vanity, charity, obscurity. Middle English spelling ambiguite shows the French vowel, later Latinised to ambiguity in the sixteenth century under humanist influence.

  • Medieval foundation: scholastic logic treats amphibolia and ambiguitas as fallacies; Chaucerian and Lollard prose uses the word for scriptural difficulty.
  • Early modern moralisation: equivocation controversies after the Gunpowder Plot and Shakespeare's Porter on equivocation load the family with connotations of casuistry and treason.
  • Enlightenment clarification: Port-Royal logic, Locke and legal codification frame ambiguity as a defect to be purged by definition and plain style.
  • Modernist reversal: Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity in 1930, Richards, Brooks and later deconstruction turn the vice into the criterion of literary value and of language itself.

Socio-cultural context explains the swing from censure to celebration. A legal-bureaucratic culture needs univocity for contracts and statutes, so ambiguity remains a term of reproach in law. A poetic culture that prizes universality across time and readership needs polysemy, so the same word becomes praise. Contemporary extensions to constructive ambiguity in the Northern Ireland peace process and Oslo accords, to tolerance of ambiguity as a personality trait, and to strategic ambiguity in central banking and deterrence theory, show the Latin wavering image still productive. The form has barely changed since Cicero, but its valuation has inverted wherever openness is prized over closure.

English usage since 1900 shows steady expansion rather than phonetic change. OED supplements record ambiguities in the plural for tolerable double meanings, ambiguity-aversion in economics after Ellsberg in 1961, and role ambiguity in sociology for unclear job expectations. Each keeps the Latin spelling and stress on the third syllable, proof of continued learned transmission through print rather than popular sound erosion.
