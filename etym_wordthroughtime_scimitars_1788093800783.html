- Curved 'eastern' blade-name — enters English c. 1540s as an exotic novelty from the Ottoman–Persian frontier, heard in travellers' tales and chronicles of the Turkish wars.
- Orientalist emblem — from the 18th century the word becomes a stock prop of the exotic Orient: the crescent blade of the Turk, the Mameluke and the corsair, in verse, opera and painting.
- Romantic glamour and literary menace — Byron and Moore make the scimitar a synecdoche of Eastern danger and splendour; the word reads as luxury and cruelty at once.
- Fantasy re-export — 20th-century fantasy (Tolkien's The Hobbit, role-playing games, film) adopts it as the default curved blade of eastern or monstrous warriors; the plural 'scimitars' becomes a battle-narrative word.
- Shape-adjective today — in modern technical and everyday use the word has detached from warfare entirely, meaning simply 'curved like a scimitar' (scimitar oryx, scimitar bill, scimitar dunes, scimitar winglets); the perception has cooled from weapon to curve.
