PAMPHLET — the biography of a name that became an object. Few English words have a more purely literary origin, for the whole word is a proper noun that lost its capital letter. It begins in ancient Greece with the personal name Pámphilos (Πάμφιλος), "beloved of all", a compound of pân, "all", and phílos, "dear, loving" — the same two elements that later produced philosophy (love of wisdom), philanthropy (love of humankind), and, from the "all" side, panorama, panacea, and Milton's pandemonium. Neither component has a fully secure Indo-European pedigree: Greek pâs, "all", and phílos, "dear", are both words for which specialists — notably Robert Beekes — have entertained a Pre-Greek substrate origin, so the honest record stops at Greek rather than inventing a PIE root for them. The proper name itself, however, has a firmly documented second life. That second life began in the twelfth century. Around 1180 an unknown poet composed a short, witty Latin poem titled Pamphilus, seu de Amore — "Pamphilus, or Concerning Love" — a schoolroom comedy about a young lover, traditionally though falsely attributed to Ovid. The poem became one of the most popular texts of the medieval curriculum: it was copied, read, and memorized across Europe, and because it was short it circulated not in grand bound codices but in cheap, small, unbound gatherings of pages — the medieval equivalent of the leaflet. Students and booksellers took to calling such a little unbound copy by the title-name of the poem in its diminutive form, Pamphilet, "little Pamphilus". From that object-name the term slid by a perfectly natural metonymy from the particular text to the class of object — any short unbound booklet — and crossed into Anglo-Norman and Middle English. English panflet is recorded by 1387, and by the sixteenth century pamphlet was an ordinary English word for a short, unbound, usually polemical treatise. The age of print completed the word's character. The printing press made the pamphlet the cheapest printed thing a man could buy, and from the Reformation onward it was the engine of controversy: Luther's opponents and supporters, the religious polemicists of the sixteenth century, the English Civil War's flood of Leveller and Royalist pamphlets, the great pamphlet wars of the eighteenth century — all made the word a byword for the printed battle. In France the literary pamphlet was a national art form, the weapon of Voltaire, Pascal's Provincial Letters, and the political writers of the Revolution; in England the pamphlet was the medium of Swift's Modest Proposal and Burke's Reflections. The word's democratic, pugnacious colouring was fixed in these centuries: the pamphlet was the forum of the opposition, the poor man's press, the genre of one burning cause argued in a handful of pages.
  • Sound and form: the double -m- of English pamphlet (through Old French pamflet) reflects the medieval spelling; the word has kept its shape essentially unchanged for six centuries.
  • Derived family: pamphleteer (recorded 1642, "one who writes pamphlets") and pamphleteering; pamphlet war for a sustained printed controversy.
  • Socio-cultural role: the cheapest medium of public opinion, the target of censorship from the Tudor licensing laws onward, and therefore a central character in the history of press freedom.
By Ruskin's century the pamphlet was a familiar and slightly old-fashioned weapon. When he says that what became Modern Painters — five volumes over seventeen years, one of the monuments of art criticism — was originally intended as "a short pamphlet", the word performs a quiet irony: the modest, fighting form in which he meant to write was swelled into the monumental treatise the world actually received. The sentence preserves the whole social memory of the word — immediacy, cheapness, partisanship, the angry voice in the pamphlet war — and contrasts it with the vast book that accidentally resulted. The modern fate of the word is a slow eclipse and a stubborn survival. Everyday use has largely handed its jobs to report, brief, leaflet, and brochure, and the physical pamphlet itself has receded before digital media. But the word survives with its old character intact: a pamphlet is still a short, pointed, usually argumentative booklet for a single cause, and its name still carries the ghost of a medieval poem about love and the noise of five centuries of printed battles.
