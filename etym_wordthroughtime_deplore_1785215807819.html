- 16th century lamentation - enters English from Latin meaning to weep bitterly or bewail; to deplore is first to mourn, to lament a loss aloud with grief.
- 16th-18th century grief for wrongs - widens from mourning losses to lamenting wrongs, evils, and misfortunes; the word mixes grief with the wish that the thing had not been.
- 19th century moral disapproval - gains its modern censure: to deplore is to regret deeply while expressing strong disapproval, as the Mill biographer deplores the exaggeration of Mill's references to his wife; sorrow and condemnation are blended.
- Contemporary use - primarily means to strongly disapprove of something; the overtone of weeping is largely worn away, and the word is formal, appearing in public statements and editorial prose.
