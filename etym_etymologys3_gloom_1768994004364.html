The biography of gloom is the biography of light going out — and of the old conviction that a dark sky and a dark heart are the same event. The word stands at the head of a very old Germanic family built on the root of "glow." Its ultimate ancestor, the PIE root *ghel-, meant "to shine, to gleam, to be bright," and it also produced the colour-words "yellow" and "green" (the colour of shining vegetation). Its reach across the family is enormous: Greek khlōros "green-yellow" gave English chlorine and chlorophyll; Latin helvus "honey-yellow" was a rustic colour-word of agricultural Rome; Sanskrit hari "tawny, tawny-green" is the colour of the Rig-Vedic sun-horses; Lithuanian žalias "green" and Old Church Slavonic zelenŭ keep the hue in the Balto-Slavic world; and the Germanic branch gave *gelwaz "yellow" and *gulþą "gold." At the very centre of the root, then, is radiance.

Within Germanic the root produced a family of shine-verbs and shine-nouns in the gl- cluster: *glōaną "to glow" (Old English glōwan), *glīmaną "to glimmer," and the noun *glōmun- "twilight, dusk" — Old English glōmung, "the gloaming," that suspended hour when the glow of the day is almost spent. Here is the pivot of the whole biography. Where "glow" keeps the root's brightness and "glimmer" keeps a faint sparkle, the glōm- grade shaded the meaning toward the dying of the light. Old English glōmung named twilight; a rare gloss recorded the noun glōm "twilight" itself; and from that "fading glow" the sense moved, by a short and natural step, to darkness pure and simple.

The Middle English record then adds a second, human dimension. The verb gloumen (c. 1300) meant "to look sullen, to frown, to be dejected" — the physical lowering of a face in displeasure or misery. The noun gloum, glome followed with "darkness, obscurity," and by c. 1500 "gloom" was firmly established as a word for the dark. It is at this point that the double life of the modern word is fixed, because in the medieval and early-modern worldview the two things were continuous: a heart darkened and a sky darkened alike, both being the withdrawal of light from a place — the world or the spirit. Hence by 1590 the noun meant "a sullen look"; by the 1620s it meant "partial or total darkness, dimness"; and by the mid-18th century (1744) it had acquired "melancholy, dejection, despondency" as a recognized sense of its own.

The word then had a long and distinguished literary career, and its two senses fed each other. Milton uses the dark sense for the infernal regions (the "gloom" of Tartarus in Paradise Lost), where darkness is both physical and moral. The eighteenth-century graveyard poets and the Gothic novelists — Walpole, Radcliffe, Lewis, and Poe after them — made "gloom" a fixture of atmospheric dread: the blackness of ruined abbeys, damp vaults, and haunted landscapes. The Romantics attached the word to moody scenery and to the mind itself, and the Victorians turned "gloom" into a name for a whole climate of feeling: "gloom and doom," "a cloud of gloom," "to cast a gloom over a company." In the twentieth century the word migrated easily into the language of economics and public affairs — "economic gloom," "market gloom," "a mood of national gloom" — where it names pessimism as a collective atmosphere rather than a personal feeling.

Bullet points on the semantic trajectory:
  • c. 1300 — verb gloumen "to look sullen, frown."
  • c. 1500 — noun gloom "darkness, obscurity" established.
  • 1590s — noun sense "a sullen look, a frowning countenance."
  • 1620s — sense "partial or total darkness, dimness."
  • 1744 — sense "melancholy, despondency" recorded.
  • 19th–21st c. — the mood sense rises to everyday dominance; the physical-darkness sense retreats into literary and atmospheric use — exactly the register the source passage needs.
Sound-shift note: the word's gl- onset belongs to the large English sound-symbolic cluster gl- (glow, gleam, glimmer, glare, glisten, glass), where the initial gl- seems to carry "light" or "the look" across dozens of words; Grimm's law had earlier turned the PIE velar *gh (from *ghel-) into the Germanic /g/, giving the modern voiced onset. The final -m of gloom, and of its sister gloaming, preserves the old -m- that marked the "fading" grade of the root.

Final note: gloom is thus a paradox resolved — a word of radiance that became a word of darkness and then of sadness, moving from light, to dying light, to the dark, to the darkening of the spirit. It now serves double duty: a heavy physical darkness almost old enough to be a substance, and the quiet name of the world's melancholy — both of them alive in the single sentence that tears a dotted gloom in a cave that never knew light.
