- 14th Century (Late Middle English, c. 1380s) - Borrowed via Old French and Late Latin by Geoffrey Chaucer in his translation of Boethius's *De Consolatione Philosophiae*; signifies a gratuitous addition, surplus gift, or bonus reward beyond what is strictly due.
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Scientific Revolution) - Firmly specialized in Euclidean geometry, formal logic, and natural philosophy (attested in Billingsley's Euclid, 1570); denotes a proposition that follows immediately and effortlessly from a demonstrated theorem without separate proof.
- 18th to 19th Century (Enlightenment to Victorian Era) - Broadens from technical mathematics into political philosophy, jurisprudence, and literary criticism, characterizing natural logical deductions and inevitable social consequences of political doctrines.
- 20th Century - Enshrined in global diplomatic history through the "Roosevelt Corollary" (1904) to the Monroe Doctrine, while flourishing in economic theory and sociology to describe inevitable systemic byproducts of industrial modernization.
- 21st Century - Continues as an essential high-register analytical term across academic prose, legal theory, and translation criticism, pinpointing the downstream conceptual consequences of ideological decisions.
