Latin and Romance origin: barred is the past participle of the verb "to bar," which descends from Latin barra, "a bar, rod, beam, barrier." Latin barra is first attested in the 1st century BCE and is itself of uncertain origin: the most widely repeated theory is that it was borrowed from a Gaulish (Celtic) substrate word for a "beam, pole, pointed rod" — a plausible transfer, since the word appears late in Latin and is confined to the Romanic languages (French barre, Spanish barra, Italian barra), with no solid Italic or Indo-European etymon. Some older dictionaries guessed at a link to Greek or to a PIE root of "stick, pole," but none is accepted. The honest statement is: Latin barra "bar, beam" appears in the classical period, probably from a Celtic source, and its deeper ancestry is unknown.

Old French and English: Old French had the noun barre (10th-11th centuries: a bar, bolt, barrier) and the verb barrer ("to bar, to bolt, to fasten with a bar; also to mark with bars"). English borrowed the noun "bar" in the 12th century and the verb "to bar" in the 13th century (Middle English barren): "to fasten with a bar," "to block up," "to obstruct." The past participle barred is attested from the 13th century onward in all these senses. The word took the sense "to forbid, exclude" from the 16th century ("barred from the kingdom," "barred from the guild"), developing from the image of a physical barrier placed across a way or door.

The striped sense: "to bar" also meant "to cross with bars, to mark with stripes" — the verb describing the application of bars of colour or shade, and the participle describing surfaces so marked. This pictorial sense is ancient in the word's history: a barred surface is one ruled or banded like the rods of a grating. In heraldry, "barred" describes a field crossed by horizontal bars; in natural history, "barred" marks plumage or fur crossed by bands (the barred owl, Strix varia); in astronomy, the "barred spiral galaxy" (20th century) is a galaxy whose arms spring from a central bar of stars. The Tolkien sentence — "a pale sky barred with black" — uses exactly this sense: bars of black cloud striping the pale sky, as if the sky were a field ruled with bands.

Legal and professional "bar": the noun "bar" developed a famous special sense: the railing or bar in a law court that separates the judges from the parties — hence "the bar" (the legal profession), "called to the bar" (admitted as a barrister), and "barred from practice" (expelled). "Time-barred" and "statute-barred" describe claims that can no longer be brought because the legally allowed time has passed. These uses turn the simple image of a physical bar into a set of institutional prohibitions.

Semantic history summary:

  • 13th century: "barred" = fastened with bars, bolted; blocked up.
  • 14th-15th centuries: extended to obstruction of roads, passes, and gates.
  • 16th century: extended to persons — "barred" from entry, from a right, from office; the prohibitive sense is fixed.
  • 17th century onward: the striped sense ("marked with bars") becomes established in heraldry, textiles, and natural description; "barred" = "banded, striped."
  • 18th-21st centuries: institutional uses grow (legal bar, barred from practice, time-barred); the striped sense stays alive in nature writing, design, and astronomy (barred galaxies); "barred" for "prevented/prohibited" is standard in journalism and law.

Cultural profile: "barred" neatly spans the physical and the institutional: the same word describes a prison window's iron rods, a door bolted shut, a person excluded by law, and a sky striped with clouds. Its history from a doubtful Celtic or Latin "beam" through Old French to a thousand modern uses shows how a concrete object word becomes a set of metaphors for obstruction, exclusion, and pattern. In Tolkien, "the stars were coming out behind him in a pale sky barred with black" — the word makes the sky a heraldic field crossed by bars, and the sense of confinement and danger the bars imply suits the hobbit's fearful venture into the Mountain.
