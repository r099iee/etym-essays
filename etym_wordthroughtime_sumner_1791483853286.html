- 13th–14th century, England - a surname for the summoner, the ecclesiastical court officer who cited sinners and debtors; the name carries the unpopularity of petty legal authority.
- 14th century - Chaucer's Summoner fixes the type in literary memory as red-faced, corrupt and lecherous, giving the name a faint comic stain.
- 17th–18th century - the surname crosses to New England and becomes an ordinary respectable family name, its occupational sting long forgotten.
- 1811–1874 - Charles Sumner of Massachusetts makes the name a watchword of abolitionism and Radical Reconstruction; to be a Sumner in Boston is to be on the radical edge of antislavery politics.
- 1872–1910 - William Graham Sumner at Yale turns the name into a synonym for laissez-faire, academic freedom and the “forgotten man”; it now suggests a doctrine as much as a family.
- 1880s–1940s - after his death the label “social Darwinist” attaches to him, and the name comes to stand for hard-hearted competition, especially in progressive and social-gospel polemic.
- 1906–1960s - Folkways makes mores, ethnocentrism and in-group standard vocabulary; the name is cited respectfully in sociology while his politics fall out of favour.
- 1944–present - Hofstadter's critique fixes the negative image, then libertarian and revisionist scholars reclaim him as a proto-public-choice thinker; the name becomes a site of political argument.
- 21st century - beyond academia the surname is mostly encountered in place names, in the phrase “the forgotten man”, and in the given name of the singer Sting; its intellectual charge survives mainly in textbooks.
