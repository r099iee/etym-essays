Orcrist is a proper noun from J. R. R. Tolkien's fantasy canon: the name of an Elvish sword — the "Goblin-cleaver" — carried by the dwarf Thorin Oakenshield in The Hobbit (1937). In the reading context, after the Battle of Five Armies, the Elvenking lays Orcrist upon Thorin's tomb beneath the Lonely Mountain, honouring the fallen dwarf-king with the blade that had been taken from him in captivity.

Object facts (verifiable from Tolkien's text):

  • Orcrist was forged long before the events of The Hobbit, in Gondolin, the Hidden City of the High Elves in the First Age — the same city that produced its companion blade Glamdring ("Foe-hammer"), which Gandalf carried.
  • It was found, with Glamdring and the small sword Sting, in the cave of the three trolls (Tom, Bert, and William); Elrond examined the blades, read their runes, and translated their names: Orcrist means "Goblin-cleaver" (literally the Elvish words for "orc" and "cleaver").
  • Thorin carried it through the quest. In Goblin-town the goblins recognized the ancient blades with terror, calling Orcrist "Biter" and Glamdring "Beater"; the nicknames record generations of goblin corpses.
  • When Thorin was captured by the Elves of Mirkwood, the Elvenking took the sword — it is not returned during Thorin's confinement, and this very confiscation is what the context sentence recalls ("the elvish sword that had been taken from Thorin in captivity").
  • After the Battle of Five Armies Thorin dies of his wounds; the Elvenking lays Orcrist on his tomb, and the book records the belief that it gleamed in the darkness whenever foes pressed near — the characteristic light of Elvish blades when enemies approach.

Meaning of the name:

  • Sindarin (the Grey-elven language Tolkien devised): orch "orc, goblin" + crist "cleaver, sword" — "Orc-cleaver".
  • Tolkien's own translation in the story is "Goblin-cleaver"; the goblins' "Biter" is their dread nickname.
  • As a constructed word it has no polysemy in the ordinary-language sense: it exists to name one artifact. Its "layers of meaning" are: (1) the literal gloss; (2) the in-universe prestige of Gondolin manufacture; (3) the terror it carried among goblins; and (4) its modern status as an icon of fantasy weaponry in Tolkien fandom, films (the 2012–2014 adaptations show Thorin's sword), and licensed games.

Secondary facts for completeness:

  • Orcrist does not appear again after being laid on the tomb; its story ends with Thorin's. The sword's literary epitaph is the song-saying that it gleamed when enemies pressed near.
  • By contrast Glamdring remains with Gandalf through The Lord of the Rings — the two blades of Gondolin diverge in the narrative, one following the story onward, one sealed with its bearer.
  • The dwarves never name the sword in their own tongue; it is the Elves — Elrond first, the Elvenking of Mirkwood last — who read the runes and the name. The name is therefore doubly Elvish, in origin and in use.

Register note: as a vocabulary item, Orcrist is a name rather than a common word; its "definition" is the complete object history above, and its closest relatives are the other named blades of Tolkien's legendarium rather than ordinary synonyms.
