- 16th-century literal sense - 'a whisker' is anything that whisks or brushes, a nimble flicking thing; applied first to the hair of the face, which flicks and brushes like a wisp
- 17th-18th century - the word shifts from any face-hair to the emphatic hair of the upper lip and cheeks, the moustache and its companion growth; a marker of a man's countenance in an age of beards and periwigs
- 19th century - the Victorian side-whisker comes into its own: 'whiskers' name the long, flowing hair worn on the cheeks, at once fashionable and faintly comic, and the word moves on to the sensitive facial bristles of cats, dogs and other beasts
- Contemporary sense - the tactile vibrissa of animals is the primary sense, and by a further step 'whisker' means the very smallest degree of anything, so that to win 'by a whisker' is to win by a hair's breadth, and 'not to stir a whisker' is to make no movement at all
