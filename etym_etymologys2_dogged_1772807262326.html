The adjective DOGGED is a native English derivation from the noun dog; the noun itself, however, is one of the famous puzzles of English etymology, and there is NO secure PIE chain for it. The stages below state the attested record and mark the unknown origin explicitly. - Old English docga "a dog, especially a strong or mastiff-like dog" (sparsely attested, 11th century; the ordinary Old English word for the animal was hund "hound"). Ultimate origin unknown: proposals range over a diminutive or nursery formation, an unattested Germanic borrowing, and an unidentified loan, but none is secure, and no PIE root for dog is reconstructable → - Middle English dogge "dog" (attested from the 12th–13th centuries; spreads and becomes the ordinary word for the animal) → - Early Modern / Modern English dog "domestic dog" (from the 16th century the general word, largely displacing hound in everyday use) → - English to dog "to follow closely, to track persistently, to hound" (verb derived from the noun, attested from the 16th century) → - English dogged (adjective; noun + adjectival suffix -ed, "having the character of a dog, dog-like"), first attested in the 14th century with "currish, surly, malicious" senses, shifting toward "tenacious, grimly persistent" from the 16th–18th centuries → - DOGGED "tenacious, grimly persistent" (modern adjective). Comparative note (not a PIE chain): the older English word hound (Old English hund) continues the PIE root *ḱwṓn- "dog" (cf. Latin canis, Greek κύων kyōn, Sanskrit śvan-), the same root as German Hund. English dog is NOT from that root; it is an etymologically opaque native word that came to displace hound for the general animal. Consequently dogged is built on the opaque noun dog, not on *ḱwṓn-, and any deep PIE derivation of dogged would require a PIE origin for dog that is neither attested nor reconstructable. Debated points: the origin of dog is genuinely unknown; suggested connections do not extend to PIE, and this gap is stated explicitly rather than filled by guesswork.
