- Late 14th c. — the fickle mind: appears in Middle English as newefangel in Chaucer's poetry, describing fickle, novelty-hungry *people* eager to grasp every passing trend.
- 15th–16th c. — religious and cultural condemnation: wielded during the English Reformation by conservative clerics and social critics to denounce 'new-fangled' church doctrines and foreign Italianate fashions.
- Late 16th c. — the participial shift: the suffix -ed is attached by popular analogy, shifting the word from describing fickle humans to labeling the novel customs, garments, and inventions themselves.
- 18th c. — Augustan linguistic and technological satire: Swift and Pope use new-fangled to mock rapid slang innovations among youths ('sea Yahoos') and the bizarre schemes of Royal Society projectors.
- 19th c. — the Industrial Revolution backlash: widely adopted by craftsmen, mariners, and rural folk to grumble against steam engines, railways, mechanical looms, and telegraphy.
- 20th c. — archetypal curmudgeon's complaint: popularized in twentieth-century literature, radio, and cinema as the quintessential grumble of grandfathers facing television sets, rock-and-roll, and microwave ovens.
- Today — affectionate and ironic tech-skepticism: used humorously or self-deprecatingly to describe smartphones, generative AI algorithms, smart appliances, and fleeting digital slang.
