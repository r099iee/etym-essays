- 1st c. BCE — Latin cognatus is a legal and familial term: a blood relative, as opposed to an agnatus traced through the male line; it is sober, precise and confined to the law courts.
- Medieval Latin — cognatus survives in canon and civil law as a general word for kinship, often paired with agnatus in discussions of inheritance.
- 14th–16th c. — French cognat and English legal writing use the word for maternal or blood kin; it remains a learned, bookish term.
- 1640s — English adopts cognate as an adjective meaning “of the same blood, related by birth”; within a decade it extends to “of the same nature or kind.”
- Late 18th–19th c. — Comparative philology makes “cognate languages” and “cognate words” technical terms; the word becomes the everyday instrument of the Indo-European hypothesis.
- 20th c. — Linguistics formalises the notion (cognate sets, cognate percentage, false cognates), while biology and immunology borrow it for shared ancestry and specific receptor pairing.
- 21st c. — The word is routine in language technology (“cognate detection”), in academic prose about “cognate disciplines,” and in popular writing about language families; its tone is scholarly but no longer obscure.
