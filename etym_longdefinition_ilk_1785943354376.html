ILK (noun; used almost exclusively in the phrases "of that ilk" and "he and his ilk") — kind, sort, class, or category of people or things, usually implying a shared and often unfavourable or disreputable character. In modern English the word is short, dense, and slightly cutting: "politicians of that ilk", "you and your ilk" dismiss a group as a breed, a faction, or a type. Its older and still living Scottish meaning is quite different — "of the same (name and place)" — which makes ilk one of the strangest cases in English of a word whose modern sense grew out of a misunderstanding of an ancient phrase. Core senses and usages
  • Modern colloquial sense (the dominant sense): a class or kind of person, typically with a dismissive, derogatory, or amused tone. "He and his ilk" is the fixed formula: it lumps a person together with a type the speaker does not admire, and it is a favourite of political and editorial prose.
  • Neutral-to-technical sense: in formal, encyclopaedic, or scientific writing ilk can be used without open hostility, simply as a stylistic variant for "class, genus, or category" — though even here it tends to carry a faint smile.
  • Scottish legal-heraldic sense (the ancient sense): in the style of Scottish landed gentry, "X of that ilk" means "X of the same name and place" — that is, the laird whose surname is identical to the name of his estate. "Macintosh of that ilk" means Macintosh of Macintosh, "Chisholm of that ilk" Chisholm of Chisholm. This usage, still current in Scottish peerages and estate documents, preserves the word's original meaning, "the same".
  • Archaic demonstrative sense: in older English and Scots, that ilk meant simply "that same", as a demonstrative — "the ilk man", "this ilk day". This is the sense from which everything else descends.
Why the word means what it does — the semantic bridge Ilk is the modern survivor of the Old English word ilca, "the same", which in turn comes from a Proto-Germanic compound meaning literally "having that shape", "that-like" — the demonstrative i- "that" joined to the root of "like". In Middle English ilke meant "the same", and the phrase that ilk meant "that same one". The bridge to the modern sense is a small but famous accident of Scottish history. From the fifteenth century, Scottish lairds styled themselves "X of that ilk" — "X of that same", i.e., "of the estate bearing his own name". When English readers and writers met this formula in Scottish titles, novels, and gazetteers, they misread it: to them "of that ilk" looked exactly like "of that kind" or "of that class", since the phrase appeared where one would expect a category word. In the eighteenth century this reanalysis hardened into a new noun: Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) recorded "ilk; Scotch. Same", and by the nineteenth century ilk was an ordinary English noun meaning "kind, sort, class". The modern, slightly contemptuous use ("he and his ilk") is the final stage: the word carried with it the flavour of the foreign, the gentry, and the factional, and so it lends any group it names a faint air of being a dubious breed. Register and connotation Today ilk is informal-to-formal, most at home in journalism and speech-making, and almost always pejorative or arch. It collocates with possessive adjectives (his ilk, their ilk) and with of-phrases (of that ilk). Its short, heavy form makes it emphatic: three letters carry a whole gesture of dismissal.
