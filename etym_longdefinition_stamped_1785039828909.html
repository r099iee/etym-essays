STAMPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb stamp, used of bringing the foot down heavily and forcibly upon the ground to express emotion or create noise — and, by the same pressing logic, of impressing a mark upon paper, metal, or another surface with a die or instrument. In the Great Goblin's cavern, the word catches the brute joy of the goblin mob: the bound dwarves are driven in, and the crowd laughs, stamps, and claps, turning the captives' humiliation into a percussive festival of noise. The feet are not placed but slammed down; each impact is a jeer, and poor little Bilbo at the back, nearest to the whips, is the special target of the taunt. The monosyllabic crunch of st- and -mp mirrors the thudding sound it names, so the sentence delivers sound and motion at once.

Etymologically, stamp descends from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to support, place firmly," which in Germanic took a nasalized form meaning "to pound, crush." The same pounding logic produced both the foot-thud and the imprint: the verb first meant to pound or crush in a mortar, then to strike the foot down, then to strike a die onto a surface. The word therefore links the stamping foot of anger with the stamp on an envelope.

Core senses in detail:

  • Primary — foot-stamping (verb): To bring the foot down heavily and forcibly, once or repeatedly, to express anger, impatience, excitement, or to make a percussive sound. "The crowd stamped their feet on the cavern floor in unison."
  • Primary — to crush or pound (verb): To beat, crush, or bruise with a heavy implement; to tamp down. "He stamped out the embers of the camp fire."
  • Extended — to imprint with a die (verb): To press a design or mark into a surface with a die, seal, or instrument; to mark a document or envelope. "The letter was stamped with the official seal."
  • Extended — the mark itself (noun, stamp): The mark, impression, or seal so made; by extension any distinctive mark or hallmark. "The story bore the stamp of truth."
  • Extended — the instrument (noun, stamp): The die, seal, or instrument used to make an impression; also an official stamp of approval.
  • Extended — the postage stamp (noun): The adhesive paper label that prepays postage, introduced in Britain in 1840; the most familiar modern use of the noun.
  • Extended — character or type (noun): Kind, sort, or class: "a man of that stamp"; also a heavy fall or blow, "a stamp of the foot."
  • Extended — the past participle (STAMPED): Marked, endorsed, or certified (a stamped document); crushed or tamped down (stamped-out embers); of feet, brought down heavily (the goblins stamped).

Semantic bridge and rationale:

  • From pounding to marking: The ancient sense "to pound, crush" underlies both modern lives: the pestle pounding in a mortar is the same motion as the foot slamming down and the die striking paper. Pressure plus impact equals stamp.
  • From noise to imprint: The transfer from foot-thud to die-impression took place in the 15th–16th centuries as metalworking and printing grew; the stamp of a mint or seal is the residue of force applied.
  • From imprint to identity: The figurative senses — "the stamp of genius," "a man of that stamp" — treat a distinctive mark as the sign of a person's or thing's character, a metaphor fixed since the 16th century.
  • Register: The word is plain and forceful in the foot sense, neutral and technical in the marking sense, and idiomatic in the figurative uses.

Modern usage contexts:

  • Emotion and ceremony: Stamping the feet remains the classic public gesture of anger, impatience, or emphatic approval (applause with stamping).
  • Postal and official: A stamped envelope, a stamped passport, a stamped receipt — all carry the official imprint of approval or payment.
  • Industry and manufacture: Stamping is the technical term for pressing shapes out of sheet metal with a die; stamped parts are everywhere in engineering.
  • Idiom: "stamped out" (extinguished or eradicated), "stamp of approval," "bear the stamp of," "stamped with the mark of."

In short, STAMPED fuses suddenness, flat-soled impact, and expressive purpose; in the Great Goblin's hall that purpose is ritualized mockery — the whole mob beating out its glee with the crushing weight of the ancient word.
