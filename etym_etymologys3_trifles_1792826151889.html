The history of trifles is the history of a shrug given grammar. It begins in Old French, where trufle or trifle meant mockery, raillery, an idle or deceiving tale — talk that fills the ear while starving the mind. When Middle English borrowed it around 1300, it named exactly that: a false or frivolous story, the kind of tale told by idlers to pass an evening.

From tales the meaning widened with the ease of water finding level ground. If idle talk is a trifle, why not an idle pursuit, an idle ornament, an idle worry. By the late fourteenth century the verb triflen had appeared beside the noun, meaning to speak idly and then to act idly, to dally, to waste sweetness on desert air. The noun and verb fed each other for six centuries: trifles are what triflers handle, and trifling is what one does with trifles.

The sixteenth century added the famous dessert, and scholars still dispute its bridge. One account says the dish — sponge, wine, jam, custard, cream layered in a glass — was so light and quickly assembled from odds and ends that cooks called it a trifle beside substantial baked works. Another hears the verb of frothing: cream whipped into a standing froth is literally trifled up out of nothing. Either way the dessert preserves the word's lightness lovingly rather than scornfully, and it remains the one trifle English welcomes instead of dismissing.

Shakespeare and his age gave the word its moral electricity. Trifles light as air are to the jealous confirmations strong as proofs of holy writ made the noun the seed of tragedy, while heroes who trifle with vows, crowns, or affections play the fool on the edge of ruin. Tolkien inherits exactly this charge: Elven-smiths who call the lesser rings trifles speak with the serene arrogance of makers, and the narrator's quiet rejoinder — yet still dangerous for mortals — replays the old warning that masters misjudge the weight their playthings carry for weaker hands.

Phonetically the word barely moved across seven centuries: French trufle softened its vowel toward trifle, Middle English levelled the ending, and modern plurals added a hissing -s that suits the word's dismissive breath. No great sound shift touched it, which fits a word that has always preferred to slip past unnoticed rather than to march.

Stages and bridges at a glance:

  • French mockery to English falsehood: borrowing carried the sense of deceiving talk whole into Middle English, the seed of all later minimising.
  • Talk to things: generalisation widened worthless words into worthless objects, worries, sums, and delays — the great leap that made the modern noun.
  • Noun to verb and back: triflen moralised the noun into conduct, so that handling trifles became the character flaw of trifling, which in turn deepened the noun's scorn.
  • Scorn to sweetness: the dessert branch redeemed the lightness as delight, proving the same semantic stuff could feast as well as dismiss.
  • Dismissal to quantifier: a trifle tired bleached the noun into polite understatement, the drawing-room descendant of the medieval shrug.
  • Arrogance to warning in Tolkien: the source sentence revives the Shakespearean irony where the mighty misname peril as practice, and the lesser rings outgrow their makers' contempt.

Today trifles lives comfortably in all its layers at once: the philosopher's dismissal, the hostess's dessert, the lover's quarrel over nothing, and the craftsman's fatal underestimation. Few short words carry so complete a theory of value — that worth lies not in the thing but in the eye that weighs it, and that the eye of power is a poor scale for the peril of the weak.
