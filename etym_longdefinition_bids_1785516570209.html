Bids is the third-person singular present indicative form of the verb bid, and in the sentence "The Lord of the Eagles bids you to bring your prisoners to the Great Shelf" it carries the oldest surviving English sense of that verb: to command, order, enjoin, or formally invite. The word's register is distinctive — authoritative yet courteous — because its deep etymology is not "command" but "ask." Modern English bid is a famously polysemous verb, and each of its senses deserves separate treatment. SENSE 1 — COMMAND, DIRECT, ENJOIN (the archaic/literary sense, the sense of the card): - Means to order or direct someone to do something, often softened with the courtesy of a request: "He bade them enter"; "She bade him farewell." - Grammar: this sense historically follows the strong pattern bid / bade / bidden. In contemporary use the strong forms bade and bidden are archaic or literary, while the present-tense forms (bids, bidding) remain current and unmarked; Tolkien, a philologist writing with deliberately archaic diction, exploits exactly this dignity. - Register: quasi-Biblical, courtly, chivalric — the natural vocabulary of fairy tale, fantasy, and period fiction. The Eagles of Tolkien's legendarium are majestic, sentient servants of the Vala Manwë, and their "bidding" is courteous command, a summoning dressed in the polite fiction of request. SENSE 2 — FORMALLY INVITE, SUMMON: - "They were bidden to the feast"; "bid someone welcome." A courteous summons to an occasion; the politeness of the verb's "asking" past is strongest here. SENSE 3 — OFFER A PRICE (auction and commerce): - "She bid £500 for the painting"; the noun: "a bid for the contract," "a takeover bid." Modern and dominant in business contexts. Historically this sense descends from a different Old English verb, bēodan "to offer, proclaim," which merged with biddan in Middle English. - Grammar: weak forms bid / bid / bid; the past is now usually bid rather than bade in this sense. SENSE 4 — DECLARE IN CARD GAMES: - In bridge and other trick-taking games, to "bid" is to declare how many tricks one proposes to win; "the bidding" names the auction phase of the game. This sense extends the commercial "offer" sense into the rules of play. SENSE 5 — ATTEMPT, STRUGGLE TOWARD A GOAL: - "a bid for power," "an escape bid," "a bid for the presidency." The noun has broadened from a competitive offer to any serious attempt or effort, by metaphoric extension of the auction's competitive gesture. SENSE 6 — ARCHAIC AND IDIOMATIC FOSSILS: - "to bid farewell" (to say farewell), "to bid defiance" (to offer resistance), "to bid adieu," "to bid good morning." Formulaic survivals of the strong verb's older range of "saying" and "wishing." HISTORICAL BRIDGE: All the command-and-invite senses descend from Old English biddan, "to ask, entreat, pray, beseech" — a humble word. In a strictly hierarchical society, when a superior "asked," the request functioned as a command dressed in courtesy, and over centuries the verb climbed the social ladder from "ask/pray" to "command/enjoin." The auction and card-game senses arrived by a separate route, through the merger with bēodan "to offer, proclaim," and then themselves bred the abstract "attempt" sense. Bids, therefore, is the tense-and-person form of a single word that carries within it an entire history of social power, courtliness, commerce, and play.
