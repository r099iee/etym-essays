I. The Indo-European inheritance (c. 4500–2500 BCE). The verb heave descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *keh₂p- "to seize, grasp, take", the same root that produced Latin capere "to take", Latin capax "capacious", and Greek kṓpē "handle, oar-loop". The semantic link between "seizing" and "lifting" is the act of grasping something and raising it: to take hold of a load is to prepare to lift it. In Proto-Germanic the root was reshaped with the denominative suffix -janą to form *hafjaną "to lift, raise", a class VI strong verb whose past stem *hōf- shows the characteristic a → ō ablaut (the pattern of shake/shook).

II. Old English hebban (8th–11th c.). The Old English reflex was hebban, with the paradigm hebban – hōf – hōfon – hafen "to lift, raise". It is attested in the earliest poetry: in Beowulf (manuscript c. 10th c.) warriors hebban shields and swords; the same verb raises voices in song, exalts God in religious verse, and lifts a corpse onto its pyre. The derivative heofon "heaven" is probably a separate formation from the same root — literally "the raised, arched place" — so heave and heaven are distant cousins. Already in Old English the verb split between literal raising and figurative exaltation.

III. Middle English: weakening and widening (12th–15th c.). Under the general re-shaping of the English verb system, hebban became heven (also heuen), and the strong past hōf yielded to hevede and finally heaved, though the nautical strong form hove survived as a fossil. Middle English heven broadened: "to raise up", but also "to throw, toss", "to raise a cry or shout", "to raise from the dead" (in devotional texts), and "to exalt". The noun heve "a lift, a throw, an upheaval" appears by the 13th century. Chaucer uses the verb of physical and emotional raising alike.

IV. The nautical century (14th–19th c.). From the 14th century the sea became the word’s natural habitat. "Heave ho!" — the rhythmic cry coordinating a crew hauling on a rope — is recorded in Middle English (c. 1300) as heue ho, and by the age of sail "heave" was standard for hauling up anchors, yards, and cables, and for "heaving to" (bringing a ship to a standstill). The sailor’s world also produced heave in sight (a ship rising over the horizon) and the fossil past hove. It is from this nautical register that the modern phrase "the heave-ho" (dismissal, mid-20th century) ultimately derives: to give someone the heave-ho is to throw them out as a crew throws cargo.

V. Bodily and scientific extensions (16th–19th c.). By the 16th century the verb attached itself to the body: to heave a sigh treats breath as a heavy load pushed out; by the late 18th century to heave alone meant "to retch, vomit" — the gut’s upward spasm read as a heave. Natural philosophy and geology took up the noun in turn: the earth "heaves" in earthquakes; a geological heave (19th century) is the horizontal displacement along a fault (contrast throw, the vertical); frost heave (20th century) is the swelling of frozen ground. The derived noun upheaval (19th century) transferred the earth-motion to politics and society.

VI. Modern English (20th–21st c.). Today heave is compact and muscular: weightlifters heave barbells, seas heave in swells, chests heave with exertion or grief, stomachs heave in nausea, and "heave-ho" remains a comic idiom of dismissal. Its register has never fully gentrified — it still sounds like effort. The word illustrates a classic English pattern: an Indo-European root of grasping, carried through Germanic strong-verb morphology, re-levelled into a weak verb, colonised by the sea, and then exported into medicine, geology, engineering, and everyday slang, all while preserving its original image of lifting something heavy against gravity.

Key literary attestations

  • Beowulf (manuscript c. 10th c., composed earlier): hebban of shields and swords in battle.
  • Chaucer (late 14th c.): heven of physical and emotional raising.
  • Shakespeare, The Tempest (1611): the sailors’ song with "Heave ho!" — the cry enters literature.
  • Smollett and the sea-novel tradition (18th c.): "heave" as the workhorse verb of the forecastle.
  • Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937): "Heave ho! Splash plump! Down they go, down they bump!" — the old cry alive in children’s adventure.
