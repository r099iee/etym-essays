ENSUING is the present participle (used as adjective) of English ensue, a verb borrowed from French, ultimately from Latin. The chain runs: - PIE *sekw- "to follow" (reconstructed root of the follow-word-family across Indo-European; sister branches: Greek hepomai "I follow", Sanskrit sacate "follows, accompanies") → - Latin sequī "to follow, to come after, to result" (deponent verb; attested Classical Latin) → - Latin insequī "to follow upon, to come after, to pursue" (prefix in- "upon, after" + sequī; attested Classical Latin) → - Old French ensuivre / ensivre "to follow after, to result" (from the popular Latin development of insequī / insequere; attested from the 9th–12th centuries) → - Middle English ensue(n) "to follow, to come after, to result" (attested from the 14th century) → - English ensue "to follow as a consequence or in time" → - English ensuing (present participle lexicalised as an adjective, "following as a result"; attested from the 16th century) → - ENSUING "happening afterward or as a result". Notes on sister forms and debated points: - The same PIE *sekw- root produced many Latin follow-words now in English: consequence, consequent (Latin consequī), sequence and sequel (Latin sequēla "that which follows"), pursue and prosecute (Latin prosequī), second (Latin secundus "following, second"), and social / associate (Latin socius "companion, one who follows with"). - Greek hepomai "I follow" and Sanskrit sacate are cognate sister branches of *sekw-, not stages of the English chain. - Some scholars connect Germanic *sehwan- "to see" (English see, sight) with *sekw- on a specialised "follow with the eyes" reading; this is a debated side-branch and is NOT the direct path of ensue. - The direct path of ensue is strictly Latin → French → English, not a native Germanic inheritance of *sekw-.
