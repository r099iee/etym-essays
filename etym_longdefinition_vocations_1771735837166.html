VOCATIONS, plural noun (singular VOCATION). In the Gibbon passage the word refers to the new specialized professional callings that grew up within the discipline of history in the early nineteenth century. The sentence explains that professional historians reached beyond classical scholarship and textual criticism to draw on archaeology, philology, anthropology, sociology, and economics, thereby creating "new vocations of searchers for facts and movements and forces." A vocation here is a distinct professional identity and career — a calling — and the plural signals that one unified discipline had fragmented into many specialized roles: the economic historian, the social historian, the archaeologist-historian, each with its own methods, training, and journals. The core senses of VOCATION:
  • A calling; a strong inner summons to a particular kind of work. The religious origin of the word survives in this sense: a vocation is work one feels divinely or morally summoned to do, not merely a job one chooses for pay. "She found her true vocation in teaching."
  • A career or occupation for which one has a natural aptitude and sense of purpose. In modern secular use, vocation names the work a person is suited for and drawn to: "Many doctors feel their work is a vocation, not just a job."
  • A professional role or calling within an institutional framework (plural especially). This is the sense in the passage: the distinct, institutionalized specializations of a discipline — the "vocations" of the historian, the archaeologist, the economic analyst. The plural collects a field's differentiated professional identities.
  • In Catholic and Anglican usage, the call to the priesthood or religious life. "To have a vocation" in this sense is to be called by God to a life of religious service, the earliest English sense of the word (15th century).
Secondary and extended senses, with historical bridges:
  • Vocational (adjective). Of or relating to a calling or occupation, now chiefly in vocational education and vocational training — instruction aimed at preparing students for a trade or profession rather than for university. The bridge: any work one is trained and suited for, not just learned professions.
  • Avocation (noun). Literally "a calling away" — a minor occupation or hobby pursued in addition to one's main work. The Latin prefix a(v)- "away from" redirects the root vocare "to call": one's avocation is what calls one away from one's vocation. Often confused with vocation in careless usage.
  • Profession and occupation as near-synonyms with different shades. Profession emphasizes formal training, credentials, and learned standing; occupation is the neutral word for any paid work. Vocation adds the dimension of inner suitability and a sense of summons that the other two lack.
  • Calling as a virtual synonym. Calling is the plain-English twin of vocation, with the same religious ancestry; vocation carries slightly more formal, institutional weight, which is why the passage can speak of creating new vocations within a discipline.
The historical bridge that explains the polysemy: Latin vocatio (from vocare, "to call," itself from vox, "voice") meant simply "a calling" — in Roman law, a summons to court. Christian Latin gave it a spiritual sense: God's call to the individual. The Vulgate uses vocatio for Paul's word about being called to the Christian life (Romans 11:29, 1 Corinthians 7:20). English took the word in the 1430s in exactly this religious sense — a divine summons, especially to the religious life. In the 16th century, under Reformation theology, the sense broadened: the Protestant reformers taught that every honest occupation is a calling from God, so that one's daily work itself became a vocation. In the 19th century, as professions professionalized and specialized — precisely the process the passage describes — the word took on its institutional career sense, and the plural vocations named the differentiated professional roles within a field. Usage and collocations:
  • Common collocations: true vocation, find one's vocation, sense of vocation, vocational training, vocational guidance, new vocations, a vocation for teaching, answer a vocation, calling and vocation used interchangeably.
  • Register: formal to semi-formal; retains a faint religious resonance even in secular contexts.
  • Family words: vocation, vocational, vocationally, avocation, avocational; calling; profession; occupation.
  • Antithesis: drudgery, job, gig, employment — words that stress work as mere livelihood without the dimension of summons or suitability.
