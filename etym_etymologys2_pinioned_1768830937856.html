PIE *pet- 'to fly, to rush, to dash' (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE; attested by Sanskrit pátati 'he flies', Greek pétesthai 'to fly', Latin petere 'to rush at, to seek') → Italic *petnā- 'the winged one; wing, feather' → Latin penna 'feather, wing' (attested 3rd c. BCE, Plautus), with the variant form pinna 'feather, wing, fin' (sound shift: *petnā- → penna by cluster assimilation of -tn- to -nn-) → Latin pinnula 'little feather, little wing' (diminutive of pinna, Classical Latin) → Vulgar Latin *pinnio 'wing, pinion' → Old French pignon 'wing, pinion, flight feather' (12th c.) → Middle English pinion 'wing, wing-joint, flight feather' (14th–15th c.) → English to pinion 'to clip the wings of (a bird); to bind or hold the arms of (a person) tightly' (16th–17th c.) → English pinioned (past participle of to pinion; the target root word) NOTE ON NUANCE: The English noun pinion also acquired a mechanical sense, 'a small toothed wheel' (16th c.), taken from French pignon 'cogwheel'. That French word is generally derived from Latin pinea 'pine-cone' (the toothed wheel resembling a pine-cone's overlapping scales), which is a DIFFERENT word from French pignon 'wing'. The two were conflated into a single English pinion, and the origin of the cogwheel sense is genuinely debated: some scholars connect it to the wing/feather word by the fan of spokes of a wheel, but the pine-cone derivation is the more widely accepted. The derivation chain above follows the ornithological line, which is the certain path to the past participle pinioned.
