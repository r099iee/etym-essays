In the yamen courtroom scene, an usher is not merely a ceremonial doorkeeper but a low-ranking official who manages access, maintains order, and — crucially — channels informal knowledge to the magistrate. When Yu-cun is about to issue a warrant against the murderer's dependants, one of the ushers signals him with his eyes not to proceed. That glance converts the office of "attendant" into that of a political gatekeeper: the man controls who enters the legal process and, through insider knowledge of the four great families and the local corruption, who is protected from it. The tone is bureaucratic and worldly; the usher's authority rests less on rank than on knowing how power actually works behind the robes of office. The full sense inventory is as follows:
  • Primary sense — "a person who shows people where to sit": in theatres, cinemas, churches, concert halls, and weddings, the attendant who conducts guests to their seats and hands out programmes or orders of service.
  • Courtroom sense — "an official acting as doorkeeper or attendant": the officer of a law court who keeps the doors, admits parties, calls for order when the judge enters, and manages the movement of people in and out; the sense operative in the Red Chamber passage, where the usher's warning glance is part of the court's informal intelligence system.
  • Household and ceremonial sense — "a doorkeeper or porter in a great household": historically the officer of a royal or noble household who guarded the chamber door and controlled admission, reflected in titles such as gentleman usher and usher of the hall.
  • Educational sense — "an assistant schoolmaster, an under-master": in the English grammar schools of the 16th–19th centuries, the usher was the junior teacher who drilled the younger boys, standing below the master or headmaster.
  • Verb — "to show or lead (someone) in": "he was ushered into the room"; to conduct or escort a person to a seat or into a presence.
  • Verb, figurative — "to usher in": to inaugurate or introduce, especially of a new era, state of affairs, or fashion: "the discovery ushered in a new age." The figurative sense derives from the physical act of admitting a person through a door.
  • Rare figurative noun: one who precedes or introduces — a forerunner; a literary use, e.g. "the usher of a new age."
The semantic core is access and order. Whether in a courtroom, a theatre, or a palace, the usher stands at the boundary of a space and manages who may cross it and how they behave once inside. That is why the word can move so easily between justice, entertainment, and ceremony: the office is always about threshold control. The courtroom usher in the passage exercises the office's oldest privilege — the right to see who comes and to whisper to power what should happen next. Compared with near-synonyms, the boundaries are sharp:
  • Doorkeeper / porter: guard a physical threshold and need not direct people to seats or understand judicial strategy; an usher does more than admit — he places and orders.
  • Attendant: a general servant or companion; an usher is an attendant with a specific spatial and procedural function.
  • Bailiff / court officer: enforces order, serves writs, and may arrest; an usher manages access and announcement, with less coercive power.
  • Marshal: a higher-ranking ceremonial officer, often with military or police authority; an usher is humble and administrative.
  • Concierge: a modern hotel doorman/porter who assists guests with services; an usher operates inside an institution and directs seating and order.
  • Page / footman: domestic servants who run errands and attend persons; an usher holds a defined gatekeeping office.
Collocations and phrases:
  • Courtroom: "the usher called for silence," "usher of the court," "the judge's usher."
  • Theatre and ceremony: "the usher showed them to their seats," "wedding ushers," "church usher."
  • Figurative verb: "usher in a new era," "usher in reforms," "ushered into the office."
  • Historical titles: "gentleman usher," "usher of the black rod," "usher of the hall."
In the passage, the word's courtroom sense does full work: the man is at once a functionary of order and a broker of the informal knowledge that decides whether justice runs its course. The usher is the quiet official who knows the real map of power, and his signal — a glance — is the physical embodiment of the office's true function: controlling what reaches the magistrate.
