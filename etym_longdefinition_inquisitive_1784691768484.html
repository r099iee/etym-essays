Inquisitive is an adjective meaning given to inquiring, asking questions, or searching into matters: curious, enquiring and, in its pejorative sense, prying or meddlesome. The word is remarkable for covering both a virtue and a vice. Used of a scholar, a child, or a scientist, it denotes an eager and laudable desire for knowledge — "an inquisitive mind"; used of a neighbour or a reporter, it suggests intrusive curiosity about what is not one's business — "inquisitive eyes", "too inquisitive for his own good".

Polysemy: the full semantic range.

  • Laudatory sense (intellectual curiosity): eager to learn, enquiring, intellectually curious — "an inquisitive child", "the inquisitive spirit of science"; the virtue of the seeker after knowledge.
  • Pejorative sense (prying): intrusively curious about the affairs of others, meddlesome, nosy — "inquisitive neighbours", "inquisitive eyes"; the vice of the busybody.
  • Neutral sense (active questioning): simply given to asking questions and searching into matters, with the moral colour supplied by the context — as in the passage where the dwarves warn that the less inquisitive one is, the less trouble one finds.
  • Derived forms: the adverb inquisitively (in an enquiring or prying manner) and the noun inquisitiveness (the quality of being inquisitive, either eager or meddlesome).
  • Related historical and institutional senses: the noun inquisition (a search or inquiry; specifically the institution of the Inquisition), inquest (a judicial inquiry), inquiry and enquiry (formal questions) — all members of the same Latin family built on inquirere "to seek into".

Spelling and usage notes. The spelling follows the Latin stem inquisit-: the British pair enquire/enquiry preserves the Old French route, while inquire/inquiry shows the Latin one, and both spellings are now current with no real difference of meaning. Inquisitive itself is spelled with the Latin qu- (not the French que-), reflecting its direct descent from Late Latin inquisitivus via Old French inquisitif.

The sense in the context. In the passage from "Roast Mutton", one of the dwarves warns the company: "the less inquisitive you are as you go along, the less trouble you are likely to find." The word here carries its practical, cautionary nuance: in lands that are "none too well known" and "too near the mountains", where travellers seldom come, the roads are unguarded, and the local king is unheard of, prying curiosity is not a virtue but a liability. Asking questions advertises that you are a stranger, invites suspicion, and stirs up trouble of exactly the kind a small, poorly armed party cannot afford. The warning belongs to the novel's folk-wisdom tone — the voice of prudent travellers counselling the same discretion that later keeps the company alive — and it gives the adjective an almost economic force: curiosity is a commodity that costs trouble.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. Inquisitive can be usefully distinguished from its near-synonyms. Curious is the neutral, often admirable desire to know, without any implication of effort; enquiring (the more formal British term) suggests systematic, polite investigation; prying is wholly pejorative, the intrusive searching into private affairs; meddlesome adds active interference to mere curiosity; nosy is the informal, slangy word for intrusive curiosity; probing suggests a deliberate, pointed testing; searching describes a thorough examination. Inquisitive holds the middle: the strongest positive word for intellectual thirst, yet one that tips easily into censure — a searching that is a virtue when the thing sought is knowledge and a liability when it is other people's business.
