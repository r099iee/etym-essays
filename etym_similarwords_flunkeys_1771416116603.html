• Lackey: Similar because both are pejorative words for a servile attendant; differs because lackey emphasizes running at the master's side and personal attendance, while flunkey emphasizes the livery and the ceremonial station at the gate.
  • Footman: Similar because both denote a liveried male servant; differs because footman is neutral and purely descriptive, while flunkey adds unmistakable condescension and a hint of ceremonial display.
  • Valet: Similar because both are male servants; differs because a valet is an intimate body-servant who dresses and tends one gentleman, while flunkeys are public, undifferentiated, and decorative.
  • Butler: Similar because both are male household servants; differs because a butler heads the male staff and manages the cellar and dining room with dignity, while a flunkey is low-ranking and interchangeable.
  • Minion: Similar because both are servile subordinates of a powerful figure; differs because a minion is a favoured, valued underling, while a flunkey is anonymous, replaceable, and uniformly dressed.
  • Menial: Similar because both denote low-rank attendants; differs because menial stresses manual, degrading toil, while flunkey stresses costume, posture, and ceremonial display.
  • Retainer: Similar because both denote household servants in service to a great family; differs because retainer is neutral and slightly archaic, implying long, loyal service, without flunkey's mocking edge.
  • Hireling: Similar because both imply service for pay rather than loyalty; differs because a hireling is anyone hired, often for a sordid task, with no suggestion of livery or ceremonial household display.
  • Toady / sycophant: Similar because both denote an obsequious flatterer of the powerful; differs because toady and sycophant name the act of flattering, while flunkey names a liveried servant and extends that image to a servile follower.
