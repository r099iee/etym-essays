Issued is the simple past and past participle of the verb issue, a word with two great branches: an intransitive branch meaning "to go or come out, to emerge, to flow forth," and a transitive branch meaning "to send out, publish, or distribute." In "He issued from the Gate, the waters rose in fierce whistling steam," Tolkien uses the older, literary sense: Smaug came out of the Gate — emerged from it in a single dramatic movement. The verb is the perfect epic word for emergence: formal, decisive, and slightly archaic in this literal use, exactly as a dragon deserving ceremony would have it.

Primary senses — the intransitive branch (emergence).

  • To come or go out of a place: "he issued from the castle," "troops issued from the gates" — formal and literary today, the sense Tolkien uses; it is the direct descendant of the Latin exīre "to go out."
  • To flow, pour, or be emitted from a source: "water issued from the rock," "smoke issued from the chimney," "blood issued from the wound" — of liquids, gases, light, and sound; the sense of a current emerging from a source.
  • To result or arise from: "difficulties that issue from a bad decision," "wisdom issuing from experience" — the sense of outcome, from the noun's oldest meaning "result."
  • To descend or be born from (archaic): "issued of a noble line" — the offspring sense, surviving from the legal phrase "without issue" (without children).

Primary senses — the transitive branch (sending forth).

  • To send out officially: issue an order, a command, a decree, a warning, a summons, a warrant — the authoritative utterance of an institution or ruler.
  • To publish or put into circulation: issue a book, a magazine, a newspaper, a stamp, a coin, shares, bonds, banknotes — the sense of the noun "an issue" (a number of a publication; an emission of currency).
  • To distribute or supply: issue rations, ammunition, uniforms, equipment, passports, identity cards, refunds — to give out to those entitled, often in a military or bureaucratic context.
  • To emit: issue a sound, a smell, a light (rare but attested); the emission sense bridges the two branches — what goes out of a source is issued from it.

Secondary senses of the related noun (why the verb means all this).

  • The noun issue (13th c.) meant "way out, exit; outcome, result; offspring" — from Old French issue "exit," the feminine past participle of issir "to go out." Every modern noun sense hangs on this: "the issue at hand" (the point that has come out of the discussion), "a new issue of the magazine" (a number sent out), "the issue of a loan" (money put out into circulation), "without issue" (no offspring came out of the marriage), "at issue" (the question standing out between parties).
  • The verb is a 16th-century derivation from the noun (or directly from the French participle): to issue is to perform what the noun names — to make an exit, to produce an outcome, to send forth a publication or supply.

Register and usage notes.

  • The literal emergence sense ("issued from") is now formal-literary; in ordinary speech we say came out, emerged, or stepped out. Tolkien's "He issued from the Gate" deliberately uses the high register, and the sentence's ceremonial tone matches the dragon's last exit.
  • The official sense is the dominant modern use: statements, passports, warrants, shares, and rations are issued every day by institutions; the word carries an administrative, quasi-legal weight.
  • Derived forms: issuer (one who issues), issuance (the act), reissue (to issue again); the noun issue in modern colloquial English has also come to mean "problem" ("what's the issue?"), a late 19th–20th century development from "point in question."

Why the word means all this. The entire family hangs on one Latin image: exīre, "to go out" (ex "out" + īre "to go"). Old French made a noun of its past participle — "the going out," an exit — and English took the noun, then the verb. Everything that issues is something that goes out: a man out of a gate, water out of a rock, an order out of a king's mouth, a banknote out of the mint, a problem out of a situation. The semantic bridge between the dragon and the passport office is simply the oldest sense of all: coming forth.
