- 14th c. — Late Middle English adoption: Borrowed via Old French genitif from Latin grammatica texts, strictly functioning as a specialized grammatical term for the case of nouns marking possession, origin, or lineage.
- 16th–17th c. — Renaissance and Early Modern grammatical debates: English vernacular grammarians wrestled with how to classify the native -'s ending, often misanalyzing it as a contraction of the pronoun his (the famous 'his-genitive' fallacy, as in 'the King his palace') before clarifying its morphological status.
- 18th–19th c. — Classical philological standardization: Firmly codified in schoolroom grammars of Latin, Greek, and English as the cardinal case of possession, partition, and adnominal complementation, serving as an essential tool of rigorous pedagogical parsing.
- 20th–21st c. — Structuralist and generative linguistics: Expanded beyond pedagogical declension tables into formal syntactic theory; analyzed as an abstract structural Case, a Determiner Phrase (DP) specifier, or an enclitic phrase clitic, while retaining its familiar textbook meaning for students of foreign languages.
