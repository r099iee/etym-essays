STINGY is a purely English word — not borrowed, not Latinate, but formed on native soil in the seventeenth century from the humble verb to sting. Its history shows how a physical sensation (the smart of a bee, a barb, a pointed object) can be transferred first to temperament and then to the economics of generosity, and how a word born with the meaning "sharp, biting" came to mean "mean with money." 1. The Indo-European and Germanic base. The verb sting descends from the PIE root *steg-/*steng- "to be sharp, to prick, to pierce," a root whose pointedness is attested across the family: Latin stimulus "goad, sting" and instigare "to goad, incite" come from a variant grade, as does Greek stízō "to prick, mark with a point." In Germanic the root produced the strong verb Proto-Germanic *stinganą, which entered Old English as stingan — a word for piercing with a sharp point, used of bees, thorns, spears, and pain. The Middle English stingen continued this physical core: to sting is to wound with a point and to cause the sharp, smarting sensation that follows. Nothing about money appears yet; the word's earliest life is entirely bodily and sensory. 2. Figurative extension of the verb. Long before the adjective existed, the verb and noun sting had begun to go figurative. The Bible and moral prose spoke of "the sting of death," "the sting of conscience," and "stinging words" — uses in which the physical prick becomes a metaphor for sudden, sharp mental or emotional pain. The idea crystallized that a sting is not just a wound but a smarting reproach: something that pricks and punishes. This figurative stock of "the sting as a moral prick" is the essential bridge to the adjective's meaning. 3. Formation of the adjective. In the seventeenth century English began forming the adjective stingy with the productive suffix -y ("full of, characterized by"), on the model of countless such formations (moody, sandy, stony). The earliest recorded senses are revealing: stingy first meant "sharp, biting, pungent" — said of things that sting the tongue or senses — and, applied to persons, "ill-tempered, mean-spirited." A stingy person was literally someone who "stings" others: sharp-tongued, prickly, ill-natured. The word's career began, that is, as a portrait of a disagreeable temperament, not of a tight purse. 4. The shift to economics: 1670s–1700s. The decisive semantic change happened within a generation. Because a "stingy" (ill-tempered, pricking) person was also likely to be one who refused, denied, and withheld, the word began to attach to the act of giving: to be stingy with money or food was to refuse in a way that "stings" the asker. By the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries the financial and material sense — "niggardly, tight-fisted, reluctant to spend" — had crystallized and begun to dominate. The older "sharp-tongued, ill-tempered" sense faded, surviving mainly in dialect and in the transferred meaning "scant, meager" applied to portions. The semantic engine was metaphor: refusal felt like a wound; the tight hand pricked expectation. 5. Modern standard sense. In modern English stingy is almost exclusively about ungenerosity:
  • Money: stingy with money, a stingy tip, a stingy salary, a stingy pension — the dominant financial sense.
  • Quantities: a stingy portion, a stingy serving, a stingy amount — meager, grudgingly doled out.
  • Intangibles: stingy with praise, affection, time, information — the extended moral sense prominent in the card's sentence about the elders and their approval.
  • Effort and warmth: a stingy performance, a stingy gesture — giving less than is owed.
The word has become a permanent fixture of the English moral vocabulary for small-mindedness in giving, sitting alongside niggardly, parsimonious, and miserly, but distinguished from them by its homely, physical, genuinely English pedigree — the sting of the bee carried, through metaphor, into the sting of refusal. 6. Cultural and sociolinguistic color. Because stingy is native, short, and colloquial, it is the everyday word of family life and popular culture ("Don't be stingy! Share!"), while its more learned rivals occupy formal prose. It retains a faint tactile vividness absent from mean or frugal; speakers feel the "sting" in the word even when unaware of its etymology. It is a model case of semantic pejoration through metaphor — a physical prick becoming a moral defect — and a reminder that many moral words in English are transferred sensory vocabulary.
