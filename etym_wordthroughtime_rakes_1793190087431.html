- Old English — the farmyard implement: raca names the wooden hay rake of Anglo-Saxon meadows, a peasant tool of the summer harvest.
- Medieval — manorial haymaking: whole villages turn out with rakes at haytime under the reeve’s eye; the word is pure rural labour, sung in harvest songs.
- 16th–17th c. — the libertine homonym arrives: rakehell clips to rake, and the Restoration stage fills with rakes who never touched a hayfield.
- 18th–19th c. — garden and empire: the iron garden rake standardises for seedbeds and gravel walks, from cottage plots to botanical gardens.
- 20th c. — leisure and machinery: spring-tine lawn rakes serve suburbia while tedders and balers push hand rakes from commercial hayfields.
- Today — the allotment and the autumn clear-up: rakes survive in gardens, zen gravel, and beach clean-ups, with the casino and the costume-drama senses keeping the word’s wilder company.
