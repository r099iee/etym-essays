- [Old English–Middle English: the written thing] - beside the strong verb wrītan, Old English has the noun (ge)writ, “a writing, document, scripture” — the word denotes written matter itself, before any legal narrowing.
- [Middle English: the participle splits] - the clipped participle writ competes with writen / ywritten, following the pattern of biten > bit, riden > rid; meanwhile the noun writ narrows toward the courts, “a formal legal order” issued by the king.
- [Early modern: the fossil phrase] - the longer form written wins as the standard participle; writ survives as a deliberate archaism in the King James Bible and in legal and poetic diction, kept alive by ceremony rather than grammar.
- [17th century onward: writ large] - the participle lives on almost exclusively inside fixed, resonant idioms — writ large (“written out in big letters,” hence plainly manifest, magnified), writ in stone, writ on the wall — each carrying permanence and gravity.
- [Contemporary: the legal noun and the fixed idiom] - as a participle writ is a fossil, archaic outside a handful of phrases; as a noun it is a purely legal term (a writ of habeas corpus), while writ large now means simply “manifested on a grand scale” — the elevated, biblical tone that gives Tolkien's “meter writ large” its solemn flourish.
