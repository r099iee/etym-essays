Derivation chain for grievously: 1. Proto-Indo-European *gʷreh₂- 'heavy' (also reconstructed in the full grade as *gʷerh₂-; the root underlies a large family of 'heavy/weighty/grave' words across the daughter languages — Greek barus 'heavy', Sanskrit gurú 'heavy, venerable', Latin gravis 'heavy' — and is one of the best-attested reconstructions in the family). 2. Latin gravis (adjective) 'heavy, weighty, severe, oppressive' (attested from the earliest Latin records, 3rd c. BCE; the initial labiovelar *gʷ was delabialized to g before the vowel, giving gr-, and *h₂ lengthened the vowel). The same Latin stem yields gravitas 'heaviness, dignity, gravity' and the verb gravare 'to load, burden, oppress' (source of English aggravate). 3. Old French grever (verb) 'to burden, oppress, injure, afflict' (from Latin gravare) and the Old French adjective grief / grevos 'heavy, burdensome, painful, severe' (the adjective built with the suffix -os, from Latin -osus, 'full of, characterized by'; spellings vary in the manuscripts as grevos, greveus, grious). 4. Middle English grevous (adjective) 'grievous, heavy, burdensome, painful, harmful, severe' (attested from the late 13th/14th c.; borrowed from Anglo-French; the -griev- spelling reflects Old French palatalization of g before a front vowel, pronounced /dʒ/). 5. Middle English grevousli / grevously (adverb) 'grievously, heavily, severely, painfully' (14th c.; formed from the adjective + the adverbial suffix -ly, from Old English -līce 'in the manner of, like'). 6. Modern English GRIEVOUSLY 'in a manner causing severe harm, suffering, or sorrow' (modern spelling with -iev- settled from the 16th c.; standard pronunciation /ˈɡriː.vəs.li/). Note on the derivation: the -ous of grievous is a Romance suffix (Old French -os < Latin -osus), not the native English -ous — English borrowed the whole adjective from Old French rather than building it. The -ly suffix, by contrast, is native English. The chain is thus a hybrid: an inherited Indo-European root, a Latin word, a French borrowing, and English morphology — a typical biography of a modern formal English adverb.
