- [Late 18th c.] - coined in German academic lectures by Karl Morgenstern to define novels focusing on the organic inner education and formation (Bildung) of the protagonist - [perception: admired in German intellectual circles as the quintessential literary vehicle of Enlightenment humanism and personal cultivation]
- [1906–1910s] - popularized internationally by German philosopher Wilhelm Dilthey in Das Erlebnis und die Dichtung, canonizing Goethe's Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship as the archetypal masterwork of the genre - [perception: embraced as a crucial structural category in comparative literature and aesthetic theory]
- [Mid-20th c.] - naturalized into English literary criticism, expanding beyond classical German realism to encompass Victorian coming-of-age novels (Dickens, Brontë, Eliot) and modernist psychological trajectories (Joyce, Lawrence) - [perception: widely recognized as an indispensable genre term denoting any narrative of adolescent maturation and societal socialization]
- [Late 20th–21st c.] - expanded globally and across media to include female, postcolonial, queer, and cinematic narratives of identity formation, while occasionally applied metaphorically in journalism to describe political or biographical transformations - [perception: a prestigious, ubiquitous cultural concept representing the universal struggle to forge selfhood against societal pressures]
