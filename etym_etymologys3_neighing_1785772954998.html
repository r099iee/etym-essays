1. Sound first, morphology second. Neighing has an unusually transparent origin story for an English word: it is acoustic imitation lexicalised into grammar. Proto-Germanic speakers appear to have built an imitative stem *hnaijan- that mimicked the breathy, high, vibrating onset of a horse's call. Old English hnægan preserves that initial hn- complex — an aspirated nasal onset well suited to the acoustic attack of the real-world cry. As English phonology simplified, the h- colour of hn- eroded; Middle English writings show neyen, neighen, and related spellings, and the modern diphthong /eɪ/ settles into the familiar neigh. The participle neighing is thus the continuous face of a word that has always pointed at the same animal noise.

2. A stable core meaning. Unlike abstract moral adjectives, neighing did not undergo dramatic semantic revolutions. Its core has remained "(of a horse) to utter its characteristic cry" from Anglo-Saxon agricultural and martial contexts through chivalric romance, early modern husbandry manuals, Romantic landscape writing, Victorian cavalry scenes, and modern veterinary or children's prose. What did change is metaphorical reach and literary texture: authors learned to use choruses of neighing as soundscape, to contrast soft nickers with loud neighs, and occasionally to animalize human laughter as neighing — a transfer that adds comic or contemptuous colour.

3. A patchwork of Germanic imitations. Sister Germanic languages often chose different imitations — German wiehern, Dutch hinniken, Old Norse gneggja — underscoring that this is a domain of parallel onomatopoeia rather than a single inherited PIE verb with neat cognates everywhere. Each language recreated the horse cry in its own phonotactic materials; the English solution happens to be the one built on an aspirated nasal onset. This explains why the etymological "family" of neigh is small and why no deeper PIE reconstruction is attempted.

4. Literary attestations and cultural roles.

  • Chivalric romance and medieval verse use neighing for warhorses and battle scenes, where the sound carries martial excitement.
  • Husbandry and veterinary manuals of the early modern and Victorian periods treat neighing as a plain, factual verb of animal behaviour.
  • Romantic and Victorian landscape writing turns neighing into an acoustic detail of rural idylls — dawn stables, wild herds, storm-panicked horses.
  • Twentieth-century fiction and children's literature keep the word fully current, and the metaphorical "neighing with laughter" survives as a mildly comic or unflattering transfer to human sound.

5. The broader European picture. Latin hinnīre and French hennir show the same communicative need solved on Italic and Romance ground; English did not borrow them for ordinary use, keeping the native Germanic form. In scientific register one may speak of "equine vocalization," but neighing remains the living English word. Its evolution is therefore less a story of meaning change than of sound change and register persistence: a native, imitative, unpretentious word that English has kept for over a thousand years as the default name for the horse's cry.
