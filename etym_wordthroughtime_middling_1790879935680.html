- 14th–15th Century - Coined in Middle English as a neutral descriptor for intermediate spatial location, intermediate size, or moderate degree.
- 16th–17th Century - Emerged as a pivotal sociological descriptor in England for the "middling sort" (the emerging burghers, yeomen, and merchants between nobility and peasantry).
- 18th–19th Century - Widely adopted in commerce to classify commodities of intermediate grade; acquired an evaluative tone of mediocrity or lack of distinction in art and intellect.
- 19th–20th Century - Proliferated in vernacular idioms (especially American and British colloquialisms like "fair to middling" regarding health or fortunes).
- Contemporary Era - Retains dual role as a neutral socioeconomic/technical term and a mildly pejorative critique of uninspired, average performance.
