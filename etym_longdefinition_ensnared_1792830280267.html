Ensnared, the past participle of ensnare, means caught in a snare — a loop, net, or noose that tightens round its victim — whether the trap is literal wire in a wood or a figurative web of flattery, debt, or power. The word always implies craft on one side and unwary movement on the other: the victim walked, flew, or agreed its way in, and now every struggle tightens the hold.

In the source sentence the Nine Mortal Men are victims of exactly this craft: Nine he gave to Mortal Men, proud and great, and so ensnared them. Sauron's Rings are baited gifts — honours that are really halters. The proud and great, confident in their strength, take what is offered and discover too late that acceptance was capture: the Rings preserve and prolong them only into wraithdom. Tolkien's and so ensnared them lands with terrible quietness after the gift-giving, because the trap was the gift all along.

Core meaning in detail: Literally, ensnared describes animals taken by hidden loops — rabbits ensnared in wire, birds ensnared in nets, fish ensnared in weirs. The mechanism matters: a snare is not a cage but a one-way device, easy to enter and tightening on exit, usually set by a hidden setter and found by a victim who never saw it. Figuratively, the same mechanics govern human traps: people are ensnared by promises, by fine print, by charm, by addiction, by surveillance, by obligations that looked like favours. The common thread is asymmetry of knowledge — the setter knows it is a trap; the victim learns afterwards — and the participle ensnared freezes the story at the moment of realisation, when freedom is already gone.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Caught in a literal trap or net (earliest physical sense): a fox ensnared in a poacher's wire; flies ensnared in the spider's web. The bridge is the noun snare plus en- meaning put into: to put into a noose. Nature writing, hunting law, and pest control keep this sense exact and everyday.
  • Lured and trapped by deceit or temptation (central figurative sense, from the 1600s): ensnared by promises of quick riches; ensnared into signing. The bridge carries the hidden-loop mechanics into human dealing: the con, the cult, and the predatory contract all work like wire in a run — invisible until sprung. Moralists and journalists favour this sense.
  • Entangled in love, beauty, or charm (romantic register): ensnared by her smile; his heart ensnared. The bridge softens the trap into allure: the lover is a willing victim, and the snare is woven of glances. Poetry uses it half-playfully, admitting the captive enjoys his cage.
  • Caught by authority, law, or obligation (institutional sense): ensnared in red tape; ensnared by debt; ensnared in litigation. The bridge generalises the setter from hunter to system: bureaucracies and creditors lay procedural snares — clauses, deadlines, penalties — that tighten exactly like wire when the subject struggles.
  • Enslaved by power or corruption, especially through gifts (epic and political sense, Tolkien's use): kings ensnared by flattery; nations ensnared by aid that became dependence. The bridge makes the gift the jaw of the trap: what looked like generosity was purchase. This is the Ring-sense — corruption delivered as honour — and political writing reaches for ensnared whenever patronage turns to bondage.
  • Entangled in error, sin, or addiction (theological and psychological sense): ensnared by habit; a conscience ensnared in guilt. The bridge internalises the setter: appetite and repetition lay the loops, and the victim and the hunted are one person. Sermons and self-help alike picture relapse as re-entering the same snare.

Register and connotation: Ensnared is literary and judgmental — it always faintly blames the setter and pities the victim, implying the game was rigged. It sounds graver than trapped (which can be accidental) and craftier than caught (which can be fair): there is always a suggestion of bait, patience, and hidden design. Its favourite victims are the proud, the curious, and the trusting — those whose very strengths walked them in — which is why epic, scripture, and scandal sheets all prize it: the greater the victim, the more terrible the little loop.
