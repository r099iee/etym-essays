Sinew denotes a tough fibrous cord of connective tissue that joins muscle to bone — a tendon — and, by extension, bodily strength, toughness, or any sustaining force. In its most literal anatomical sense it is the pale, glistening band you feel behind the ankle (Achilles tendon) or behind the knee; in its figurative sense it is power itself, as in "the sinews of the economy."

The core meaning rests on material qualities: sinew is strong in tension, slightly elastic, whitish, and stringy. Unlike flesh, which tears, or bone, which breaks, sinew stretches and holds. Pre-modern peoples therefore treated it as natural cordage, and the word still carries that craft memory. In the source sentence, cords of animal sinew bind wooden handles to stone or bone tools — a precise technological detail, since wet sinew shrinks as it dries, clamping joints tighter than any plant-fibre lashing, and sinew-glue seals gaps.

Principal senses in contemporary and historical use:

  • Anatomical tendon: a band of dense regular collagenous tissue transmitting muscular pull to bone. Examples include Achilles sinew, hamstring sinews, sinews of the hand. Technically overlapping with tendon and formerly with ligament and nerve.
  • Animal material / cordage: prepared sinew strips chewed, pounded, split, and twisted into thread, bowstrings, snowshoe webbing, and glue. Deer, elk, buffalo, and whale sinews were staple materials for Indigenous North American, Siberian, and medieval European bowyers.
  • Bodily strength, muscular power: often in the plural — "a man of great sinew," "sinewy arms" — emphasizing lean, corded, enduring strength rather than bulk.
  • Figurative source of strength: "money is the sinew of war" (Bacon after Latin nervi belli pecunia), "tourism is the sinew of the island economy," "volunteers are the sinews of the club." Here sinew means indispensable supporting element.
  • Nerve in older texts: King James and Shakespearean use where sinew translates nerves or vital channels; now obsolete anatomically but vital for reading older literature.

Domain-specific extensions include:

  • Archery and music: sinew-backed bows where sinew laminated to wood increases cast; early catgut strings sometimes called sinews.
  • Cookery and butchery: sinewy meat is tough with gristle, requiring slow cooking; to sinew meat is to trim it.
  • Botany by analogy: leaf-sinews for midribs and veins in older herbals, now called nerves or ribs.
  • Engineering metaphor: cables, stays, and bracing described as sinews of a bridge or aircraft wing.

Polysemy bridges are transparent: from visible tendon → to harvested cord (same stuff, removed) → to strength (what tendons confer) → to any strength-conferring resource (money, people, infrastructure). Adjectival sinewy preserves both branches: sinewy meat (full of literal gristle, negative) versus sinewy prose or sinewy limbs (lean, taut, powerful, positive). To understand sinew is to understand how English once thought of the body as a strung bow — muscles as wood, sinews as string, will as archer — and how that image still braces our talk of strong economies, strong teams, and strong bodies.
