- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Appears as *galga* or *gealga* (singular), denoting a wooden cross, execution pole, or the Christian cross upon which Christ was crucified (as in the heroic Old English poem *The Dream of the Rood*); carries profound solemnity and sacred horror.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - Develops into the plural form *galwes* / *galowes*, reflecting the physical construction of two upright posts joined by a crossbeam; sheds its sacred Christian associations and becomes exclusively anchored in the violent penal system as the common public apparatus for hanging thieves, murderers, and traitors.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c., Elizabethan to Jacobean Era) - Deeply woven into street ballads, Shakespearean drama, and rogue literature; associated with famous permanent execution sites like Tyburn Tree in London; gives rise to idiomatic expressions like "gallows-bird" and jokes about "dancing on nothing."
- 18th to 19th Century - The singular/plural ambiguity crystallizes (treated as a singular noun with a plural form: "a gallows"); evolves from slow strangulation to the mechanical drop-trapdoor system; enters Romantic and Decadent literature (Baudelaire, Hugo, Melville) as an existential symbol of societal cruelty and moral self-reckoning.
- 20th Century to Present - As public hanging and capital punishment are abolished across Britain and Europe, the literal apparatus recedes into penal history, but *gallows* endures vividly in psychological terminology ("gallows humor"), political rhetoric, and literary allegory.
