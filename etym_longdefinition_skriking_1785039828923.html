**SKRIKING — the archaic northern word for shrieking** At its plainest core, **skriking** (the present participle of the dialectal verb *skrike*) means uttering sharp, piercing, grating screeches — a high, thin, wailing cry forced from the throat. The word is chiefly Northern English and Scots: a regional, dialectal, and now largely archaic form corresponding to standard "shrieking." Its hard initial cluster /skr-/ and its long vowel give it a harsher, more grating texture than the smoother standard word, and it is precisely this archaic, northern, Norse-flavoured quality that a philologist-writer like Tolkien chose to mine for epic effect. **Primary sense: the piercing cry** To skrike is to cry out in a sharp, piercing, shrill voice — to screech, shriek, or utter a high-pitched, grating cry, whether of terror, pain, or violent emotion. In northern dialect usage the verb also meant "to weep loudly" and "to complain," so that skriking covers both the pure shriek of alarm and the noisy, wailing complaint of a child or a mourner. The word is onomatopoeic at its root: the cluster /skr-/ with a high vowel reproduces the acoustic harshness of a grating, high-pitched outcry, the same imitative family that produced shriek, screech, screak, and the name of the shrike bird. **The literary sense in *The Hobbit*** Tolkien places skriking at the very top of the goblins' auditory chaos in the darkness: "yells and yammering, croaking, jibbering and jabbering; howls, growls and curses; shrieking and skriking." The word is deliberately archaic and dialectal — a Northern English and Scots form, all but obsolete by the twentieth century — and it lets Tolkien add a second high-pitched register alongside "shrieking," doubling the ear-splitting tenor of the mob's panic while lending the passage the texture of ancient northern speech. Skriking sounds older, harder, and more guttural than shrieking, as if the word itself were being dragged out of the deep layers of English dialect history. For a philologist who built his mythology on Old English and Old Norse, the choice is a signature: the goblins do not merely shriek, they skrike, in a word that carries the remembered sound of the Scandinavian north. **Polysemy and secondary modern meanings**
  • The piercing cry (primary): to skrike is to shriek or screech — the harsh, high-pitched outcry of terror, pain, or frenzy. "The child was skriking with fear."
  • Northern dialect "to weep loudly": in Yorkshire, Lancashire, and Scots usage, to skrike could mean to cry or wail noisily, especially of children — the wailing sense is close to the shriek of a distressed infant.
  • Northern dialect "to complain": extended from wailing, skrike could mean to complain querulously or persistently — the word's grumbling extension, the northern cousin of "to grizzle" or "to whinge."
  • Of birds and animals: the skrike of birds — the harsh cry of the shrike, the butcher-bird whose very name (1540s) comes from the same root meaning "shrill-caller" — keeps the word anchored in the natural world of harsh avian cries.
  • Of mechanical or grating noise: in dialect and literary use, skriking can describe any harsh, grating, high-pitched sound — the skriking of iron wheels, of hinges, of a saw — the word's acoustic harshness extending beyond the voice to any piercing scrape.
  • The standard parallel "shrieking": the same Middle English verb split in the 16th century, the southern form being reshaped to shriek while the northern form kept its hard k as skrike; the two remain near-synonyms distinguished by register and geography.
**Historical bridge and semantic reasoning** Skriking's senses all radiate from one acoustic image: a sharp, harsh, grating cry. The word descends from Proto-Germanic *skrīkōnan, "to screech, cry out shrilly," and entered Middle English around 1200 under strong Scandinavian influence in the Danelaw, from Old Norse skrækja and skríkja, "to screech, twitter, cry out." The hard /k/ of skrike preserves the Old Norse consonant exactly, where the southern, mainstream form shriek softened it in the 16th century — the standard word took the French-flavoured smoothing, while the northern dialect word kept the Scandinavian grit. The word's dialect career kept it close to raw, unrefined, emotional noise — the shriek, the loud wail, the querulous complaint — and its archaic flavour is exactly what makes it valuable to a philologist: skriking sounds like the old northern heroic world, the register of Norse saga and Old English verse, not of polite London speech. **Register and usage** Skriking is archaic, dialectal, and deliberately literary. It is at home in Northern English dialect writing, in Scots, and in the archaising prose of writers like Tolkien who mined the older strata of the language for texture. It must be distinguished from *shrieking* (the standard, softer, French-flavoured form), from *screeching* (which stresses disagreeable, undignified shrillness), from *screaming* (loud, sustained, and often terror-filled at a lower pitch), from *howling* (deep and long), from *squealing* (thin and reedy), and from *squeaking* (the short, thin, mouse-like noise explicitly excluded by the shortDefinition — skriking is a full, harsh, piercing shriek, not a tiny squeak).
