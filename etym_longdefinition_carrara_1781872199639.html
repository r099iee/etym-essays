In Pater's catalogue of aesthetic objects, "the hills of Carrara" stand for landscape as a source of specialized pleasure—not generic "pretty scenery," but a place whose very stone has shaped Western sculpture. Carrara, in the Apuan Alps of Tuscany, is the classic Italian marble district: white and blue-gray stone quarried since Roman times and sought by Michelangelo and countless later sculptors. Pater lists those hills with La Gioconda, Pico of Mirandola, and other "virtues," treating the landscape itself as if it were a herb, wine, or gem—an entity that affects the observer with a unique impression. The tone is quietly elevated and sensory: Carrara is named not for industrial output but for the refined, almost tactile idea of pure marble light in the mind's eye of the aesthete. The place and its stone. Carrara is a town and comune in the province of Massa-Carrara, in northwestern Tuscany, at the foot of the Apuan Alps. The district is one of the world's most important sources of high-quality white and blue-gray marble, quarried continuously since antiquity. The Romans worked the stone, known as marmor Lunense, "marble of Luna," from the nearby port of Luni, and used it in imperial buildings and public monuments. In the Renaissance the quarries supplied Michelangelo, who personally selected blocks at Carrara for works such as the Pietà, the David, and the Medici tombs, and the name became synonymous with the finest sculptural marble in Europe. Today Carrara is both a working quarry district and a global luxury brand, with grades such as statuario, Calacatta, and Carrara white used in architecture, sculpture, and interiors. The name and its neighbours. As against bare marble, which names the material anywhere, Carrara localizes excellence and history—the prestige quarry rather than the generic stone. Alps or Tuscany are broader geographic frames without the stone-specific fame; quarry is industrial and process-focused and lacks the poetic contour of "the hills of Carrara"; Paros or Pentelic evoke rival classical marbles (Greek) and would shift the cultural map away from the Italian Renaissance continuity Pater's list quietly assumes. Only Carrara fuses toponym, material reputation, and the English literary habit of citing those particular hills as an aesthetic touchstone. Polysemy and modern usage:
  • Place name: the town and comune of Carrara in the Apuan Alps, Tuscany; by extension "the hills of Carrara," the marble-bearing mountains above the town.
  • Material name: Carrara marble, the white and blue-gray stone quarried there, and its grades—statuario, Carrara white, Carrara C—used as a benchmark of fine marble.
  • Attributive and brand use: "Carrara marble," "Carrara white," "the quarries of Carrara," in architecture, sculpture, and luxury interiors.
  • Cultural and literary metonym: "the hills of Carrara" as an aesthetic touchstone of pure sculptural light in English criticism, especially in Pater's Renaissance.
  • Administrative name: the province of Massa-Carrara, and the historic Duchy of Massa and Carrara (the Cybo-Malaspina state).
Collocations and register. Collocations include Carrara marble, the quarries of Carrara, the hills of Carrara, Carrara white, statuario (a grade of Carrara marble), and technical phrases such as marmor Lunense in the Roman context. Register ranges from art history and architecture to high literary prose (Pater) and luxury commerce; in criticism it signals cultivated reference—knowing the source of the stone of Michelangelo's David and of countless church façades—rather than casual travel writing alone.
