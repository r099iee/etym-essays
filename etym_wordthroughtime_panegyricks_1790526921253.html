- Classical Greek (5th-4th c. BCE) - panēgyrikos logos; formal celebratory oration delivered at solemn national assemblies (panēgyreis) like the Olympic games; patriotic, civic, and philosophical.
- Hellenistic & Roman Era (1st-4th c. CE) - Latin panegyricus adopted; formalized into the imperial panegyric genre; Pliny and the Panegyrici Latini deliver elaborate ceremonial eulogies to emperors; high political and courtly register.
- Renaissance & Early Modern English (1500s-1600s) - English borrows panegyrick; court poets (Dryden, Donne) compose elaborate verse panegyrics for royal accessions, weddings, and aristocratic patrons.
- Eighteenth Century (1700s) - The spelling panegyrick / panegyricks flourishes; satire masters (Swift, Pope, Sterne) contrast excessive panegyric with scathing satire, giving the word an ironic potential for empty flattery.
- Victorian & Modern English (1800s-present) - Spelling simplifies to panegyric / panegyrics; word transitions from an active poetic genre into a sophisticated lexical term for any effusive, uncritical praise or grand rhetorical tribute.
