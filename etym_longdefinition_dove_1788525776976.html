A dove (noun) is primarily defined in zoology as any of numerous stout-bodied, small-headed birds belonging to the family Columbidae within the order Columbiformes, characterized by dense, soft plumage, short slender bills with a fleshy basal cere, short legs with four unwebbed toes, and a characteristic low, melodious cooing vocalization. While the terms dove and pigeon are taxonomically interchangeable and denote members of the same avian family, ornithological convention generally reserves dove for smaller, more slender, long-tailed species (such as the mourning dove, ringneck dove, and diamond dove), whereas larger, stockier varieties are typically designated as pigeons. Renowned for their rapid, direct flight powered by strong breast muscles, doves feed predominantly on seeds, grains, and small fruits, and uniquely produce "crop milk" (a nutritious secretion from the lining of the crop) with which both parents nourish their altricial young.



Beyond its strict ornithological identity, the word dove possesses an exceptionally rich polysemous landscape spanning mythology, religion, political science, monetary economics, literature, and interpersonal endearment:




  • Spiritual and Religious Symbol of Peace and the Divine: Across ancient Near Eastern, Judeo-Christian, and classical Mediterranean traditions, the dove is the foremost universal emblem of peace, divine favor, purity, and spiritual renewal. In the Book of Genesis, a dove returned to Noah's Ark carrying an olive leaf in its beak, signifying that the floodwaters had receded and that God had made peace with humanity. In Christian theology and iconography, the Holy Spirit is almost universally depicted in the bodily form of a descending white dove, representing divine grace, baptismal cleansing, and celestial inspiration. In ancient Greco-Roman mythology, the dove was sacred to Aphrodite (Venus), the goddess of love, beauty, and fertility, symbolizing romantic passion and gentle devotion.


  • Political and Geopolitical Jargon (Hawks vs. Doves): In international diplomacy and political analysis, a dove designates a politician, diplomat, or statesman who favors negotiation, non-violent conflict resolution, multilateral treaties, and diplomatic concessions over military intervention, aggressive rearmament, or bellicose foreign policies. Coined during the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 and popularized during the Vietnam War, the political dichotomy between "hawks" (militarists) and "doves" (peacemakers) remains a central framework for analyzing foreign policy debates worldwide.


  • Economic and Central Banking Sense (Monetary Dove): In macroeconomic theory and financial journalism, a dove (or monetary dove) refers to a central bank policymaker (such as a governor of the Federal Reserve or the European Central Bank) who prioritizes stimulating economic growth, expanding employment, and maintaining low interest rates over strictly curbing inflation. This contrasts directly with a monetary hawk, who advocates tightening monetary policy and raising borrowing costs to prevent inflation.


  • Term of Affection and Poetic Endearment: Historically in English literature and lyric poetry, dove has been employed as a tender, intimate term of endearment for an innocent, gentle, or beloved person, especially a maiden or child (e.g., "my gentle dove"), emphasizing soft-spoken innocence and unwavering fidelity.


  • Literary and Chromatic Imagery: In aesthetic and fairy-tale literature, the physical details of the bird evoke striking sensory metaphors. A notable instance occurs in Oscar Wilde’s fairy tale The Nightingale and the Rose, where the Rose-tree describes its blossoms as being "as red as the feet of the dove," alluding to the vivid, coral-red coloration of the dove's legs and feet (podotheca), contrasting with its soft white or pearl-grey feathers to create an exquisite image of romantic beauty and tragic sacrifice.



The word functions primarily as a noun, but also occurs in informal or dialectal English as the archaic past tense of the verb dive (though standard modern English generally prefers dived, dove remains prevalent in North American English). The avian noun descends from Proto-Germanic roots signifying gentle flight and dusky plumage, evolving over centuries into a cornerstone of Western symbolic vocabulary.
