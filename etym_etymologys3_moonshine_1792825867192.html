The moonshine story is told in two halves that met a thousand years ago and never parted. The first half is the moon: PIE *meh₁-, to measure, because the moon measured the month — the night clock by which hunters, farmers, and priests told time. It gives Germanic *mēnô (moon and month in one word, as they still are in every Germanic tongue), Old English mōna, and a calendar of cousins from Greek mēn (month) to Latin mensis. The moon was humanity's first almanac, and its name remembers the job.

The second half is the shine: PIE *skāi-, to gleam and shimmer, through Germanic *skīnaną to Old English scīnan — the bright verb of polished shields, glittering mail, and sunrise on water. Compounded in Middle English as moneshine, the pair first meant only the moon's light, poetry's favourite lamp, and centuries of lovers, madmen, and navigators used it so. Then the metaphor did its quiet work: moonlight is lovely and utterly ungraspable, and by Tudor times anything showy and hollow — alchemists' promises, courtiers' flattery, lovers' vows — could be dismissed as moonshine, brightness with nothing in the hand.

Stages of the journey:

  • PIE clock and gleam (before 2000 BCE): *meh₁- measures months by the moon while *skāi- names shimmer — timekeeping and glitter, the compound's two parents.
  • Germanic moon-month (c. 500 BCE–500 CE): *mēnô is moon and month at once, and *skīnaną shines over shields and seas — the vocabulary thickens toward poetry.
  • Old English lamp (9th–11th c.): mōna and scīnan live side by side in poems and gospels; the compound moneshine kindles in the Middle English manuscripts that follow.
  • Tudor emptiness (15th–16th c.): glow becomes metaphor — moonshine for illusion, flattery, and alchemy's shining lies, the first step from lamp to laugh.
  • Yankee nonsense (19th c.): American speech fixes moonshine as the standard jolly dismissal of tall talk — all moonshine, mere moonshine — echoed in newspapers and novels.
  • Night stills (late 18th–20th c.): excisemen in Scotland, Ireland, and Appalachia learn that the smoke they hunt by day rises from stills fired by night — the untaxed spirit takes the moon's name, and Prohibition (1920–1933) makes moonshine world-famous, chased by revenuers and sung in a hundred ballads.
  • Three moons today: garden moonshine for lovers and poets, political moonshine for columnists mocking bright promises, and legal craft moonshine in mason jars — the outlaw romance distilled, taxed, and sold back to the grandchildren of the revenuers.

Attestation glitters through the canon: Shakespeare's lovers are moonshine-mad, Milton's Eden sleeps in it, Mark Twain's Yankees dismiss bunkum as moonshine, and the Carter Family's mountain dew runs by its light. Few compounds have stretched so far from so plain a start — two monosyllables, a lamp and a gleam — to cover romance, rubbish, and rotgut without ever ceasing to mean, at bottom, light you cannot hold.
