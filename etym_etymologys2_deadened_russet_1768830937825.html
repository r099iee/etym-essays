CHAIN 1 FOR DEADENED: PIE *dʰew- (also written *dʰeu-) — "to die" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) ↓ PIE *dʰew-tó- / *dʰeu-tó- — "dead, having died" (perfect participle of the root) ↓ Proto-Germanic *daudaz — "dead" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE–200 CE) ↓ Old English dēad — "dead" (attested c. 8th–11th c.) ↓ Middle English ded / deed — "dead" (attested 12th–15th c.) ↓ Modern English dead — "dead" (present day) ↓ dead + causative suffix -en → deaden — "to make dead, to deprive of feeling or force, to dull" (verb first attested c. 1610s) ↓ deaden + -ed → deadened — "made dull, muted, numbed" (attested 17th c. onward) CHAIN 2 FOR RUSSET: PIE *h₁rewdʰ- — "red, ruddy" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) ↓ Latin russus (via *rudʰ-s-o-, the s-extension of the root) — "red, reddish-brown" (attested Classical Latin) ↓ Old French rous — "red, reddish-brown" (Gallo-Romance development of Latin russus; attested c. 9th–12th c.) ↓ Old French rousset — "reddish-brown; coarse red-brown woollen cloth" (diminutive of rous, with the suffix -et; attested c. 12th–13th c.) ↓ Middle English russet — "reddish-brown; coarse homespun reddish-brown cloth" (attested 14th c.) ↓ Modern English russet — "reddish-brown; a russet apple or potato variety" (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE: For DEADENED, the PIE root *dʰew- "to die" is standard, but the formation of Proto-Germanic *daudaz (whether directly from the PIE participle *dʰeu-tó- or through a Germanic weak-verb *dawjaną "to die" whose participle supplied the adjective) is discussed in the literature; the participle route is the mainstream account. For RUSSET, Latin russus and ruber both descend from *h₁rewdʰ-, russus through the variant *rudʰ-s- (s-extended stem); the French and English steps are securely attested and uncontroversial.
