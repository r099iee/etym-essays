Medallions, plural of medallion, is a noun with three living senses in modern English, and in the passage it works in the second and most pictorial of them. Sense one is numismatic and honourific: a medallion is a large medal, a stamped or cast token of metal, usually of gold, silver, or bronze, struck to commemorate a person, an event, or an achievement, and larger than an ordinary coin. Sense two, the decorative sense at work in the text, is a round or oval ornamental panel or motif containing a design, an emblem, a portrait, or a pattern, either sculpted in relief, woven into fabric, moulded in plaster, painted on a ceiling, or printed on a surface, and repeated at intervals as a decorative scheme. Sense three is culinary: a medallion is a round slice or cut of meat - typically of beef, veal, or pork - cut across the grain into a neat coin-like disc, the shape again echoing the coin and the medal. Behind all three senses lies a single spatial idea, the frame: a medallion is always a shape enclosed, a bounded field in which one emblem sits, whether the emblem is the head of a prince, the portrait of a statesman, a tiny dragon, or a fillet of beef. In the sentence from David Hawkes's translation of The Dream of the Red Chamber, "In the middle of the kang was a dark-red bolster with a pattern of medallions in the form of tiny dragons, and a long russet-green seating strip in the same pattern," the word works in its fully decorative sense: the medallions are woven or embroidered roundels on the bolster, each enclosing the figure of a dragon, repeated over the dark-red ground. The choice of "medallions" rather than "circles" or "roundels" imports the vocabulary of Western decorative art and numismatics into the Chinese interior, and it does so with deliberate ceremony. A medallion is a framed, self-contained emblem, and by framing the dragon - the supreme sign of imperial and celestial authority in Chinese iconography - the pattern converts a cushion into a statement of rank. The tiny dragons locked inside their medallions suggest that every part of the room, even the bolster against which one leans, is woven from the family's claim to status; the pattern makes power ornamental, and ornament itself a medium of identity. The narrator quietly asserts that the dragon-bolstered kang of the Jia household can hold its own against the emblem systems of any court on earth, for the same framed device that decorates a Renaissance medal decorates the furniture of a Chinese great house. The polysemy of the word can be set out fully:
  • Numismatic / honourific sense (from the 17th century): a large medal struck to commemorate a person, event, or achievement; larger than a coin and often of finer metal. The root notion is scale and dignity - a medallion is the "big medal", the honour enlarged.
  • Decorative / ornamental sense (from the late 17th century): a round or oval panel, motif, or ornament enclosing a design, emblem, or portrait; carved in stone or wood, moulded in plaster, woven, embroidered, painted, or printed; used singly or repeated in a pattern. This is the sense in the text.
  • Architectural sense: a circular or oval plaque or relief set into a ceiling, wall, or pediment, often containing a portrait bust, a scene, or a monogram; also the recessed or raised frame itself.
  • Heraldic sense: a round or oval charge or panel containing a device, found on shields and crests; related to the roundel family of charges.
  • Fashion and jewellery sense: a large pendant or brooch, round or oval, often bearing a portrait, a lock of hair, or an engraved design, worn on a chain - the Victorian locket and the commemorative pendant are the modern heirs.
  • Culinary sense (19th century): a round slice or cut of meat cut across the grain into a coin-like disc, e.g. "veal medallions with a cream sauce"; the shape preserves the coin-and-medal idea in the kitchen.
  • Figurative / metonymic extension: any framed, self-contained unit - a set of boxed stamps is sometimes called a "medallion block", and in numismatics a medallion is a medal of exceptional size; the word also lends its name to decorative repeats in quilting, tiling, and carpet design.
The historical bridge that ties the senses together is the frame and the scale. Because a medallion is a "large medal", and because medals habitually carry a framed portrait or emblem on their face, the word migrated naturally from the coin-maker's art to the decorator's: whatever is enclosed in a round or oval frame and set into a larger surface - a plaster ceiling, a woven bolster, a plate, a building facade - came to be called a medallion. The culinary sense followed the same logic of shape: a round cut of meat is a medallion because it looks like a large coin. Register matters too: the word is mildly formal and artistic, at home in museum catalogues, decorating manuals, and descriptive fiction, and almost never in ordinary casual speech. In the passage it is therefore the perfect instrument of tone: ceremonial, hierarchical, and quietly cosmopolitan, binding a Chinese bolster to the whole European tradition of the framed emblem from Pisanello's portrait medals to the stucco medallions of a Baroque ceiling.
