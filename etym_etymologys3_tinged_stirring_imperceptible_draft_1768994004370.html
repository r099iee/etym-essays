tinged. The biography of tinged begins in a tub of dye. Its ancestor is the PIE root *teng-, "to soak, wet, dip," a verb that in Greek (téngō, "I moisten") never escaped its watery simplicity — a rare and poetic verb, confined in Greek to the wetting sense, with no developed colour vocabulary of its own. It was Latin that gave the dipping a purpose: tingere, "to dip, to stain," whose past participle tinctus yielded tinctura, "a dyeing," which entered English as tincture (both the alchemical and the general sense of "a slight infusion"); and through Old French teindre and its participle teint the root reached Middle English, producing in the sixteenth century the English verb tinge, "to color lightly, to touch with a trace of something," whose past participle is our word tinged. The collateral Romance family is compact — Italian tingere and tinta, Spanish teñir, Portuguese tingir, Catalan tenyir, French teindre and teint — all still dipping and dyeing in the service of the same root, while the English offshoots taint and stain carry the root's darker side: taint (via Anglo-French teinter, "to dye," with the notion of staining and sullying) and stain (probably via Old French desteindre, "to discolor") preserve the ancient fear that dipping is also contamination.

The semantic movement is the story. A dip that once soaked a whole cloth became, by the early modern period, a touch so slight it only stains the edges — a change of colour that leaves the substance itself untouched. That is exactly the word's work in this passage, where darkness is not a flood but a tincture mixed into the air: "tinged with a slight and doubtful darkness." The old physical sense, to dip a garment into dye, has retreated, and the modern word lives almost entirely in metaphor — air, voices, memories, and moods are tinged with something. The word has also kept its faint note of contamination: to be "tinged with regret" is to carry a colour one did not choose, and to be "tinged with irony" is to have something foreign mixed into the purity of one's tone.

Phonetically the word tells the story of a root that was reshaped twice — once by Latin (where -ng- became the nasalized tinct- of the participle) and once by French (where Latin -ng- became Old French -nd- in teindre and teint), before English re-borrowed and re-levelled it as tinge. The voiced -dge of the modern verb is a Middle English softening of the Old French -nd- cluster. In collocation, tinge/tinged takes with plus a colour or feeling, and its register is literary and slightly delicate: it is the word a writer chooses when the colouring seems added from outside rather than intrinsic, a wash laid over a surface that remains otherwise itself. It differs from tint (a lightened, pastel amount of a colour), shade (a darkening toward black), tone (an adjustment of brightness or warmth), and hue (the bare name of a colour) precisely because it keeps the image of the dye-bath — something has been dipped, soaked, touched, and the whole is changed without being dominated. Tinge is the verb for the small, diffused addition that changes everything without ever dominating it.

stirring. Stirring reaches back to the PIE root *(s)twer-, "to rotate, turn, whirl," the vocabulary of anything that turns itself around. The root's family is vast across the ancient languages: it appears in Greek tormos, "a lathe, a turning," and in the Latin turbāre, "to disturb," the ancestor of English turbid, disturb, and turbine — all built on the same image of whirling, churning motion. The Germanic branch kept the physical sense: Proto-Germanic *sturjaną "to stir, disturb, move briskly" produced Old English styrian, "to move, agitate, rouse," and English stir, in use from the thirteenth or fourteenth century, has carried that sense of beginning motion ever since.

The noun stirring names the first faint motion out of stillness — a tremor, a ripple, an awakening — and the same stem reached into feeling: a "stirring speech" moves the heart, "a stirring of hope" is the first movement of a feeling that was not yet awake. This double register is not accidental but structural. The root *(s)twer- describes circular agitation — the turning of a stick in a pot, the churning of water, the whirl of dust — and from "to agitate" it was a short step to "to agitate the feelings," a step taken independently in Latin (turbāre also meant "to disturb the mind") and in Germanic. The word is thus a master of the threshold, used precisely when something is about to become something else — a wind not yet a wind, an emotion not yet a decision, a movement not yet a motion.

In literary use the noun has had a distinguished career as the word for pre-conscious or pre-visible stirring: the "stirring of the dust" in a road, the "stirring of the sap" in spring, the "first stirrings of doubt" in a mind. Coleridge and the Romantics loved the word for exactly this reason — it names the moment of genesis, the tremor that precedes all form. In this passage "the stirring of an almost imperceptible draft" stands exactly on that line: the movement is real, but so early and so faint that it cannot yet be dignified with the name of a breeze. The word's double register — physical motion and emotional arousal — has kept it alive for eight centuries, always describing the first tremor, the moment just after stillness, the beginning of a beginning. And because the noun is also the gerund of the verb, it can hover between an event ("a stirring") and an ongoing process ("the stirring of"), which gives the passage its sense of a motion caught mid-gesture, arrested at the very threshold of becoming perceptible.

Sound change reinforced the word's career. The Old English verb styrian shows the typical Germanic development of the root's *st- cluster, kept intact where the Greek and Italic cousins lost or reshaped their s- (so-called s-mobile: the same phenomenon by which Latin sternere, "to strew," stands beside Germanic streowian, English strew). Middle English stiren levelled the old verb classes, and the modern stir shows the regular loss of the weak verb's dental ending in the base form. The noun stirring, built with the ubiquitous -ing suffix, preserves both the verbal sense (the gerund: "the act of stirring") and the substantival sense (the event: "a stirring"), a dual citizenship that no other word in this sentence enjoys and that the passage exploits to the full. Morphologically, stirring is at once process and product — the trembling of the air and the beginning of the draft are the same thing seen as activity and as event, which is precisely the sensory ambiguity the sentence wants: the adventurers cannot tell whether they feel motion or the onset of motion, and the word is built to preserve exactly that uncertainty.

imperceptible. Imperceptible is a word of the Latin schools, built of three Latin pieces and reaching back to one of the most productive roots in the family. Its deepest ancestor is PIE *keh₂p-, "to seize, grasp," which became Latin capere, "to take, seize" — a root so productive it gave English capture, conceive, receive, accept, occupy, prison, and cable, among a hundred others. With the prefix per-, "through," Latin built percipere, "to grasp thoroughly, to take in with the mind or senses" — the verb of perception itself, still visible in modern perceive and perception. From percipere came the adjective perceptibilis, "that can be grasped or perceived," and Late Latin negated it with in- to form imperceptibilis, "not graspable," which English adopted in the sixteenth century as imperceptible.

From the first, the word was a scientist's and philosopher's instrument: it names whatever is too fine, too slow, or too gradual for the senses to register — imperceptible atoms, imperceptible gradations, an imperceptible difference. The word belongs to the vocabulary of the new science of the seventeenth century, which was obsessed with what lay below the threshold of sensation: Galileo's imperceptible increments of motion, Locke's imperceptible ideas, the imperceptible particles of the corpuscular philosophers. It retains that analytic, formal register even today, and it is the preferred word whenever a writer wants to mark the exact boundary of what human faculties can verify. It differs from invisible by spanning every sense — an imperceptible sound, an imperceptible smell, an imperceptible movement — and from subtle by being a neutral statement of fact rather than a compliment: subtle implies craft and delicacy positively appreciated, imperceptible simply records that the thing escapes the senses.

The word's grammar reinforces its meaning: it collocates overwhelmingly with almost, nearly, virtually, and as good as, because its natural habitat is the boundary — a thing is imperceptible when it is just below the threshold, and speakers are forever qualifying how close to the line it comes. In this passage it is the word that sets the scale for the whole sentence: the draft is "almost imperceptible," felt at the boundary of what the skin can swear to — a darkness so fine it cannot be seen to be dark, a movement so faint it cannot be called motion, a current so slight it can barely be said to exist. The word performs the central act of the entire passage: it makes the reader hold a perception at the very edge of certainty, and doubt it exactly as the adventurers do.

Its morphology is worth pausing over, because it is a little masterpiece of Latin word-building. The prefix in- is the same negative that appears in incapable, invisible, and insufficient; the root -per- is the preposition "through" (as in perforate, "to bore through"); and -cep- is the zero-grade of capere, "to seize," the form that appears before consonants (compare conceive, deceive, receive). The whole assembly — in + per + cap + the adjective suffix -ibilis — means, step by step, "not (in-) graspable (-ibilis) through (-per-) seizing (-cep-)." Perception is, etymologically, a grasping through, and imperceptibility is the failure of that grasp. Latin was the great coiner of such negative technical adjectives in Late Antiquity and the Middle Ages, when philosophy and theology needed precise words for what lay beyond the human faculties, and imperceptibilis belongs squarely to that schoolroom tradition. When English borrowed it in the sixteenth century, it imported not just a word but a way of seeing — a vocabulary for the limits of the senses that the new science of the seventeenth century would put to work on atoms, gradations, and increments far below the threshold of observation. The word thus stands at the meeting point of two histories: the ancient philosophical interest in what escapes the senses, and the modern scientific interest in what lies below the threshold of measurement. In a single literary sentence it can still perform both duties at once — marking the exact limit of what Maspic's senses can verify while quietly invoking the whole Western tradition of thinking about imperceptible change.

draft. Draft is one of the most successful polysemes in English, and every one of its senses comes from a single image: drawing. Its ancestor is PIE *dʰreǵʰ-, "to draw, drag, pull," a root attested across the family — in Greek trékhein "to run" (from "to draw oneself along") and drómos "a course," in Latin trahere "to draw, drag" (the ancestor of tractor, traction, trail, and train), and in the Germanic verb family that concerns us. Proto-Germanic *draganą, "to draw, drag, pull," produced Old Norse draga and Old English dragan, "to draw, drag" — the very verb that still underlies modern draw and drag themselves.

From the verb English made the noun draught, "the act of drawing," and the semantic fan opened outward in one of the most remarkable radiations in the language. A current of air is air drawn into a room — the earliest and most bodily sense, recorded from the fourteenth century. A first version of a document is a thing drawn up in rough — hence "a draft of a letter." A drink is a liquid drawn from a cask — hence "draft beer" and "on draught." The depth a ship needs is the water it draws — hence "a ship of nine feet draft." Conscription draws men into service — hence "the draft." A banker's draft is an order drawn on a bank. A team of horses draws a load — hence "draft animals." Even draughts, the board game, involves pieces that are drawn forward across the board, and a draught in the nineteenth century could be the dose of medicine drawn from a bottle. Every sense is a different object of the same act: pull.

The spelling draft, a phonetic respelling of the sixteenth century, gradually split off from draught (which British English kept for several of the same senses, with its silent gh), and modern American usage folds nearly all the meanings into draft, while British English divides them between draught (air, beer, ship's depth, chess move) and draft (document, conscription, bank order). The split is a living lesson in how orthography, pronunciation, and meaning interact: one word, spelled two ways, carrying a dozen senses, all united by the prehistoric image of pulling.

In this passage the word supplies the physical close of the sensory chain: "the stirring of an almost imperceptible draft upon their faces." It is the only one of the four words that the skin can verify — a narrow, steady, inward current of air, the cold seam that finds its way into a warm room, or, here, into a sunless cave. The word's long career from "to drag" to "a small current of air" is a perfect demonstration of how a single concrete act of pulling can generate, over three thousand years, a family of meanings that seem to have nothing to do with one another — and how one of them, the humblest, can be the exact word for a breath of darkness on a man's face. Every sense of draft is an object that has been drawn; the draft the adventurers feel is air that has been drawn into the cave from somewhere else, bringing with it the faint, doubtful darkness that "tinges" the whole scene — the entire sensory chain, from soak to stir to boundary to pull, ending in a breath on the skin.
