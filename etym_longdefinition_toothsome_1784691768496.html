Toothsome is an adjective meaning pleasing to the tooth — that is, delicious, appetising, palatable, tempting to the taste, having a full, rich flavour that invites the eater to bite and chew. It is applied first of all to food and drink, and by a natural and ancient extension to the odour of food, when the aroma alone seems to promise a delicious mouthful. The word carries a slightly old-fashioned, hearty, sensual warmth: a toothsome dish is not merely elegant or refined, but substantial and satisfying, the kind of cooking that makes the mouth water and the appetite stir — a roast, a rich stew, a sweet pie, a piece of cheese that fills the mouth with savour. It belongs to a small family of English adjectives built with the suffix -some (from Old English -sum, "characterised by, tending to"), a group that includes wholesome, handsome, wearisome, quarrelsome, and burdensome; toothsome is a native English coinage of the sixteenth century, first recorded about 1565, formed directly from the noun tooth.

In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the narrator remarks of the trolls' cooking that "There was a fine toothsome smell." The adjective performs a small synaesthetic transfer: the smell of roasting mutton is so rich and meaty that it registers, before a single bite is taken, on the sense of taste — the tooth — itself. The word is exactly right for the moment: the dwarves (and the narrator) are travellers hungry after a long day's road, and a "fine toothsome smell" is precisely the promise that makes hunger irresistible. Tolkien's sly comedy is that this mouth-watering aroma belongs not to a comfortable inn but to three trolls, and that the smell which the party will gladly follow may lead them into the very pot. The adjective's old-fashioned, hearty warmth is a deliberate part of the joke — the phrase is cosy enough for a Shire fireside, absurdly cosy for a troll campfire.

Polysemy and secondary senses. Because the word is built from the plain noun tooth, its senses all cluster around the mouth, the bite, and the appetite, and it has acquired a small but real set of secondary uses:

  • Of food and drink (primary): delicious, appetising, palatable — a toothsome morsel, a toothsome pasty, toothsome fare.
  • Of aroma and savour (extended): applied to the smell of cooking, so that the scent itself is experienced as tasty — Tolkien's "fine toothsome smell"; also used in tasting notes of strong-flavoured food and drink (cheese, wine, cured meats) where the odour promises the flavour.
  • Of persons (jocular/figurative): since the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries a person may be called toothsome in the sense of attractive, alluring, appetising to desire — a playful transfer from the appetite for food to the appetite for a person, preserving the word's slightly naughty, indulgent flavour.
  • Of offers, ideas, and prizes (figurative, literary): anything tempting may be called toothsome — a toothsome reward, a toothsome story, a piece of gossip or a bargain that one "cannot resist"; the appetite-metaphor is applied to ambition, curiosity, or greed rather than to hunger.
  • Archaic literal (almost obsolete): rarely, "furnished with teeth" — but this literal sense has been almost entirely displaced by toothed; what survives in toothsome is not "having teeth" but "pleasing to the teeth".

Semantic history and register. The historical bridge from "of the tooth" to "delicious" is the metonymy of the palate: the tooth is the emblem of eating and tasting, so what pleases the tooth pleases the eater. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word settled in cookery writing as a hearty, sensual term for rich, substantial fare — "satisfying rather than merely elegant", the cooking that makes the mouth water. In the nineteenth century it carried a faintly genial, old-fashioned warmth, was extended jocularly to people, and began to feel slightly nostalgic; in the twentieth and twenty-first it has retreated from plain speech and survives as a consciously literary or slightly archaic adjective, at home in fairy-tale narration, historical fiction, food journalism with a period flavour, and descriptions of feasts. Its nearest plain-spoken modern rivals are tasty and yummy; its most elevated rival is delectable; its most technical rival is palatable. None of them carries toothsome's distinctive mouth-and-bite picture, and none of them does what the word does in Tolkien's sentence — make an entire roast, unseen, irresistible by smell alone.
