balustrade — an architectural railing formed of a continuous horizontal rail (the handrail or coping) supported at regular intervals by a series of short, pillar-like posts called balusters, used to line balconies, terraces, staircases, parapets, and bridges. The baluster is the defining element: a turned or carved shaft, often swelling in the middle in a vase- or bottle-like profile, that gives the balustrade its characteristic rhythm of vertical supports, like a row of small urns holding up the rail. Where a solid wall blocks the eye, a balustrade is open and skeletal, letting light and air pass between its posts while still preventing a fall; it is at once a barrier, a handhold, and an ornamental parade of identical verticals. In the passage from Mervyn Peake's Gormenghast, the winning carvings are stood upon the balustrade of the balcony, in full view of the crowd below, while the Earl of Groan calls for their authors to come forward. Here the balustrade is far more than a safety barrier: it is the heraldic shelf and stage of the ceremony, the architectural threshold that separates the Earl on his height from the crowd beneath, and the frame upon which the year's finest work is publicly displayed. The tone is one of formal elevation — the balustrade lifts the objects of honour into view and gives the rite its vertical geography: above, the castle and its Earl; below, the silent crowd; between them, the chosen carvings, exhibited upon the railing as upon an altar, the Earl's summons falling upon them from his balcony as the year's verdict upon the year's craft. The very word carries the dignity of classical architecture: it promises proportion, symmetry, and a measured rhythm of supports, and the passage plants the ceremony's display exactly where that dignity is most legible. The word's senses fan out in a graded series, and each shade must be distinguished:
  • Strict architectural sense — the open railing of balusters and rail proper to classical and classical-revival building: balconies, terraces, loggias, staircases, and roof parapets.
  • Loose sense: any railing of posts and rail — by extension, any fence-like barrier of vertical posts carrying a continuous rail, including plain domestic and public railings.
  • The baluster (the part) — the individual turned post; strictly a balustrade is the completed series of balusters carrying the rail, and confusion between part and whole is common (compare banister, the folk corruption of baluster used of stair rails).
  • Figurative architecture of display — in literary usage, and vividly in the passage, the balustrade becomes a shelf, stage, or threshold on which honour is exhibited: the visible framework of elevation.
  • Design and technical vocabulary — in architecture software, CAD libraries, and interior design, "balustrade" is the standard term for the stair or balcony railing assembly, and the word survives as a technical module name in digital design tools.
The word's nearest neighbours must be carefully distinguished. A parapet is a low, solid protective wall along the edge of a roof or balcony, with no posts and no rail; a balustrade is open and skeletal by contrast, letting light and air pass between its supports. A railing is the generic term for any fence-like barrier of bars or posts, lacking the balustrade's implication of turned, ornamental balusters. A banister is the everyday, folk-evolved word for the stair rail and its posts, whereas balustrade is the formal architectural term that also covers balconies and terraces. A handrail is the single horizontal bar grasped for support, with no requirement of posts beneath it. Etymologically the balustrade's baluster descends from the pomegranate blossom — the Greek balaustion, whose half-open, vase-shaped calyx the Renaissance turners saw echoed in the swelling profile of their turned posts — so that the word carries, embedded in its very form, the memory of a flower turned into stone: a row of small urns holding up the rail, as the mnemonic says, along a Georgian balcony.
