• Bog: Similar as a waterlogged, peat-accumulating wetland (mire); differs fundamentally in ecology because a bog is *ombrotrophic* (fed exclusively by rainwater), highly acidic, nutrient-deficient, and dominated by *Sphagnum* peat moss, whereas a *fen* is *minerotrophic* (fed by mineral-rich groundwater), neutral-to-alkaline, nutrient-rich, and dominated by tall reeds, rushes, and sedges.
  • Marsh: Similar as a wetland dominated by herbaceous (non-woody) plants such as grasses and reeds; differs because a marsh is defined by shallow, fluctuating surface water that typically lacks the thick, continuous accumulation of peat that characterizes a true fen.
  • Swamp: Similar in being an inundated, soggy wetland environment; differs because a swamp is ecologically defined by the predominance of woody plants and mature trees (such as cypress, mangrove, willow, or alder), whereas a fen is predominantly an open, tree-less or scrubby mire dominated by sedges.
  • Mire: Similar as an overarching scientific and geographical term for peat-forming wetlands; differs because *mire* is the broad superordinate category that encompasses both fens (minerotrophic) and bogs (ombrotrophic).
  • Morass: Similar in describing a low, soggy, treacherous tract of marshland; differs because *morass* carries strong stylistic and metaphorical connotations of an overwhelming, entrapping, and confusing situation from which it is difficult to extricate oneself.
  • Quagmire: Similar in denoting soft, yielding, waterlogged ground that shakes underfoot; differs because *quagmire* specifically emphasizes the physical hazard of sinking into deep mud and is frequently used metaphorically for hazardous, inescapable dilemmas.
  • Slough: Similar as a muddy, stagnant wetland depression or inlet; differs because a slough is typically a muddy secondary channel, backwater bayou, or swampy ravine characterized by deep, sticky mud, often carrying moral overtones of despair (e.g., the *Slough of Despond*).
