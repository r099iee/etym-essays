1. The Iranian background. The chain begins in Persian. Middle Persian šamšēr and New Persian šamšīr both mean "sword"; from the Sasanian period onward it is the ordinary word for the blade, and classical Persian poetry — above all Firdawsī's Shāhnāma, completed c. 1010 — swarms with šamšīr, usually the sweeping curved sword of the hero. The traditional parsing splits the word as šamš-šīr, literally "lion's claw" (šīr = lion), a poetic image that suits a curved, slashing blade; modern linguists regard the reading as plausible but unproven. The deeper origin of šamšīr is unknown — some scholars suggest an older Iranian compound for "blade", others an early loan into Persian — and responsibly, no PIE root can be supplied for the word; this is precisely the kind of case where "unknown" must be said out loud rather than papered over.

2. The road into Europe. The blade itself reached European attention through the Crusades, through Byzantine contact, and through the trading cities of Venice and Genoa, whose merchants knew Ottoman and Mamluk markets; but the word appears in Romance only in the 15th century — Middle French cimeterre (15th c.) and Italian scimitarra — and the Romance shapes do not fit šamšīr cleanly: the -imitarra / -imeterre vocalism before the final -r is irregular, which is the reason the OED still says "of uncertain origin". The honest summary is that the Romance forms were almost certainly reshaped from an eastern sword-name, with Persian šamšīr by far the best candidate, and that the exact route of transmission (Venetian trade? Ottoman war captives? translated Persian and Turkish chronicles?) is undocumented.

3. Spelling chaos in early English. English takes the word in the 1540s, spelled simitar, symitar, cymitar and semitar in the first decades; the modern spelling scimitar (with sc-) settles only in the 18th century, apparently under the influence of Latin-looking restorations or of "scintillant" associations. Pronunciation has wobbled between /ˈsɪmɪtə/ and /ˈsɪmɪtɑː/; the plural scimitars is the form most frequent in narrative prose. The word entered as an exotic novelty — a weapon of the "Grand Turk" and the "Moor" — and its earliest attestations cluster in travel accounts, histories of the Ottoman wars and court pageants.

4. Orientalism and the Romantic century. The 18th and 19th centuries turned the scimitar into a portable symbol: the crescent blade of the "Terrible Turk", the pirate's curved steel in Byron's The Corsair (1814) and The Giaour, the East of Moore's Lalla Rookh (1817), the Mamluk and the Algerian corsair in French and English painting. It is the age in which "scimitar" detaches from any particular weapon and simply means "the curved sword of the Oriental", loaded with menace and magnificence in equal measure; historical writing on the fall of Constantinople (1453), the sieges of Vienna, and the Barbary wars is full of it, and the image of the "scimitar of Islam" hardens into a cliché of popular history.

5. The fantasy after-life. With the end of the sword as a working weapon in the 19th century the word might have died; instead it was re-exported by fantasy. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) — the very book our context sentence comes from — puts "scimitars of steel" in the hands of goblins; Dungeons & Dragons and the whole role-playing tradition treat the scimitar as a distinct weapon class with its own game statistics; film and video games keep it as the eastern, curved alternative to the knightly sword and the fencing rapier. A word that entered English as a report from the Ottoman frontier now names a weapon most speakers have never seen outside fiction.

6. Pure shape in modern science. None of the modern technical uses involves swords at all: the scimitar-horned oryx (the Sahelian antelope, now extinct in the wild and bred back in captivity), the scimitar babbler of Asian scrub, scimitar-blade propellers, scimitar winglets and the scimitar-shaped dunes photographed on Mars all borrow the word for one property — a single, deep, sickle-like curve. The full semantic trajectory, then, is concrete weapon → oriental emblem → fantasy stock → geometric adjective; each stage is driven by the same visible curve, and the word is a small case study in how lexical meaning migrates when the object it once named ceases to be part of daily life.
