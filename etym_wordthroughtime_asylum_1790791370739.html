- 5th c. BCE - *Classical Greek ἄσυλον (ásylon)*: Designated a sacred, inviolable sanctuary (temple, altar) where fugitives and debtors were legally protected from violent seizure.
- 1st c. BCE–1500s - *Roman Law & Medieval Christendom*: Maintained in Latin (*asylum*) and canon law as the right of sanctuary attached to consecrated churches and holy shrines.
- 1600s–1700s - *Early Modern English*: Entered the English language to describe any safe refuge or secure shelter from political persecution or personal misfortune.
- Late 1700s–1800s - *Enlightenment Medicine*: Repurposed by psychiatric reformers (Pinel, Tuke) to name humane, tranquil retreats for the mentally ill ('moral treatment' in psychiatric asylums).
- Mid-to-Late 20th c. - *Stigmatization & Deinstitutionalization*: The psychiatric usage decayed into a chilling synonym for abusive, custodial madhouses, leading to its abandonment in medicine.
- 1951–Present - *International Refugee Law*: Solidified globally under the UN Refugee Convention as 'political asylum'—the fundamental human right of refugees to seek protection from persecution.
