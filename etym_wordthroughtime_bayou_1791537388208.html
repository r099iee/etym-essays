- Pre-18th century (Indigenous Southeastern Woodlands / Muskogean) - A neutral Choctaw topographic term (*bayuk*) for any river, stream, or flowing watercourse, integral to local navigation and territorial description.
- 18th century (French Colonial Louisiana) - Borrowed by French settlers, soldiers, and coureurs des bois as a specialized hydrologic term for the meandering, slow-moving bayous of the Mississippi delta, carrying associations of navigable inland waterways and swamp topography.
- 19th century (Acadian / Cajun Diaspora & Frontier Lore) - Became intrinsically identified with Cajun and Creole wetland cultures, romanticized in American literature as a mysterious, wild labyrinth of cypress swamps, alligators, and pirate havens (e.g., Jean Lafitte).
- 20th century to Present (Cultural Iconography & Environmental Fragility) - Solidified as a global symbol of Louisiana identity, Cajun music, and Southern Gothic atmosphere, while shifting in scientific discourse to represent a critically threatened, eroding wetland ecosystem vital for coastal hurricane buffering.
