Definition.’Twas is the traditional contraction of it was, formed by eliding the vowel of it. It belongs historically to ordinary spoken Early Modern English — anyone might have written it — but for the last two centuries it has been registered as deliberately archaic and literary: the stock-in-trade of verse, ballad, and nostalgic prose, and above all fixed in popular memory by Clement Clarke Moore’s opening line, “’Twas the night before Christmas.”

  • Formation. ’Twas is a proclitic reduction: the unstressed pronoun it loses its vowel and merges with the following was, exactly parallel to ’tis (“it is”), ’twere (“it were”), ’twill (“it will”), and ’twould (“it would”). The apostrophe marks the suppressed vowel. Because it is fixed to the past of “to be,” the contraction normally introduces a state, a scene, or a time: “’Twas autumn,” “’Twas a dark and stormy night,” “’Twas ever thus.”
  • Register and connotation. Modern readers hear ’twas as elevated, quaint, or mock-heroic. In the source context — Wordsworth’s Prelude — the contraction is authentic early nineteenth-century poetic diction: its metrical economy (two syllables squeezed into one beat) lets the iambic line flow, and Wordsworth uses it as naturally as any Elizabethan. In contemporary writing, unironic use reads as pastiche; ironic or affectionate use (“’twas ever thus”) is a knowingly archaic flourish.
  • Distinction from related forms. ’Twas means “it was” and nothing else. It is not “that was” (dialectal twuz/’twer cover that role), and it sits in a family with ’twasn’t (“it was not”) and ’tis (“it is”). In verse the apostrophe is frequently omitted (“Twas”), a purely typographical liberty that does not change the pronunciation.
  • Folk and literary fame. The word’s cultural centre of gravity is Clement Clarke Moore’s poem “A Visit from St. Nicholas” (1823), whose first words have made ’twas the most quoted archaic contraction in American English. Beyond Christmas, the form saturates ballads, sea shanties, and period fiction as a rapid stylisation of the past: “’twas three brave sailors,” “’twas in the merry month of May.”
  • Grammar note. ’Twas is the finite verb of its clause: “’Twas autumn” = subject ’t (it) + copula ’was. The pronoun is normally unstressed, and the contraction is impossible when “it” carries emphasis (“it WAS autumn” cannot be “’twas AUTUMN was”). This constraint — only unstressed pronouns contract — explains why the form thrives in verse, where metre keeps the pronoun light, and why it sounds wrong when stressed in modern dialogue.

Why it persists. The contraction survives almost entirely because of poetry’s appetite for syllables: one metrical beat instead of two. Every age that has written ballads, hymns, or Christmas verse has found ’twas a ready-made start, and the tradition of opening a story with it has hardened into a genre signal — dramatic, retrospective, gently archaic — that no amount of prosaic rationalisation has displaced. It is, in effect, a word kept alive by its own cadence.
