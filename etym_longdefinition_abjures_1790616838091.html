Abjures is the third-person singular present of the verb to abjure, meaning to renounce, repudiate, or give up formally and solemnly, especially upon oath, as in she abjures violence, he abjures his former faith, literacy by its nature abjures oral powers. The core is not casual dislike but public, binding renunciation, a speech act that says I swear off this, I disown it, I will no longer claim or practise it. In law and religion to abjure historically meant to swear to abandon heresy, to forswear allegiance, or to swear to leave the realm, with oath, ritual, and penalty behind the words.

In the source sentence, literacy by its very nature abjures the powers of oral expression means alphabetic writing inherently forswears, casts off, and denies itself the breath, gesture, intonation, and presence of speech, not by accident but by constitutive oath of its technology. The choice of abjures over lacks or omits moralizes the loss, literacy is pictured as a convert who solemnly renounces its oral past to gain silent visual permanence, and the history of prose style becomes a long effort to win back what was sworn away. The verb thus carries paradox, what is abjured continues to haunt, and must be rebuilt inside the very system that renounced it.

Contemporary use extends abjure from courtroom and altar to politics, culture, and personal ethics. Politicians abjure racism, artists abjure commercialism, scientists abjure speculation, dieters playfully abjure sugar, always with a flavour of principled, almost theatrical rejection. The form abjures with final s marks habitual or generic truth in present tense, literacy abjures, power abjures ambiguity, he abjures, timeless and gnomic rather than reporting a single event. It is formal, Latinate, and slightly archaic, chosen when renounce sounds too plain and deny sounds too defensive.

  • Legal-religious renunciation sense: to forswear upon oath, e.g. to abjure heresy, to abjure the realm. Polysemy bridge: directly from Latin abjurare to deny on oath, preserving oath-plus-away structure, where ab- marks turning away and jurare marks swearing.
  • General solemn rejection sense: to repudiate belief or practice, e.g. she abjures luxury, he abjures nationalism. Polysemy bridge: via secularization where oath weakens to solemn declaration, but formality remains, turning legal ritual into ethical stance.
  • Constitutive-denial sense, as here: for a system to exclude by nature, e.g. literacy abjures voice, print abjures gesture. Polysemy bridge: via personification where a medium is granted will and oath, so that technical incapacity is figured as willed renunciation, making absence morally legible.
  • Reflexive self-denial nuance: to abjure oneself or one's past, e.g. to abjure former self. Polysemy bridge: via reflexive extension where renouncer and renounced coincide, converting outward oath into inward conversion narrative.

Grammar and register reinforce solemnity. Abjures governs direct object of belief, allegiance, practice, or power, and less commonly abjure doing something. It is transitive, perfective, and decisive, one abjures once and for all, unlike suspends or questions. Syntactically it prefers abstract and ideological objects over concrete things, one abjures idolatry, not apples, except for comic inflation. To say literacy abjures orality is therefore to elevate media change to the level of creed, excommunication, and conversion, preparing the argument that prose style is a counter-reformation trying to smuggle voice back into a church that swore it away.
