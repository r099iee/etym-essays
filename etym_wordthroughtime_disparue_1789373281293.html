- 11th to 13th Century - Emerges in Old French as the past participle (*disparue*) of the verb *disparoir* / *desparoir* (derived from Vulgar Latin *disparēre*); used in medieval verse and romance to describe knights, phantoms, or sunlight vanishing from sight.
- 14th to 16th Century - Evolves through Middle French with the inchoative stem conjugation (*disparoistre*); increasingly employed across French Renaissance literature (Ronsard, Montaigne) to lament the fleeting transience of human beauty and youth.
- 17th to 18th Century - Standardized in Classical French (*disparaître* / *disparu* / *disparue*); expands into formal French jurisprudence and administrative maritime registers to denote individuals lost at sea during shipwrecks.
- 1804 (Napoleonic Era) - Formally codified in the French *Code Civil*, establishing *le disparu / la disparue* as a distinct legal category for persons missing in circumstances of mortal peril whose death is judicially certified.
- 1925 - Immortalized internationally as the title of Marcel Proust’s posthumous sixth volume, *Albertine disparue*, permanently entwining the word with modernist psychological themes of romantic obsession, grief, and phantom memory.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Continues as a standard high-register word in French cultural life, wildlife conservation (*espèces disparues*), and international Proustian scholarship following the 1986 restoration of Proust's preferred manuscript title.
