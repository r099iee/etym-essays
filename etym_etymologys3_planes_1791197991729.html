The evolution of planes in the tool sense is a textbook case of a Latin adjective becoming a concrete object, then radiating into mathematics and the sky while keeping its flat core. The plural adds regular -s to Middle English plane, borrowed from Old French plane, feminine of plan, from Late Latin plana "planing tool", substantivised from Latin planus "flat, level, even", itself from PIE *pele- "flat, broad, to spread" - the same root that gives plain, plan, floor, and Greek platys "broad".

PIE *pele- denoted flat expanse. In Italic it yielded Latin planus (flat ground, level character) and planum (flat surface). Roman carpenters called their smoothing sole-board plana, literally "the flat one", because its flat bottom rides the wood. This metonymy - flat thing for tool that makes flat - is the founding semantic leap. Late Latin building manuals and Isidore's Etymologies list plana among joiner's gear.

  • Latin to Old French (200-1300): Gallo-Roman craftsmen keep plana which Old French regularises to plane (feminine). Initial pl- stays, intervocalic -n- stays, final -a weakens to mute -e. The masculine adjective plan "flat" coexists. Anglo-Norman joiners bring both to England after 1066.
  • Middle English adoption (1300-1500): English already has native smoothing words (smoothing-iron, adze) but adopts plane for the framed tool with iron blade set at an angle. Guild rolls of London joiners list "planes" alongside "sawes" exactly as in the source passage. Pronunciation anglicises to /plaːn/ then /pleɪn/ after the Great Vowel Shift.
  • Standardisation of form (1500-1800): Wooden jack planes, trying planes, smoothing planes, moulding planes diversify. Joseph Moxon's Mechanick Exercises (1678) illustrates a dozen. Iron bodies arrive in the 19th century with Leonard Bailey's patents. Spelling stays plane, plural planes, pronunciation /pleɪnz/.

While the tool stabilises, the flat idea branches. Geometry takes Latin planum as plane - a flat two-dimensional surface extending infinitely (plane geometry, inclined plane). Joinery takes the verb to plane - to make flat. Aviation takes French planer "to glide, soar on a flat wing" plus aéro- to make aéroplane, clipped to plane in American English. The carpenter's tool, the mathematician's surface, and the airliner are thus cousins, not homonyms by accident.

  • Socio-cultural context: The plane marks the rise of finished furniture and panelled interiors. Rough-sawn boards suffice for barns; planed boards signal comfort, literacy (smooth writing desks), and mercantile wealth. The 18th-century cult of mahogany could not exist without trying planes capable of true surfaces.
  • Idiom and metaphor: To plane away means to smooth difficulties; a plain / plane pun runs through poetry (flat honesty vs smoothing). Inclined planes become one of the six simple machines in physics teaching, linking planes to levers and wedges in the same curriculum as the source list.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer's carpenter tools, Pepys's navy joinery accounts listing planes, Ruskin praising hand-planed surfaces against machine uniformity in The Stones of Venice, and modern hand-tool revivalists (James Krenov, Toshio Odate) celebrating the whisper-thin shaving as meditation.
  • Stone-age bridge: The source passage lists stone planes - flint or sandstone rubbers used to smooth wood or bone before metal blades existed - showing the function predates the Latin name. Archaeologists still call them planes by analogy.

From flat PIE expanse to Roman flat-iron to French joiner's pride to American airliner, planes proves how one flat adjective can plane the world smooth.
