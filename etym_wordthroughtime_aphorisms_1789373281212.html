- 16th Century (Renaissance) - Borrowed into English from French *aphorisme* and Late Latin *aphorismus*, initially referring strictly to clinical principles in the tradition of the Hippocratic *Aphorismoi*.
- Early 17th Century - Expanded into general philosophy and science by Francis Bacon in *The Advancement of Learning* (1605), who praised aphorisms as an open, inquiring method of presenting knowledge in contrast to rigid scholastic dogmas.
- Late 17th to 18th Century - Elevated into a prestigious literary and social art form under the influence of the French moralists (La Rochefoucauld, La Bruyère), becoming synonymous with polished reflections on human vanity and psychological hypocrisy.
- 19th Century - Reached radical philosophical heights with Friedrich Nietzsche, who used aphorisms as dynamic ideological weapons ("forms of eternity") to shatter systematic German metaphysics.
- 20th Century to Present - Celebrated as the ultimate vehicle of concentrated psychological insight and literary elegance (prominently within Marcel Proust, Franz Kafka, and Emil Cioran), while broadly describing any memorable, quotable philosophical truth.
