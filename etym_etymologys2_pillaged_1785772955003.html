1. Latin pilus 'a hair' (classical) — of debated further origin. No securely reconstructed PIE root is agreed: de Vaan records no convincing etymology for pīlus, though a possible link to Greek pílos 'felt, felted wool' and to a PIE base for hair-and-wool has been discussed. State explicitly: the deeper ancestry is uncertain, and the English word does not depend on it.
2. Latin pilāre 'to deprive of hair, to pluck, to peel, to make bare' (classical); in late and medieval Latin extended from 'strip of hair, skin, bark' to 'strip of goods, plunder, rob' — the family's semantic engine is 'removing a covering' → 'removing possessions'.
3. Old French piller 'to plunder, rob, pillage' (from the 12th c.); noun pille 'booty, spoil'.
4. Old French pillage 'plundering, pillage, booty' (12th–13th c.), formed on piller with the noun suffix -age.
5. Anglo-Norman / Middle English pillage (noun, 14th c.) and pillagen (verb) 'to plunder, spoil, strip of goods' — borrowed during the Franco-English warfare of the fourteenth century.
6. English pillage (noun and verb).
7. English pillaged — the regular past tense and past participle (pillage + -ed), the exact form on this card.

DEBATE NOTE: the transfer from 'hair-stripping' to 'robbery' is sometimes glossed by Roman practices of shearing and stripping captives, but the economical lexical account is simply that pilāre 'make bare' → 'strip of possessions'. Both accounts appear in the scholarship; the Romance specialization to robbery is secure from the Old French attestations.
