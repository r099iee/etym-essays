- 13th c.: 'matines' = the night office before dawn, the longest office of monastic life — ascetic
- Medieval lay piety: Books of Hours make 'Matins' (of the Virgin) the model morning prayer of the devout laity — devotional
- 1549 Reformation: Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer reshapes the dawn offices into Anglican 'Matins' (Morning Prayer), a public parish service — communal
- 17th–18th c.: 'matins' generalised for any morning worship; poetic extension to the dawn itself ('the matin bird') — literary
- 19th c.: the Tractarian (Oxford Movement) revival restores the technical liturgical sense — High Church
- 20th c. (Vatican II, 1970): the Roman rite renames matins the 'Office of Readings' and detaches it from dawn; 'Matins' survives in Anglican, traditionalist, and historical usage — specialist
- Today: 'matins' = morning office, dawn song, or metaphor for any habitual morning observance — literary and liturgical
