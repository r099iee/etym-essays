• Mash: Similar because 'mush' is a dialectal variant of 'mash' and both name crushed, softened matter; differs because 'mash' keeps the verb sense of crushing (mash the potatoes) and is the older word, while 'mush' is the food- and sentiment-word.
  • Porridge: Similar because both are soft boiled cereals; differs because porridge is the oatmeal gruel of Britain, while mush is the cornmeal porridge of America.
  • Pap: Similar because both are soft spoon-fed foods for the helpless; differs because pap is the bread-and-milk infant food, mush the cornmeal porridge, and 'pap' has its own anatomical 'breast' sense.
  • Gruel: Similar because both are thin soft cereal foods; differs because gruel is strained and watery, a food of the sick, while mush is a substantial staple.
  • Slush: Similar because both name soft, semi-liquid masses (of snow, mud, sentiment); differs because slush is specifically wet melting snow or crushed ice, and 'slushy' sentiment is tearful where 'mushy' is gushing.
  • Sludge: Similar because both are thick soft semi-solids; differs because sludge is sewage or industrial muck — dirty and inert — where mush can be food.
  • Pulp: Similar because both are crushed soft masses; differs because pulp is the technical word for crushed organic matter in paper-making and fruit-juicing.
  • Goo / Ooze: Similar because both name soft, formless, sticky masses; differs because goo and ooze are slangy and emphatically unappetising, while mush has a neutral or affectionate food sense.
  • Slop: Similar because both are soft semi-liquid messes; differs because slop is swill for animals or prisoners, mush is a legitimate (if plain) meal.
  • Mawkish: Similar because both condemn cloying softness of feeling; differs because mawkish is an adjective for nauseating sentimentality, while mush is the noun naming that sentiment.
  • Schmalz: Similar because both name excessive, gushing emotion in art; differs because schmalz (literally rendered chicken fat) is specifically kitsch emotionalism, and 'mush' keeps the food image of something too soft to chew.
  • Shaky: Similar because both describe instability; differs because shaky is the general word for tremor (hands, voices, ladders), while rickety specifies a loosely-jointed, about-to-collapse structure.
  • Wobbly: Similar because both describe unsteadiness; differs because wobbly is casual and implies loose rocking motion, while rickety implies weakness and imminent collapse.
  • Unsteady: Similar because both mean not firm; differs because unsteady applies to gait, hands, prices — anything lacking firmness — while rickety is concrete about weak joints.
  • Ramshackle: Similar because both describe badly built, dilapidated structures; differs because ramshackle stresses careless, patchwork construction, while rickety stresses the wobble of age and wear.
  • Tumbledown: Similar because both describe buildings near collapse; differs because tumbledown is about a whole ruin, while rickety can apply to a single wobbly chair.
  • Dilapidated: Similar because both mean fallen into disrepair; differs because dilapidated is the formal word for neglected buildings, without the wobble image.
  • Flimsy: Similar because both describe weak structures; differs because flimsy means thin, light and insubstantial by design, while rickety means worn, loose-jointed and unsafe.
  • Frail: Similar because both describe physical weakness; differs because frail applies to delicate bodies and health, while rickety implies a specifically skeletal, bony instability.
  • Tottering / Teetering: Similar because both describe movement at the edge of collapse; differs because these are words of the action of swaying, while rickety is the state of being loose-jointed.
  • Decrepit: Similar because both mean worn out by age; differs because decrepit describes the overall worn-out state of persons or things, while rickety picks out the wobbling joint.
