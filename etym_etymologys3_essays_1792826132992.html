The essay begins on a balance. Its deepest root is PIE *h₂eǵ-, to drive and lead — the verb of motion that gives Latin agere (to do, drive, act), ancestor of a vast family: agent, action, prodigal, exact. Prefixed with ex- (out), agere became exigere: to drive out, and hence to weigh and examine — the assayer drives the dross out of the metal to learn its worth. Late Latin nominalised the act as exagium, the weighing itself, then the standard weight, the set of scales — trying imagined as putting the world on a balance.

Old French wore the weight down to essai: a trial, a test, a sample of goods — the craftsman's test piece and the volunteer's first attempt in one handy noun. Borrowed into Middle English as both assay and essay, it worked first in mines and mints: to assay gold was to weigh its soul. Then, in 1580, Michel de Montaigne titled his wandering self-portraits Essais — Attempts — and Francis Bacon's Essays (1597) carried the new literary sense into English: short prose that attempts a subject rather than exhausting it. The laboratory kept the ass- spelling, literature the ess-, and the twins have spelled apart ever since while meaning one trying.

Stages of the journey:

  • PIE driving (before 2000 BCE): *h₂eǵ- moves herds, armies, and actions — doing as driving — across the daughter languages.
  • Latin action (1st millennium BCE): agere does and drives in law courts, farms, and legions; compounds multiply — exact, prodigal, ambassador's agency.
  • Weighing out (classical–late Latin): exigere (to weigh, test) and exagium (the balance, the standard) turn action into assay — trying as measuring.
  • French trial (12th–14th c.): essai is the test piece, the sample, the attempt — craftsmen's and volunteers' word alike.
  • English metals (14th–16th c.): assay/essay in mints and mines — gold essayed in the cupel, ores tried by fire.
  • Montaigne's invention (1580–1600s): the Essais make attempting a literary form — Bacon, Jonson, and a century of periodicals follow, and the schoolroom eventually inherits the genre.
  • Everywhere at once (18th c.–today): critical essays, photo essays, essay films, builders' essays in new crafts — and Tolkien's Elven-smiths, whose lesser rings were only essays in the craft: practice pieces, trifles to their makers, perils to mortals — the oldest sense gleaming through the newest mythology.

Attestation runs from mint indentures recording assays of sterling, through Montaigne's tower library and Bacon's counsels, through Addison's Spectators and Lamb's Elia, to the modern classroom's five-paragraph essay and the engineer's prototype essay. From balance to book to ring-forge, the word has kept its first modesty intact: an essay never claims to be the last word — only a weighed, honest try.
