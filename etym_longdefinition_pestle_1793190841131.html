Pestle is the club-shaped pounder that belongs with the mortar as oar belongs with boat: a heavy rounded implement — stone, hardwood, porcelain, or iron — grasped in the fist and driven down and around inside the bowl to crush, bruise, and blend whatever lies beneath it.

In the source sentence the word is the witch's driving oar: driving with her iron pestle — Baba Yaga standing in her iron mortar and propelling it through the forest with the giant pestle, rowing the air as a boatman rows water. The domestic partnership of bowl and pounder is thus turned into hull and oar, and the kitchen's quietest tool becomes an engine of flight.

Core implement sense in detail: A pestle is shaped for the hand and for impact at once: a thick rounded head for striking and grinding, narrowing to a grip, heavy enough that its own weight does half the work. The user pounds straight down to crack seeds and pills, then grinds in circles to reduce them to powder, the mortar's hollow holding the material against the blows. Surviving pestles run from Roman bronze to the modern pharmacist's porcelain and the Mexican tejolote that works the molcajete.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hand pounder used with a mortar (primary, from Middle English pestell): the crusher of pepper, pills, and pigment. The bridge is direct inheritance from Latin pistillum, the pounder, unchanged in office for two millennia.
  • Baba Yaga's driving staff (folktale extension): the iron pestle that rows the flying mortar. The bridge is shape and handling: a stout staff gripped in both hands already resembles an oar, so the tale needs only to launch the bowl.
  • Engineering and mill machinery termed pestle-like (technical analogy): stamp-mill pestles and pounding machinery described by analogy with the hand tool. The bridge is the vertical pounding action, mechanized but morphologically faithful.
  • Pestle-shaped anatomical and botanical structures (scientific metaphor): clubbed organs and processes described as pestle-shaped in older natural history. The bridge is pure form: anything knobbed at the working end borrows the kitchen name.
  • Figurative crushing force (literary): armies or arguments that pestle opposition down. The bridge carries the pounding action into abstraction, grinding resistance as grain is ground.
  • The verb pestle and pestling (derived action): to pound with a pestle, as cooks pestle garlic with salt. The bridge is the regular English conversion of tool into act, the implement generating its own verb.

Register and connotation: pestle is homely and honest — the smell of crushed basil, the pharmacist's counter, the laboratory bench — yet it carries a latent comedy of scale, because anything pounded small can be imagined pounding large. The folktale iron pestle plays exactly on this: the familiar kitchen club grown to the size of a tiller. Say the word and hear stone on stone, the oldest percussion in cookery, steady and unhurried and faintly soporific.
