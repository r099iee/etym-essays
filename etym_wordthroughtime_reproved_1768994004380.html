- 14th c. - the verb reprove enters English (Middle English repreven) with a double face: to censure or rebuke, but also - in theology and law - to disprove, refute, or convict an argument of error; it is a word of pulpits and courts.
- 15th–16th c. - the 'disprove/refute' sense flourishes in religious controversy: to reprove a heresy is to prove it false; the word carries the gravity of a formal verdict passed against an error.
- 17th–18th c. - the refutation sense fades and reprove hardens into the moral corrective: to rebuke with a view to amendment, distinct from mere anger; lexicographers gloss it as 'to check, admonish, blame'.
- 19th c. - reprove settles into the genteel register of parents, tutors, and clergymen - mild but firm correction, affectionate and authoritative; 'reproved' becomes the natural speech-tag of a calm superior correcting a naive listener.
- 20th–21st c. - now distinctly literary and old-fashioned; a modern speaker would say 'scolded' or 'called out', so choosing reprove today implies a deliberate, patrician mildness - the condescending patience of someone who corrects without ever raising his voice.
