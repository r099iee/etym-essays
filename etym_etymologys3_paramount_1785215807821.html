Essay 1: The first root — *per-, "through, forward, across." The first half of paramount is one of the most widely distributed roots in the entire Indo-European family. Proto-Indo-European *per- meant "through, forward, across, over," and its descendants are so numerous that they form a kind of map of European history. In Latin the root gave per, "through, by, over," the preposition of passage and agency, and a whole dynasty of derived words: per- as a prefix of completion ("per-fect," "through-done"), pro, "forward, before" (source of English pro-, prior, proceed), and the family of passage — porta, "gate," portus, "harbour," portāre, "to carry" — all built on the same idea of going through and over. In Greek the root appears as perí, "around, about," the source of English peri-, perimeter, and periphery; in Sanskrit as pári, "around, about," a common prefix in Hindi and other Indo-Aryan languages; and in Germanic as the ancestors of English fore-, for-, and from. Everywhere the root carries the sense of motion or extension across a space — passing through a valley, over a ridge, around an obstacle — and it is from this ancient sense of crossing that Latin per came to be used of agency and manner, "by means of."

The Romance languages inherited Latin per and in most of them it simplified, under the ordinary sound changes of the early Middle Ages, to par: Old French and Anglo-Norman par, "by, through," the ordinary preposition of instrument and passage. It is here, in the French of the eleventh to thirteenth centuries, that the first half of our word takes shape. The preposition par was prodigiously productive in Old French, where it formed dozens of compound prepositions and adverbs — par devant, "before," par après, "afterwards," par delà, "beyond" — each built on the same pattern of par plus a local adverb. Among these was par amont, literally "by the upward," the phrase meaning "above, at the top, over all." The word amont itself (from Latin ad montem, "to the mountain," i.e., up the slope) supplied the sense of upwardness, and par fixed the manner: par amont is "by the upward way," hence "situated above, over everything."

When the phrase passed into Anglo-Norman legal French — the language of the English law courts after 1066 — it fused into a single word, paramont, "above, over all," attested from the thirteenth century. The fusion is the point at which the two ancient roots, *per- and the root of "mountain," cease to be separate words and become a single instrument of hierarchy: par a mont, the prepositional phrase, becomes paramount, the adjective of supremacy. It is characteristic of the word's biography that its force is always relational — to be paramount is to stand over something — and that relationality is written into its very bones: the first root contributes the "over," the crossing, the position above; the second contributes the height, the mountain, the summit. Together they name not simply "great" but "over-topping," the thing that rises above everything else in its field.

Essay 2: The second root — *men- / *mon-, "to project, stand out," and the mountain. The second half of paramount is the ancient word for the mountain itself. Latin mōns, genitive montis, "mountain," descends from a Proto-Indo-European root of eminence and rising, traditionally reconstructed as *men- or *mon-, "to project, stand out, rise above," a root of prominence shared with words for towers, ridges, and heights. The derivation, though traditional, is not perfectly secure — modern scholarship notes that the Latin formation is not fully regular, and some authorities leave the ultimate root uncertain — but the semantic picture is clear: the mountain is, by definition, the thing that stands out above the land, the archetype of what projects upward from the earth. From Latin mōns the Romance languages inherit their ordinary words for the great heights: French mont, Italian monte, Spanish monte, and the English loanwords mount, mountain, mountaineer, montane, and the American place-name Montana, "mountainous." (Note that English mountain is a Latin-Romance loan, not an inherited Germanic word; the native Germanic stock had its own words for hills, and the Latin word arrived with the Norman vocabulary of land, law, and landscape.)

The decisive French development came with the phrase a mont, contracted from Latin ad montem, "to the mountain." In the logic of a river-valley landscape, the mountain was the natural image of "up": to go "to the mountain" was to go up, and so a mont / amont became a general adverb and preposition meaning "upward, above" — the opposite of aval, "downward" (from ad vallem, "to the valley"). Medieval French and Anglo-Norman speakers used amont of the upper reaches of a river, of the upward part of a hill, and, by extension, of anything situated above: en amont, "upstream, above." The word is a small fossil of a world in which orientation was read from the landscape — up the mountain, down the valley — rather than from an abstract coordinate system.

Joined to the preposition par in the phrase par amont (Essay 1), amont supplied the image of height, and the fused paramont carried both meanings into English in the thirteenth century: spatially "above, over all," and — by the transfer to human affairs that occupies the next chapter of the word's life — "supreme in rank, above all others." The mountain thus leaves its literal landscape and becomes the standing metaphor of the feudal and social summit: just as the peak overtops every other height, so the paramount thing overtops every other concern, and the paramount lord overtops every other lord. When Mill writes that Harriet Taylor's influence "became paramount," the ancient mountain is still faintly audible in the word — an influence that rises above all the other peaks of his mental life as the high peak rises above its foothills.
