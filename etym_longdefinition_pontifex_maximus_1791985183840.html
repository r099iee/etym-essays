Pontifex Maximus is a Latin title used unchanged in English: pontifex, “pontiff” or member of the priestly college, and maximus, “greatest”. Together they name the head of the collegium pontificum, the chief priest of the Roman state religion, an office held for life and vested with authority over the calendar, the laws of marriage and burial, the Vestal Virgins and the state cult. In the source context the phrase is used of Julius Caesar, elected to the office in 63 BCE, the year of Cicero's consulship and of the Catilinarian conspiracy.

In English the title has three reference points. It denotes the Roman office and its holder; it denotes the pope, who bears it in official titulature; and it is used jocularly of the dominant figure in a profession or field. The first sense is historical and classical, the second ecclesiastical and diplomatic, the third ironic and literary.

What the office was

  • Head of the college: the pontifex maximus presided over the collegium pontificum, a body that grew from a handful of members to fifteen under Sulla and sixteen under Caesar.
  • Religious jurisdiction: the college controlled the calendar and intercalation, the fasti, the rules of marriage (confarreatio), adoption (adrogatio), wills and burial, and supervised the Vestal Virgins, the flamens and the rex sacrorum.
  • Residence: the pontifex maximus lived in the Domus Publica on the Via Sacra, beside the Regia, the ancient cult centre traditionally attributed to Numa.
  • Tenure and election: the office was held for life; after the lex Domitia of 104 BCE the pontifex maximus was elected by the tribal assembly, whereas previously he had been co-opted by the college.
  • Political weight: the office was not a magistracy, but it gave its holder sacrosanctity, public authority and control of time itself — the calendar by which elections, courts and festivals ran.

Caesar's election and the imperial take-over

Caesar's campaign for the office in 63 BCE was one of the most expensive of his career; he is said to have borrowed vast sums and to have told his mother that he would return either as pontifex maximus or not at all. He defeated Quintus Lutatius Catulus and Publius Servilius Isauricus and held the office until his death in 44 BCE. It gave him a platform independent of the consulate and, after 46 BCE, the authority to reform the calendar with the help of Sosigenes. Augustus waited until Lepidus died in 12 BCE, then took the title, and from that point it was absorbed into the imperial package. The emperors appear as PONTIFEX MAXIMUS on coins and inscriptions for three centuries; Gratian, under Christian influence, refused the title in 382 CE, and it disappears from the imperial style.

From emperor to pope

The Latin word pontifex survived as a general term for a bishop, and summus pontifex became the standard Latin title of the bishop of Rome. The fuller Pontifex Maximus is common in Renaissance and modern papal usage — on coins, in inscriptions and in the Annuario Pontificio — and English “supreme pontiff” is its direct calque. Protestant polemic from the sixteenth century onward treated the title as proof that the papacy had inherited a pagan imperial office; the historical reality is more gradual, since the popes took over a late-antique ecclesiastical usage of pontifex rather than a single act of transfer. Either way, the phrase now has two living senses: the Roman priest and the pope.

English afterlife

  • Pontiff: the everyday English descendant, meaning the pope or, more generally, a bishop.
  • Pontifical: belonging to a pope or bishop; also, by a familiar semantic slide, pompous and dogmatic.
  • Pontificate: to officiate as a pontiff, hence to speak with an air of infallible authority — the most common modern use.
  • Jocular: “the pontifex maximus of the theatre” for the undisputed elder of a profession.

The two words

Pontifex is traditionally analysed as pons “bridge” plus -fex “maker”, hence “bridge-maker”; the -fex element is certain, the pons element and the semantic motivation are not. Maximus is the superlative of magnus “great”, built on the stem of magis “more” and ultimately on Indo-European *méǵh₂- “great”, source also of Greek mégas and Sanskrit mahā-. In the title it means “greatest in rank”, not “very large”: the pontifex maximus is the first among the pontiffs.

Register, connotation and collocations

The phrase is elevated, classical and slightly ceremonial. In historical writing it is neutral; in ecclesiastical writing it is formal and honorific; in journalism it is often ironic, as when a long-serving editor or conductor is called “the pontifex maximus of Fleet Street”. Typical collocations are “elected Pontifex Maximus”, “the office of Pontifex Maximus”, “the emperor as Pontifex Maximus”, “the pope's title of Pontifex Maximus”. The phrase is not used for mere experts: it implies institutional authority, often of a quasi-religious kind.

Why it matters in this reading

The passage places Caesar's election in 63 BCE, the year of Cicero's consulship and the Catilinarian conspiracy, and immediately links it to the coalition of 60 BCE. That sequence is the point: the priesthood was one of the assets Caesar brought to the alliance, alongside his consulship and his army. For a Roman politician the pontificate was not a pious accessory but a permanent seat at the centre of public life — a reminder that in Rome religion and politics were never separate departments.
