The history of INDULGENCE is a two-thousand-year record of how a word migrated from the imperial bureaucracy of Rome, through the sacramental economy of the medieval Church, into the polemics of the Reformation, and finally into the advertising of modern consumer capitalism. Every one of these stops left a fossil in the modern meaning, which is why the word today can mean a papal certificate, a coddled child, a chocolate bar, and a rhetorical plea for patience all at once.

Roman Origins

The chain begins with the Classical Latin verb indulgēre, "to be kind to, to yield to, to grant a favor, to pardon." Its precise origin is debated: the older claim that it is in- + dulcis ("sweet") is a folk etymology that modern handbooks reject; some scholars reconstruct an unattested root *dulgē- with the sense of "to be soft, to bend," but the evidence is thin. What is certain is the noun indulgentia, used by Cicero and the imperial administration for the clemency by which a magistrate remitted a punishment, a governor spared a province, or an emperor forgave a tax. Indulgentia was thus not indulgence but clemency - a favor flowing downward from power.

The Church and the Treasury of Merit

When the Roman Empire Christianized, the Church adopted this juridical vocabulary wholesale. By the High Middle Ages (11th-13th centuries) canon lawyers had built the doctrine of the thesaurus meritorum, the Treasury of Merit: the infinite surplus of good works earned by Christ and the saints could be drawn upon by the Pope, who could remit the temporal punishment - penance on earth or time in Purgatory - still owed for sins whose eternal guilt had been forgiven in confession. Early indulgences rewarded heroic acts: crusading, pilgrimage, almsgiving. Indulgence theology was refined at the universities (Thomas Aquinas and Boniface VIII's bull Unam sanctam and the Jubilee of 1300), and the word passed into English in the late 14th century through Old French, appearing in Chaucer's Pardoner's Tale, whose Pardoner hawks papal relics and indulgences to gullible pilgrims - already a satirical jab at religious greed.

The Reformation Rupture

In the late 15th century the papacy monetized the system to fund grand projects, above all the rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica under Pope Leo X. Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel's marketing - "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs" - provoked Martin Luther to post his 95 Theses in 1517, a direct assault on the sale and theology of indulgences. The Council of Trent (1545-1563) responded by reaffirming indulgences as doctrine while banning the abuses of sale. In Protestant England under Henry VIII and Elizabeth I the damage was permanent: indulgence became a byword for Romish corruption, superstition, and self-serving laxity. It was in this negative atmosphere that the word began to secularize, first describing the over-indulged passions and unruly children of the morally lax.

Secularization and Consumerism

In the 17th century the political sense flourished - Charles II's Declarations of Indulgence (1662, 1672) attempted to suspend the penal laws against religious dissenters, and a century later Joseph Addison, Samuel Johnson, and Jane Austen used the word for parental over-permissiveness and comfortable self-satisfaction in gentry life. During the 19th and 20th centuries, industrial advertising seized the word and inverted its moral charge: the indulgence that had once been a sin to be remitted became a reward to be earned, a rich dessert, a spa afternoon, a guilt-free luxury. Today the word is a small museum of European history, able to say clemency, corruption, coddling, and chocolate in a single breath.

Key Historical and Literary Milestones

  • 1st c. BCE: Latin indulgentia in Cicero and imperial inscriptions as a virtue of clemency.
  • c. 1380 (Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales): The Pardoner sells papal indulgences, satirizing religious greed in English.
  • 1300 (Boniface VIII): First papal Jubilee declares a plenary indulgence, institutionalizing the doctrine.
  • 1517 (Martin Luther, 95 Theses): Theological attack on the sale of indulgences triggers the Protestant Reformation.
  • 1545-1563 (Council of Trent): Reform of indulgence practice and prohibition of commercial sale.
  • 1672 (Charles II, Declaration of Indulgence): Political suspension of penal laws against dissenters.
  • 1813 (Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice): Parental coddling and emotional over-permissiveness in gentry households.
  • 20th-21st century: Consumer marketing repackages "indulgence" as an affordable luxury treat.
