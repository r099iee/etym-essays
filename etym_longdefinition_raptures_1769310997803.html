raptures (noun, plural): intense feelings or expressions of delight, ecstasy, or enthusiasm; the plural form of rapture (an overwhelming state of being carried away by joy, wonder, or love). Used especially in the set phrase "in raptures" ("she was in raptures over the music"), and in singular for both ecstatic joy and the theological event of being caught up to heaven. Primary sense
  • Intense pleasure or joy — raptures denotes transport of delight so strong that it feels like being seized or carried away: "The crowd was in raptures," "He spoke of his travels with raptures." The plural emphasizes repeated or overflowing bursts of ecstatic feeling and its audible expression — gasps, exclamations, enthusiastic praise.
  • In raptures (over/about) — the dominant idiom, used for someone utterly delighted by a work of art, a landscape, a meal, a performance: "She was in raptures over the garden." It sits between genuine ecstasy and (in modern, slightly ironic or hyperbolic use) theatrical enthusiasm.
Secondary and extended senses
  • The Rapture (Christian eschatology) — with the definite article and capital letter, the theological doctrine that at the end of time the faithful dead and living believers will be bodily caught up ("raptured") into the air to meet Christ, based on 1 Thessalonians 4:17 ("we shall be caught up together... to meet the Lord in the air"). This is the most famous modern use of the singular noun, and it directly preserves the original Latin meaning of violent seizing and carrying off.
  • Ecstatic transport (singular, literary/religious) — rapture as a state of mystical or aesthetic ecstasy in which the mind seems lifted out of itself: the rapture of the saints, the rapture of first love. This is the sense that bridges the earthly and the divine.
  • Archaic sense of seizure (rare/obsolete) — the oldest English sense, "a carrying off by force; abduction; seizure," preserved only in archaic or historical reference and in the theology above.
The semantic bridge (why rapture means joy)
  • Latin rapere means "to seize, snatch, or carry off by force." The core image is violent taking — a wolf seizing prey, a soldier carrying off plunder, a man abducting a woman (the "Rape of the Sabine Women," where "rape" meant capture).
  • Medieval Christian mysticism seized on this verb to describe the soul's experience of God: in ecstasy the soul is "rapt," snatched out of itself, carried up to heaven against its own weight. Saint Paul's being "caught up to the third heaven" (2 Corinthians 12:2) was the biblical warrant.
  • From the sacred, the word leaked into the profane. By the seventeenth century rapture names any feeling so strong that it seems to seize the heart — delight in music, beauty, love. The violent Latin verb is sublimated into the highest joy: to be in raptures is to be carried off by happiness itself.
Usage, register, and nuance
  • Raptures is high-literary and emotional in register; it is at home in Austen, in Romantic poetry, and in elegant prose, and it now often carries a slightly hyperbolic or playful tone ("she was in raptures") when used of ordinary enthusiasm.
  • The plural is preferred for the act of expressing delight (cries of rapture are emitted in bursts), while the singular is used for the state or the theological event.
  • The word is a close cousin of ecstasy (Greek ek-stasis, "standing outside oneself") but keeps the distinct image of being taken, seized, carried — an image that makes raptures feel involuntary, overwhelming, and almost passive before the overwhelming object of delight.
