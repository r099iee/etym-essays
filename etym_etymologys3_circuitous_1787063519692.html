Origins: two Indo-European roots in one word

Circuitous is a learned English adjective, coined in the 17th century from the Latin noun circuitus “a going around, a circuit”, with the English adjectival suffix -ous. Because Latin circuitus is itself a compound — circum “around” plus īre “to go” — the word ultimately rests on two distinct Proto-Indo-European roots: *kʷel- “to turn, revolve, move around” (the source of the “circle” half) and *h₁ey- “to go” (the source of the “go” half). The two halves travelled separate paths through the ancient languages before fusing in the classical Latin verb circumīre.

The “around” half: *kʷel- → circus → circum

The Indo-European root *kʷel- “to turn, revolve” is one of the family's great “wheel” roots: it produced Greek kýklos “circle, wheel” (whence English cycle), Sanskrit cakra “wheel” (chakra), and Old English hwēol (wheel). The Greek word kýklos passed into Latin as circus — “ring, circle, arena” — first for the round race-tracks and arenas of Greek and Etruscan tradition, and later for the Circus Maximus and the imperial games. From the noun circus, Latin formed the adverb and preposition circum “around, about” (strictly the accusative of the noun, used adverbially). Circum then became a hugely productive prefix in Latin, appearing in circumdare “to surround”, circumferre “to carry around”, circumspicere “to look around” (whence circumspect), and, decisively for us, circumīre.

The “go” half: *h₁ey- → īre

Latin īre “to go” descends from Proto-Indo-European *h₁ey- “to go”, the root also behind Greek eîmi “I shall go” and, in many Indo-European languages, the ordinary words for “go” and “way”. In Latin the root produced one of the language's most irregular but most frequent verbs: eō, īre, iī, itum. Its participle itus and its compounds are everywhere — exīre “to go out” (whence English exit), perīre “to go through, to perish”, obīre “to go to meet” — and the same root surfaces in English borrowings like initiate, transition, and transit. The fusion of circum “around” and īre “to go” gives circumīre “to go around, to encircle, to evade”, and from that verb Latin formed the noun circuitus — “a going around, a circuit, a revolution” — with the suffix -tus.

Latin stage: circuitus

Circuitus (from circum + itus, past participle of īre) is fully attested in classical Latin. It names a circuit in several technical senses: the orbit of a heavenly body (the “circuit” of the planets), the circumference or perimeter of a place, a tour or patrol that goes around, and — in rhetoric — a roundabout period or a circuitous way of speaking. This rhetorical sense is remarkable: already in antiquity, circuitus could describe a way of expressing oneself that goes around the point. The seed of the modern “circuitous argument” is thus present in Latin itself. Latin also had circuitiō, “a going around”, and an adjectival use of the participle circuitus, -a, -um “wound around, encircled”.

English adoption: 17th century

English took the noun circuit from Old French circuit (from Latin circuitus) in the 14th century, with meanings ranging from the legal “circuit” of a judge's travel to the electrical and electronic “circuit” of modern times. The adjective circuitous was formed in English — from Latin circuitus + -ous — around 1646. Early use is literal and learned, describing the roundabout paths of travel and natural phenomena. In the 18th and 19th centuries the word extends to argument and reasoning; in the 20th it extends to process and procedure, and it acquires its mildly critical flavour of wastefulness and evasion.

Phonetic and orthographic notes

  • English circuit and circuitous keep the Latin circui- spelling but pronounce it /sɜːkjuː-/ — the second i having been absorbed into a /juː/ glide, the standard English reflex of Latin iu after circ-.
  • The stress pattern of circuitous (/sɜːˈkjuː.ɪ.təs/) differs from that of the noun circuit (/ˈsɜː.kɪt/): the adjectival suffix -ous pulls the stress onto the syllable before it.
  • The word has four syllables and no silent letters in the standard pronunciation, though the -ous ending reduces to /əs/.

Debated and nuanced points

  • The exact route by which Greek kýklos entered Latin as circus is not fully documented; transmission via Etruscan is often assumed, and the forms show the characteristic Etruscan-to-Latin treatment of Greek k. Some scholars posit a shared Italic borrowing rather than a direct Greek-to-Latin transfer.
  • Since both *kʷel- and *h₁ey- are reconstructed roots, their precise phonological shape (especially the laryngeal and the labio-velar) is a matter of comparative reconstruction, but the meanings “turn” and “go” are secure and uncontested.
