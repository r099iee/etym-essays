Demure is a word whose whole history is about staying put. It begins with an Indo-European root reconstructed as *mer-, 'to delay, hinder, hold back', which Latin turned into mora, 'a delay', attested from the third and second centuries BCE in the comic playwrights Plautus and Terence, and from mora came the deponent verb moror, morārī, 'to delay, tarry, linger' — the posture of someone or something that does not hurry on. Latin then prefixed the intensive or directional dē- to form dēmorārī, 'to tarry, linger, remain, stay behind', a verb used in classical prose of persons lingering in a place, of souls remaining, of matters remaining pending. This is the trunk of the family; the semantic journey of the adjective is the gradual conversion of a verb of lingering into an adjective of composed quiet.

As the spoken Latin of Gaul became French, dēmorārī contracted and weakened into Old French demeurer, 'to stay, remain, dwell', already attested in the ninth-century Sequence of Saint Eulalia, one of the earliest monuments of the French language; for a millennium the verb has been the ordinary French word for remaining and living somewhere. The adjective in English, however, came through the Anglo-French of the English law courts and administration, the dialect of French spoken in England after the Norman Conquest. The past participle of demurer in this Anglo-French was demure, and from the notion of a person "settled" — staying put, not moving or rushing — the participle slid toward "settled in mood, composed, staid, grave". English borrowed it in the late fourteenth century (Chaucer's age) as an adjective meaning 'sober, sedate, grave', applied to grave looks and composed behavior. The OED notes the possibility that the English form was also shaped by a near-homophone, Old French meür, 'ripe, mature', which would have reinforced the sense of a settled, finished, self-possessed person — a scholarly debate about a single vowel that has no bearing on the main line of descent.

The shift of meaning across the sixteenth century is the crucial one. Where the earlier word meant simply 'grave, sober, composed', by the seventeenth century it had specialized toward the modest, quiet, self-effacing behavior idealized — especially in women — by polite society: the demure maiden who kept her eyes lowered, her voice soft, and her dress modest. The semantic bridge is stillness: the person who lingers, who stays put, who does not push forward, became the person of composed modesty, and modesty became a gendered ideal of decorum. Sound changes along the way were modest — the Old French de- prefix settled to modern English /dɪ-/, and the final vowel weakened, giving the modern pronunciation /dɪˈmjʊə/ — while the spelling standardized as demure, and the word family grew a new branch: the noun demureness and the adverb demurely.

The nineteenth century added the ironic turn. As ideals of feminine modesty became more theatrical, demure began to be used of a modesty that was studied — a demure smile that conceals a bold thought, a demure question that is really a challenge — and this knowing, playful register now sits alongside the sincere one. The late twentieth and twenty-first centuries added two more layers. In feminist-influenced commentary, the word is often examined critically, as the label of a socially imposed quietness; at the same time the 2024 viral phrase "very demure, very mindful", from creator Jools Lebron, repurposed the old adjective as a badge of composed, understated, mindful self-presentation — workplace polish worn as a deliberate aesthetic, spread across TikTok and Instagram in billions of views. It is a striking end to a long history: the Latin verb for lingering, which became the English adjective for staying quiet, has been reborn in the digital age as an aspirational style. The same family, meanwhile, sent demur (to object, to delay) down a separate branch, so that modern English keeps both the modest adjective and the objecting verb as doublets from one root — a root whose oldest meaning, 'delay, hold back', is still faintly audible in both.
