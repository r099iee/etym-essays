POSITS is the ordinary third-person singular present of the English verb posit, a learned borrowing from the Latin past-participial stem posit- of pōnere “to place, put, set down”. The chain: 1) PIE *apo- “off, away, from” (reconstructed; a preverb and adverb of separation, reflected in Sanskrit ápa “away, off”, Greek apó “from, away”, Latin ab/ā “away from”, and Germanic *af-, Old English of “off, from”) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) PIE *seh₁- “to let go, release, leave” (reconstructed; the verbal base underlying Latin sinere “to let, allow, leave”; the reconstruction of the root is debated — some scholars set *sē(i)- “to let, to release” — but the Latin stem itself is secure) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 3) Latin *po- “away, off” (the reduced form of the ablative prefix seen in Old Latin composition, related to ab “away from”) fused with sinere “to leave, let” to form Old Latin *posinere “to put down, set, place” (the compound’s sense: to set something in place and leave it there) — attested in the archaic Latin period. 4) Classical Latin pōnere “to put, place, set, lay down; appoint; pitch (camp)” (the standard verb of placement, contracted from *posinere; attested from the earliest Classical Latin) — 3rd–2nd c. BCE. 5) Latin positus “placed, set, situated” (past participle of pōnere; the supine stem posit- is the base of position, positive, deposit, expose, impose, propose, and of the English verb itself) — attested in Classical Latin. 6) English posit “to set down as a premise, to assume as true for the sake of argument; to place” (verb, borrowed from the Latin participle stem in the mid-17th century; a scholarly Latinism entering through philosophical and scientific writing) — attested from c. 1640s. 7) English posits “assumes, puts forward (a premise)” (third-person singular present, formed by the regular suffix -s on the verb) — POSITS. NOTE ON DEBATE: The Latin stages (pōnere < *posinere < *po- + sinere) are standard and well attested. The only genuinely debated point is the PIE root behind Latin sinere: the traditional reconstruction is *seh₁- “to let go, release”, but some authorities prefer a different laryngeal set or set *sē(i)-, and the exact pre-Latin shape of sinere is not fully resolved. The English side of the chain — from Latin positus to the mid-17th-century loan posit and its inflected form posits — is fully attested and uncontroversial.
