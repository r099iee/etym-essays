The biography of senate begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *sen-, whose core meaning is simply "old, aged." That root is attested across the family: Sanskrit sana- "old," Greek formations built on hénos (aged, last year's produce), Gothic sineigs "old," Old Irish sen "old," and — decisively for our word — Latin senex (genitive senis) "old man." The root's prestige rests on an ancient cultural assumption shared by Indo-European-speaking societies: age is the natural basis of authority, counsel, and memory. To be old is to have seen more, and the one who has seen more should advise the community. This equation of age with authority is written into the very structure of the word.

Latin exploited the root richly:
  • senex "old man" produced senior "older, elder" (source of English senior, French seigneur, Spanish señor, Italian signore — the "elder" who is also a lord), senectus "old age," senilis "of old age" (English senile), and the verb senēscere "to grow old."
  • From senex Latin formed senātus — literally "council of elders" — with the abstract/institutional suffix -ātus, the same suffix that gives magistrātus "magistracy." The senate was thus by its very name an institution of seniority.
  • The member was senātor "senator," and the body's decrees were senātūs consulta; from these come English senator, senatorial, and the learned loan senatus consultum.
In early Rome the senate's legitimacy was not democratic representation but accumulated age, rank, and memory. Members were typically former magistrates drawn from the patrician and later the noble plebeian elite; their collective decrees (senātūs consulta) guided policy even when popular assemblies held formal sovereignty. Under the Republic the senate became the real steering committee of the state — managing foreign policy, finance, and military commands — so that "the senate and the Roman people" (SPQR) named the whole political order. Livy's and Cicero's accounts give the institution its lasting literary face: a body of gray-headed patres ("fathers") whose weight of experience outweighed the votes of the crowd.

The institutional and semantic afterlife under the Empire is what Gibbon exploits. Emperors still sought senatorial ratification, still addressed the senate, still used its language of majesty, yet the real levers of force lay with the army and the imperial household. The word's reference stayed fixed while its substance changed: the name continued to signal supreme deliberative dignity even when power had moved elsewhere. It is precisely this gap — name and appearance on one side, force and reality on the other — that makes "the senate appeared to possess the sovereign authority" a sentence of political irony rather than description.

The medieval transmission carried the model abroad:
  • Latin senātus passed into Old French as senat, and into Middle English as senat / senate by the 14th century, carrying both the Roman historical sense and a transferable model for any high deliberative chamber.
  • The Renaissance and Enlightenment re-activated the Roman model: Venice's Senato, the parlements and "senates" of early modern republics, and finally the United States Senate (framed 1787) made the word a living constitutional label rather than a relic. The American founders deliberately chose "senate" precisely to import the idea of a stabilizing council of elders and experience.
  • The academic senate of universities extends the same logic into learning — a council of senior scholars legislating for an institution, the intellectual analogue of the elders' council.
Modern English keeps the classical resonance ("the Roman Senate") alongside constitutional reality (the U.S. Senate), while the adjective "senatorial" and the abstract "senatorial dignity" preserve the root's equation of age, rank, and counsel. Across every transfer — from Rome to Venice to Washington to the faculty meeting — the word has carried one stubborn idea: that the experienced and the senior should deliberate for the whole. That is the PIE assumption of *sen- made institution, empire, and constitution.
