Handcarts, plural of handcart, are small carts moved by human hands rather than animals or engines: a flat bed or box on two wheels with shafts or handles, pushed or pulled by one or two people carrying market stock, luggage, parcels, or garden loads. They are the step up from the wheelbarrow, bigger, steadier, and built for the road rather than the flowerbed.

In the source sentence the road to the gate is blocked with barrows and handcarts, and the pairing sketches a village logistics chain: garden barrows for soil and compost alongside larger road-going handcarts borrowed for crates, chairs, presents, and party furniture. Bag End's lane has become a loading bay, and the plural tells us half the Shire has lent its wheels to the birthday preparations.

Core meaning in detail: A typical handcart has two relatively large wheels ( steadier than a wheelbarrow's one), a flat load-bed with low rails, and two pull-shafts or push-handles; the loader balances the weight over the axle so one person can haul a hundred kilos or more. Unlike a barrow it is not tipped to empty but unloaded by hand, and unlike a wagon it needs no horse, which is why it belongs to porters, hawkers, station staff, and removal men — everyone whose freight is too big for shoulders but too small for engines.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Two-wheeled hand-pushed goods carts (primary modern sense, from 17th c.): market handcarts, station handcarts, the coster's barrow-cart. The bridge is composition itself: hand plus cart, a cart scaled to hands, and the source sentence shows the classic use, borrowed wheels blocking a lane before a feast.
  • Street-vendor and hawker carts (commercial sense): handcarts of fruit, flowers, and hot chestnuts, licensed pitches on wheels. The bridge is trade without premises: the handcart is the cheapest shop in town, and whole streets in every city are named for the trade it made possible.
  • Railway, station, and warehouse handcarts (industrial sense): porters' platform trolleys, mail-room floats, builders' handcarts. The bridge is freight's first and last mile: even in the age of trains and lorries, parcels begin and end on a handcart pushed by someone in a uniform.
  • Golf and sports handcarts (leisure sense, 20th c.): two-wheeled pull-carts for golf bags. The bridge is miniaturisation: the same axle-and-handles logic shrunk to carry one bag around eighteen holes, the caddie's work made democratic.
  • Child's and toy handcarts (domestic sense): the red wagon of the driveway, the festival handcart full of picnics. The bridge is play as apprenticeship: children haul exactly what porters haul, only smaller and happier.
  • Punishment and procession carts (historical): the tumbril-cart of legend, dung-carts and night-soil carts of old towns. The bridge is civic labour at its lowest: the handcart has always done the jobs no one celebrates, and proverbs about being put in the cart remember it.

Register and connotation: handcarts sounds industrious and slightly old-fashioned — porters in caps, market mornings, village fêtes — honest manual work before the van arrives. It carries no glamour but much goodwill: the handcart is the vehicle of helpers, lenders, and neighbours, and a lane blocked with handcarts reads as community effort, not traffic failure. Modern logistics parks still push them daily under newer names, proof the shape outlives the word.
