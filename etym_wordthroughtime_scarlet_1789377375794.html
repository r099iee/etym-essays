- 11th to 12th Century - Borrowed into Western Europe via the Crusades from Old French *escarlate* (from Persian *saqirlāt* / Arabic *siqillāt*), designating an extraordinarily costly, opulent luxury broadcloth (irrespective of color) prized among royalty.
- 13th to 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Specializes almost exclusively to fine woollen fabrics dyed with expensive kermes insect dye; the word's meaning decisively transfers from the textile weave to the radiant orange-red color itself.
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor & Reformation Era) - Adopted into English biblical translations (Tyndale, Coverdale, Cranmer, Geneva) to translate Hebrew *tola'at shani* and Greek *kokkinos*, immortalizing scarlet as the color of imperial majesty, clerical pride, and catastrophic moral sin (*"though your sins be as scarlet"*).
- 17th to 18th Century - Institutionalized in the British Army as the signature uniform color of the infantry regiments ("redcoats"); simultaneously applied in clinical medicine to *scarlet fever* (*scarlatina*) due to its characteristic rash.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely used in novelistic literature (Jane Austen, Charlotte Brontë, Charles Dickens, Oscar Wilde) to describe intense emotional blushing, shame, or fury; permanently canonized in American literature by Nathaniel Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* (1850).
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains prestigious cultural, liturgical, and aesthetic resonance, celebrated across fashion, high-end cosmetics, automotive design, and sports iconography as the quintessential color of passionate intensity.
