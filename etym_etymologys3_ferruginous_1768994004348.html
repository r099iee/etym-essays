The biography of ferruginous begins not in Indo-European speech but in a gap. The Latin noun ferrum, "iron," has no credible PIE ancestor: every surrounding Indo-European language coined its iron-word independently — Greek sidēros (of uncertain origin), Sanskrit ayas, Germanic īsarn (which became English "iron" and German "Eisen"), Celtic isarnon — but Latin's term stands alone, and scholars take it to be a loanword absorbed during Rome's early centuries, likely from Etruscan or another Mediterranean substrate that supplied iron technology along with its name. The early attestations (Plautus, Ennius) show ferrum already fully Roman, its foreign origin faded beyond recovery. This rootless start matters for the whole word-family: ferruginous descends from a name whose deepest past is, frankly, unknown, and any responsible account says so plainly.
  • From iron to rust (Latin): from ferrum the Romans grew a small orchard of derivatives, and the first and most fateful was ferrugo (genitive ferruginis): the reddish-brown tarnish that iron wears — rust. The Romans saw rust as a disease of metal and of crops alike: ferrugo was also a rust-fungus attacking grain, so that the same word served the corrosion of metal and the blight of wheat. The colour sense, "rust-red," was fixed early and appears in the poets describing bronze and armour tarnished to a dusky red.
  • The adjective (Late Latin): Late Latin shaped ferruginosus, "rusty, rust-coloured, iron-bearing," by the ordinary suffix -osus ("full of"). This is the direct parent of the English word, and it already carried the double meaning — composition and hue — that English inherited intact.
  • Adoption in English (17th century): English took ferruginous in the middle of the seventeenth century (first recorded c. 1661), chiefly through geological and medical writers. Ferruginous earths, ferruginous springs (mineral waters charged with iron), and ferruginous sandstone entered the vocabulary of natural philosophy at the very moment when mineralogy and medicine were becoming empirical sciences.
  • Scientific spread (18th–19th centuries): mineralogy kept the word for iron-stained strata and crusts; biology adopted it for plumage and pelage — the Ferruginous Hawk, ferruginous ducks — where it now names not composition but the specific dusky reddish-brown of iron oxide; medicine found "ferruginous bodies" lodged in the lungs, the mineral dust that fibrous breathing deposits and that ironizes the tissues. That medical resonance is exactly what the story's warning exploits: "a lungful of that ferruginous dust" means a breath that deposits rust inside a man.
  • Semantic drift documented: the crucial shift is the colour detaching from the metal. A ferruginous hawk contains no iron; it merely looks rust-coloured. Over three centuries the word moved from "full of iron" toward "coloured like iron rust," so that today its commonest use in biology names appearance, while its commonest use in geology still names composition — the two senses braided by the visible fact that rust is what iron does.
  • The same root elsewhere: ferrum's other English offspring show how the family divided: ferrous and ferric (the two oxidation states of modern chemistry), ferriferous ("iron-bearing"), ferreous ("of iron," now archaic and figurative), farrier (a horse-shoer, via Old French ferrier from Latin ferrarius "blacksmith"), and ferro- compounds (ferroconcrete, ferromanganese). Romance kept the metal itself: Italian ferro, French fer, Spanish hierro, Portuguese ferro, Romanian fier — and family names such as Ferrari, Ferreira, and Hierro preserve the old word to this day.
In the literary sentence of the passage the whole history converges. A PIE-less loanword entered Latin as the name of the commonest metal; its derivative ferrugo named the tarnish; the adjective ferruginosus fused rust and rust-colour; and English science carried that fusion into the mineral dust of an imaginary Mars, where a lungful of it reads as a lungful of corroding metal. The word's staying power lies precisely in that fusion: it remains one of the few adjectives in the language that cannot fully separate a colour from its chemistry, and the Martian storm is a perfect test-case — dust that is not merely red, but red in the way that iron itself decays.
