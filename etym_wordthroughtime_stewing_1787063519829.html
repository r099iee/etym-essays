- Late Middle English stewen "to bathe, take a hot bath" — from Old French estuver, "to stew, bathe in a hot room."
- 14th–15th century — shifts to the culinary sense of simmering or braising food in liquid.
- 16th century — the noun stew names both the dish and the hot, crowded (later disreputable) bath-house; "stews" come to mean brothels in Tudor slang.
- 17th–18th century — the figurative sense emerges: to "stew" over one's troubles, simmering like food in a pot.
- 19th–20th century — the idiom "stew in one's own juice" becomes fixed, and stewing takes its modern double life as cooking and as anxious brooding.
