The historical lineage of signification traces a profound arc through Western intellectual history, beginning with physical carvings on stone and boundary markers, advancing through Roman civic law and rhetoric, developing into scholastic theology, and culminating in modern semiotic theory. At its ultimate root lies the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *sek-, signifying ‘to cut, sever, or notch’ (the same ancient root that gave Germanic languages saw, scythe, and sickle, and Latin secāre, ‘to cut’). In Proto-Italic culture, an identifying mark or boundary indication was literally something incised, notched, or hacked into the bark of a tree or the face of a stone marker; through the addition of the nominal formant -no-, this produced Proto-Italic *sek-no-, which developed into Latin signum. A signum was therefore originally a tangible, carved token: a military standard carried before a Roman legion, a personal seal pressed into wax, a celestial constellation marked out in the night sky, or a distinctive badge worn by an individual.

During the Roman Republic, the need for expressive compound verbs led Roman speakers to fuse signum with facere (‘to make, do’, from PIE *dʰeh₁-, ‘to set, put’), creating the verb significāre, literally ‘to make a sign, to signal, to indicate, or to intimate’. From this verb, Roman writers formed the abstract verbal noun of action significātiō using the productive suffix -tiō. In Classical Latin oratory and philosophy—most notably in the rhetorical writings of Cicero and Quintilian—significātiō assumed two complementary roles: first, the literal semantic meaning or force of a spoken word (the sense attached to vocal sound), and second, a subtle rhetorical figure in which more is suggested to the listener than is openly expressed in words (a deliberate intimation or pregnant hint). Simultaneously, in Roman public life, significātiō referred to an overt demonstration of popular feeling, such as the rhythmic clapping, cheers, or shouts with which an assembly greeted a popular general or magistrate.

  • Classical Antiquity (1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE): Latin significātiō flourishes in Roman rhetoric, jurisprudence, and grammar. In Justinian’s digests, it denotes the legal intent or plain meaning of statutory phrasing and testamentary bequests.
  • Patristic and Scholastic Era (5th–13th c.): Saint Augustine, in De Doctrina Christiana, establishes the Christian philosophy of signs, defining a sign as something that causes another concept to enter the mind beyond the impression it makes on the senses; scholastic thinkers like Thomas Aquinas and William of Ockham expand significatio into the cornerstone of medieval semantics, distinguishing between the sign, the mental concept, and the external thing.
  • Anglo-Norman Transmission into Middle English (late 14th c.): The word entered English following the Norman Conquest via Old French significacion. It is documented in Middle English literature around 1380–1400 in the writings of Geoffrey Chaucer (e.g., The House of Fame and The Parson’s Tale) and John Wyclif’s biblical translations, where it regularly denoted celestial omens, prophetic dreams, and the spiritual symbolism of Christian sacraments.
  • Empiricist Epistemology (17th–18th c.): In Book III of John Locke’s monumental An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), ‘Of Words’, Locke placed signification at the very center of British philosophy: words, he argued, have no natural or intrinsic connection to physical objects; rather, their signification is purely arbitrary, established by social convention to stand as outward signs of internal ideas.
  • Victorian Aesthetic and Natural Theology (19th c.): Writers such as John Ruskin applied signification to the sensory world. In Modern Painters (1843–1860), Ruskin insisted that the physical universe is an intentional aesthetic communication whose ‘signification’ must be read by the painter and critic alongside its physical laws and emotional impact.
  • Twentieth-Century Semiotics: Ferdinand de Saussure’s Course in General Linguistics (1916) re-engineered the term as French signification, defining it as the dynamic structural value generated by the union of the signifier (sound-image) and signified (mental concept), sparking the structuralist and semiotic movements of Barthes, Lévi-Strauss, and Eco.

Phonologically, the word travelled from Classical Latin /sɪŋ.nɪ.fɪˈkaː.ti.oː/ (where the velar nasal [ŋ] preceded [n]) into Old French /siɲ.ni.fi.kaˈsjõ/, shedding the hard velar stop in favor of a palatal nasal. When borrowed into Middle English, the French spelling was adopted, but English speakers restored the Latinate velar stop /ɡ/, yielding late Middle English /sɪɡ.nɪ.fɪ.kaːˈsjuːn/. Following the Great Vowel Shift of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the long /aː/ in the penultimate syllable underwent diphthongization to /eɪ/, and the final unstressed syllables /-sjʊn/ palatalized smoothly into the modern English suffix /-ʃən/. Today, the Received Pronunciation /ˌsɪɡ.nɪ.fɪˈkeɪ.ʃən/ preserves its dignified four-syllable cadence, bearing the distinct imprint of four millennia of semantic refinement: from an incised cut on a primitive boundary stone to the highest reaches of philosophical analysis.
