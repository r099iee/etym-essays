To understand bid one must first accept that modern English bid is not the descendant of a single word but the accidental merger of two Old English verbs whose forms happened to converge. The story therefore has two trunks and one crown, and every sense of the modern word is a twig of one trunk or the other.

Trunk one: the offerer. The first ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰewdʰ-, “to be awake, to perceive, to observe”, with its derived sense “to make known, to announce”. This root is rich in Indo-European descendants: Sanskrit bodhati “wakes, perceives”, Greek peúthomai “to learn, to hear”, Old Church Slavonic bljusti “to watch, guard”, and Gothic biudan “to offer, command, proclaim”. In Proto-Germanic the root produced the strong verb *beudaną, “to offer, proclaim, command” (class II), whose descendants include Old Norse bjóða “to offer, invite”, German bieten “to offer” (with befehlen “to command” from a prefixed form), and Old English bēodan. The semantic core is “to make known, to put forward”: to offer, to announce, and from announcing, to command. The sound changes are the classic workings of Grimm's Law: the PIE voiced aspirate *bʰ becomes Germanic *b, and *dʰ becomes *d, so *bʰewdʰ- yields *beud-; the root then inflects as a class II strong verb with its characteristic ablaut (Old English bēodan, bēad, budon, boden).

Trunk two: the asker. The second ancestor is the PIE root *bʰedʰ-, “to bend”. The semantic path is a gesture: one bends oneself before another in supplication, and so the root comes to mean “to ask, to entreat”. The Proto-Germanic verb *bidjaną, “to ask, entreat, pray”, yields Gothic bidjan, Old Norse biðja, Old High German bitten (modern German bitten “to ask, request”), and Old English biddan. The derivation of *bidjaną from “bend” is the standard modern view — argued in detail by Ringe and Kroonen — though some handbooks still mark it as uncertain; the Germanic forms themselves are beyond dispute. Old English biddan was a class V strong verb (biddan, bæd, bǣdon, beden) meaning “to ask, entreat, pray”, and its verbal noun gebed “prayer” is the ancestor of modern bead — the rosary bead is literally a “prayer”.

The collision. In Middle English the two verbs — bēodan and biddan — fell together as bēden / bidden, sharing a single paradigm and eventually a single spelling, bid. The merger is the reason one English verb can mean both “I bid you farewell” (announce) and “bid him come” (command, from bēodan), and both “bid the bride” (invite, ask, from biddan) and “bid one's beads” (pray). The two past forms bade and bid still coexist uneasily in the same verb, and the participle bidden survives alongside the leveled bid.

The auction and the attempt. The commercial sense — “to offer a price” — was present in embryo in bēodan “to offer”, and is attested in English from the 13th century; auctions and markets gave it its permanent home. From the 19th century the noun bid escaped the auction room entirely and came to mean any determined competitive attempt: a bid for power, a bid for the presidency, a takeover bid. The card table contributed the bridge sense, in which players “bid” the tricks they contract to take — an auction structure embedded in the game itself, complete with overbids and underbids.

Literary life. The literary career of the word is long and distinguished. The King James Bible uses bid, bade and bidden constantly for divine and royal command, giving the word an archaic grandeur that later writers exploit deliberately. Shakespeare's characters bid one another farewell, good night and defiance. In the 20th century, Tolkien's prose — including the source sentence, “we bid him consider well the claims that have been urged” — reaches for the same antique register, deploying the command sense as a deliberate archaism in the mouth of an envoy. Meanwhile the prayer sense, once the property of every English churchgoer, survives only in the “bidding prayer”, in dialect, and in the ghost of bead. Modern English thus keeps, in a single four-letter word, a fossil record of announcing, commanding, offering, asking, inviting, praying, greeting and competing.
