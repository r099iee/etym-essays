• a stone’s cast: Similar because it names the identical thrown-stone short distance, often in older or biblical style; differs because a stone’s cast sounds archaic and scriptural, while a stone’s throw is the living everyday idiom.
  • spitting distance: Similar because both measure nearness by a bodily projectile and mean very close; differs because spitting distance is coarser, closer, and more colloquial, suggesting crowded confrontation rather than a comfortable stroll.
  • shouting distance: Similar because both define nearness by what the body can do across it, hearing versus throwing; differs because shouting distance stresses being able to call out and be heard, while a stone’s throw stresses being able to walk there in a moment.
  • earshot: Similar because both describe a small interval judged by the unaided senses; differs because earshot is strictly about hearing range and often figurative, while a stone’s throw is about walkable ground distance.
  • a short walk: Similar because both promise nearness on foot; differs because a short walk is literal and prosaic, giving travel time, while a stone’s throw is idiomatic and pictorial, giving an image of flight.
  • close by: Similar because both place something in the immediate neighbourhood; differs because close by is plain and colourless direction language, while a stone’s throw adds rustic texture and a sense of measurable ground.
  • next door: Similar because both mean practically adjacent; differs because next door claims shared walls or fences, while a stone’s throw allows a field, a road, or a screen of trees between.
  • around the corner: Similar because both reassure that something is near and easy to reach; differs because around the corner hides the goal just out of sight beyond a bend, while a stone’s throw usually keeps it visible across open ground.
