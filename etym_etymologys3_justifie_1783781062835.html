Justifie is a compound, and its biography is really two biographies joined at the seam: the story of just (from Latin iūstus, "just") and the story of -ifie (from Latin -ficāre, "to make"). Each element descends from its own Proto-Indo-European root, and each has its own long arc from ancient abstraction to the famous line of Milton's Paradise Lost (1667), where the poet undertakes "to assert th' Eternal Providence, / And justifie the wayes of God to men." SECTION ONE — THE "JUST" ELEMENT (PIE *yewes-, Latin iūs, iūstus). The first root is PIE *yewes-, whose meaning is reconstructed as "law, rule, what is binding, what is in right order." The sister languages of Indo-Iranian preserve the same etymon in Sanskrit yóḥ, "health, welfare, well-being" (already in the Rigveda), and in Avestan yaoš, "health, vigor" — evidence that the root originally denoted what is sound, lawful, and in proper order, before the Italic branch narrowed it to the institutional idea of law. In Italic the root became Proto-Italic *iowes- and then Latin iūs (genitive iūris), "law, right, justice, legal right" — the word at the centre of Roman jurisprudence, from which iūstus, "just, righteous, lawful," was derived with the ordinary adjectival suffix -tus, and from which in turn iūstitia, "justice," was built. Latin iūstus lies behind the modern English just and justice, both of which reached English through Old French (juste, justice), and it is this word that furnished the first half of our compound: iūstificāre, "to make just." The crucial semantic development took place in Christian Latin. The Greek New Testament used the verb dikaióō, "to pronounce righteous," and Latin Christian writers — Tertullian and above all Jerome's Vulgate — coined or adopted iūstificāre to translate it. The causal sense "to make just" was thus bent toward the forensic-religious sense "to declare righteous": the verb by which God accounts a sinner righteous. It was this Christian Latin word that passed into Old French as justifier (attested from c. 1080 in the Chanson de Roland) and then into Middle English as justifien (first attested c. 1350), where it carried both the legal sense "to show to be just or lawful" and the theological sense "to declare righteous." SECTION TWO — THE "-FIE" (MAKING) ELEMENT (PIE *dʰeh₁-, Latin faciō, -ficāre). The second root is PIE *dʰeh₁-, "to put, place, set, make" — one of the most productive roots in all of Indo-European. Its Hellenic reflex is Greek títhēmi, "I put," the source of thésis and of the verb's vast family; its Indo-Iranian reflex is Vedic Sanskrit dadhāti, "he places," whence dharma-, "what is established," and modern Hindi dhar, "to hold, place"; its Germanic reflex is the root of English do, did, done, and deed. Its Italic reflex, Proto-Italic *fakiō (with an added *-k- extension), became Latin faciō, "I make, do" — the verb of a thousand compounds. In Latin, facere was reduced in compounds to the combining suffix -ficāre, "to make [something] into [something]," so that iūstificāre literally means "to make just," just as māgnificāre means "to make great." This suffix descended through Old French as -fier (justifier, magnifier) and entered English as the productive verb-forming suffix -fy, so that the English verb justify is, morphologically, exactly "just + -fy," "to make just." SECTION THREE — SOUND, SPELLING, AND THE EARLY MODERN FORM. The English word thus arrived through a chain of borrowings: Latin iūstificāre → Old French justifier → Middle English justifien → modern justify. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the ending was printed variously as -ifie, -efie, -ifye, and -fy; Milton's 1667 text spells the verb justifie (and the noun plural wayes), and that -ie spelling is simply the orthographic fashion of the period, later regularized to -y. The suffixal -fy, despite its English sound, is entirely Latinate: no Germanic sound change applies to it, since the word was a loan, not an inheritance. SECTION FOUR — THE REFORMATION AND MILTON'S THEODICY. The theological sense — "justification by faith" — became the hinge of Protestant theology in the sixteenth century: Luther's doctrine that God justifies (declares righteous) the sinner through faith alone, not works. In English this made justify a word heavy with doctrinal weight: to justify a person is to declare them righteous before God. Milton inherited that vocabulary and gave it its grandest literary form. The stated purpose of Paradise Lost is to "assert Eternal Providence / And justify the ways of God to men" — to prove, against the scandal of evil and suffering, that divine providence is just. It is a theodicy (Greek theós "god" + díkē "justice"), and the verb sits at its centre: to justify God's ways is to show them to be right, using the very word that Christian Latin used to translate "to declare righteous." SECTION FIVE — SEMANTIC DRIFT TO MODERN SENSES. After the early modern period the word's senses multiplied along three lines: (1) the moral-intellectual line — "to show to be right or reasonable" (to justify an action, a belief, oneself), which is the everyday sense today; (2) the theological line — "to declare righteous," preserved in doctrinal language and echoed whenever the word is used of vindication before a higher authority; and (3) the typographic line — "to justify text," i.e., to space words so that lines align evenly with both margins, which descends from the older causal sense "to make exact, to adjust to a standard," and which entered printing terminology in the seventeenth century and lives on in every word processor. Each of these branches, including the modern printer's "justify," descends ultimately from the Latin compound that meant "to make just." CONCLUSION. Justifie thus carries two Indo-European roots in a single word: PIE *yewes-, the "law" that became Latin iūs and iūstus, and PIE *dʰeh₁-, the "put/make" that became Latin faciō and the suffix -ficāre. Joined in Christian Latin as iūstificāre, the compound was borrowed through French into English, where the Reformation sharpened its theological edge, Milton gave it its most famous sentence, and the printer's trade gave it an unexpected second life in typography. The word is a small museum of Indo-European history, Latin jurisprudence, Christian doctrine, and English literature in one.
