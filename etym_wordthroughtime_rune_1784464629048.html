“Rune” entered English naming the letters of the old Germanic alphabets — the angular characters carved into wood, bone, and stone by northern peoples.
“Rune” from the earliest use carried the double meaning of “secret” and “whisper” alongside “letter” — the same word-family meant both a written character and something hidden or spoken low, so runes were felt to be secret writing.
“Rune” through the 18th–19th centuries was taken up by antiquarians and Romantics as the token of cryptic magic — runes became mysterious symbols charged with hidden power, and fiction layered the mystique onto the alphabet.
“Rune” today holds both senses at once: a real letter of an ancient Germanic alphabet and any mark felt to be cryptic or magical — mapmakers’ runes that mark a hidden door are precisely this double sense made concrete.
