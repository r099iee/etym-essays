- Old English (800–1100) - Attested as *twīġ* / *twī*, rooted in the numeral *two* (*twā*), referring literally to the forking or bifurcation of a branch into two shoots.
- Middle English (1100–1500) - Spelled *twigge*, *twyg*, referring widely to small tree shoots, kindling, birdlime snares (*limed twigs*), and switches.
- Early Modern English (1500–1700) - Frequent in proverbial wisdom regarding child-rearing ("as the twig is bent, the tree's inclined" - Pope) and pastoral nature imagery (Shakespeare).
- Late 18th–19th c. (Colloquial British verb shift) - Developed an idiosyncratic British slang verb meaning "to catch sight of, comprehend, realize, or understand" ("I twigged his plan").
- Contemporary (Ecological, Sensory, Metaphorical) - Retains core botanical meaning as the ultimate terminal unit of tree crowns, symbol of fragility, and ubiquitous element of forest floor ambiance.
