CHAIN: FROM A NEGATIVE PREFIX AND A LATIN VERB OF PLACING TO INDISPOSED (5 attested/reconstructed stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European negative particle *ne- "not" (with zero-grade *n-) (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE). It yields the Latin privative prefix in- "not" (as in incapax "incapable," infirmus "infirm").
  2. Latin pōnere "to put, place" (attested from the 1st century BCE). The verb is a contracted form of an earlier *posnere, built from the prefix po- plus sinere "to put down, let go, leave." The ultimate Indo-European base of sinere is not securely known; several proposals exist but none commands consensus, so the chain cannot be carried below Latin with certainty.
  3. Latin dispōnere "to set in order, arrange" (attested from the 1st century BCE), from dis- "apart, in different directions" + pōnere "to put, place." Its past participle is dispositus "arranged, set in order."
  4. Late Latin / Medieval Latin *indispositus "disordered, not properly arranged" (in- "not" + dispositus; attested in medieval Latin use from about the 13th century). In medieval medicine the word described a body whose humours were not properly set in order.
  5. Middle French indisposé "not set in order, unwell" (14th–15th century), borrowed into English. The participle sense "unwell" enters English in the late 16th century (about 1590), and the sense "not inclined, averse" is established by the 17th century.

→ Modern English INDISPOSED (the exact form of this card).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The PIE root underlying Latin sinere (and hence ponere) is unknown; older dictionaries proposed a connection to a root meaning "to throw or let go," but this is not securely established, and the chain must honestly stop at Latin.
  • An earlier date sometimes given for the English word (14th century) is not supported by the surviving record; the attested borrowing from Middle French belongs to the 16th century, and the fully developed adjective senses to the 16th–17th centuries.
