- (14th c., Middle English ajoinen “to unite, ally”) - A verb of action: joining people, allies, or things together; the register is legal and political — alliances, causes, and attached clauses.
- (16th c., 1560s) - The spatial pivot: “to adjoin” comes to mean “to be contiguous with, to lie next to”; the participle “adjoining” is born as a spatial adjective for fields, rooms, and lands — the register becomes topographical and descriptive.
- (17th–19th c.) - Consolidation in property law: “adjoining land”, “adjoining owners” become terms of art in deeds, surveys, and statutes — the word acquires legal weight and administrative neutrality.
- (20th c.) - The hotel industry fixes “adjoining rooms” (connected by a door) as a commercial contrast to “adjacent rooms”; the adjective thrives while the verb recedes to formal writing.
- (today) - “Adjoining” sounds factual and slightly formal: it implies a shared wall or boundary rather than mere distance, and it carries a gentle legal-bureaucratic chill — “next door” remains the warm everyday rival.
