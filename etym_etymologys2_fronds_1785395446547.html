CHAIN: FROM LATIN TO FRONDS (5 stages — no secure PIE root)

  1. Latin frōns, frondis (feminine, consonant stem) "leaf, foliage, leafy branch" (attested from the classical period, e.g. in the poets and in agricultural writers). NO securely reconstructed PIE root: de Vaan records no good etymology and Etymonline likewise marks it "of uncertain origin"; the word is treated as native Italic stock whose deeper ancestry is unknown. Do not map a PIE breaking or budding root onto it.
  2. Late Latin fronda "leafy branch, frond" (reformed feminine noun from the stem frond-, attested in late and medieval Latin).
  3. Botanical Latin fronda, carried into early modern botany; English naturalists borrowed the word directly from botanical Latin in the eighteenth century.
  4. English frond (noun), first recorded 1784, "the leaf of a fern or similar plant" (a learned, direct borrowing from Latin frōns / frond-).
  5. FRONDS (plural, regular -s — the exact form of this card).

HOMONYM GUARD

  • Latin frōns, frontis "forehead, brow" is a different root entirely; it is the source of English front, forehead and frontier, and has NO connection with the foliage word.
  • French fronde "a sling" (from Latin funda "a sling") is unrelated to the botanical word, though the homonyms sometimes collide in French.
  • Older guesses linking frōns to a PIE root of budding or breaking, or to Greek words for flowers, are rejected by modern scholarship (de Vaan).
  • Greek phýllon "leaf" and thallós "young shoot" are unrelated; the word is an Italic isolate within the Indo-European landscape.
