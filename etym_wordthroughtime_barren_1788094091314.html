- Sterile women (c. 1200): 'barein' first describes women and female animals unable to bear children — a dynastic and legal word, blunt and unemotional
- Sterile land (13th–14th c.): the word extends to fields and soil that bear no crops; famine-era England makes 'barren land' a matter of life and death
- The Bible's word (16th–17th c.): the King James Version (Sarai, Rachel, Elizabeth, the barren fig tree) gives 'barren' scriptural gravity and a double meaning — physical and spiritual fruitlessness
- The mind's sterility (17th–18th c.): 'barren of ideas', 'barren imagination' — the orchard metaphor transfers to intellect and creativity
- The romantic wasteland (19th c.): explorers and poets apply it to Arctic barrens and sublime empty wastes; 'barren' becomes a landscape aesthetic
- Today: applied to people it sounds harsh and is replaced by 'infertile'; it survives for land, outcomes and emotional emptiness — with a permanent biblical echo
