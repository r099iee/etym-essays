BLEAT belongs to one of the oldest and most transparent word-families in Indo-European: a family of imitative (onomatopoeic) formations that reproduce the thin, wavering call of the sheep in the very shape of the word. Prehistoric speakers did not need to borrow a name for this cry — they heard a noise and re-created it in their mouths, which is why strikingly similar words appear across branches of the family that had been separated for millennia. The reconstructed base is usually given as Proto-Indo-European *bhle- (or *bhlē-), "to howl, cry out, bleat," an expressive root whose descendants include Ancient Greek βληχή (blēkhē) "bleating; the wailing of children," Latin flēre "to weep, lament" (with the derived flēbilis "lamentable" passing through Old French feble into English feeble), Old Church Slavonic blejati and Russian блеять (blejat') "to bleat," and the entire Germanic group.

The Germanic branch took a characteristically concrete turn. A Proto-Germanic verb *blētijaną "to bleat" was built with the -t- formative that Germanic uses for many animal-cry verbs, and it appears in Old English as blǣtan, recorded from the early medieval period and the everyday word for the sound of sheep in the fields. On the Continent the family survived in Middle Dutch blaten, Middle Low German blaten, and Old High German blāzzen "to blow, blare, bleat," whose modern descendant is German blöken "to bleat." The near-identity of these forms across Germanic testifies to the word's antiquity within the branch: it was not imported but inherited, and the same imitative impulse that shaped it in one speech community evidently repeated itself, or was carried along, wherever Germanic peoples kept sheep.

The crucial semantic shift in English was the extension from animal sound to human speech. Because the sheep's cry is thin, trembling, and powerless, bleat was naturally applied, from an early date, to human utterance marked by helplessness, fear, or self-pitying complaint. Middle English bleten already carried both senses — the literal animal cry and the figurative feeble cry or complaint — and the pattern never died out. The noun, recorded c. 1500, named "the cry of a sheep, goat, or calf," and by the sixteenth century the metaphorical noun and verb were firmly in place: "bleating excuses," a "bleating" coward, voices "bleating" their fears to unhearing masters.

Phonetically the word has been remarkably stable. The Old English long vowel ǣ developed through the Great Vowel Shift into the modern /iː/, so that blǣtan became blēten (Middle English) and finally bleat, with the spelling ea representing the new long e. The initial cluster bl- was never lost, and it is itself expressive: English uses bl- in a whole cluster of words for weak, noisy emission (blare, bluster, blubber, blat, blow), reinforcing the sound-symbolic character of the word. Dialect and Scots kept parallel forms, notably blate, "bashful, sheepish," which derives the very idea of shyness from the sheep's retiring nature — a reminder that for centuries English speakers read the animal's temperament directly from its cry.

Socio-culturally, the word has always done double duty. In the literal register it is the plain, neutral farming word, current in husbandry, zoology, and hunting, where a "bleat-call" imitates a fawn or lamb to draw game. In the human register it is a weapon of contempt: to call a person's complaint a "bleat" is to deny it dignity, to reduce protest to the noise of livestock. That is precisely why Tolkien's goblins sing Yammer and bleat! — the verb completes the dehumanization of the captives, stripping them of human speech and leaving only the sound of sheep awaiting slaughter. Modern usage keeps both lives: a shepherd still hears the bleat of his flock, and a newspaper still sneers at the "bleating" of critics, preserving across eight centuries the ancient bridge between the flock and the powerless.
