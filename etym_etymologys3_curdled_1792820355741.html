The past of curdled is mercifully short — and honestly cloudy, like whey. Its parent noun, Middle English crud or curd, surfaces in the 1400s meaning the coagulated part of soured milk, the lumps pressed toward cheese. Before that, nothing certain: no Old English ancestor waits in the manuscripts, no continental cousin raises its hand. Etymologists have auditioned candidates — Old Irish cruth (form, shape, curd), a hypothetical native word for pressed or crowded matter, even a purely expressive formation from the sound of squeezing — and the OED, with unusual frankness, leaves the question open. Some words are born in the dairy and not in the library, and curd may be one: coined by hands, not by scribes.

What happened next is textbook English morphology. Around the 1580s speakers added the frequentative suffix -le — the same ending as in drizzle (to keep dripping), sparkle (to keep sparking), and tussle elsewhere in this very batch — forming curdle: to keep making curds, to go on curding until smoothness surrenders. The vowel rounded from crud to curd under the influence of the following r, spelling wobbling for a century before settling. Curdled, the participle, followed the verb into print within a generation, first of milk left standing, then — in a leap of imagination that made the word immortal — of blood.

From dairy to dread:

  • 15th c.: crud and curd name pressed milk-solids in household accounts and cookery notes; cheese-making is the respectable face of curdling, souring its shameful twin.
  • 1580s – 1600: curdle and curdled enter print; the verb belongs first to dairymaids and cooks — milk, cream, and possets turning in the pan.
  • 17th c.: the blood-curdling leap — dramatists discover that fear might thicken the veins like vinegar in milk; to curdle the blood becomes stage shorthand for terror, used by Beaumont and Fletcher's heirs and every ghost-tale since.
  • 18th c.: folk belief codifies the physics: thunder turns milk (true, via warmth breeding bacteria), so thunderous faces, scolds, and witches are credited with the same power in proverbs, jest-books, and novels from Fielding to Austen.
  • 19th c.: French-trained chefs make uncurdled sauces the test of civilisation — Carême's emulsions must never split — while industrial Cheddar turns deliberate curdling into factory science; Dickens curdles blood, milk, and characters' affections with equal gusto.
  • 20th – 21st c.: food science names the proteins (casein micelles, denaturation, pH 4.6) without killing the poetry; curdled now serves horror films, kitchen disasters, and political columns (curdled idealism, a curdled plot) — and Tolkien's pony-trap gossip (1937) preserves the folk original in amber.

Literary attestation runs from kitchen to charnel house. Shakespeare, characteristically early, has the mechanism without quite the word — witches' milk-white imagery and blood grown cold — while Jacobean horror completed the compound and made curdle the blood immortal. Eighteenth-century novelists applied folk dairy-physics to scolds and prudes: Smollett's and Burney's formidable ladies curdle rooms as they enter them, the direct ancestors of Tolkien's pony-trap driver rattling toward Bywater. Victorian science then explained the joke without spoiling it: Pasteur showed warmth souring milk and rennet clotting it, proving the folk right about thunder (hot close weather) and wrong about glares — though any reader who has faced a truly thunderous face over breakfast will side with the folk. Curdled endures because it fuses disgust, fear, and comedy in one white lump: the only word that is at home in the dairy, the morgue, and the morning room.
