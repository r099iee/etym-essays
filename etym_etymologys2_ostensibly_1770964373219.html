Single root word: ostensibly, the adverbial form of the English adjective ostensible. Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *ten- "to stretch, extend, spread" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives: Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches", Ancient Greek teínein "to stretch", Latin tendere "to stretch, extend", Old English þennan "to stretch" (Germanic *þanjaną). 2. Latin tendere "to stretch, extend, aim, direct" — attested in classical Latin. 3. Latin ostendere "to stretch out in front of, present, show, display, manifest" — obs-/ob- "towards, in front of" + tendere — attested from Plautus (3rd–2nd centuries BCE) and frequent in Cicero. 4. Latin ostēnsus (also ostentus) "shown, displayed" — past participle of ostendere; the same stem gives the frequentative ostentāre "to show off, display" (source of English ostentatious). 5. Latin / Late Latin ostēnsibilis "that may be shown, displayable, apparent" — ostēns- + -ibilis; recorded in medieval Latin. NOTE: attestation of this specific adjective is thin; the French and English forms may have been built directly on the Latin pattern rather than inherited as a continuous word. 6. French ostensible "that can be shown; apparent, professed" — attested from the 17th century (c. 1660s). 7. English ostensible "capable of being shown; apparent, plausible, professed" — attested from the 1730s. 8. English ostensibly — ostensible + adverb suffix -ly, "on the surface, apparently, seemingly (usually implying doubt)" — attested from the late 18th century. Final node: ostensibly. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The deep chain (*ten- → tendere → ostendere) is certain. The only nuance is the intermediate adjective: Latin ostēnsibilis is thinly attested, so the English word is best treated as formed from Latin ostēnsus plus the -ible/-ible suffix by way of French, rather than as a direct unbroken inheritance from a firmly attested Latin noun.
