The history of legatee is the history of Roman law, medieval church diplomacy, and early modern English probate practice compressed into a single legal noun. It begins not with inheritance at all, but with the act of gathering, choosing, and sending.

The ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *leg- meaning "to collect, gather," which also produced Greek legein "to speak, gather," Latin lex, legis "law" (a gathering of rules), and legere "to read, pick out." In Italic, a derived verb *leg-ā- developed the specialised sense "to depute, commission someone picked out from a group." Classical Latin lēgāre thus meant both "to send as an envoy, appoint by commission" and, crucially for our word, "to bequeath, appoint an heir by will" — the testator commissions property to pass after death as a general commissions an envoy to act in life. The semantic bridge is appointment and delegation.

From lēgāre came the past participle lēgātus, literally "one who has been appointed," used as a noun for a lieutenant-general, ambassador, or provincial governor, and later for papal legates. Old French borrowed it as legat, and Middle English as legate around 1200-1300, almost entirely in the ecclesiastical-diplomatic sense: a cardinal-legate, the Pope's deputy. Parallel to this, Latin had lēgātum, neuter participle used for "a bequest, legacy" — that which is bequeathed. English borrowed legacy in the 14th century for the gift itself.

  • Roman law foundation: In Roman testamentary practice, legatum was a specific gift in a will, distinct from hereditas (the whole inheritance). The legatarius was its recipient. English common law preserved this distinction.
  • Middle English gap: For two centuries English had legate (sender's deputy) and legacy (gift) but no dedicated word for the recipient. Documents used legatary (from Late Latin legatarius), first attested c. 1450, or circumlocutions like "he to whom legacy is bequeathed."
  • The -ee innovation: In the 17th century, Anglo-French legal English productively formed recipient nouns in -ee (from Old French past participle -é, from Latin -atus): grantee, lessee, mortgagee, appointee, devisee. On that analogy, legate + -ee was coined, first recorded in the 1670s (Blount's law dictionary era). The formation is transparent: the -or/-er gives, the -ee receives.
  • Dickensian attestation: Dickens's phrase "sole residuary legatee" in A Christmas Carol (1843) is technically precise. A residuary legatee takes the residue — what remains after debts, taxes, and specific legacies. By making Scrooge sole executor, administrator, assign, residuary legatee, friend and mourner, Dickens piles six lonely "soles" to stress that Marley left everything to one grasping man, and that man had no human tie beyond paperwork.

Phonetically the development is uneventful: Latin long ē shortened in French, stress shifted in English to the final syllable under French influence, giving modern /ˌleɡ.əˈtiː/ with secondary stress on leg- and primary stress on -tee, exactly like grantee, refugee. Spelling wavered between legatee and legattee in the 18th century before settling on single t.

Socio-legal context matters. The rise of legatee coincides with the post-Restoration explosion of written wills among the English middle class, the Statute of Frauds (1677) requiring written wills for land, and the growth of Doctors' Commons probate courts. Victorian reform — the Wills Act 1837, the Court of Probate 1857 — made legatee a household word encountered in every family death. Dickens, who worked as a law clerk and court reporter, uses it with clerkly relish.

  • Modern usage: Today legatee remains formal legal English, slightly archaic in America where beneficiary dominates, but alive in Britain and Commonwealth probate. Lawyers distinguish specific legatee, general (pecuniary) legatee, and residuary legatee. Outside law it survives as a deliberate archaism for Dickensian flavour.
  • Debate note: The PIE root *leg- "collect" as source of Latin legare is standard (de Vaan, Etymonline), but some older dictionaries derived legare from lex directly. The difference is trivial — both go back to the same root. The formation legatee itself is undisputed.

In short, legatee travelled from gathering to choosing to sending to bequeathing to receiving: the one who is chosen to receive what the dead have gathered.
