- Late 1300s seizing and grasping together — to apprehend a felon and to apprehend a truth arrive as one Latin import; hand and mind grasp as one act, often with theological overtones
- 1500s legal officer’s word — statutes fix to apprehend as the proper term for lawful arrest, gaining formal, procedural dignity over catch, take, or nab
- 1600s philosopher’s word — Locke and Hobbes elevate mental apprehension as the mind’s first direct grip on simple ideas, distinct from full comprehension; connotation becomes intellectual and precise
- 1700s–1800s anxious anticipation — novels normalize to apprehend danger, ruin, or censure; the word acquires a nervous, foreboding colour, becoming the polite term for dread among genteel speakers
- Modern three-track stability — police apprehend suspects, students apprehend concepts, worriers apprehend trouble; the three senses coexist without confusion, all retaining a formal, written, slightly detached flavour
