- 14th c. — Middle English creates 'singly' by appending native '-ly' to borrowed Anglo-Norman 'single'; denotes acting one by one, individually, or without companions.
- 15th to 16th c. — Prominent in chivalric and Tudor drama for single combat ('to duel singly') and marital status ('to live singly', unmarried).
- 17th to 18th c. — Adopted by Enlightenment empiricists and natural scientists (Bacon, Boyle) to describe analyzing chemical elements and natural phenomena in isolation.
- 19th c. — Occasionally conflated in literary prose with 'singularly' to mean exceptionally, but primarily settles as a formal adverb of discrete enumeration.
- 20th to 21st c. — Crucial technical counterpoint in Gestalt psychology and cognitive science (demonstrating that sensory cues are not perceived 'singly' but holistically); widely used in data processing, logistics, and legal protocols.
