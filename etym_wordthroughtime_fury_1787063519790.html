- Antiquity (Greek Erinyes, Latin Furiae) - 'fury' is a vengeful goddess who hunts wrongdoers - the word trembles with supernatural terror and moral punishment
- 14th c. - Middle English 'furie' enters via French - violent personal rage plus the raging of storms, fire, and battle - bound up with the deadly sin of wrath
- 16th-17th c. - Shakespeare's age makes fury intensely psychological and dramatic - 'in a fury' names an overpowering, almost maddening anger; the mythic Furies return in high art; furor poeticus keeps fury an inspiration as well as a fit
- 18th-19th c. - fury goes public and political - mob fury, revolutionary fury, the fury of battle and weather - journalism makes it the loudest headline word for violent anger
- 20th c. - 'fast and furious' freezes fury into a phrase of sheer speed - markets, sports, and entertainment borrow it as a synonym for intensity, no anger required
- 21st c. - fury is the standard noun for righteous collective anger (climate fury, fan fury, online fury) while the mythic Furies live on in culture - the word still means a rage too big to contain
