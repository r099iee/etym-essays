speculate is a verb with two great lives in English, both of which descend from a single ancient image of watching. In its intellectual sense it means (1) to engage in conjectural or theoretical reasoning, forming opinions or hypotheses without complete empirical evidence; and (2) to reflect deeply and systematically upon a subject, to contemplate with the mind's eye. In its economic sense it means (3) to buy, sell, or deal in commodities, stocks, property, or currency with the expectation of profiting from price movements, i.e. to act upon uncertain expectations of the future. Etymologically the verb is a descendant of Latin speculārī, "to watch, observe, spy out", from specula, "a watchtower or look-out post", from specere, "to look at, observe" — the same root as English spectator, spectacles, inspect, respect, prospect, spy, and scope. To speculate is, at the root, to watch for what might be seen — to keep a lookout, to scan the horizon of the possible.

In the passage from Mill's Autobiography from which this note is drawn — "It is idle to speculate on the possible results of a different treatment" — the verb is used in its intellectual sense. Mill is dismissing as futile (the old sense of "idle": vain, groundless) any conjecture about the counterfactual question of how his education might have turned out under different treatment — a question that can never be tested and therefore can yield no knowledge. The nuance is of fruitless hypothesis-mongering set against the discipline of what can actually be known: speculation, precisely because it ranges beyond the evidence, is here pronounced a waste of time. It is the language of a philosophical empiricist ruling a question out of court as fundamentally sterile.

The word's senses, past and present, are best surveyed point by point:

  • To form a theory or conjecture without firm evidence — "historians speculate about the motives of the emperor": the everyday intellectual sense, mildly depreciated since the 19th century, now meaning guesswork without firm evidence.
  • To reflect deeply and systematically upon a subject — "to speculate on the nature of the universe": the older, dignified philosophical sense, inherited from Medieval Latin speculātiō, abstract contemplation, where speculation was high intellectual activity in the service of knowledge, not mere guessing.
  • To engage in financial dealing on uncertain expectations — "to speculate in stocks, in property, on the exchange": the economic sense, born in the 18th century amid the stock markets of London and Amsterdam, and now so dominant that it often colours the whole word with the risk of gambling.
  • The older, now archaic sense, "to spy out, to watch" — the literal sense inherited from Latin speculārī, "to keep a lookout", attested in early English and surviving chiefly in the related noun speculator and in the adjective speculative in its etymological dress.
  • The transitive construction — "to speculate that...": "commentators speculate that the decision was political": used with a that-clause to introduce a conjectured proposition.

Historical semantic bridge. All these senses flow from one root metaphor: the lookout. Latin specula was a watchtower; speculārī was what a sentry did; and from "watching" the word extended to "watching with the mind", i.e. contemplating and theorising. Medieval Latin turned speculātiō into the technical term for abstract intellectual contemplation, and English adopted the verb in the 1590s in that high philosophical sense. The financial sense — to act on uncertain expectations of the market — arose in the 18th century as a further extension of "watching for a sign": the speculator watches the market the way the sentry watches the horizon, acting on what he thinks he can see coming. The intellectual and the commercial speculator are thus cousins under one roof: both look beyond present evidence and both commit themselves, in one way or another, to what they think they glimpse.

Boundaries, constructions, and register. Speculate differs from conjecture (which emphasises guesswork from incomplete clues and is often forensic), from guess (casual, unreflective estimation), from hypothesize (the scientific act of framing a testable proposition), and from theorize (building an explanatory framework). It collocates with on, about, and as to ("speculate on/about/as to the future"), with that-clauses, with adverbs like wildly, idly, merely, and in economics with in ("speculate in property"). The intellectual sense is dignified or mildly depreciated depending on context; the financial sense is tinged with risk and the suggestion of gambling. The related adjective speculative runs the same double course (speculative philosophy versus speculative investment), and the agent-noun speculator is today used overwhelmingly of the financial operator.
