uncovetous means not covetous: free from greedy desire for what belongs to others, without envy, acquisitiveness, or grasping. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita explaining how seeing nearly all the noblemen's houses in England as a child did him good, because he looked on them in reverent and healthy delight of uncovetous admiration — it is a moral-aesthetic claim. He admired Warwick Castle without wanting to own it, pull it down, or level Brunswick Square up to it. To be uncovetous is to be able to delight in grandeur without resentment: happier, he says, to live in a small house, and have Warwick Castle to be astonished at, than to live in Warwick Castle and have nothing to be astonished at. The politics follow: it would not make a plain London square more habitable to demolish a castle out of envy.

Outside Ruskin, uncovetous is a rare, literary, chiefly ethical and theological adjective. It translates the Tenth Commandment — thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, wife, servant, ox, ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's — into character: one who keeps that commandment habitually. A Victorian sermon praises an uncovetous clergy content with a curacy; a biography notes his uncovetous temper in money matters; a will commends her uncovetous care of the poor. The noun is uncovetousness, the adverb uncovetously (to look, to give, to refuse uncovetously). All are uncommon; modern prose prefers unselfish, generous, content, unmaterialistic, non-acquisitive, leaving uncovetous to deliberate archaism, pulpit quotation, and Ruskin discussion.

  • Core moral sense: not greedy, not envious (head sense): e.g. an uncovetous heart, uncovetous eyes, to remain uncovetous amid luxury. Opposed to covetous, greedy, grasping, acquisitive, envious, mercenary, avaricious. The focus is inward desire, not outward action: one may handle great wealth uncovetously (as a steward) and may covet while poor. Theological writers distinguish covetousness (idolatry of having, Colossians 3:5) from envy (sorrow at another's good); uncovetous covers both absences at once.
  • Ruskinian aesthetic sense (source sense): uncovetous admiration as a way of seeing. For Ruskin, covetous looking consumes its object — the tourist who wants to own the view, the developer who wants to quarry the crag, the leveller who wants to pull the castle down. Uncovetous looking reveres without possessing: to draw, to study, to thank. The phrase reverent and healthy delight makes the ethics physiological: envy sickens, admiration nourishes. The semantic bridge is eye-as-hand: to covet is to steal with the eyes before the hands follow.
  • Political-economic sense: anti-levelling contentment: in Ruskin's argument, uncovetous citizens tolerate inequality because they value astonishment over ownership. A small house plus a Warwick Castle to visit beats a universal Brunswick Square with nothing to admire. Modern readers hear paternalism, but the logic is aesthetic economics: wonder is a public good that private envy destroys. Hence Ruskinian socialism is paradoxically uncovetous — against both miserly hoarding and envious demolition.
  • Pastoral-ministerial sense: content with little: ecclesiastical use, e.g. an uncovetous parson, uncovetous service for a stipend. St Paul's I have learned in whatsoever state I am therewith to be content and Chaucer's Parson (he was nat covetous) stand behind it. Here uncovetous implies cheerful sufficiency rather than ascetic refusal; the uncovetous clergyman may enjoy dinner without scheming for a bishopric.
  • Extended modern paraphrase sense: unmaterialistic, non-consumerist: translators render ancient aphilargyros (loverless-of-silver) or modern Danish hygge-contentment as uncovetous when they want biblical weight; e.g. an uncovetous lifestyle in a throwaway age. The bridge is analogy: first-century silver-love and twenty-first-century upgrade-lust feel like the same vice, so the old negation is revived for new gadgets, houses, and honours.
  • Grammatical and collocational notes: predicative (to be uncovetous of praise, of land) and attributive (uncovetous admiration, temper, heart); governs of for the thing not coveted. Adverb uncovetously is regular but vanishingly rare; noun uncovetousness survives in divinity indexes. Negative prefix un- is fully transparent, so speakers coin it afresh even who never met it in print — yet dictionaries record it since the 16th century as established, not nonce.

Tone is earnest, approving, and slightly sermon-like. To call admiration uncovetous praises it as both beautiful and good; to call a man uncovetous canonises his poverty as virtue. Ruskin, who inherited wealth and despised both miser and leveller, needed exactly this word: neither rich nor poor in spirit, but rich in astonishment and poor in grasping — reverent and healthy at once.
