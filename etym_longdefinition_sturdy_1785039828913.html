STURDY is an adjective meaning strongly and solidly built; robust, firm, well-knit, and capable of enduring hardship, wear, or rough treatment. It describes something compact in build yet packed with strength — a body or object whose firmness and resistance are trustworthy. Crucially, sturdy carries no implication of size: a creature or object can be perfectly sturdy while small, which distinguishes it from words that imply bulk (stout, burly). A sturdy little pony is natural; a "burly little pony" is nearly a contradiction. Literary context — The Hobbit. The card's sentence laments the ponies the company loses: "I am afraid that was the last they ever saw of those excellent little ponies, including a jolly sturdy little white fellow that Elrond had lent to Gandalf, since his horse was not suitable for the mountain-paths." The adjective works hard in that short span. It combines compact solidity with quiet reliability; it sits warmly between "jolly" and "little," so the tone is affectionate, admiring the pony as one admires a dependable companion; and it quietly foreshadows the hardship of the mountain-paths ahead, since only a sturdy beast could be trusted on such passes. The pathos is deepened retroactively: the sturdy little mount, so reassuringly described, is exactly the one that never comes back, lost in the goblin darkness. Polysemy and related senses. Beyond its central meaning, sturdy has branched into several distinct uses:
  • Of persons and animals — physical build: strongly and solidly built; firm and robust of frame. "A sturdy farmer," "a sturdy little pony." This is the oldest modern sense, applied to people from the late 14th century.
  • Of objects, materials, and structures: solid, resistant to wear, damage, or pressure. "A sturdy table," "sturdy boots," "a sturdy bridge." Since about 1400 the word has been applied to trees, walls, and other constructions.
  • Figurative — firm in purpose or resolve: robust of character, staunch, unyielding. "Sturdy independence," "a sturdy faith." The physical firmness transfers to moral or intellectual solidity.
  • Historical — "sturdy beggar" (c.1400): in English poor-law language, a sturdy beggar was a vagrant physically strong enough to work and therefore, in the harsh terms of the Elizabethan statutes, a culpable idler rather than a deserving pauper. The phrase preserves the old, harsher sense of sturdy as "strong, violent, able-bodied" in a social-judgment context.
  • Veterinary pathology — "sturdy" or "gid": a disease of sheep caused by a tapeworm larva lodging in the brain, producing staggering and giddiness. Here the word links back to its original sense "stunned, giddy" (see etymologyS2) — a relic of the meaning the adjective abandoned in ordinary use.
  • Obsolete and archaic senses — fierce, violent, rebellious: in Middle English, sturdi meant "hard to manage, reckless, violent; stern; disobedient, rebellious." These senses died out by the 17th century but are essential for reading older texts: a "sturdy knave" in Chaucer's age was a violent one, not a solid one.
  • Noun use (obsolete): occasionally sturdy was used as a noun meaning a sturdy person, or "giddiness" itself; both uses are now obsolete.
Semantic boundaries. Strong is the general word for power, physical or abstract; robust stresses vigorous health and powers of recovery; tough emphasises endurance and resistance to damage; hardy is used of creatures or plants able to withstand cold and hardship; stout leans toward thick-set bulk, sometimes fatness; burly is big, heavy, and strong, of persons; stalwart adds loyalty and courage to bodily sturdiness. Only STURDY carries no excess of size — a little creature can be perfectly sturdy — and that compactness of meaning is exactly why Tolkien reaches for it.
