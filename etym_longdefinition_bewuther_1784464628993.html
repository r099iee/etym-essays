BEWUTHER (verb; chiefly dialectal, Northern English, and rare — most familiar today from Tolkien's literary use; also used as the participial adjective bewuthered) — To fluster, bewilder, confuse, or discompose someone as if they had been violently blown about, buffeted, and battered by a roaring, howling wind; to put a person into a state of agitated, breathless, storm-tossed confusion. Core sense and grammatical range. As a transitive verb, bewuther describes the action of wind or of overwhelming circumstance upon a person; as a participle, bewuthered names the resulting condition — one who is winded, harried, physically knocked about, and mentally scattered, like a man who has stood too long in an open gale. The word is built transparently from the intensifying Germanic prefix be- ('thoroughly, all over, all around') and the northern dialectal verb wuther ('to blow with a dull, continuous roaring sound,' said of the wind), the very verb enshrined in the title of Emily Brontë's 1847 novel Wuthering Heights. In Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), Bilbo Baggins is described during 'the most awkward Wednesday he ever remembered' as 'altogether bewildered and __________ed,' and the word is widely regarded as a Tolkien coinage or revival of a genuine regional formation. Polysemy and secondary senses. Because bewuther is a rare literary-dialectal formation rather than a high-frequency lexicalized verb, its senses form a small but genuine set, each linked by the master-image of violent wind:
  • Literal meteorological sense: to blow about violently, said of wind and weather; a transitive extension of wuther itself, used of exposed places where the wind 'wuthers' and roars.
  • Figurative psychological sense: to fluster, confuse, and discompose, as if the mind itself had been knocked about by a storm; this is the sense in which Tolkien pairs it with bewilder.
  • Physical-emotional sense: to leave someone breathless, harassed, or overwhelmed by a 'storm of activity' — noise, errands, unexpected visitors — so that one is at once fatigued and mentally scattered.
  • Participial / adjectival sense: bewuthered, 'in the condition of being thoroughly wuthered,' used of persons (rarely of exposed places) who have been buffeted and confounded.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The bridge runs from meteorology to psychology. In northern English dialect, wuther names the sound and force of wind roaring down a valley or across open moorland; a person caught in such weather is physically buffeted, deafened, and disorientated. The prefix be- intensifies and transitivizes the state, exactly as in befuddle, bewilder, beset, belabour, and begrudge. The bodily experience of being blown about is then mapped onto the mind: a person whose thoughts are knocked about by overwhelming circumstance is said to be bewuthered. This is the same metaphorical route by which bewilder passed from 'to lose in the wilderness' (wilder, a wild wasteland) to 'to confuse utterly,' and it explains why Tolkien — a West Midlands philologist with a deep love of dialect texture — chose the word to complete the pair: Bilbo is simultaneously lost in a mental wilderness and blasted by a domestic gale of dwarves, bells, and angry words. Connotation, register, and modern status. The word carries a strong flavour of literary northernness: exposed moorland, rough weather, plain blunt speech. It is not formal standard English but a vivid, almost onomatopoeic regional word whose wuth- root seems to mimic the dull roar of wind. In modern usage its chief resonance is literary and Tolkienian; outside close reading of The Hobbit and of Tolkien's philological essays it is very rare, and it is chiefly prized by readers and linguists as an example of how one philologist's delight in regional vocabulary could reanimate a nearly lost word. Related formations. The family includes wuther (verb), wuthering (noun and participial adjective, as in Brontë's title), wuthery and wuthered (adjectives meaning 'blowy, wild, exposed'), and the intensified bewuther/bewuthered treated here. All share the same onomatopoeic wind-root and the same moorland atmosphere.
