REMONSTRATE is a learned Latin-derived verb built on one of the most productive mental roots in Indo-European, and its biography shows a clean, well-attested drift from 'thinking' to 'warning' to 'showing' to 'protesting by showing back'. The PIE root *men- and its wide sisterhood. The chain begins with the reconstructed PIE root *men- 'to think, to be mindful'. That root feeds a remarkable sisterhood across the family. In Indo-Iranian it yields Sanskrit manas 'mind, thought' and the mythic first man Manu; in Greek it appears in ménos 'spirit, intent, force' and the verb maimai 'to be eager' (related to English mania); in Germanic it underlies mind, mean 'to intend', memento (through Latin), and the German verb mahnen 'to admonish'. In Latin its most important reflex is mens, mentis 'mind', which gives mental, mention, comment, and dementia. The deep meaning everywhere is inner awareness—the mental activity that later branches into remembering, warning, and pointing out. Latin specializes thinking into warning. Within Latin, a causative-advisory verb monēre 'to warn, advise, put in mind' developed: literally 'to make someone think of something'. From monēre come a host of English words: monitor 'one who warns or checks', admonish 'to warn earnestly', premonition 'a forewarning', and monumentum 'a memorial' (literally 'something that reminds'). The ancient Romans, who saw warnings in omens and prodigies, also formed monstrum 'a portent, a divine sign' —something the gods 'showed' as a warning—before that noun narrowed over time to the fearful 'monster'. From this same 'show' nexus Latin created monstrāre 'to show, point out, make visible': the act of setting something before the mind's and the eye's gaze. This base is enormously productive in Romance: it gives demonstrate, French montrer, Italian mostrare, Spanish mostrar, and, via Old French monstrer 'to show, to assemble for inspection', English muster (to gather troops for review). Medieval Latin adds the prefix: 'show back'. Medieval and early modern Latin prefixed re- 'back, in return' to monstrāre, producing remonstrāre 'to demonstrate again, to show back, to point out in return'. The semantic step is exactly the one the modern verb still performs: to remonstrate is to hold reasons up against a person's conduct—to show back to them the fault they have shown or overlooked. English borrowed the verb in the early seventeenth century (c. 1620s) in precisely this sense of reasoned protest, either from the Latin infinitive remonstrāre or from its past participle remonstrātus. Political and religious remonstrance. The related noun remonstrance quickly became a political keyword for formal statements of grievance: the most famous English instance is the Grand Remonstrance presented to Charles I in 1641, a long catalogue of parliamentary complaints that helped launch the English Civil War. In the religious sphere, the Dutch Remonstrants of 1610—followers of Arminius—'remonstrated' by formally setting forth their five articles of protest against Calvinist predestination, giving the word its lasting flavour of formal, written, doctrinal objection. Because of these historical uses, remonstrate and remonstrance stayed in a high register: parliamentary prose, petitions, religious controversy, and literary narrative. Semantic drift in summary and the novel's use. The drift is orderly and complete: think → put in mind / warn → portent shown → show → show back as objection. The moral force of the modern verb still carries the old idea of pointing something out that the other party is expected to see. In the Aroma–Bao-yu scene, that history of 'showing back' is exactly right. Aroma does not command Bao-yu; she remonstrates—she demonstrates to him, again and again, the grounds of her concern, as a servant and devoted companion who has no coercive power. His refusal to notice is a refusal to look at what she shows. The word, whose whole biography is about making someone see what they should, thus names at once her principled protest and its futility.
