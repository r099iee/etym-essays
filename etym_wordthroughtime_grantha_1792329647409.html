- c. 1000–500 BCE — In Vedic and early classical Sanskrit, *grantha* meant a physical knot, joint, or cord binding items together — perceived purely as a concrete functional connection or mechanical tie.
- c. 500 BCE–1st millennium CE — Shifted metonymically in literary circles to denote verses tied together into a coherent composition, and physically to manuscripts tied with string through pierced palm-leaf folios — perceived as an intellectual work, treatise, or book.
- c. 6th–12th century CE — Adopted in Southern India under the Pallava and Chola empires as the technical name for the specialized Brahmic script invented to render Sanskrit phonetics on palm leaves — revered as the elite scholarly script of Tamil Brahmins.
- 16th–17th century CE — In northern South Asia, embraced by the Sikh Gurus as the exalted title for their assembled sacred canon (*Adi Granth* / *Guru Granth Sahib*) — revered with sovereign devotional status.
- 19th–20th century CE — Yielded daily printing to modern Tamil and Malayalam typography while remaining active in Vedic patasalas; studied by global linguists as the mother script of Southeast Asian writing systems.
- 21st century — Standardized in digital typography and Unicode; recognized as a crucial epigraphic bridge for South and Southeast Asian intellectual history.
