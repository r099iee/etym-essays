- 14th c. France — the high leap: Old French soubresaut names the startled jump of deer and dancers, a leap clean over an obstacle.
- 1520s — England’s tumblers: travelling acrobats bring the trick as the somerset, turning it for pennies at fairs while English ears reshape the French prefix into familiar somer-.
- 16th–17th c. — fools and courtiers: jesters somersault in Shakespeare’s England; the trick becomes the emblem of topsy-turvy misrule and holiday license.
- 17th–18th c. — the moral flip: preachers and pamphleteers turn the trick into metaphor — turncoats and weathercocks turn somersaults in politics and religion.
- 19th c. — circus and gymnasium: the great age of the travelling circus and German Turnen makes the somersault a science, drilled, judged, and perfected.
- 20th c. — sky and screen: stunt pilots loop it, divers double it off high boards, film heroes backflip through explosions; Tolkien’s dragon turns one over Bywater.
- Today — the universal flip: from Olympic floor routines to playground grass, the somersault remains humanity’s favourite proof that the body can fly, briefly, in a perfect circle.
