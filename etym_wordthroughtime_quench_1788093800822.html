- Old English (8th–11th c.) – "cwencan": to extinguish fire and light; already applied to thirst and to sin — water-versus-fire as moral image.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) – "quenchen": thirst, desire, and the Spirit (Wyclif: "quench not the spirit"); metallurgical cooling appears.
- 1611 – The Authorised Version: "Many waters cannot quench love" (Song 8:7) and "Quench not the Spirit" (1 Thess 5:19) — love and zeal are flames.
- 17th–19th c. – Everyday fire-talk plus industrial steel-quenching; the word enters engineering and metallurgy.
- 20th–21st c. – Science re-borrows it: fluorescence quenching, superconducting quench, reactor quenching; the colloquial "quencher" drink; still the everyday word for thirst and fire.
