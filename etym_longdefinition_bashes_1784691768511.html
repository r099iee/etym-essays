Bashes is the plural of the noun bash: a heavy, violent blow — and, by a natural and ancient extension, the visible mark that such a blow leaves, a bruise, swelling, or contusion rising on the flesh. The word belongs to the great family of blunt-impact vocabulary in English: it pictures something hard coming down on something softer, and it keeps in the modern ear its companion senses of smashing and battering. As a noun it names both the strike and its result; as a verb, to bash is to strike so heavily that the object is smashed or dented in. A bash is heavier than a rap, cruder than a knock, less elegant than a stroke: it is the word of cudgels, fists, falls, and swollen knuckles. Primary senses The noun bash (singular) names a single heavy blow; the plural bashes collects such blows — and, metonymically, the marks they leave. The verb is recorded in the seventeenth century with the sense “to strike violently, to smash in,” and the noun followed in the early nineteenth century (1818) as “a heavy blow.” The move from verb to noun, and from the blow to its bruise, is a textbook case of metonymy: the cause (the strike) stands for the effect (the wound), exactly as a cut names both the act and the incision.
  • Physical blow: a heavy, blunt, forceful strike, usually delivered with something hard — a fist, a club, a door, a rock. “He gave the panel a bash.”
  • Resulting injury: a bruise, swelling, contusion, or lump left on the body by such a blow. “Burns and bashes to remember.”
  • Violent verb: to hit hard enough to damage or deform; to smash or batter. “The wave bashed the boat against the rocks.”
Secondary and modern extensions
  • British “have a bash”: to make an attempt, take a try at something — recorded from the mid-twentieth century. The idiom grew out of the sense “to take a blow at,” softening “a swing at” into “a go at.”
  • American “a bash” = a lively party: recorded from the 1930s, probably from the verb’s sense of vigorous action (a “bashing” good time); the shared idea is energy and force, redirected from fighting to festivity.
  • Figurative “to bash” = to criticize savagely: in journalism and public debate, to attack someone verbally — “teacher-bashing,” “bash the banks.” The verbal cudgelling of the earliest sense transferred from fists to words.
  • Compounds and derivatives: bashed (struck hard, smashed in), bashing (a sustained beating, physical or verbal; often in compounds such as gay-bashing), bash-up (a brawl or a rough repair).
Word-use notes and register Bashes sits in the informal, physical register of English. It is more vivid than blows, more comic than wounds, and more schoolboy than contusions. In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2, Tolkien has two of the three angry trolls nursing “burns and bashes to remember” after Thorin’s fight: the burns come from the blazing branch Thorin swings, the bashes are the heavy bruises and lumps laid on them with it, and “to remember” plays on both senses — injuries too severe to forget, and tokens that keep the insult alive. The word keeps the violence squarely comic: Tolkien does not say wounds or injuries but bashes, the schoolboy word of thumps and swelling knuckles, which keeps the fight at the level of rough-and-tumble rather than bloodshed. Related words and boundaries
  • Blow is colourless and general; bash adds the picture of heavy, blunt impact.
  • Bang emphasises the sound of the impact; thump its dullness; whack the implement; clout the direction and target (now dialectal or dated).
  • Bruise and contusion name the result rather than the cause; bashes alone covers both the strike and its mark at once.
Origin in brief The verb bash is first recorded around 1640 with no known Middle English ancestor, and its origin is genuinely obscure: scholars propose either a Scandinavian source (Old Norse *basca, Swedish basa, Danish baske “to beat, thump”) or a native expressive blend of bang and mash in the family of crash, dash, and squash. Both channels are plausible and may have reinforced one another; the OED labels the origin “obscure.” What is certain is that the word is an echoic coinage of the impact family, and that by the nineteenth century it had bred the noun and, with it, the plural bashes.
