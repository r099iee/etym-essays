Leven in the source sentence is Loch Leven: the shallow freshwater loch at Kinross in Perth and Kinross, some six kilometres long, famous for the island castle where Mary Queen of Scots was imprisoned in 1567-1568 and forced to abdicate. Ruskin as a boy — reading Scott's Abbot at Kinross itself and The Monastery in Glen Farg, and believing that the White Lady lived by the Ochil streamlet as certainly as the Queen of Scots in the island of Loch Leven — uses it as bedrock reality against which fairy belief is measured. If queens certainly lived on islands, ladies might certainly live by burns. The capital L and the frame Loch ... mark a proper noun; the word has no common-noun meaning in this sense.

Geographically Loch Leven sits at 107 m above sea level on the River Leven catchment: it drains east by the River Leven (also called the Leven Water) some 26 km through Leslie and Glenrothes to the Firth of Forth at Levenmouth. The loch is eutrophic, studded with seven islands — Castle Island with Lochleven Castle (14th-16th c. Douglas stronghold), St Serf's Island with its Augustinian priory (12th c.), and bird islands — and ringed now by the Loch Leven National Nature Reserve, a Ramsar wetland for pink-footed geese, ducks, and ospreys, and by the heritage trout fishery (brown trout, Loch Leven trout strain) celebrated since the 17th century. The town of Kinross stands at its west end; the motorway hums along its south shore; walkers circle it on the 21-km Heritage Trail.

  • Head sense: Loch Leven (Kinross) and its island castle: e.g. prisoner in Loch Leven, she escaped from Loch Leven in May 1568. History: Mary, defeated at Carberry Hill, was brought to the Douglas castle in June 1567, miscarried twins, signed abdication in favour of infant James VI, and escaped a year later with the aid of Willie Douglas stealing the keys. Scott retells it in The Abbot (1820), which Ruskin read on the spot. The name thus means captivity, abdication, escape, and Douglas loyalty-turned-treachery.
  • River sense: the River Leven (Fife) and its namesakes: e.g. salmon in the Leven, mills on the Leven. Scotland has three major Levens — the Fife Leven from Loch Leven, the Dunbartonshire Leven from Loch Lomond to the Clyde at Dumbarton, and Highland Loch Leven (a sea-loch at Glencoe) with its own Water of Leven — plus English Levens in Cumbria and Yorkshire. All share the same Celtic water-name; context disambiguates. The Fife Leven powered flax, paper, and linoleum mills; the Dumbarton Leven powered dyeing and shipbuilding; the Highland Leven drowned in Campbell-MacDonald memory.
  • Archaic common-noun homonym: leven (lightning): quite distinct in origin, Middle English leven, levyn, levin meant a flash of lightning or a thunderbolt; e.g. Spenser's swift as leven, Shakespeare's levin-brand, the surname Levin. Pronounced /ˈlɛvɪn/ and now obsolete except in poetry and surnames, it comes from Germanic light-words, not from Celtic water. Dictionaries list it separately; Ruskin does not mean it, but any full account of leven must warn readers that Leven the loch and leven the lightning are homographs with wholly different histories. A third spelling variant, leven for leaven (yeast), is a mere early-modern spelling.
  • Surname and title sense: Leven as family and earldom: e.g. Alexander Leslie, 1st Earl of Leven (covenanting general), the town of Leven (Fife), Levenmouth. The Fife port takes its name from the river mouth; the earldom (created 1641) from the territory; the surname from residence. The bridge is normal place-to-person metonymy.
  • Literary-tourist sense (source nuance): Loch Leven means Scott pilgrimage: to read the Abbot at Kinross is to sit by the real water with the novel open, matching castle windows to chapters. Victorian steamers, railway posters, and modern interpretation boards all sell the same loop — loch, castle, novel, queen. Ruskin's child-mind closes the circuit: because the queen certainly lived there, the White Lady may live in the next glen.
  • Natural-history sense: Loch Leven trout and geese: anglers say a Leven trout for the fast-growing, pink-fleshed brown trout strain stocked worldwide; birders say Leven in October for 20,000 pink-feet roosting. Here the proper noun becomes a biological trademark, like Norfolk Horn or Aberdeen Angus.

Spelling is fixed as Leven for loch and river (never Levin, Leaven in modern maps), pronounced /ˈliːvən/ with long ee and reduced second vowel, quite unlike eleven or leaven /ˈlɛvən/. The archaic lightning word, by contrast, is now spelled levin when revived, to avoid confusion. To write Loch Leven is to invoke queens, Douglases, trout, and geese at once — a small shallow water with an outsized place in Scottish memory.
