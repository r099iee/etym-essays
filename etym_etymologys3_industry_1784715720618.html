The history of industry is the history of a metaphor that built a civilisation: a Latin word meaning literally 'building within' came to name the personal virtue of diligence, and then, in the space of two centuries, migrated from the diligent person to the factories and sectors those diligent persons built. Each stage of that journey is visible in the word's form and in the societies that used it.

The Indo-European root: spreading and building. The word traces to the Proto-Indo-European root *sterh₃-, 'to spread out, extend, strew', a root whose physical sense is clear from its daughters: Greek στόρνυμι (stórnymi) 'I spread, strew', Sanskrit stṛṇāti 'he strews', Latin sternere 'to spread out, lay flat' (whence English stratum and, via strata, street), and Germanic *strawjaną 'to strew' (English strew). The bridge from 'spreading out' to 'building' is natural and ancient: to build is to lay materials out and pile them up in order. In Proto-Italic the root gave *strō- 'to spread, build', and in Latin this became the verb struere, 'to build, construct, arrange, pile up' — the source of English construct, instruct, and obstruct, all of which preserve the image of building or piling within a structure of meaning.

Latin: the word is built. Upon struere Latin formed the adjective industrius, 'diligent, painstaking, active', by prefixing the archaic indu-, an old by-form of in- meaning 'in, within' (the same element appears in indutiae, 'truce', and in indagare 'to track'). Industrius is thus literally 'one who builds within' — a person who constructs diligence and achievement from the inside, out of their own resources, exactly as a builder lays materials out and piles them into a structure. From the adjective came the abstract noun industria, 'diligence, purposeful activity, assiduity', attested in the first century BCE. The Roman conception is revealing: industry was an inner construction, the steady piling up of effort within the person, and the word was used of statesmen, generals, and scholars as their most practical virtue.

Through French into English: the virtue sense is settled. Latin industria passed into Old French as industrie, 'skill, activity, diligence', and into Middle English in the fourteenth century as industrie. In early modern English the word settled firmly into the virtue sense — habitual diligence and untiring application — and this remained its primary meaning for three hundred years. Biographies, conduct books, and sermons praised industry as the foundation of character and of fortune; an 'industrious' man was the ideal of the age. The word could also mean 'skill, clever contrivance' in older English, a sense now obsolete but visible in the French and Latin heritage.

The migration to economics: from person to process. The decisive change came in the eighteenth century. Political economists — above all Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776) — made 'industry' the engine of national prosperity: it was the diligent labour of the population that produced wealth, and 'the annual industry of every nation' was the fund that supplied its wants. The word, which had named the person's inner virtue, began to name the productive activity itself and then the organised sphere in which it happened. The Industrial Revolution completed the migration: by the nineteenth century 'industry' meant the factory system, the manufacturing sector, and each organised branch of production — the cotton industry, the iron industry — and 'industrial' named the new world of machines, mills, and labour relations. The older virtue sense receded but never died: it remains alive in biography and literary praise, exactly as in the present card, where 'His industry was stupendous' describes the relentless scholarly labour of a writer who produced on an infinite variety of subjects.

Sound changes and the settled form. The word's shape changed little in English — Latin industria through Old French industrie to English industry — since it arrived late, through the learned and French channels, after the great English vowel shifts. The stress settled on the first syllable, and the spelling with -y fixed in the early modern period. What changed was not the form but the meaning, and that change — from inner diligence to organised production — is the word's real story, a story of how an ancient metaphor for personal effort came to name the collective machinery of the modern economy while still, in every biography, remembering the single diligent mind that 'builds within'.
