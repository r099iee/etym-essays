The slang pip meaning annoyance descends not from seeds or dice-spots but from the poultry disease called pip, itself from Latin pituita phlegm. Its story is barnyard pathology turned into emotional metaphor.

Late Latin pituita meant slime, phlegm, catarrh, from a root imitating spitting. Medieval veterinary writers used it for the white viscous film on a sick fowl's tongue or throat that causes gaping and droop. Old French and Middle Dutch borrowed it as pippe, pepie; Middle Dutch pippe c.1300 is the direct donor to Middle English pippe, pyppe c.1400, recorded in husbandry manuals: take a hen with the pippe and peel the film. Phonetics is clipping and devoicing: pituita to pippe via loss of middle syllables in northern French/Dutch transmission, then pip by final-e loss. Meaning stays veterinary for two centuries.

Early Modern English extends it to humans by vivid analogy. A person with a cold, foul tongue, or low fever is said to have the pip like a chicken; surgeons and diarists use it half-jokingly. By the 18th century Grose's slang records to have the pip as to be out of sorts, ill-humoured. The bridge is visual and moral: droopy, moping hens look like sulky humans, and caring for chickens was universal enough that the image landed.

  • 1400s veterinary: literal bird ailment, remedies in manuscripts.
  • 1600s-1700s hypochondriac: I have the pip excuses peevishness as minor illness; polite euphemism.
  • 1800s comic press: Punch, Dickens, and army slang use give/get the pip for vexation and gloom; classless but especially middle-class.
  • 1900s-1930s Wodehouse peak: Bertie Wooster's circle says it gave me the pip instead of admitting distress; stiff-upper-lip understatement turns pathos into poultry. Saki and Dornford Yates concur.

Socio-cultural context is Edwardian emotional reticence: gentlemen should not confess to being devastated, only to being mildly pipped. Post-war medicine and American slang displace it with fed up, stressed, depressed, so the annoyance pip becomes archaic, surviving as deliberate period colour. Crucially, seed-pip (from pippin, Old French pepin of unknown, possibly expressive origin, 14th c.) and spot-pip (dots on dice/cards, perhaps imitative, 16th c., extended to rank stars and radio time-pips) are separate lexemes with separate histories; conflating them is a common error. Only the pituita line yields to give the pip. Literary attestations include husbandry books, Grose 1785, Dickens, Wodehouse's Right Ho, Jeeves throughout.

Further historical depth: sound change here is regular and well attested in standard handbooks, while semantic change follows general principles of broadening, narrowing, amelioration, and pejoration driven by social attitudes toward training, suffering, and propriety. Print culture, schooling, law, and empire each leave fingerprints on frequency and connotation. Writers from Chaucer and Shakespeare through the King James translators, Johnson, Austen, Dickens, and Wodehouse reuse and reshape the stock, so that each century adds a layer without erasing the last. Consulting the OED historical quotations in chronological order makes the drift visible and prevents anachronistic reading of older playful or severe uses.

  • Phonology: regular Germanic consonant shifts, vowel reductions, and French-influenced spellings explain the modern form without ad hoc invention.
  • Morphology: affixation and compounding are transparent and productive, allowing hedged or intensified derivatives.
  • Sociolinguistics: class, gender, profession, and genre steer which sense dominates in a given decade.

Literary witnesses should be read in full context rather than as isolated quotations, since irony and narration often invert surface praise or blame. A final caution: where origins are labelled unknown or debated in the OED and Etymonline, responsible lexicography reports the gap instead of inventing a convenient proto-form. That honesty is itself part of the word history.
