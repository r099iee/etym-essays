- [Late 15th–16th c. / Early Modern English emergence] — Borrowed via Old and Middle French estrangement, initially describing the physical removal, banishment, or practical keeping at a distance of persons or properties — [perception: understood as an external act of displacement, turning a countryman into a stranger or outsider]
- [17th c. / Jacobean & Caroline Eras] — Rapidly shifted inward to capture moral, psychological, and spiritual alienation, prominently appearing in religious prose and theological commentaries to denote the soul's alienation from God and divine grace through sin — [perception: regarded as a profound spiritual tragedy and moral transgression of communion]
- [18th c. / Enlightenment & Legal Jurisprudence] — Formally codified in civil, ecclesiastical, and marital jurisprudence (estrangement of affections / de facto physical separation of husband and wife without absolute dissolution of marriage bonds) — [perception: perceived as a serious social scandal and disruption of patriarchal civic order]
- [19th c. / Romantic & Victorian Eras] — Elevated into a central theme of domestic literature (Austen, the Brontës, Dickens, Eliot), representing the quiet, corrosive emotional chasms opening between parents and children, siblings, or lovers due to pride, class rigidity, or moral hypocrisy — [perception: viewed with deep melancholic pathos as an agonizing, self-inflicted domestic tragedy]
- [20th–21st c. / Contemporary Psychological & Anthropological Era] — Standardized in family systems psychology, sociology, and literary mythography (including Tolkien's portrayal of humanity's estrangement from Hobbits or nature), denoting the voluntary or involuntary cessation of contact between kin — [perception: recognized as a widespread, complex psychological phenomenon involving trauma, boundary-setting, or gradual historical drift]
