FLINT (noun) denotes a hard, glassy, microcrystalline (cryptocrystalline) form of quartz — a fine-grained variety of the mineral silica — typically dark gray, brown, black, or bluish-black, which occurs as nodules in chalk and limestone and breaks with a sharp, curved conchoidal fracture producing razor-edged fragments. Two properties made flint uniquely valuable in human history: its conchoidal fracture allows it to be knapped (flaked) into razor-sharp tools, blades, arrowheads, and hand-axes; and it is hard enough to strike white-hot sparks when rubbed or struck against iron or steel. Flint is thus simultaneously the most ancient tool-stone and the most ancient fire-starter of the human species, and the two roles are encoded in the very word.

In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 4, the idiom "before you could say tinder and FLINT" measures the speed of the goblin ambush by the time needed to utter the two words of the fire-making formula. Flint and tinder were the inseparable halves of the traditional fire-maker's kit: the flint was struck against a piece of steel to shed sparks, and the tinder caught them. The phrase "tinder and flint" became a fixed pair — the two names always travelling together — and Tolkien turns it into a folk-measure of instantaneous speed, the same grammatical family as "before you could say Jack Robinson." The homely, fireside register of the words contrasts with the brutal, supernatural swiftness of the goblins' violence, lending the scene its dark comic texture.

  • Polysemy 1 — the mineral/rock (primary concrete sense): the cryptocrystalline quartz stone, used since the Stone Age for tools and weapons; the geological core sense.
  • Polysemy 2 — fire-striking stone: the piece of flint struck against steel (or a flintlock mechanism) to produce sparks; the hearth and firearms sense, inseparable from tinder in the historical kit.
  • Polysemy 3 — flintlock mechanism: by the 1680s, flint names the flintstone in the flintlock musket; the whole firing mechanism is called a flintlock, and a soldier or gun so armed may be called "a flintlock." The semantic bridge is the same spark-striking function.
  • Polysemy 4 — figurative hardness (flinty): since Old English, flint and the derived adjective flinty mean "extremely hard, unyielding, obdurate," applied to hearts, wills, persons, or glances; a "flinty-hearted" person is pitiless. The bridge is the rock's impenetrable hardness.
  • Polysemy 5 — skinflint: from c. 1700, a skinflint is a miser — one who would "skin a flint" (that is, flay a stone) to save a penny, an image of absurd frugality; here flint stands for the hardest, most valueless object imaginable.
  • Polysemy 6 — flint-glass and industrial uses: flint-glass is heavy, brilliant lead-crystal glass so called because crushed flint silica was originally used in its making; flint also appears in archaeology ("flint tool"), and as a proper noun in place names (e.g., Flint, Michigan) and in the pop-culture surname Flintstone (from the 1960s cartoon The Flintstones, punning on the Stone Age).
  • Polysemy 7 — figurative catalyst: in journalism and politics, "flint" appears in "tinder and flint" style collocations meaning the sparking agent or the essential ingredient that ignites a situation.

Why the secondary meanings developed: every figurative use of flint is anchored in one or both of its two physical miracles — conchoidal fracture (sharpness, splitting) and spark-striking hardness. Hardness gives flinty "obdurate, pitiless" and skinflint; sparking gives flintlock and the tinder-and-flint pair; both together give flint its role in fire and weaponry from the Stone Age to the present. The word has stayed remarkably stable because the material itself has never lost its utility.

Distinctions from near-synonyms: chert is the general geological term for the rock of which flint is a specific, dark, nodular variety found in chalk; quartz is the parent mineral, flint being its opaque, microcrystalline form; quartzite is a metamorphic rock formed from quartz sandstone that lacks conchoidal fracture; obsidian is volcanic glass, equally sharp but glossy, black, and formed by volcanic cooling, used by peoples without flint; silex is the old Latin/poetic word for flint and survives in "silica"; steel is not a stone at all but the carbon-hardened iron that strikes the spark from the flint.
