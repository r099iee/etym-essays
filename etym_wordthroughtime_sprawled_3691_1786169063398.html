- Middle English era: "sprawled" (from spraulen/sprowlen) named convulsive, uncontrolled limb-movement — the thrashing of a body in struggle or death-throes — perceived as violent, uncoordinated, purposeless motion.
- Early Modern period: the violence drains out; to sprawl comes to mean lying or sitting with the limbs spread carelessly wide — a relaxed yet ungainly posture, the body spilling past its composed outline without design or dignity.
- 18th-19th centuries: the posture sense settles as the dominant one — sprawled limbs signal abandon, weariness, or deep absorption, and the word gains its modern flavour of accidental, slightly grotesque dishevelment rather than chosen elegance.
- 20th century to present: the image expands beyond bodies — "urban sprawl" transfers the careless outward scattering of limbs to the unchecked spread of cities — and sprawled now reads as the default word for anything flung out over too much space in an unplanned way.
