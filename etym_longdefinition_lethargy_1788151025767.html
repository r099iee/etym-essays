Core meaning.Lethargy is a state of deep physical and mental sluggishness: a dull, persistent drowsiness in which the body feels heavy, attention wanders, and the will is slow to stir. It denotes more than ordinary tiredness, carrying the implication of a morbid, habitual, or else deeply absorbed inertia that resists effort. In Will Durant's The Story of Civilization, excerpted above, tropical heat produces both lethargy and disease: the word there wears its classical weight, a somnolence of body and spirit that siphons human energy away from art, learning, and the other "inessentials" out of which civilization is made.

The medical sense. In clinical use, lethargy is a pathological drowsiness or stupor — the unshakeable sleepiness of fever, infection, encephalitis, metabolic imbalance, or drug effect. Galenic medicine classed it among the humoral disorders, imagining the brain cooled or clogged by excess phlegm, and Renaissance physicians distinguished a true lethargia from ordinary sleep. The terminology survives in neurology: the epidemic encephalitis of 1917–1926 was named encephalitis lethargica, "the sleepy sickness", and its survivors were famously described by Oliver Sacks in Awakenings. A person so affected is lethargic; the clinical picture is of a patient who can be roused but sinks back the instant stimulation ceases.

  • Physical torpor: heavy limbs, slowed movement, unshakeable drowsiness.
  • Mental inertia: dulled attention, sluggish thought, inability to be stirred to interest.
  • Figurative / institutional: "voter lethargy", "economic lethargy", "the lethargy of reform" — nations, markets, and organisations described as asleep.
  • Aesthetic: the languid stillness of a hot afternoon or a becalmed sea; a mood, not a diagnosis.

Polysemy and the semantic bridge. The leap from "morbid sleep" to "general apathy" is a transfer from the sickbed to everyday life: anything that reduces a person, a market, a parliament, or an age to a sleepy crawl can now be called lethargic. The word never wholly loses the Greek shadow beneath it — lēthē, "forgetfulness" — so that lethargy implies not merely rest but an oblivion in which duties, pleasures, and dangers alike slip from mind. This is precisely why the word is so often a rebuke in moral and political prose: the lethargic are not merely tired, they are forgetful of what matters. In modern usage the range is wide:

  • Metabolic: post-meal lethargy, the mid-afternoon slump.
  • Economic: lethargic trading, a lethargic housing market.
  • Psychological: lethargy in depression; the word overlaps the clinical territory of psychomotor retardation.
  • Criticism and media: "lethargic pacing", "lethargic performance" in reviews of films and sport.

Register and behaviour. A formal-to-neutral noun of educated discourse. It collocates with overcome, sink into, rouse from, shake off, and is almost always dyslogistic when applied to persons or institutions; only in mild self-deprecation ("a little Sunday lethargy") does it soften. Unlike rest, calm, or sleep, it implies energy unjustly withheld — the organism's testimony against its own purposes, and a civilization's testimony against its own decline.
