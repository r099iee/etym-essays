compendium is a noun (plural compendiums, and in learned use compendia) naming a compact work that gathers the substance of a larger one: a summary, digest, systematic handbook or ordered collection. In the source it denotes Mario Nizzoli’s Thesaurus Ciceronianus, which was claimed to be a compendium of “all pure Ciceronian language” — a complete, sorted treasury of the vocabulary that a Ciceronian stylist might legitimately use.

Etymologically the word is a metaphor from the scales: Latin compendium is “that which is weighed together”, hence “a saving”, and then “a shortcut, an abridgment”. The modern sense preserves both halves of that image — the gathering (com-, “together”) and the economy (pendere, “to weigh”). A compendium promises that the whole has been weighed, selected and balanced into portable form.

Primary senses

  • A concise treatment of a subject: “a compendium of Roman law”, “a compendium of physics”. It implies completeness of coverage in miniature rather than mere brevity; the reader is meant to need nothing else.
  • A collection of examples, texts or items: “a compendium of medieval recipes”, “a compendium of errors”. Here the emphasis falls on the gathering rather than on abbreviation, and the parts may be reproduced rather than digested.
  • A systematic handbook or manual: the word often appears in titles — Aquinas’s Compendium theologiae, Descartes’s Compendium musicae — where it signals an ordered, teachable summary designed for students.

Secondary, technical and idiomatic senses

  • Boxed game sets (British): “a compendium of games” is a box containing several board games with shared pieces and boards. The sense extends the “collection in one container” idea to physical objects and is now one of the commonest everyday uses in Britain.
  • Legal and canonical: a “compendium” of a code or of statutes is a systematic abridgment used for teaching or reference; the word is traditional in the titles of institutional summaries and in canon-law literature.
  • Book history: Renaissance and Baroque compendia — of anatomy, fortification, rhetoric, music, even witchcraft — were a dominant textbook genre, digesting ancient and modern authority into portable, examinable form.
  • Modern data and marketing jargon: “a content compendium”, “a data compendium” — a curated aggregation, often digital. The word lends gravity and an aura of completeness to what may in fact be a list.
  • Faint classical echoes: Latin compendium could mean a “saving”, a “profit” or a “short way”; English keeps only traces, as when a compendium is praised for “saving the reader time” or for offering “a short road” to a subject.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge

The Latin noun was built from com- “together” and pendere “to weigh”: to weigh things together is to balance accounts, and a balance that comes out in one’s favour yields a saving. From “saving” the word moved to “short way” — a route that saves distance — and then to “abridgment”, a book that saves reading. Classical Latin uses compendium for a shortcut and for making short work of a matter (compendium facere); the transferred literary sense, a work that spares the reader labour by condensing a larger one, was available to Latin and became dominant in post-classical and medieval usage. The further shift to “collection” is natural: a book that gathers the best of many books is both shorter than the whole and larger than any part, and the word came to name the gathering as much as the shortening.

Register and collocations. The word is formal, bookish and faintly antiquarian. It lends dignity and implies orderly completeness. It collocates with of plus a subject (“a compendium of knowledge”, “a compendium of useful information”) and with title-like capitalization. Unlike summary, it suggests a durable reference work; unlike encyclopedia, it suggests selection and brevity rather than exhaustive alphabetization. The adjective compendious (“concise but comprehensive”) and the rare verb compend preserve the same idea of profitable abbreviation.

In this reading. To call the Thesaurus Ciceronianus a compendium of “all pure Ciceronian language” is to make a strong claim about completeness and purity: the book promised the whole vocabulary of one author, sorted and certified, so that a pupil could write like Cicero without reading all of Cicero. The word’s promise of economy — an entire style weighed together into one volume — is precisely what the Ciceronian purists wanted, and precisely what later critics of mere Ciceronianism attacked as a shortcut that substituted inventory for thought.
