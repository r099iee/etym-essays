- [5th–4th c. BCE: Classical Greece] - coined as the Greek adjective blásphēmos (evil-speaking, slanderous, scurrilous), used by Demosthenes, Plato, and Aeschylus to describe any malicious defamation, verbal abuse, or ill-omened utterance spoken against mortal citizens or public figures - [perception: condemned as shameful slander, civic malice, and an inauspicious disturbance of public assemblies]
- [3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE: Hellenistic Judaism & Early Christianity] - radically narrowed in the Septuagint and Greek New Testament, transforming from general civic slander into the specific, ultimate sin of reviling the God of Israel, claiming divine honors, or profaning the holy Name - [perception: viewed with absolute horror as the supreme theological crime punishable by divine wrath and mortal stoning]
- [4th–15th c.: Christian Roman Empire & Middle Ages] - Latinized as blasphēmus and codified in canon law, civil codes (Justinian Code), and scholastic theology as a capital offense and a mortal sin of the tongue, often prosecuted by inquisitorial courts - [perception: regarded as an existential threat to Christendom that invited plagues, famines, and military defeats upon the entire community]
- [16th–18th c.: Reformation, Common Law & Enlightenment] - imported into Middle/Early Modern English (via Old French blasfeme), established as the common law crime of blasphemous libel to protect state religion, while Enlightenment philosophers (Voltaire, Locke) began arguing for freedom of conscience - [perception: fiercely punished by the state as political subversion, but increasingly critiqued by intellectuals as tyrannical religious persecution]
- [19th–20th c.: Victorian Judicial Liberalization] - courts progressively restricted the crime to scurrilous, profane, and indecent abuse of faith rather than honest intellectual skepticism; common law blasphemy crimes were progressively repealed across western democracies - [perception: shifted from a state-enforced capital sin into an offensive breach of social civility and public decorum]
- [Late 20th–21st c.: Secular Hyperbole & Cultural Clashes] - widely bleached into colloquial hyperbole for culinary, artistic, or cultural violations (e.g. pineapple on pizza), while remaining a flashpoint of deadly global geopolitical tension regarding free speech, satire, and minority religious sensitivities - [perception: in secular society, a witty, colorful exaggeration for nonconformity; in traditional theological communities, an unforgivable insult to sacred identity]
