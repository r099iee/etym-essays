LINEAR CHAIN FOR "bucking" (present participle of "to buck", "to leap with an arched back", said of a horse)

Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *bhuĝo- (c. 4500–2500 BCE) — "he-goat, ram". A root reconstructed for the male of certain horned animals. Its precise vocalism is debated (some reconstruct *bhug-), and it is one of a small set of pastoral terms whose reflexes cluster in the western and Indo-Iranian branches.

Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic: *bukkaz (c. 500 BCE – 1st millennium CE) — "he-goat, buck". The PIE root becomes a masculine noun for the male goat, later extended in Germanic to the male deer.

Stage 3 — Old English: bucca (before 12th century, attested in Old English) — "he-goat, buck". The same stem also yields Old English buc / bucc "male deer". Cognate forms appear across Germanic (Old Norse bukkr, Dutch bok, German Bock) and beyond (Avestan būza "he-goat", Old Irish bocc "he-goat").

Stage 4 — Middle English: buk, bucke (13th–15th centuries) — "male deer; he-goat". The noun continues as the ordinary word for a male deer or goat.

Stage 5 — English noun: buck (16th century onward) — "a male deer, goat, or other male animal".

Stage 6 — English verb: buck (19th century, American English) — "to leap with the back arched and legs stiff, as a horse trying to unseat its rider". This is a denominal verb: the behavior of a leaping buck (male deer/goat) was transferred to horses, and the animal's name became the name of the movement. First recorded in the American West; by the late 19th century the figurative sense "to resist, oppose" (buck the trend) develops.

Stage 7 — English: bucking (19th century onward) — the present participle/gerund of buck, used as a noun ("a violent bucking") and as an adjective ("a bucking bronco"). BUCKING ◄ TARGET

NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: (1) The PIE reconstruction *bhuĝo- is traditional (Pokorny) but the root's exact shape and spread are debated; some scholars treat the goat-word as a possible non-Indo-European substrate or areal term, and Beekes urges caution. (2) The semantic transfer from male-deer behavior to horse behavior is transparent but not directly attested — we cannot watch the moment the noun became the verb in living speech, though the 19th-century American record makes the direction clear. (3) Do NOT confuse this family with the unrelated word buck "dollar" (from buckskin), pass the buck (from a poker marker), or the obsolete verb buck "to wash clothes in lye"; these are separate etymons.
