lapping is the present participle of the verb lap, and in the source text it describes water moving against rock — the deepest stream "flowed lapping and bubbling" — the soft, repeated, musical washing of water that is the word's most poetic sense. The same form also describes an animal drinking with its tongue, and a cluster of figurative uses that all turn on the same gentle, repetitive action.

PRIMARY SENSE — WATER WASHING GENTLY

  • Of water: to wash against something in a series of gentle, soft splashes: "the waves lapped the shore", "the tide lapped at the sea-wall", "water lapped the sides of the boat".
  • The motion is gentle, rhythmic, and repeated — lapping water is the sound of a calm lake or a slow tide, the opposite of crashing, pounding, or surging.
  • The image is almost always soothing: lapping is the water-sound of peace, solitude, and drowsiness — lullabies and lakes lap.
  • Tolkien's "flowed lapping and bubbling" adds the companion word bubbling: the stream is alive with small liquid sounds, gentle and musical, at the foot of a cliff.

SECONDARY SENSE — DRINKING WITH THE TONGUE

  • Of an animal (or an indelicate person): to drink by scooping liquid up with the tongue: "the cat lapped the milk from the saucer", "the dog lapped at the puddle".
  • The motion is quick, repeated, and slightly noisy — the sound of lapping is the shared feature with the water sense (see the bridge below).
  • Figurative: "to lap up" = to accept or absorb greedily and uncritically: "she lapped up the compliments", "the audience lapped up every word" — the eager, gulping animal becomes the model of uncritical enthusiasm.

THE SENSORY BRIDGE BETWEEN WATER AND DRINKING

  • The two senses are joined by sound: a dog lapping milk makes the same soft, repeated slapping noise as water against a shore — lexicographers treat the splash sense as either an imitation of that sound or a direct extension of the drinking sense; either way, "lapping" names a sound before it names a motion.
  • This sound-first identity is what makes the word so effective in poetry: "lapping" carries the whole scene — the tongue, the wave, the rhythm — in one onomatopoeic syllable.

TERTIARY SENSE — WRAPPING AND OVERLAPPING (a distinct homonym that has fused in the form)

  • The verb lap (from Old English læppa "a fold, flap, skirt of a garment") means "to fold, wrap, or wind around": "to lap a bandage around a limb", "lapped in luxury" (enfolded, wrapped in comfort — a favourite poetic phrase), "lapping" in the sense of overlapping edges.
  • From "overlap" comes the racing sense: to lap a competitor = to complete a full circuit more than they have, so that one's path "laps over" theirs — "he lapped the field twice"; a "lap" of a track is one circuit.
  • From "fold" also comes the noun lap: the flat front of the body where a seated person's garment folds — "the child sat on her lap".
  • These senses are etymologically separate from the splash/drink verb, but the identical form means modern speakers feel them as one word: "lapping" in a race report and "lapping" at the shore are perceived as the same gentle circling motion.

FURTHER TECHNICAL AND FIGURATIVE USES

  • Of time and schedules: "lapping" staff or shifts = overlapping rosters (computing and shift-work).
  • Of engineering: "lapping" = polishing with a lap (a rotating tool); to "lap" a surface is to smooth it by rubbing with a revolving disk — a technical sense from the rubbing/overlap family.
  • Of sound: "lapping" is used of any gentle repetitive liquid sound — "the lapping of oars", "lapping water in a cave".
  • Of the mind: "lapping up" news, gossip, praise — the greedy drinking metaphor applied to information.

REGISTER AND STYLE

  • Lapping is a gentle, sensory, poetic word; it belongs to nature writing, lullabies, Romantic and pastoral poetry, and the description of calm water.
  • The drinking sense is homely and slightly comic when applied to humans (someone "lapping up" praise is a little animal-like).
  • Tolkien's use — a stream flowing "lapping and bubbling" under an inland cliff — is the word at its most musical: water as a living, murmuring presence.
