disposition (noun) — the word's senses cluster around two ideas inherited from its Latin ancestor disponere, "to place apart, set in order": the ordering of a person's inner qualities into a settled mental and emotional character, and the placing or ordering of things in space, in law, or in management. In everyday modern English the first cluster dominates — disposition most often names a person's natural temperament — but the second cluster is alive and standard in legal, military, financial, and technical registers. 1. Natural temperament and character (primary, everyday sense)
  • A person's inherent, settled qualities of mind and character; the natural or usual cast of temperament. "He was a man of cheerful disposition" means cheerfulness was his standing condition, not a passing mood.
  • Because it names the underlying climate of a personality rather than a single moment of feeling, disposition contrasts with mood (a passing state): a person has a cheerful disposition but is in a good mood today.
  • It is used of animals and even things as well as persons ("a gentle disposition of the breed"); applied to things it shades toward "tendency" (see sense 3).
2. Arrangement, positioning, and deployment (spatial and military sense)
  • The way things are placed or arranged in a particular order: "the disposition of the troops", "the disposition of furniture in the room".
  • In military usage the plural dispositions means the tactical arrangement of forces or the measures taken to meet an attack.
  • In rhetoric and classical prose it means the arrangement or ordering of the parts of a speech or argument — one of the five classical canons of rhetoric, in which dispositio names the ordering of invention into an effective sequence.
3. Inclination, tendency, or propensity (psychological leaning)
  • A natural tendency to behave or act in a certain way: "a disposition to optimism", "a disposition toward secrecy". Here the word names a leaning or bent, weaker than a fixed trait and stronger than a whim.
  • In medicine, a disposition to a disease is the constitutional tendency or susceptibility to develop it; the common compound predisposition names this susceptibility explicitly.
4. Legal and financial sense: disposal, settlement, transfer (law and finance)
  • Disposition is the standard legal term for the settlement, transfer, or disposal of property, an estate, or assets — "the disposition of the estate", "disposition of assets in the will".
  • In criminal justice, a case's disposition is its final outcome or settlement (conviction, acquittal, dismissal, plea deal).
  • It also names the power or right of control over something: "funds at one's disposition", "the matter is at the board's disposition".
5. The semantic bridge that unites these senses
  • The Latin noun dispositio literally means "a placing apart" (dis- "apart, in different directions" + positio "a placing"). Classical Latin already used it for the arrangement of words, troops, or parts of a building.
  • Late medieval medicine transferred the arranging metaphor from the outer world to the inner person: a person's temperament was thought to be the arrangement of their bodily humours and qualities, and so "disposition" came to name the ordering of the mind and character.
  • The financial sense is the same spatial metaphor applied to property: to dispose of an asset is to "place it apart" — to set it away from oneself by sale, gift, or settlement.
  • Thus all senses share one root picture: things — whether troops, words, property, or qualities of mind — set apart and arranged in a definite order. The modern word is therefore polysemous not by accident but by inheritance.
Register and usage notes
  • Disposition is formal and literary; in ordinary speech "temperament" or "character" often replaces the temperament sense, and "layout" or "arrangement" the spatial sense.
  • Distinguish disposition (settled character) from predisposition (a prior leaning, especially to a disease or to an opinion), and from disposal (the act or power of getting rid of something).
  • The adjective disposed ("well disposed toward") and the verb dispose ("dispose of the matter") continue the same root family in daily use.
