scholastic is an adjective — and, less often, a noun — from Latin scholasticus, Greek skholastikos, “of a school, devoted to study”. Its modern meanings fan out from that single educational core into three distinct zones: the medieval, the institutional and the pejorative.

In the source passage the medieval sense is at issue. “Arguments went on about the propriety of certain scholastic treatises in terms of whether or not they were written in language purely Ciceronian.” The treatises are works of scholastic theology and philosophy — the dialectical products of the medieval universities — and the argument is a humanist one: may the style of the Schoolmen be measured against Cicero’s? The adjective therefore carries, in this context, a faintly adversarial edge, since the humanists who judged such works by Ciceronian standards generally found them barbarous.

Sense 1 — of the medieval schools (scholasticism)

Relating to the theology, philosophy and teaching method of the universities of western Europe from the eleventh to the fifteenth century, characterised by the use of Aristotelian logic, the disputed question and the systematic summa.

  • The method. Lectio (reading and commentary), quaestio (the posing of a problem with arguments for and against) and disputatio (formal public debate) formed a machinery for reconciling authoritative texts.
  • The authorities. Scripture, the Fathers, Peter Lombard’s Sentences (c. 1150) and, from the thirteenth century, the whole corpus of Aristotle, read through Boethius and the Arabic commentators.
  • The masters. Anselm and Abelard in the twelfth century; Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventure and Duns Scotus in the thirteenth; William of Ockham and Jean Buridan in the fourteenth.
  • The doctrinal divide. The great scholastic quarrel between realism and nominalism over the status of universals shaped the whole later history of philosophy.
  • The contrast. Historians distinguish scholastic theology, argumentative and systematic, from monastic theology, exegetical and contemplative.

In this sense the word is descriptive and neutral in modern scholarship, but it was not neutral when it was coined in its hostile form: the humanists used it to name a method they thought barbarous.

Sense 2 — of schools and education (neutral, chiefly American)

The oldest and simplest descendant of the Greek: pertaining to schools, schooling or study. It survives chiefly in formal and administrative usage.

  • scholastic achievement, scholastic aptitude, scholastic records, scholastic year — the vocabulary of school reports and admissions offices.
  • scholastic sports and scholastic journalism — American terms for activities organised by secondary schools rather than by clubs.
  • The Scholastic Aptitude Test, introduced in 1926 and later renamed the Scholastic Assessment Test before the initials SAT were made to stand for nothing at all.
  • Scholastic as the name of the American publisher of children’s school magazines, which fixed the word in the minds of generations of pupils.

Here the word is wholly neutral, slightly formal and faintly old-fashioned; it belongs to the forms sent home to parents rather than to the classroom itself.

Sense 3 — pedantic, over-subtle (pejorative)

Concerned with formal distinctions for their own sake; hairsplitting; remote from practical or humane concerns. The collocations are telling: scholastic quibbles, a scholastic distinction, scholastic pedantry, scholastic subtlety.

The semantic bridge is historical rather than logical. Humanists and Reformers attacked the Schoolmen as men who had replaced eloquence and piety with syllogisms, and the adjective absorbed their contempt. Once “scholastic” had been used for a century as a term of abuse in the quarrel over Ciceronian Latin, it could be transferred to any argument that seemed to prefer technicality to truth.

Sense 4 — the noun

  • A Schoolman. “The scholastics” is the standard collective name for the medieval university theologians.
  • A medieval student or master. In university records a scholasticus is a member of the schools, as opposed to a townsman.
  • A Jesuit in training. In the Society of Jesus a scholastic is a member in the long period of philosophical and theological study between the novitiate and ordination; the house where they study is the scholasticate.
  • A pedant. An archaic English use, now rare, preserved in the older dictionaries.

Register and collocations

The educational sense is neutral and institutional; the medieval sense is scholarly and descriptive; the pejorative sense is openly hostile. The word becomes an insult precisely when it is opposed to original, practical, humane or eloquent. Its typical partners are theology, philosophy, method, disputation, subtlety, quibble, achievement and aptitude.

Why the distinction matters here

The humanist quarrel of the early sixteenth century — Erasmus’s Ciceronianus (1528) is its most famous document — made “scholastic” a stylistic as well as a doctrinal label. To ask whether a scholastic treatise was written in “language purely Ciceronian” is to apply rhetorical criteria to theology, and therefore to treat the Schoolmen as competitors in an art they never claimed to practise. The source’s phrase catches that argument exactly, and the adjective carries both of its historical charges at once: the medieval method and the humanist contempt for it.
