BLURB is, as a noun, a short piece of promotional writing - typically a few sentences of praise or description printed on a book's dust jacket, cover or title page, and by extension any brief puff for a film, play, product or event. "It was claimed in the blurb to a later edition that he rewrote it five times" shows the classic use: the publisher's jacket copy making an enticing claim about the book within. As a verb, to blurb means to write such praise or to be advertised by it: an author blurbs a debut novelist; a novel is blurbed by its famous admirers. The word belongs to the trade vocabulary of publishing but is now universally understood.

The prototypical blurb has a recognisable rhetoric. It names the work a "sensation", a "triumph", "unputdownable", "heartbreaking", "the book of the year"; it quotes, often selectively, from advance reviewers ("Gripping! - The Times"); it sketches plot without spoiling it and character without complicating them. Its function is frankly commercial: to convert a browser into a buyer in under a minute. Because of this, blurb carries a built-in note of scepticism absent from neutral words like summary or description. To call something "mere blurb" or "publisher's blurb" is to hint at exaggeration, puffery and interested praise - the fulsome advertisement Gelett Burgess originally mocked.

Anatomy matters: publishing distinguishes the front-cover blurb (one or two quoted phrases), the back-cover blurb (the main descriptive paragraph plus endorsements), the flap blurb (front and back inside flaps of a hardback jacket), the cover quote or endorsement (a named writer's praise line), and the logline or strapline (the one-sentence hook). In the digital age the blurb has migrated to Amazon product pages, Netflix synopses, press releases and social-media endorsements, but its length and logic are unchanged: a hundred-odd words of temptation. Related jargon includes jacket copy (the neutral trade term), puff (the frankly contemptuous term), advance praise, pull-quote and tagline.

  • Jacket/cover praise (core noun sense): promotional sentences on a book; e.g. "the blurb promises five rewrites". Exactly the source sense.
  • Any short promotional write-up: film posters, album stickers, conference programmes, app-store descriptions; e.g. "the festival blurb". A natural generalisation from books to all media.
  • Endorsement quote: a famous person's public praise used in advertising; e.g. "to get a blurb from a Nobel laureate". Here the blurb is the testimonial itself.
  • Verb - to praise promotionally: "she blurbed his first novel"; "the book is heavily blurbed". Regular verb (blurbed, blurbing); the agent noun blurber and the victim blurbee are occasionally joked about in the trade.
  • Verb - to inscribe a blurb: publishers "blurb" a book when they write its jacket copy; authors "blurb" when they supply quotes for others - two directions of the same commerce.
  • Colloquial extension - splutter or burst: by echo of blurt, blubber and babble, some speakers use blurb for a short confused outburst ("he emitted an incoherent blurb"), though dictionaries treat this as marginal wordplay rather than an established sense.

Polysemy here is shallow because the word is young - barely a century old - but its connotations are rich. The bridge from "jacket puff" to "any hype" follows the path of the object itself: as books competed with films, records and apps, their sales talk kept its nickname. The sceptical edge survives everywhere: readers have learned that blurbs exaggerate, that "gripping" may mean merely competent, and that even the claim in the source sentence (five rewrites) arrives to us mediated by a blurb's commercial interest. To understand blurb is therefore to understand a small piece of media literacy - who is praising whom, on whose cover, and to whose profit.
