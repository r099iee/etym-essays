- [Old English to Middle English: neutral creeping] - Old English slincan means "to creep, crawl, glide" - a movement-word for serpents and vermin, neutral and purely physical; Middle English slinken keeps "to creep, move stealthily."
- [16th-17th century: the moral turn] - The neutral sense "crawl" narrows and darkens: slink comes to mean moving so as to avoid notice, then irreversibly moving with an air of guilt or shame - the crouched, self-diminishing gait of one caught or defeated; this moralization of a movement-word is the central event of its biography, and idioms form around it (to slink away, to slink off with one's tail between one's legs).
- [17th century: the rural branch] - In stock-raising, a slink calf is one born prematurely - the animal having "slipped away" before its time - the same root sense of gliding withdrawal turned to something that escapes before its season.
- [Contemporary sense] - Slinking denotes a permanent, sinuous, self-effacing furtiveness - moving as one who knows, or feels, that he has no right to be seen - the present participle making the guilty motion a lasting condition rather than a single act.
