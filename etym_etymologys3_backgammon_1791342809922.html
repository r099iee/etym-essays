The name backgammon represents a compound coined in Early Modern English during the reign of King Charles I to identify a specific, newly standardized variation of the ancient board game family known across medieval Europe as tables. Its linguistic makeup combines two native Germanic elements: the directional noun back and the archaic dialectal noun gammon, meaning a game or sport.

  • The Ancient Heritage of “Tables”: Long before the word backgammon existed, its structural ancestors dominated Mediterranean and European leisure. The Roman Empire played ludus duodecim scriptorum (“the twelve-lined game”) and later tabula (“board” or “table”), which used twenty-four points and three dice. Carried across Europe by legionaries and merchants, tabula entered medieval languages as the generic term for the board: Old French tables, Medieval Latin tabulae, and Old English tæfl. In medieval England, playing “at the tables” was an aristocratic passion mentioned alongside chess in chivalric romances and Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales.
  • Element 1: The Etymology of “Back”: Old English bæc derives from Proto-Germanic *bakam (signifying the back or rear part of a body), an isolated Germanic noun with obscure deeper Indo-European connections. In Middle English, bak expanded its adverbial and prepositional force to denote return to an original location or movement in reverse. In the compound backgammon, lexicographers have identified three compelling explanations for why this element was chosen:
    • The Re-entry Rule: The most widely accepted philological theory holds that the game was named for the rule where a struck piece (a blot) is removed from the track and forced to travel all the way back to the start, re-entering via the opponent’s inner board.
    • The Home-Bearing Rule: A secondary theory suggests that the objective is to bring all of one’s pieces back to one’s own home board before bearing off.
    • The Folding Board: A third, historical material theory notes that backgammon was traditionally played inside the folding wooden cases whose exterior back was painted as a chessboard or draughts board, making it the game played on the “back of the board.”
  • Element 2: The Etymology of “Gammon” (Game): The second constituent, gammon, is a direct phonetic survival of Old English gamen (meaning joy, glee, mirth, amusement, or athletic contest). Proto-Germanic *gamaną was a collective compound built from prefix *ga- (“together”) and *mann- (“person, human”), literally denoting “people coming together in joyous communion.” While standard Southern Middle English gradually contracted gamen into the monosyllable game (through loss of the unstressed final syllable), northern and regional dialects preserved the disyllabic form with various phonetic shifts, yielding gammon. Thus, gammon in backgammon is simply an archaic, dialectal doublet of our modern word game.
  • Refutation of the Welsh Folk Etymology: In the 18th and early 19th centuries, antiquarian writers (most notably Thomas Pennant in 1778) asserted that backgammon derived from Welsh bach (“little”) and cammawn (“battle”), romanticizing the game as an ancient Celtic miniature combat. Modern historical lexicography, spearheaded by Walter William Skeat and the Oxford English Dictionary, thoroughly demolished this claim: Welsh cammawn is a rare poetic borrowing from Latin scamnum, and Welsh itself borrowed the name of the game directly from English as bacgamon. The compound is incontrovertibly English.
  • Coincidence with Culinary “Gammon”: English possesses an identical homophone gammon meaning cured ham or the hind leg of pork. This culinary term has an entirely unrelated Romance ancestry, arriving in Middle English from Anglo-Norman gスキーon and Old French jambe (leg), descending from Late Latin gamba (hoof or leg). The two words are purely accidental homophones.
  • Literary and Cultural Crystallization: Attested in print by the 1630s and 1640s, back-gammon swiftly superseded older versions of tables in England. When Edmond Hoyle codified its rules in 1743, the name became permanent. Across the 18th and 19th centuries, it was the definitive fireside recreation of the British upper-middle class, setting the stage for Wodehouse’s depiction of Aunt Dahlia using the game to pin down her nephew Bertie Wooster.

Through its two components, backgammon preserves both the memory of a struck checker journeying back to start and the ancient Germanic word for collective human joy.
