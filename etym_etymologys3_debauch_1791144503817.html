The English word debauch is a 16th-century borrowing of Middle French desbaucher (modern débaucher), literally “to lead away from the bauche” — where bauche meant a beam, work-bench, or by extension the workshop. The founding image is wonderfully concrete: the apprentice lured off his bench, the servant Decoaxed from his post, the soldier talked into desertion. From ‘enticing away from work’ the verb slid, already in French, to ‘seducing into idleness and vice’ — the semantic bridge being that truancy from duty was assumed to end in sensual mischief.

  • The French prehistory: Old French desbauchier is attested in the sense of scattering, dislodging, and corrupting; by the 15th–16th centuries desbaucher firmly means to debauch, to corrupt morals, to entice into dissipation. Prefix des- continues Latin dis-/dē- (apart, away), the standard Romance reversive. The base bauche is the puzzle: it plausibly relates to beams and building timber, and by metonymy to the joiner’s bench and the workshop. Whether the compound meant “to chop off the beam” (structural ruin as metaphor) or “to draw the worker from his bench” is genuinely debated among etymologists, and honest dictionaries flag both readings.
  • Entry into English (1590s): the verb arrives in Elizabethan English meaning to corrupt, pervert, or seduce from loyalty — “to debauch the affections,” “to debauch servants from their masters, soldiers from their colours.” Early use is as much political and religious as sexual: recusants ‘debauched’ Protestants and vice versa. Shakespeare’s age already hears the sensual overtone, but the core is still corruption of allegiance.
  • The noun is born (1640s): Civil War and Restoration England nominalises the verb: a debauch is one bout of the behaviour, and debauchery the system of it. The Restoration court — Rochester, Sedley, the Merry Gang — gives the word its permanent colour: midnight taverns, broken windows, forced toasts, and the morning-after sermon. From here the noun’s meaning narrows decisively toward drink, food, and sex, shedding the military-desertion sense.
  • Sound and spelling: English keeps the French spelling minus the s (desbaucher → debauch) while anglicising the vowel: modern RP /dɪˈbɔːtʃ/. The noun’s plural debauches /-ɪz/ preserves the soft -ch-. Related forms proliferate: debauched (adjective, corrupted), debauchee (the corrupted person, on the refugee/payee pattern), debauchment (rare, the act).
  • Semantic drift to the present: Victorian moralists made debauchery a thunder-word, which paradoxically bleached the small noun debauch into near-quaintness; 20th-century writers use it for period flavour (“Regency debauches”), and late-20th-century speakers revive it jokingly for trivial indulgences (“a sugar debauch”). The original ‘workshop’ image is long dead, but the structure survives: every debauch is still, etymologically, a truancy — a little desertion from duty in favour of pleasure.

In short: a Germanic carpenter’s beam became a French work-bench, became truancy, became seduction, became a hangover — one of etymology’s most honest parables of how labour, leisure, and moral panic share a single word.
