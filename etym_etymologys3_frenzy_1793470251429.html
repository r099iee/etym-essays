The etymological voyage of frenzy leads directly into the somatic heart of ancient medicine, where human passions were mapped onto internal anatomy. The ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷʰren-, signifying the mind, the soul, or the midriff as the physical seat of psychic life. In Ancient Greek, this root yielded the rich and versatile noun φρήν (phrēn, genitive phrenós). In Homeric epic, the phrenes were the anatomical tissues surrounding the heart and lungs—specifically the diaphragm or midriff—which the Greeks believed trembled with sorrow, swelled with wrath, and formed the physiological locus where thoughts and passions collided.

When the Hippocratic physicians and later Galen founded classical Greek clinical medicine in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, they systematized these anatomical intuitions into pathology. They designated an acute, life-threatening fever accompanied by violent delirium as φρενῖτις (phrenītis), combining phrēn with the productive medical suffix -ītis (used for inflammatory ailments). In humoral doctrine, phrenītis was caused by an excess of yellow bile overheating the cerebral membranes and the vascular channels connected to the diaphragm, driving the afflicted patient into uncontrollable hallucinations, thrashing limbs, and homicidal or suicidal fury. This condition was sharply distinguished from mania (insanity without fever) and melancholia (depression caused by black bile).

As Greek medicine was absorbed into the Roman world, phrenītis underwent an important morphological mutation. In post-classical and Late Latin medical treatises (around the fourth century CE), the word was adapted into Latin script as phrenēsis, remodeling the original suffix under the powerful influence of Greek abstract action nouns in -sis (such as genesis or paralysis). During the linguistic transition from Late Latin to Vulgar Latin and Gallo-Romance, two critical phonetic shifts occurred: the Greek aspirated plosive ph- (/pʰ/), which had been transliterated in classical Latin, shifted to the labiodental fricative f- (/f/), and the unstressed endings simplified, producing Old French frenesie by the twelfth century.

The transmission into English and subsequent semantic flowering followed a remarkable sequence:

  • Anglo-French Transmission (14th c.): Following the dominance of Norman French in England, frenesie entered Middle English manuscripts (such as Lanfranc's Science of Cirurgie and Chaucer's Knight's Tale) around the late fourteenth century, initially preserving its strict medical diagnosis of brain fever and raving delirium.
  • Phonetic Syncopation (15th c.): Through the natural rhythmic pressure of English Germanic stress—which fell forcefully upon the initial syllable—the weak medial vowel of trisyllabic fren-e-sy was dropped (syncopated), contracting the word into the punchy disyllable frenzy (spelled variously as frensy, phrensy, or frenzy).
  • Shakespeare and the Sublime Creative Paroxysm (late 16th c.): English Renaissance writers liberated the word from the sickbed. In A Midsummer Night's Dream (1595), Shakespeare forged the monumental phrase "the poet's eye, in a fine frenzy rolling", synthesizing Plato's doctrine of divine inspiration (theia mania) with the volcanic energy of poetic composition.
  • Enlightenment Political Critique (18th c.): In the age of neoclassical order, writers like Sir Joshua Reynolds and Edmund Burke wielded frenzy as a surgical weapon against factional fanaticism. Reynolds warned that the accumulated architectural and philosophical wisdom of centuries could be overthrown in a single hour by the "frenzy of a faction"—diagnosing political zealotry as a contagion of collective insanity.
  • Zoological and Journalistic Expansion (20th–21st c.): In the mid-twentieth century, oceanographers coined feeding frenzy to describe the frenetic, bloody competition of sharks attacking a carcass. Political commentators in the 1970s and 1980s eagerly seized this biological metaphor to describe media swarms hunting down embattled politicians, cementing frenzy as a staple of modern media and market analysis.

Phonetically, the modern Received Pronunciation /ˈfrenzi/ preserves the crisp, trochaic snap of the Renaissance syncopation, opening with the unvoiced labiodental fricative /f/, moving through the short open-mid front unrounded vowel /e/, and closing with the voiced alveolar fricative /z/ and short high front vowel /i/. From Homer's trembling midriff, through Galenic brain-fevers, to Shakespeare's poetic raptures and Reynolds's critique of populist factions, frenzy remains the English language's supreme term for human passion exploding past the boundaries of reason.
