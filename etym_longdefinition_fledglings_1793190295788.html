Fledglings, the plural of fledgling (also spelled fledgeling), are in the strict ornithological sense young birds that have grown their first full set of flight feathers and left the nest, hopping, fluttering, and taking short wobbling flights while their parents still feed and guard them.

In the source fragment — from hunger he came upon a bird with her ______ — the word wears this tender natural sense: a mother bird attended by her half-grown young, still downy at the edges, still gaping for food, encountered by a hungry traveller. The scene is pastoral and faintly perilous, since fledglings out of the nest are the easiest prey of the hedgerow.

Core meaning in detail: Ornithologists distinguish the nestling, naked or downy and confined to the nest, from the fledgling, feathered and mobile but clumsy. Many songbird fledglings spend several days on the ground or low branches, unable to gain height, calling constantly — the stage when well-meaning humans most often kidnap healthy chicks that needed no rescue. The parents recognise each chick’s voice and continue feeding through this awkward apprenticeship, which in small birds lasts a week or two and in eagles and owls many weeks.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Young birds newly able to fly (primary, from 16th c.): the feathered chick between nest and independence; the sense alive in the source sentence.
  • An inexperienced person, a novice (figurative, 19th c.): a fledgling poet, a fledgling company. The bridge is the wobbling first flight — new wings, unsteady, needing shelter — transferred to any beginner’s first ventures.
  • A newly founded enterprise or movement (extended figurative): a fledgling democracy, a fledgling website. The bridge carries the novice sense from persons to institutions: young, fragile, not yet proven against predators.
  • Unfledged or immature young in general (loose literary use): occasionally applied to other young creatures or even ideas. The bridge is the downy helplessness of the chick generalised into youth itself.
  • Juvenile plumage, fledgling dress (ornithological): the soft barred or spotted feathering worn at this stage, often camouflaged for ground hiding. The bridge is metonymy — the bird’s first suit names the bird itself, and guides teach beginners to read it.
  • Fledgling as adjective (attributive): fledgling wings, fledgling efforts. The noun lends itself as a modifier meaning young and untested, a grammatical promotion common to vivid nouns.

Register and connotation: fledglings is gentle, protective, and faintly literary — nature writing, spring columns, charity appeals. It invites care: to call a creature or company a fledgling is to ask the reader to shelter it. The singular often carries the novice sting (a fledgling error), while the plural keeps the nest’s warmth. Gardeners learn the kindest rule of the word: a fledgling on the lawn is not abandoned — stand off, keep the cat in, and let the parents finish the lesson.
