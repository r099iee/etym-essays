• obsession: Similar because both describe a subject that dominates the mind; differs in intensity and pathology — obsession implies compulsive, often irrational intrusion, while preoccupation can be a legitimate intellectual absorption.
  • fixation: Similar in naming a persistent focus; differs because fixation is clinical or psychoanalytic and suggests arrested development, whereas preoccupation is neutral and everyday.
  • absorption: Similar as the state of being wholly engaged; differs because absorption emphasizes the activity of being taken in (“absorption in a book”) and carries no hint that the attention is disproportionate.
  • engrossment: Similar in meaning total occupation of attention; differs in being formal and slightly archaic, and in stressing the act of engrossing rather than the habitual state.
  • fascination: Similar in describing strong attraction of attention; differs in being warmer and more positive — fascination delights, while preoccupation can be anxious or burdensome.
  • concern: Similar in naming something that occupies the mind; differs in being broader and milder, covering practical matters and duties without any implication of exclusivity.
  • interest: Similar as an object of attention; differs precisely in degree — an interest is one concern among many, a preoccupation crowds the others out.
  • monomania: Similar in denoting a single dominating idea; differs as a nineteenth-century clinical term for pathological single-mindedness, far stronger and more stigmatizing than preoccupation.
  • idée fixe: Similar in naming a fixed ruling idea; differs in being a French borrowing that suggests obsessive repetition and is often used of eccentric characters.
  • hobby horse: Similar in describing a favourite topic constantly ridden; differs in being informal and mildly mocking, with no suggestion of anxiety.
  • prepossession: Similar etymologically and historically, meaning a mind already occupied by an opinion; differs in that it now denotes bias or prejudice rather than absorbed attention.
  • distraction: Similar in describing a mind turned away from its surroundings; differs because distraction names the cause of diverted attention or the resulting confusion, whereas preoccupation names the inward claim that produces it.
