The word admireth is a magnificent linguistic specimen: a Latinate stem steeped in the psychology of wonder, wedded to an ancient Germanic grammatical suffix that embodies the formal cadence of Early Modern English. To trace its lineage is to journey from Indo-European expressions of facial emotion, through Roman rhetoric and medieval French romance, to the theological summits of the English Renaissance.

The ultimate root of the base verb is the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)mey-, which originally meant ‘to laugh, smile, or wonder.’ In the Germanic branch, this root produced native English words like smile and smirk; in the Italic branch, however, it underwent a fascinating semantic specialization toward cognitive astonishment. Proto-Italic *smeiros yielded Latin mīrus (‘wonderful, astonishing, extraordinary’), which gave rise to the deponent verb mīrārī (‘to wonder at, marvel’). The Romans then prefixed this with ad- (signifying direction toward or intensive focus), producing admīrārī: literally, ‘to cast one’s gaze toward something with wonder, to be struck with astonishment.’ From this same rich lexical family emerged Latin mīrāculum (the source of English miracle), mīrābilis (‘admirable’), and mīrātōrium (whence French miroir and English mirror—the looking glass being an instrument for gazing at wonders).

Phonetic evolution, grammatical shifts, and cultural trajectory:

  • The Romance transition and Norman transmission (11th–14th century): In the vernacular of northern France, Latin admīrārī simplified into Old French amirer or admirer. Following the Norman Conquest, Anglo-Norman clerks introduced the term into the administrative and literary dialects of England. By the late fourteenth century, Geoffrey Chaucer and his contemporaries had adopted admiren into Middle English, using it to translate classical texts and Boethian philosophy, where it denoted being overwhelmed by wonder or perplexed by unprecedented events.
  • The morphology of -eth: The grammatical ending of admireth represents one of the deepest heritages of Indo-European grammar. In Proto-Indo-European, third-person singular present active verbs took the primary suffix *-ti or *-eti (visible in Sanskrit asti, Greek esti, and Latin est). In Proto-Germanic, this became *-iþi, which under Grimm’s Law and subsequent unstressed vowel reduction produced Old English -eþ. In Middle English, this southern and Midlands inflection remained dominant across centuries.
  • The dialectal contest of the Renaissance: In Early Modern English, southern writers used -eth, while northern English dialects had long simplified the sound to -es or -s (likely accelerated by Scandinavian language contact in Northumbria). In Elizabethan London, the two forms lived side-by-side: admires was conversational, rapid, and metric-friendly for playwrights, while admireth was liturgical, grave, and aristocratic. Translators of the King James Bible deliberately standardized on -eth for its stately, unhurried pronunciation.
  • The semantic pivot: from astonishment to approbation: Throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, admire and admireth continued to signify raw astonishment rather than uncritical approval. When Hamlet exclaims in Shakespeare’s play, ‘Season your admiration for a while / With an attent ear,’ he means ‘temper your amazement.’ When Richard Hooker wrote that the world admireth the virtues of the dead, he captured that sublime moment when the contrariness of human society is arrested by the undeniable majesty of lasting wisdom.
  • Phonological changes across the Great Vowel Shift: In Middle English, the second syllable was pronounced with an open /iː/ (approximately /adˈmiːrən/). As the Great Vowel Shift reshaped the English vowel system between 1400 and 1650, this long high vowel diphthongized into /aɪ/ (producing modern /ədˈmaɪər/). The unstressed inflection -eth retained its front vowel /ɪθ/ or /əθ/, preserving a distinct third syllable that gave the verb its majestic, tri-syllabic trochaic cadence: /ədˈmaɪərəθ/.

By the opening of the eighteenth century, the northern -s had completely triumphed in standard written prose, rendering admireth an archaic relic. Yet, when modern readers encounter Hooker’s declaration in Johnson’s pages, admireth does not merely communicate a grammatical action; it rings with the solemn intellectual cadence of an age that weighed human virtue against eternity.
