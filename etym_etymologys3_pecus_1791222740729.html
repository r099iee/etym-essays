The evolution of pecus runs from Indo-European pastoralism to Roman law to modern English finance-words, without the word itself ever becoming English.

It begins with Proto-Indo-European *peku- / *peku- (conventionally written *peku-), a root meaning movable wealth in livestock, especially sheep and cattle. Unlike roots for wild animals, this root already encodes ownership: it is not animal but ownable animal-wealth. It is attested across the family: Sanskrit pacu (cattle), Avestan pasu (small cattle), Old English feoh (cattle, money, fee), Gothic faihu (property, money), German Vieh (livestock) and Fee-related fee. The Germanic branch shows the identical cattle-to-money shift independently: English fee once meant cattle-payment.

  • Proto-Italic and Old Latin: Italic inherits *peku- as *peku- neuter, giving Latin pecus, pecoris. Earliest Roman society (8th–5th c. BCE) is pastoral-agrarian; fines, bride-prices and compensations are reckoned in oxen and sheep. The Twelve Tables tradition preserves fines in livestock. Language and economy coincide: to have pecus is to have means.
  • Republican Latin — the money invention: As Rome monetises (bronze aes rude, then cast aes signatum stamped with an ox, then struck coinage from c. 280 BCE), the herd-word spawns a money-word: pecunia from pecus + -ia. Pliny the Elder explicitly records that early coins bore the ox (bos) because wealth had been in cattle. Simultaneously peculium (what a slave or son is allowed to hold as his own cattle-savings), peculiaris (pertaining to that private holding), and peculari (to steal public cattle-funds) develop. Law, finance and morality are still barnyard vocabulary.
  • Classical literary Latin: Cato, Varro (De Re Rustica), Virgil (Georgics, Eclogues), Columella and jurists use pecus technically for breeding, pasturing, branding, transhumance and sacrifice. Poets also exploit its double register: tender pastoral flock versus stupid herd. Cicero weaponises the contemptuous sense against political opponents and corrupt courts.
  • Late and Christian Latin: With a monetised empire, literal cattle-wealth recedes but the word persists in law codes (Digest, Institutes) as a property category, and in the Vulgate and pastoral Christian metaphor as flock — the faithful as sheep, Christ as shepherd, clergy as pastors. The economic sense fossilises inside derivatives while the pastoral-flock sense is theologically amplified.
  • Romance and Germanic transmission: Pecus itself does not yield a popular French or Italian everyday word for cow (those come from vacca, bos, caballus etc.), but its derivatives do: Italian pecunia (learned), French pecuniaire, peculat, particulier (from peculiaris via particularis confusion/blending). English borrows these learned forms from the 15th–17th centuries: pecuniary (early 16th c.), peculation (16th c.), peculiar (15th c., originally private property, then exclusive, then strange — because what is exclusively one's own seems strange to others).

Phonetically the chain is conservative: PIE *p- remains Latin p-; PIE *-k- remains Latin -c- /k/; the -u- stem is preserved as -us, -oris (rhotacism of intervocalic -s- to -r- in pecoris). No dramatic sound shift obscures the root; the drama is semantic: from lowing herd to abstract finance to moral vocabulary for embezzlement and oddity.

Key attestations anchor the story: Varro derives pecunia from pecus explicitly; Pliny (Natural History 18, 33.12) notes ox-stamped bronze; the ox on early Roman aes signatum bars is archaeologically attested. Modern economists and etymologists still cite pecus–pecunia as the textbook example of commodity money lexicalised, alongside Germanic fee/feoh. The source sentence compresses that whole textbook into one line: kindred words for cattle and money, ox on the coin.
