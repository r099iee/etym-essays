In this sentence pedestals appears in the figurative idiom “set the verse of Greece and Rome on the highest of pedestals” — to exalt classical poetry to a position of supreme honour, as a statue is raised on its base. The tone is formal and historical: the sixteenth-century humanists are described as raising the ancients to the highest plinths of admiration, so high that home-grown English verse, judged against them, was found wanting. The word is plural because each ancient author is imagined on his own eminence, and the phrase “the highest of” pushes the idiom to its limit — pedestals have a top, and classical verse is placed on the topmost one, an eminence too high for rivalry. PRIMARY SENSE — architecture
  • A pedestal is the free-standing base or support on which a statue, column, vase, or urn is raised — the block beneath the feet of a figure that lifts it above the eye and gives it dignity.
  • In classical architecture the pedestal of a column consists of three parts: the plinth (the flat slab at the bottom, technically the lowest course), the dado or die (the plain central block), and the cap or cornice (the moulding at the top).
  • A plinth is therefore only the lowest member of a pedestal, though in ordinary speech the two words are often conflated; a socle is a plain, unmoulded plinth.
  • A podium (from Greek pódion, “little foot”) is the raised platform on which a speaker or conductor stands or on which a building is set, not the support of a single statue.
SECONDARY SENSE — the exalting metaphor Since the seventeenth century to place (or put) someone on a pedestal has meant to regard them with reverent, often uncritical admiration — to raise a person to a position of supreme honour and to idealise them. The semantic bridge is the physical act of exaltation: the block that raises a statue also isolates and glorifies it, and the metaphor transfers that raised isolation to human beings. The idiom commonly carries an implicit warning — those on pedestals are alone, and pedestals can be toppled; “take someone off their pedestal” means to expose their faults and humiliate them. In the passage the phrase is used of literary works: the verse of Greece and Rome is “set on the highest of pedestals,” that is, treated as a canon too exalted to be questioned — exactly the reverence a neighbouring passage calls “hallowed.” TERTIARY SENSES — mechanical and design extensions
  • Engineering / machinery: a pedestal is a heavy mounting block or bearing-support that carries a shaft, motor, or machine — e.g. a pedestal bearing, a pedestal seat, the pedestal of a lathe or a crane; the “foot-stand” sense applied to the load-bearing parts of machines.
  • Furniture: a pedestal desk is a writing desk supported on two side cabinets (pedestals) of drawers; a pedestal table has a single central pillar; a pedestal sink is a washbasin mounted on a central column.
  • Nautical: a pedestal can be the support of a ship’s wheel or steering gear.
  • Display and collectibles: any small stand for a bust, trophy, vase, or ornament is loosely called a pedestal, even a simple wooden block.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE Italian piedistallo literally means “foot of a stall” (from pie di stallo): piede “foot” (Latin pēs, pedis) + stallo “stall, seat, position” (a Germanic loanword in Italian, from Frankish stal “standing place”). The word moved from the physical marble base of a statue, through Renaissance architecture books, to the psychological act of idolisation and the industrial language of heavy supports — a single word stretching from a block underfoot to the top of a mountain of reverence. The plural pedestals of the passage keeps the architectural image alive even while the idiom does its figurative work: the ancient poets stand, literally, on the highest bases the humanists could build. SYNONYM BOUNDARIES
  • Plinth: Similar because both are supporting bases beneath a structure or statue; differs because a plinth is the flat slab at the very bottom of a pedestal or building — its lowest course — while a pedestal is the whole free-standing support raised beneath a statue.
  • Socle: Similar because both are plain bases under a statue, column, or wall; differs because a socle is a low, plain, unmoulded plinth, while a pedestal is a taller composite support with several members.
  • Base: Similar because both are the supporting bottom of something; differs because base is the generic term for any bottom support and carries no sense of raising something to a position of honour.
  • Podium: Similar because both are raised platforms, ultimately from a foot root; differs because a podium is where a speaker or conductor stands, built for a person, not for exalting a statue or an ideal.
  • Rostrum: Similar because both are elevated platforms; differs because a rostrum is a public speaking platform, a stage for oratory, whereas a pedestal supports an object rather than a speaker.
