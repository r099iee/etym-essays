The oldest stratum of INFATUATION is the Latin adjective fatuus, "foolish, silly, insipid," whose own deeper ancestry is obscure. Latin fatuus has no securely established Indo-European parent; a disputed reconstruction *bhat- ("to strike, blow, to make senseless") is sometimes proposed, and an alternative hypothesis would connect the word with the verb fārī, "to speak," making a fatuus literally "one who talks nonsense" — but neither etymology commands universal assent, and the word's history is fully secure only from Latin onward. What is certain is the concrete starting point of the word's Latin career: fatuus could describe food or wine that had lost its flavor — the insipid, the tasteless — and by an easy metaphorical extension it came to describe persons who had lost their savor, their sense, their wits. From this physical and sensory image of emptiness — flavorlessness as a figure for mindlessness — the whole later family grows.

The verb infatuāre, "to make foolish," was built by prefixing in-, "into" or "in the direction of," to fatuus, and the abstract noun infatuātiō, "a making foolish," was formed in Latin to name the act or process of depriving someone of reason. In the sixteenth century the word entered English, and in its earliest attested uses it is a solemn, almost clinical word: to be infatuated was to have one's judgment deliberately destroyed, to be besotted in the most literal sense. Writers of the period could speak of men "infatuated" by greed, by false doctrine, or by bad counsel, and the word retained this flavour of spiritual and mental ruin, of a mind given over to folly, well into the seventeenth century. It is a striking fact that the word was born not as a term of romantic praise but as a term of moral and intellectual censure.

The semantic shift that produced the modern sense — an intense, short-lived, unreasoning passion for a person — was complete by the eighteenth century, when the lover replaced the madman and the fanatic as the typical subject of infatuation. The logic of the shift is transparent: the irrationality that earlier writers saw in the deluded or the heretical was recognized in the besotted lover, whose passion clouds his judgment exactly as folly clouds the mind. The word kept its old idea of "being made foolish" while relocating it from the head to the heart.

By the nineteenth century the modern sense was fully settled, and it is with this settled sense that the Victorian biographer uses the word of John Stuart Mill:

  • The word as social judgment: "Mill's infatuation was the cause of considerable trouble to his acquaintances and friends" — the phrase asserts that Mill's devotion to Harriet Taylor was unreasonable and disproportionate, an emotion that overrode the logic he preached and that his circle read as folly.
  • The word's spread in the nineteenth century: novelists, biographers, and moralists used infatuation for the short-lived, idealizing passion that their culture contrasted with steady love, and the word entered the vocabulary of everyday psychology.
  • The modern colloquial counterpart: the twentieth century produced the lighter synonym "crush," while clinical psychology coined "limerence" (1970s) for the obsessive craving of the infatuated lover — the scientific formalization of the very state the word had named for two centuries.

Today the word is common, mildly literary, and always slightly disapproving: it names an attachment that is intense but expected to fade, idealizing rather than knowing, fantasy-fed rather than grounded in acquaintance. Its register spans the psychological, the biographical, and the everyday, and its whole history is visible in a single glance — the Roman taste of flavorlessness behind the modern ache of the heart.
