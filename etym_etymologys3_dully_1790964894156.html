Proto-Indo-European Origins and Germanic Obscurity:
The roots of the adverb dully lie within the Proto-Indo-European verbal base *dʰwel- (with zero-grade variant *dʰul-), signifying to stun, cloud, make turbid, darken, or lead astray. This ancient root evolved across Indo-European dialects to express sensory obscurity and cognitive confusion—producing Greek tholos ("mud, turbidity") and Old Church Slavonic dulu ("hollow, deep"). In the Proto-Germanic branch, it generated the adjective *dwalaz ("foolish, errant, stunned") and the related variant *duljaz ("slow, blunt, stupid").

Old English Foundations and Semantic Bifurcation:
In Anglo-Saxon England, the root developed as Old English dol, meaning foolish, reckless, or intellectually sluggish (surviving modern cognates include Dutch dol, "crazy, mad," and German toll, "mad, wild, fantastic"). The Old English adverbial derivation dollīċe meant "foolishly, rashly, or stupidly." During the late Old English and early Middle English transition, English speech was heavily influenced by Middle Dutch and Middle Low German forms like dul and dolle ("blunt, mad, stupid"), which shifted the phonetic realization toward the rounded back vowel /u/ and doubled liquid /l/, creating Middle English dul / dull.

Middle English Evolution and Adverbial Consolidation:
By the 13th and 14th centuries, the adjective dull had expanded from mental stupidity into a multi-sensory physical descriptor: a knife blade without an edge was dull, an unpolished surface without shine was dull, and a stormy overcast sky was dull. When combined with the productive Germanic adverbial suffix -ly (from Old English -līċe, literally "with the body/appearance of"), Middle English dulli emerged to describe actions executed with sluggishness, bluntness, or lack of visual and intellectual clarity.

Renaissance and Early Modern Expressive Widening:
In the 16th and 17th centuries, Early Modern writers and playwrights, including William Shakespeare and John Milton, deployed dully across sensory and psychological registers. It was applied to acoustic perception (drums or bells sounding dully through mist), optic qualities (metals burning or reflecting dully), and somatic pathology (chronic internal pains throbbing dully). In Renaissance medicine, governed by humorism, a predominance of phlegm or black bile was thought to cause a person to move and perceive dully.

Victorian Psychology to Modern Nuance:
In the 19th-century novels of Charles Dickens, Thomas Hardy, and the Brontë sisters, dully became a vital psychological instrument for conveying existential numbness, working-class weariness, and the emotional desensitization caused by grief or industrial squalor. In modern English, dully remains an indispensable adverb in literary prose, clinical pathology, and atmospheric writing, capturing the tragic or protective human response of sensory and emotional dampening.
