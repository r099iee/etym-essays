Buffeted means struck, knocked, or beaten repeatedly, battered from side to side by blows, waves, wind, or the blows of fortune. A ship is buffeted by a storm; a person is buffeted by criticism, bad news, or the unpredictability of life; a tree is buffeted by the gale. The word is the past participle of the verb to buffet, which itself comes from the noun buffet, 'a blow, a cuff with the hand', and the whole family carries a sense of repeated, punishing impact — of being knocked about without mercy.

Core sense — repeated striking: The central meaning is physical battering: to be struck again and again, whether by a fist, a wave, a gust, or debris. In the source context a castaway is 'so sore and stiff, so bruised and buffeted' that he can hardly stand: the waves and rocks of the surf have pummelled him over and over, leaving him battered and dazed. The word insists on repetition and on the victim's helplessness — a buffeted person or thing is being knocked around, not fighting back.

  • Sense 1 — Struck repeatedly by physical force: of the body struck by blows ('he was buffeted by the crowd'), of ships and boats battered by wind and sea ('the little boat was buffeted by the storm'), of buildings and trees hammered by weather.
  • Sense 2 — Knocked about by the elements: a semi-technical use in sailing and meteorology for exposure to rough conditions; 'buffeting winds' is a standard collocation.
  • Sense 3 — Figurative adversity: a person 'buffeted by fate', 'buffeted by misfortune', 'buffeted by the economy' — treated as the passive victim of repeated external pressures, pushed this way and that with no control.
  • Sense 4 — Old-fashioned physical 'cuffed': the earliest English sense, 'struck with the hand or fist' — literally given a buffet; now archaic except in historical writing.
  • Sense 5 — Extended to criticism and debate: 'the government was buffeted by criticism', 'the company was buffeted by market swings' — modern journalistic idiom for sustained attack from all sides.

Physical picture and collocational range: The typical images buffeted evokes are drawn from weather and the sea: a small ship or dinghy riding out a gale, waves crashing against a harbour wall, an exposed tree bent and battered by the wind, a walker struggling against a storm. The verb also governs the body under physical assault — a crowd buffeting a prisoner, blows raining down on a boxer — and it is in this double register, storm and violence together, that the word has always lived.

Register and tone: Buffeted is a dignified, emphatic word, commoner in writing than in casual speech. It implies violence that is repeated and impersonal — the blows come from the weather, the mob, or fate, not from a named antagonist — and it almost always evokes sympathy for the victim. It is distinctly stronger than struck or hit (which can name a single blow) and close in force to battered and pounded.

Grammar and collocation: As a past-participial adjective buffeted is used attributively and predicatively ('a buffeted coastline', 'the ship was buffeted'); it takes the instrumental 'by' ('buffeted by wind and rain'); and it is often paired with bruised, as in the source text ('bruised and buffeted'), to describe the aftermath of being knocked about. The noun buffet ('a blow') survives mainly in phrases like 'the buffets of fate', and the pair 'buffets and bruises' keeps the physical violence alive.

Polysemy summary: All senses of buffeted radiate from one picture: an object or person helpless in the middle of repeated impacts. The physical sense (blows, waves, wind) comes first and remains standard; the figurative senses — buffeted by fortune, by criticism, by markets — are simply the same battering transferred from the body to the fortunes. The word has never drifted far from its violent origin: to be buffeted is always to be knocked about, whether by the storm outside or the storm of life.
