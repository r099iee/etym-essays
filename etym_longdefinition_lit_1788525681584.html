lit (verb, past tense and past participle of light; also adjective) — A classic, monosyllabic English verbal form possessing two profoundly distinct historical roots that converged in spelling and pronunciation: a motion/perching verb (the exact sense in Oscar Wilde's fairy tale) and an illumination/combustion verb. In the literary context of The Nightingale and the Rose ("she flew over to it, and lit upon a spray"), lit is the past tense of the intransitive verb to light (more commonly known in modern prose as to alight), meaning to descend from flight, land gently, and settle down upon a branch or resting place.

Primary sense (motion & perching) — descended, alighted, and settled. Historically derived from Old English līhtan (literally "to make light, disburden oneself, relieve of weight"), the verb describes the action of descending gracefully from a horse, carriage, or flight. When applied to birds, flying insects, or falling snowflakes, to light (past: lit or lighted) signifies touching down with featherweight softness, transferring one's physical weight onto a delicate perch (e.g., "the butterfly lit upon the flower", "the robin lit upon the windowsill").

Sense 2 (illumination & ignition) — ignited, illuminated, or set ablaze. Derived from a completely separate Old English verb līehtan / lȳhtan ("to shine, provide with light, kindle"), lit serves as the standard past tense and past participle of to light in contexts of fire and illumination (e.g., "she lit the candle with a match", "the evening bonfire lit up the courtyard", "a sun-lit meadow"). Prescriptive eighteenth-century grammarians favored the weak regular form lighted, but the clipped form lit established itself as the dominant standard across global English.

Sense 3 — idiomatic verbal phrases and figurative extensions. The versatility of lit has generated diverse metaphorical idioms in modern English:

  • Chance discovery (to light upon): To light upon (past: lit upon) an idea, book, solution, or forgotten artifact means to encounter or discover it unexpectedly by chance or sudden intuition ("while browsing the library archives, he lit upon a forgotten manuscript").
  • Sudden assault or rebuke (to light into): In colloquial American and British English, to light into someone means to attack them furiously, either with physical blows or with blistering verbal reprimands ("the coach lit into the players at halftime").
  • Radiant facial expression (to light up): A person's face or eyes lit up when suddenly illuminated by radiant joy, sudden understanding, or delighted surprise.
  • Aviation & Radar: In modern military aviation and electronic warfare, to be lit up means to be tracked or targeted by enemy radar systems or illuminated by searchlights.

Sense 4 — contemporary slang and socio-cultural usage. Over the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, lit underwent dramatic colloquial shifts in informal register:

  • Intoxication (early 20th c.): Originating in American jazz and beat slang in the 1910s, lit or lit up described someone visibly intoxicated by alcohol or drugs.
  • Excitement and vibrant energy (21st c.): In early twenty-first-century African American Vernacular English (AAVE) and global youth culture, lit evolved into a popular slang adjective denoting an event, party, atmosphere, or track that is extraordinarily exciting, vibrant, hype, or thrilling ("the party last night was totally lit").

Literary grace and grammatical rivalry. In high Victorian and Romantic prose, lit retains an irreplaceable sense of physical lightness and economy. While modern prescriptive grammar often leans toward alighted for avian landing, Wilde's deliberate choice of lit imparts a swift, monosyllabic rhythm that captures the nightingale's weightless, delicate descent upon the thorn-bearing rose tree.
