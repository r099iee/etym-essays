Introduction. Slithered is one of the older words in this batch, but in a special way: its ancestor is ancient, while the familiar modern sense — the sinuous glide of a snake — is relatively recent. The word descends from Old English slīðrian, itself a frequentative (a repeated-action form) of the Old English verb slīðan, “to slide, to slip”, which is also the ancestor of modern slide. The whole family goes back to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “slippery, sliding”. The history is thus a study in how a simple Germanic root was repeatedly thickened with suffixes, and how the sense drifted from “slip on ice” to “glide like a snake”.

1. The Indo-European base. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root is *sleydh-, “slippery, to slide, to slip”. It is a well-attested root with many descendants: Greek olisthánein “to slip” (with s-mobile complications), Germanic forms meaning “slide / slip”, and parts of the Slavic family (cf. Russian sled “track”) have been connected, though the Slavic connection is debated. The core Indo-European image is physical slipperiness — a foot or object losing grip on a smooth surface.

2. Germanic: the simple verb. From *sleydh- the Germanic languages formed the strong verb *slīþaną “to slide, to slip”. Its descendants are attested: Old High German slītan (“to slide”), Old Saxon and Old English slīdan (giving modern English slide). The Old English verb slīdan (a strong verb of class 1) is recorded from early West Saxon texts and means “to slide, to slip, to glide”. Already at this stage the meaning is concrete and physical.

3. Old English: the frequentative. English, however, did not take slither from slīdan directly. Old English formed a frequentative verb slīðrian, “to slide repeatedly, to slip about, to keep slipping”, by adding the frequentative suffix to the base — a common Old English pattern. The frequentative meaning is crucial: it is not one slide but a repeated, drawn-out, shambling slip. Slīðrian is attested in Old English records of the tenth and eleventh centuries.

4. Middle English: slithren. Middle English continues the word as slithren / slitheren (also slideren), meaning “to slide, to slip, to creep”. The spelling vacillates between -th- and -d- forms, reflecting the voiced dental sound and the constant pull of the synonym slide. By the fourteenth century the word is used both of slipping on surfaces and of crawling motion.

5. The semantic drift: from slipping to snake-gliding.

  • Old / Middle English: the dominant meaning is “to slip, to slide, especially uncontrollably on a smooth or icy surface”.
  • 15th–18th c.: the word coexists with slide and slip; it appears in dialect and in literary texts for sliding, crawling, or gliding, without a specifically snaky colouring.
  • 19th c.: with the rise of nature writing, of public zoology, and of the popular fascination with reptiles, slither becomes the standard word for a snake’s locomotion — smooth, sinuous, quiet gliding. The sense is now so strong that many speakers forget the word ever meant merely “to slip”.
  • 20th c.: the two senses live side by side — physical unsteadiness (“slithered on the ice”) and serpentine movement — with the snaky sense dominant in general usage and reinforced by horror and fantasy fiction.

6. Literary attestations. The word appears in Middle English devotional and practical prose; in the nineteenth century in the naturalists of Darwin’s generation and in the animal poems and prose of the period; and it is a favourite of twentieth-century fantasy and thriller prose. The source sentence — “their feet slithered on stones rubbed smooth and slimed by the passing of the dragon” — shows the older, physical sense perfectly, and in doing so links the word to the snaky imagery of the dragon itself: the ground, worn smooth by the great reptile, becomes a surface on which the heroes slip as though the serpent’s presence had made the very stones treacherous.

7. Present status. Slither is fully current: it names the motion of snakes in biology and everyday speech, describes awkward sliding in sport and weather reports, and feeds a large figurative family (slithery characters, slithering rumours, the chills that slither down a spine). The old frequentative machinery is invisible to modern speakers, but its meaning — repeated, drawn-out, shambling motion — still shapes the word’s feel.

Conclusion. Slithered carries three thousand years of Indo-European “slipperiness” through Germanic, Old English, and Middle English, changing from “slipped again and again” to “glided like a snake”, while never losing its physical grip on the idea of a smooth, treacherous surface. It is an ancient root in a deceptively modern-looking word.
