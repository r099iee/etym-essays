“Evince” means “to show or reveal clearly, to make evident, to demonstrate” — and in the crucial sentence of the chapter’s survey, “most of these works evince a deep passion for the subject”, it is chosen precisely because it outranks the everyday verbs of showing. The books in question are the old prosodic handbooks, and what they reveal is not an opinion or a mannerism but an unmistakable, demonstrable commitment: “absolute truths are proclaimed in heavy capitals, opponents despatched in savagely civil footnotes, snippets of verse triumphantly displayed like newly-discovered zoological specimens.” The word evince claims that this passion is not merely visible but incontrovertibly present — readable from the evidence as a fact is read from a document. It is the perfect verb for scholarship, because it carries the forensic and logical notion of proof: the passion is not asserted by the author but shown by the works themselves. The tone of the sentence is wryly admiring: all this ferocious energy — the capitals, the footnotes, the triumph — is itself the testimony of love, and the author, while smiling at the performance, honours the passion behind it. The modern word is relatively narrow, but its historical senses are instructive and its boundaries must be carefully drawn:
  • Core modern sense — to show or reveal a quality or feeling: always transitive, governing abstract objects such as passion, interest, concern, or skill (“the essay evinces a deep knowledge of the subject”). Formal, chiefly British, and slightly bookish.
  • Historical sense (17th c.) — to conquer, vanquish, overcome: the original English sense, taken directly from Latin evincere; now obsolete. Its brief life shows how far the word has travelled from warfare to evidence.
  • Historical sense (17th–18th c.) — to convict, refute, or disprove in argument: the middle stage of the journey, in which victory in debate becomes victory by proof; now rare and archaic.
  • 18th-century sense — to prove or establish from evidence: the decisive development, in which evince became the verb of documentary and demonstrative proof, the bridge to the modern meaning.
  • Legal parallel — evict, eviction: the dispossession sense of Latin evincere (evictus, “conquered out”) entered English law separately as evict, a cousin that split off early and kept the martial meaning of driving someone out by legal force.
The historical bridge is the metaphor of conquest. Latin evincere meant “to conquer wholly, to carry one’s point, to prove”: in Roman usage, to evince a case was to conquer it by demonstration, to win the argument so completely that nothing remained in doubt. English inherited first the martial sense (“to conquer”), then the argumentative one (“to convict by proof”), and finally the evidentiary one we use today. In modern prose evince is a verb of inference from evidence — the speaker does not claim the passion, he reads it from the works, which is exactly the logical force the passage needs. The boundaries against near-synonyms are instructive. “Show” is the plain, colourless everyday verb; it makes no claim about the quality or certainty of the display. “Display” implies deliberate arrangement for an audience, and the author uses “displayed” a few words later for the snippets of verse precisely to mark the difference: the verses are arranged and shown off, while the passion is not arranged at all — it is betrayed, involuntarily, by the very excess of the display. “Demonstrate” is methodical and logical, a step-by-step proof laid out for inspection, and it is too procedural for something as unselfconscious as passion. “Betray” captures the involuntary quality but adds the sense of exposure against one’s will, which fits a secret rather than a passion. “Indicate” is weak and tentative. “Evince” uniquely combines three claims at once: that something is visible, that it is genuine, and that it is proven by the evidence rather than merely claimed — which is why the passage trusts it to carry the whole weight of its judgement.
