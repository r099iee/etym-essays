Dwellings has one of the strangest biographies in English: a word for "home" that began as a word for getting lost. Its oldest recoverable ancestor is the PIE root *dʰeu- / *dʰwel-, a family of words about clouding, dulling, and confoundment — dust that dims the air, mud that clouds water, a mind that goes dazed. In Sanskrit the family kept its weather: dhūli is "dust," dhvāntá is "darkness." In Greek, tholós is "mud, sediment," the stuff that clouds a stream. The root's oldest temperament is obstruction and confusion — the world grown thick and unclear around a person. (It must be said honestly that the connection of the Germanic "delay/lead-astray" verb to this cloud-and-confusion family is traditional rather than certain; the deeper PIE step is a debated hypothesis, while the stages from Proto-Germanic onward are secure.)

The Germanic branch took this cloudy root and made a verb of being led astray. Proto-Germanic *dwaljaną meant "to delay, hinder, lead someone off the path," and Old English had two closely woven verbs: dwelian, "to go astray, to err, to wander," and dwellan, "to mislead, deceive, hinder, delay." To dwell in Old English was, remarkably, to be stuck — held back on a journey, diverted, kept waiting. The same family produced Old English dol, "foolish, dazed," the ancestor of dull; dwola, "error, heresy"; and dwolma, "chaos." Yet inside this vocabulary of being stranded there lay the seed of the modern sense: to be delayed is to remain, and to remain in one place long enough is to stay — and to stay is where people build homes.

Middle English dwellen enacted exactly that shift. Where the Old English verb had meant "to wander in error" and "to delay," Middle English let the delay harden into residence: to dwell came to mean "to tarry, to abide, to live," and by the fifteenth century the "reside" sense was fully in place. The noun dwelling (from Old English dwellung, "a delaying") followed it, changing from an act of lingering into a place of residence — the gerund became a building. Strikingly, the same drift happened independently in Scandinavia: Old Norse dvelja, "to delay," became Swedish dväljas, "to reside," a parallel development that confirms how natural the path from "to linger" to "to live" is in the Germanic mind.

Once the noun had settled, its modern career was one of steady formalization. From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries dwelling appears in fixed compounds — dwelling place, dwelling house — and in legal and administrative prose, where it names a place of settled residence without warmth or sentiment. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the plural dwellings became the standard ethnographic and archaeological term: cave dwellings, cliff dwellings, lake dwellings, "the dwellings of the dead." It is the word of the surveyor and the anthropologist, deliberately colder than home, chosen precisely because it can name the houses of a people — or, in speculative fiction, of a species — without presuming to know what home feels like inside.

That is the word's work in the present passage, where the smell of "animal dens" is set beside "the peculiar smell of Martian dwellings." The sentence places the two kinds of habitation side by side: the den is the animal's, the dwelling the word we extend across the gap of species, implying that whatever shelters the Martians is enough like a house to be called by the human noun of residence. The whole six-hundred-year history of the word — from "to be led astray" through "to tarry" to "to live" to "a place where one lives" — is compressed into that extension: a word that once meant being lost now names the homes of beings we have not yet met, the ultimate act of settling, made in the very moment the word itself is stretched across a planet.
