BADGER’S is the possessive singular of badger. The origin of badger itself is genuinely uncertain; the two leading theories are given here as separate candidate chains, with the uncertainty stated plainly.

CANDIDATE CHAIN A (the badge theory):
Stage 1: Uncertain ultimate origin; Medieval Latin / Anglo-Latin bagia, bagea, “a badge, mark, or sign” (attested from c. 1200; probably borrowed from a Germanic word for a sign, itself of unknown ancestry).
Stage 2: Anglo-Norman and Old French bage, badg(e), “badge, mark” (13th c.).
Stage 3: Middle English badge, “a distinctive mark or emblem” (attested c. 1300).
Stage 4: English badger, “the animal with the white badge-like stripe on its face” — formed from badge + -er (agentive), literally “the bearer of a badge” (first recorded 1520s).
Stage 5: Possessive: badger + ’s → badger’s, “of the badger” (modern English).

CANDIDATE CHAIN B (the lair theory):
Stage 1: Old French bauge, baug, “lair, den, lair of a beast” (13th c.; of Germanic origin, akin to a word for “rounded, curved”).
Stage 2: Middle English badger, “the burrowing animal that lives in a bauge / lair” (possible source; first recorded 1520s, same date as Chain A).
Stage 3: Possessive: badger + ’s → badger’s, “of the badger” (modern English).

Debated / nuanced points: The OED and other authorities mark badger as of uncertain origin. Chain A rests on the striking white facial stripe reading as a “badge,” which is the traditional and most often repeated explanation; Chain B connects the animal to its burrow via Old French bauge. Both were in circulation in the 1520s, and neither is proven. The older native word for the animal, brock (from Celtic), remained in dialect for centuries, and “badger” won out in the south before 1600. Whatever the true source, the possessive badger’s is a straightforward genitive of the modern noun, exactly the Old English -es ending that has survived unchanged in form and function.
