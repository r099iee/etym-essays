The historical trajectory of Bung spans ancient Celtic craftsmanship, medieval Hanseatic maritime commerce, Elizabethan criminal cant, and twentieth-century British and Australasian colloquial idiom.

1. Ancient Celtic Roots & Medieval Cooperage Trade

  • Gaulish *bonda: Historical lexicographers trace the original barrel-stopper term to a Gaulish (Celtic) root *bonda or *bunda, meaning a "plug," "bottom," or "stopper" (cognate with Old Irish bond "sole, base"). As wooden barrel cooperage was a renowned Celtic technology adopted by the Romans, Celtic terminology for barrel manufacturing entered Gallo-Roman speech.
  • Old French bonde & Middle Dutch bonge: In Old French, the word developed as bonde or bondon ("a plug for a cask"). Through the vigorous wine and beer trade connecting Flanders, the Low Countries, and the British Isles, Middle Dutch adopted the term as bonge or bonghe.
  • Middle English adoption: The term entered Middle English around 1440, attested in the Latin-English dictionary Promptorium Parvulorum as bonge (defined as the stopper or orifice of a tun or cask). It quickly became the standard English technical term for the heavy wooden or cork plug used by coopers and brewers.

2. Elizabethan Rogue Cant: Pockets and Cutpurses

  • The thieves' cant noun: By the late sixteenth century, bung had been appropriated by London's criminal underworld. In Elizabethan cant pamphlets (such as those by Robert Greene and Thomas Dekker), a purse, pocket, or money pouch was known as a bung, so called because it was tightly stopped or secured to hold gold and silver coins.
  • Bung-nippers: Pickpockets were notoriously termed "bung-nippers" (literally cutpurses who cut or opened bungs). This cant usage established a lasting conceptual link between the word bung, tight containers, and illicit money transactions.

3. Pugilistic Slang & The Expansion to Casual Locomotion

  • Bunged up in the prize ring: In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the verb phrase to bung up emerged in bare-knuckle prize fighting to describe striking an opponent so hard around the eye that the surrounding flesh swelled shut, sealing the eye like a barrel bung (a "bunged-up eye"). This soon broadened to nasal congestion caused by head colds.
  • Casual tossing and cramming: Concurrently, the mechanical action of slamming or driving a bung into an aperture evolved into the colloquial verb to bung, meaning to shove, cram, or toss something unceremoniously into a container or space. By the Victorian and Edwardian periods, "bung him in" or "bung it in the corner" was established throughout British colloquial speech.

4. Modern Financial Bribes & Australasian Indigenous Contact

  • The sports & political 'bung': In twentieth-century British journalism, bung was revitalized to designate an illicit under-the-table cash bribe or kickback, especially during high-profile football managerial corruption scandals in the 1990s.
  • Australian 'go bung' & Aboriginal borrowing: In Australia and New Zealand, the idiom go bung (meaning to fail, break down, or die) arose in the late nineteenth century from contact with Queensland Indigenous languages, where the Yagara/Jandai word bong meant "dead." The loanword merged phonetically with the English word bung, creating a rich regional idiomatic branch.
