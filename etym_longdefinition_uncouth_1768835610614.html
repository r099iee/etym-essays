uncouth — lacking in polish, refinement, or cultivated manners; crude, rough, awkward, or uncultured; etymologically and literally 'unknown.' The word descends from Old English uncūþ, 'unknown, unfamiliar, strange,' the negation of cūþ, 'known' — the past participle of cunnan, 'to know.' Its history is a migration from epistemology to etiquette: what was unknown became strange; what was strange became foreign; and what was foreign became unpolished — the rude conceived of as the unknowing.

Primary senses

  • Of persons — manners: lacking good manners, refinement, or grace; coarse, rude, awkward in society. This is the modern, dominant sense: 'uncouth behaviour,' 'an uncouth fellow.'
  • Of persons and things — appearance and nature: rough, unpolished, gnarled, unrefined — an uncouth landscape, an uncouth hand, features hardened and ungraceful; the physical sense of roughness without the moral accusation.
  • Of speech and style: uncouth language, an uncouth dialect — rough, ungrammatical, or unpolished to cultivated ears.

Etymological and historical senses (now rare or obsolete)

  • 'Unknown, unfamiliar, strange' — the original meaning of uncūþ in Old English, still alive in Middle English ('an uncouth land' = a strange, foreign land); the modern word preserves this buried core, so that the unpolished are still, etymologically, the un-known.
  • 'Uncanny, eerie' — a ghostly intermediary sense: what is unknown is not only strange but unsettling; this shade faded as the social sense took over.

Grammatical behaviour and derivatives

  • Adjective, used of persons, manner, appearance, and speech; the adverb uncouthly and the noun uncouthness are rare but attested.
  • Back-formation couth (1896): English extracted a positive 'couth' = refined, cultured — forgetting that the negative once contained the positive; what is 'couth' is only 'known.'

In the passage

  • The word is applied to the craftsmen of the Outer Wall: 'They were uncouth and prematurely aged.' The force is double. Physically, the carvers — aged before their time by the brutal annual cycle of carving and judgement — are rough: weathered, gnarled, unpolished, their hands and faces cut by stone dust and want. Socially and morally, they are unknown: excluded from the cultivated knowledge of the Earl's world, strangers to its refinements, cut off beyond the wall in a life so alien that 'all radiance [is] gone' from it. The word thus fuses appearance and status — the rude as the unknowing, the unpolished as the unknown, their roughness the outward sign of their exile.

Semantic boundaries with near-synonyms

  • Crude and rough name the raw state of material or manner, without the social-epistemological dimension; uncouth adds the idea of being outside the circle of the known.
  • Boorish and loutish accuse active, intrusive bad manners; uncouth is more a condition of being unpolished than an act of offence.
  • Gauche names the specific wrong-footed gesture of the uninitiated; uncouth is a whole roughness of person, manners, and knowledge.
  • Vulgar points at the common people and the taste imputed to them; uncouth keeps its epistemological core — the unpolished as the unknown.

Register

  • The word is old-fashioned in modern English, often slightly comic, yet capable of the older, bleaker force: the rude as the unknowing, excluded from the circle of the known — the sense the passage draws on, where the carvers' roughness is not a joke but an exile.
