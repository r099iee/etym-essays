Strapped is the past tense and past participle of the verb strap, and its central picture is that of binding something fast with a narrow strip of leather, webbing, or cord pulled tight and buckled, so that it cannot shift, spill, or escape.

In the source sentence Pippin and Sam strapped up their three packs and piled them in the porch: they fastened the packs' own straps, cinched blankets and provisions into neat bundles, and made each pack road-ready to be hoisted onto the shoulders. The little particle up adds completeness, the packs not merely touched but fully secured and finished, standing ready by the door like soldiers on parade.

Core meaning in detail: A strap began as a twisted band or thong, and the verb inherits that toughness: to strap is to confine with such a band drawn tight, whether buckling a rucksack, lashing luggage to a roof, or securing a patient to a stretcher. The past form strapped then serves as an adjective of readiness and restraint, describing anything held firmly in its harness, from a strapped helmet to a strapped-down crate in the hold of a ship.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Fastened with a strap or straps (primary sense, the source meaning): to strap up a pack, to strap luggage to a rack, to strap a child into a car seat. The bridge is direct: the noun naming the band becomes the verb naming its use.
  • Bound or tied down firmly (close extension): strapped to a stretcher, strapped into a parachute harness, strapped down against the storm. The bridge generalises the band to any restraint: ropes, belts, and webbing all do a strap's work.
  • Beaten with a strap (punitive sense, 18th–19th c.): the schoolboy strapped for idleness, the razor strapped on leather. The bridge turns the fastener into a flogger: the same leather band that binds can also strike, and the strap became a byword for corporal punishment.
  • Sharpened on a strop or strap (craft sense): a razor strapped before shaving. The bridge runs through the barber's leather strop, a broad strap on which the blade is drawn; here the strap is a tool of maintenance rather than restraint.
  • Short of money, broke (slang, chiefly strapped for cash, 20th c.): to be strapped at the end of the month. The bridge is the tightened belt: poverty cinches like a strap drawn hard, leaving no slack for spending.
  • Carrying a weapon, especially a gun (American slang, 20th–21st c.): to be strapped in certain neighbourhoods. The bridge runs through the shoulder holster: the gun rides in straps against the body, so the armed person is literally strap-bearing.
  • Equipped or burdened, for better or worse (colloquial extension): strapped with debt, strapped with kit, strapped for time. The bridge generalises the harness image: whatever one carries, duty or debt, rides on one like a pack.

Register and connotation: Strapped is workmanlike and reassuring in its homely senses — the strapped pack, the strapped helmet, the strapped load promise safety through tightness — but it hardens in its slang offspring, where strapped for cash confesses embarrassment and strapped as armed warns of danger. Tolkien uses the wholesome outdoor register: his hobbits are walkers checking buckles before the road, and the word carries the small satisfaction of a job neatly finished before an adventure.
