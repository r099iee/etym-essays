- Old English (c. 800–1100): Wēod (herb/weed) and wǣd (clothing/garment) — Existed as two distinct words; wēod denoted wild herbs and harmful field tares.
- Middle English (c. 1100–1500): Wede — Flourished in both senses: agricultural noxious plants and general garments ("palmer's wede", "beggar's wede").
- Early Modern English (c. 1500–1750): Weed / weeds — Garment sense narrowed to ceremonial mourning dress ("widow's weeds"); botanical sense solidified in horticulture and Shakespearean metaphors.
- Modern English (c. 1750–present): Weeds — Dominates agronomy, marine botany (seaweeds), professional idioms ("in the weeds", "weed out"), and twentieth-century colloquial slang.
