Slavic (also Slavonic) is the adjective and, less often, the noun used in English for the Slavs — the peoples of central, eastern and south-eastern Europe who speak the Slavic languages — and for everything belonging to them: their languages, literatures, folklore, music, religions and history. It is a word of medieval Latin and Byzantine Greek origin that passed into English through learned channels, and it carries an unusually heavy freight of historical implication, including the notorious link with the English word slave.

Primary sense: the language family. Slavic is one of the major branches of Indo-European, spoken today by roughly 300 million people. It is conventionally divided into three groups:

  • East Slavic — Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian and Rusyn, descended from the medieval language of Kievan Rusʹ.
  • West Slavic — Polish, Czech, Slovak, Kashubian, the two Sorbian languages of Lusatia, and the extinct Polabian of the Elbe region.
  • South Slavic — Bulgarian, Macedonian, Slovene and the Serbo-Croatian diasystem (Bosnian, Croatian, Montenegrin, Serbian), together with the historical liturgical language Old Church Slavonic.

Secondary sense: the peoples and their culture. As an adjective, "Slavic" covers the Slavs as an ethno-linguistic family rather than a political unit. One speaks of Slavic mythology (Perun, Veles, Svarog), Slavic folklore (the vila, the rusalka, the domovoi), Slavic philology, Slavic studies (often called Slavistics), and Slavic nationalism. Because the Slavs never formed a single state and are divided among many nations and churches, the adjective describes a family resemblance rather than a unified identity; the Slavic languages are famously diverse, and mutual intelligibility is high within groups but low across them (a Pole and a Bulgarian do not understand each other without study).

The language itself in history. Proto-Slavic, the reconstructed ancestor, was still relatively unified as late as the 6th–7th centuries CE, when Slavic-speaking groups expanded dramatically across eastern and central Europe; its earlier separation from the neighbouring Baltic dialects is dated very differently by different scholars, with estimates ranging over more than a millennium. The first literary standard, Old Church Slavonic, was created for the mission of Saints Cyril and Methodius to Great Moravia in 863 CE; written first in Glagolitic and then in Cyrillic, it became the liturgical language of the Orthodox Slavs and the model for later vernacular literatures.

The slave connection. English slave derives from Medieval Latin sclavus, originally "Slav", because large numbers of Slavic captives were sold into slavery in the early medieval Mediterranean and the word became a generic term for an enslaved person. The derivation is not in doubt; what is debated is the precise mechanism and the extent to which the ethnic label already carried the meaning "captive" in Byzantine Greek. The semantic stain is real and is one reason some modern writers prefer "enslaved person" and handle the etymology with care. The same Latin root also yields Slavonian, Slovak, Slovene and the region name Slavonia, which are often confused.

Tertiary and technical senses.

  • Linguistic: "Slavic" contrasts with "Baltic", "Germanic", "Romance" and "Indo-Iranian" as a branch label; "Common Slavic" and "Proto-Slavic" name the reconstructed stages; "Slavic accentology" is a specialised field.
  • Philological: "Slavic studies" or "Slavistics" is the academic discipline devoted to the languages, literatures and cultures of the Slavic world, traditionally strong in Germany, Poland, Russia and the United States.
  • Religious: "Slavic Orthodoxy" distinguishes the Orthodox churches of the Slavic world (Russian, Serbian, Bulgarian) from Greek or Romanian Orthodoxy; "Slavic Catholic" covers Poland, Croatia and Slovakia.
  • Musicological: "Slavic" describes a style of choral writing, folk-inspired nationalism (Glinka, Dvořák, Smetana, Mussorgsky) and the modal colour of Orthodox chant.
  • Political: "Pan-Slavism" (19th century) and "Slavic unity" are ideological uses that project a cultural category onto politics; they should not be confused with the linguistic term.

Confusables. Slavic vs Slavonic: largely synonymous, but "Slavonic" is older, more formal, and strongly associated with Old Church Slavonic and with British usage. Slav is the noun for a person; Slavic is the adjective and the family name. Slovak and Slovene/Slovenian are specific nations, not synonyms. Slavonia is a region of Croatia, not the whole Slavic world. Slavism can mean a Slavic linguistic feature or, in older writing, Pan-Slavic ideology.

Register. "Slavic" is a neutral, academic and journalistic word. It is not a slur, but because it was used in racial categories in the 19th and 20th centuries, careful writers avoid phrases like "the Slavic race" and prefer "Slavic-speaking peoples".
