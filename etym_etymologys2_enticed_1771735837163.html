The ancestry of this word begins with a Latin noun of uncertain origin; the prehistory of that noun is stated explicitly, and the attested chain runs from Latin to English. No PIE root is asserted for titio. Latin titio, titiōnis (masculine noun) "firebrand, burning coal, piece of glowing wood" — attested in Latin; its deeper ancestry is uncertain, with de Vaan recording no secure Indo-European cognates, and the doubled tit- shape suggesting an expressive or possibly substrate formation. No PIE ancestry can be securely reconstructed. → Vulgar Latin *intitiāre "to set on fire, kindle, ignite" — literally "to put a firebrand to (something)", formed with in- "in, on" + titio "firebrand" (reconstructed form, inferred from the Romance reflexes). → Old French enticier "to stir up, excite, provoke, incite; to set alight (figuratively)" (attested). → Middle English enticen (c. 1300) "to incite, instigate; to allure by hope of pleasure or advantage" (attested). → English entice (verb) "to attract or tempt by offering pleasure or advantage". → English enticed (past participle of entice) "attracted or tempted by offering pleasure or advantage" — ENTICED (our card). SISTER formation from the same Latin base (shown for completeness, not as the ancestry of the card): Vulgar Latin *attitiāre "to stir up a fire, poke a fire" (ad- "to, toward" + titio) → Old French atiser → Modern French attiser "to stoke, stir up, kindle a fire; to inflame (anger, hatred)". The plain "firebrand" noun family survives in Old French tison, Italian tizzone, Spanish tizón, Portuguese tição, and Catalan tió. NOTE on the debate: the uncertain origin of Latin titio is the one genuinely open question in this chain and is stated as such. The semantic development from "kindle a fire" to "stir up passions" to "attract by pleasure or advantage" is securely documented in Old French and Middle English, and the shift from the instigating to the alluring sense is visible in the English record from c. 1300 to the modern period.
