The form terse is an early-modern and now archaic spelling variant of modern terse, meaning polished, concise, and elegantly brief in language: saying exactly what is needed in the fewest well-chosen words without roughness or waste. In the source list it stands among popular evaluative labels for prose (sincere, fast-moving, lean, urbane, hard-hitting, lucid, terse, turgid, sturdy) that reviewers apply impressionistically. To call prose terse in this older spelling is to praise it as wiped clean of superfluity, smooth to the touch of the mind.

Historically terse/terse carried both a material and a stylistic sense. Latin tersus meant wiped, rubbed clean, spruce, neat, from tergere to wipe or polish. Transferred to speech and writing, it meant refined, correct, and free from blemish, then by natural narrowing, compact and pithy. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century critics praised a terse style against diffuse, tumid, or turgid writing. The archaic terse spelling appears in exactly that Augustan and Victorian criticism before orthography settled on terse, so encountering terse today signals either period quotation or deliberate archaism.

  • Core stylistic sense (shared with terse): concisely polished prose; differs from merely short because polish and aptness are required, not bare brevity.
  • Material-etymological sense: wiped clean, rubbed smooth; differs as the literal Latin ancestor sense surviving only in etymology.
  • Neat-elegant sense: spruce, trim, well-turned; differs because it stresses finish rather than compression.
  • Energetic-pithy sense: vigorous condensation with point; differs because force joins brevity, close to laconic and epigrammatic.
  • Archaic-spelling signal: use of terse marks period flavour or scribal variance; differs pragmatically, not semantically, from modern terse.
  • Misreading risk: confusion with turgid or terce (hawk, verse form); differs because those are distinct lemmata despite visual nearness.

Domain behaviour is stable across criticism, journalism, and school reports. Reviewers love terse/terse because it compresses praise for economy, control, and intelligence into one stroke, exactly the unmethodical popular vocabulary the source passage both records and distrusts. Rhetoricians contrast it with turgid (swollen), diffuse (spread thin), and prolix (tediously long). A terse sentence delights by closure; each word earns its place. Overdone, terseness hardens into curtness, obscurity, or barked commands, which is why eulogies of terseness usually add but not bare or yet lucid.

For learners, anchor terse to tersus, wipe: prose wiped down until it shines. The modern spelling terse (pronounced /tɜːs/ in RP) covers the same ground; treat terse as its ghost, useful for reading older criticism and for recognising that today's plain-style ideals have a long polished lineage from Roman rubbing to Augustan neatness to modern minimalism.

Practical counsel follows: aim for terseness by deleting throat-clearing openers, preferring strong verbs to adverbial padding, and letting one exact noun do the work of three vague ones. Read the sentence aloud; if a word can go without loss of sense, music, or courtesy, wipe it away. What remains, if the thought itself is sound, will read as terse in the best Augustan sense.
