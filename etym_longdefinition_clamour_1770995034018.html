clamour: (1) a loud and confused noise, especially the shouting of a crowd or of people speaking at once; (2) a vehement public demand, protest, or complaint — "a clamour for justice"; (3) as a verb, to make such a noise or to demand noisily — "the crowd clamoured for an answer". The word describes sound that is not merely loud but insistently, urgently vocal — human voices raised together in a wall of competing demands. It is formal and literary in modern register, carrying the image of a public outcry rather than a mechanical din.

PRIMARY SENSE — loud confused shouting (noun)

In its first sense, a clamour is a great shout or an outcry of many voices raised together, so loud and overlapping that it is no longer intelligible as individual speech — a "wall of noise." It is human (or at least vocal) sound: crowds, petitioners, and birds all clamour; machines merely make noise.

  • Clamour of voices — the sound of people all shouting at once: "the clamour of yamen runners outside," in the context card, is the urgent, official, demanding uproar of state functionaries at the door.
  • Clamour of the marketplace — the mixed shouting and bustle of a crowd of buyers and sellers.
  • Clamour of battle — the cries and clash of combat, an older literary use ("the clamour of war").
  • Figurative / animal — "the clamour of the sea-birds," "the clamour of hounds," where the sound is loud, raucous, and persistent.

SECONDARY SENSE — vehement public demand (noun and verb)

From the sixteenth century the word specialised toward protest and petition: a clamour for something is a loud, insistent public demand, and to clamour for is to demand noisily and persistently.

  • To clamour for justice / reform / an answer — the crowd insists, and its insistence takes the form of noise: "they raised a clamour for justice that could be heard throughout the city."
  • To clamour against something — to protest noisily: "the press clamoured against the decision."
  • To clamour to be heard — to fight vocally for attention or a hearing.

This is the sense that gives the word its political weight: clamour is the sound of popular grievance making itself impossible to ignore.

THIRD SENSE — the verb

The verb clamour (UK spelling; clamor in US) means to make the noise or to voice the demand: to shout loudly, to protest vociferously, to cry out insistently. It is intransitive with prepositions — clamour for, clamour against, clamour around — and in the passive "to be clamoured for." The verb is as old in English as the noun, both coming from the same Old French and Latin stock.

SPELLING AND VARIATION

  • clamour — the British and Commonwealth spelling, retained from Old French, preserving the French -our ending.
  • clamor — the American spelling, from the Latinising -or. Both are correct in their regions; the word is pronounced identically.
  • The related words clamorous (adjective), clamorously (adverb), and clamorousness all descend from the same Latin clāmor.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES

  • vs noise — general, often mechanical or ambient; clamour requires urgent, collective, human vocal intention.
  • vs din — a loud, sustained, confused sound, possibly from machines; clamour keeps the sense of demanding voices.
  • vs uproar — a sudden explosion of anger or outrage from a distinct incident; clamour is a prolonged, overlapping, petitioning outcry.
  • vs roar — deep, continuous, unified (a lion, the sea, a crowd in unison); clamour is fragmented, dissonant, many-voiced.
  • vs racket — informal, annoying noise; clamour is formal and purposeful.

HISTORICAL BRIDGES

The word descends from Latin clāmor, "a shout, outcry," from clāmāre, "to cry out," which developed beside calāre, "to call out, proclaim" — the verb of the Roman priests who "called out" the days of the month, giving us calendar. The semantic history runs from ritual proclamation to personal outcry to political clamour: the same root that once "called out" the calendar of sacred days now names the crowd's demand for justice. The sound image — many voices raised at once, insisting on being heard — is unchanged across two and a half millennia.
