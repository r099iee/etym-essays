The splitting family The word splintered belongs to one of the most expressive families in the Germanic vocabulary — the family of splitting. At its centre stands the reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb *splītaną "to split, cleave," attested across the northern languages in Dutch splijten, Low German splitten, and, borrowed into English, Middle Dutch splitten, source of our verb to split (16th c.). Around this verb grew a nest of related words: the noun splint, a thin rigid strip of wood (the sense preserved today in medicine, where a splint immobilises a broken limb), and the noun splinter, a thin sharp fragment. English took splinter directly from Middle Dutch splinter or splinte in the 14th century, probably with the -er element of the Low German form; the word first named the thing — a needle of wood or bone — and only in the 16th century did English form the verb to splinter, "to break into splinters," from it. The root word here, splintered, is the past tense and past participle of that verb, and it records the completed act: a thing reduced to splinters. A root of uncertain depth Beyond Germanic, the prehistory grows misty. Many scholars group the split-family with a Proto-Indo-European root *(s)pley- "to split, cleave," which would connect the English words to Greek words of splitting and even to Greek σπλήν, splḗn, "spleen" — an organ whose name may derive from the idea of something that divides or filters. The reconstruction is plausible, but it is genuinely debated: the shape of the root, its presence in the various branches, and the exact relationship of the Germanic forms to it are all matters on which authorities differ. The honest statement is that the Germanic family (splint, splinter, split) is securely attested from the Middle Ages onward, while the Indo-European depth is an open question. Nothing between the 14th-century English noun and the modern participle is invented; the only uncertain stages lie below the attested Germanic horizon. From wood to faction The history of the word's meaning is the history of a metaphor. For centuries splinter and splintered were physical: shields, doors, masts, and bones splintered along the grain, leaving the long, sharp, needle-like fragments that made the word's image so precise. The verb's natural lines — wood splits along its grain, bone along its structure — gave the word its special neatness: splintering is not random smashing but fragmentation along the thing's own seams. In the 20th century this physical grammar was transferred wholesale to society and politics: communities, movements, and parties began to be said to splinter, and the noun splinter became attributive in the compound splinter group, a faction that has broken away from a larger body and kept its own sharp edge. The figurative sense borrows everything from the physical one — the idea that a body has fault-lines, and that once it breaks, it breaks along them, into fragments that do not readily reunite. Tolkien's precision In The Hobbit, the word works at full physical force. The giants' rocks, tossed into the darkness, "smashed among the trees far below, or splintered into little bits with a bang": the juxtaposition of smashed and splintered distinguishes the two fates of the boulders — crushing impact on trees, then fragmentation into little bits with an audible, explosive report. The past tense makes the destruction final and absolute, and the "little bits" add a comic, almost domestic scale to the mountain's violence. It is the grain of the thing that matters: a rock does not have grain in the way of wood, so the splintering image is a deliberate exaggeration — the rock is treated as if it, too, had seams to shatter along. The word thus carries the whole history of its family: the Dutch borrowing, the physical sense of sharp fragments, the neat, final breakage, and the terrible neatness with which the giants' game demolishes the mountain. Key points
  • English splinter (noun, 14th c.) is a borrowing from Middle Dutch splinter/splinte, part of the Germanic split-family (split, splint, splinter).
  • The verb to splinter (16th c.) is formed from the noun; splintered is its past tense and participle.
  • The PIE connection (*(s)pley- "to split") is plausible but debated; the Germanic and Middle Dutch stages are secure.
  • The figurative sense — splintered communities, splinter groups — is a 20th-century extension of the physical image.
  • Tolkien uses the word for final, neat, audible fragmentation in contrast to smashing.
