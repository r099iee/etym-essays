- Classical Antiquity (5th–4th c. BCE) - Greek *phylaktērion* denoted a fortress, outpost, or guard-post (*phylassō*, "to guard"), and by extension any protective charm, talisman, or preservative medicine against evil or disease.
- Hellenistic Judaism (Septuagint & Philo, 3rd–1st c. BCE) - Greek-speaking Jewish translators in Alexandria used *phylaktērion* to render the Hebrew biblical terms *totafot* (frontlets) and *ot* (sign), conceptualizing *tefillin* as sacred safeguards of the divine covenant.
- Early Christian Era (New Testament, Matthew 23:5) - Entered Christian scripture with a polemical connotation in Jesus' rebuke of ostentatious Pharisees who "make broad their phylacteries" for public display.
- Middle English to Early Modern Era (14th–17th c.) - Borrowed into English via Late Latin *phylacterium* and Old French *filatere*; used both for the Jewish ritual tefillin and generally for magical amulets, relic pouches, and inscribed parchment charms worn on the person.
- 18th–19th Century Art History & Antiquarianism - Adopted by art historians to designate the floating inscribed parchment speech scrolls (banderoles) depicted in Romanesque and Gothic religious paintings and illuminated manuscripts.
- 20th–21st Century (Literary Theory & Fantasy/Gaming) - Roland Barthes used *phylacteries* metaphorically for textual fragments staging pleasure; simultaneously popularized in fantasy gaming (Dungeons & Dragons) as the enchanted talisman housing an undead lich's soul.
