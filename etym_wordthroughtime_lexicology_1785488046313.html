- [Late 17th c. learned coinage (1690s)] - Formed in learned Greek/Latin fashion from lexikon 'word-book' + -logia 'study, discourse'; the Oxford English Dictionary records it from the 1690s with the plain sense 'the knowledge of words'. It was an obscure, dictionary-bound word known only to scholars.
- [Two quiet centuries as a rare near-synonym (18th-19th c.)] - For nearly 200 years 'lexicology' lay dormant, used interchangeably with 'lexicography' and appearing mostly inside dictionaries themselves; the public had no need of it, and it carried no distinct meaning in common speech.
- [Disciplinary precision with structural linguistics (20th c.)] - The rise of linguistics gave the word its modern definition: the branch of linguistics that studies a language's vocabulary as a system - its layers, its structure, its change. Public perception shifted from an obscure curiosity to a named academic discipline.
- [Modern register] - Today 'lexicology' is a settled, mildly technical term of the language sciences, kept deliberately distinct from 'lexicography' (the applied craft of dictionary-making); to a general reader it still sounds bookish and taxonomic, a quiet catalogue of the word-hoard rather than a household word.
