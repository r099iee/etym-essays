• Odin: Similar because it names the same chief Germanic deity; differs because Odin is the Old Norse form (from Óðinn) popularized by Viking sagas and modern culture, while Woden is the exact Old English Anglo-Saxon form.
  • Wotan: Similar because it names the same god; differs because Wotan is the Old High German form, revived in modern German largely through Richard Wagner's operas.
  • Wuotan: Similar because it is the Old High German variant of the god-name; differs because it is the philologically more exact early German form, less familiar in English than Wagner's Wotan.
  • Thor: Similar because he belongs to the same Anglo-Saxon/Norse pagan pantheon and is the other god named in Macaulay's sentence; differs because Thor (Old English Þunor) is the thunder-god of strength, whereas Woden is the god of wisdom, war, and frenzy.
  • Tiw (Tyr): Similar because he is a Germanic sky/war god whose name gave the weekday Tuesday; differs because Tiw/Tyr is a different deity, the god of war and justice, while Woden is the god of wisdom and inspiration who gave Wednesday.
  • Frey (Freyr): Similar because he is a principal Norse deity of the fertility group; differs because Freyr is a Vanir god of peace, fertility, and harvest, whereas Woden is an Aesir god of battle-frenzy and poetic inspiration.
  • Mercury: Similar because Roman observers equated Woden with Mercury (interpretatio romana), and the equation is the reason Wednesday replaced Mercury's day; differs because Mercury is the Roman messenger-god, a very different figure, and the comparison is a Roman gloss, not genetic identity.
  • Wednesday: Similar because it preserves Woden's name in the weekday; differs because it is the name of a day of the week derived from Wōdnesdæg, not the god himself.
  • Baldr (Baldur): Similar because he is another prominent Germanic god of the Norse pantheon; differs because Baldr is the god of light and beauty, a passive victim-hero of Norse myth, whereas Woden is the restless, questing god of wisdom and war.
