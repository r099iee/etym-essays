1. The mysterious Old English background. The history of wicked begins with a word whose own origin is famously uncertain, the Old English masculine noun wicca ("sorcerer, wizard"), the male counterpart of wicce ("witch"). These words belonged to a cluster of Old English terms for practitioners of sorcery and divination, and they surface in the law-codes and homilies that condemn pagan magic from the early medieval period onward (Attestations date from about the late ninth century). Both wicca and wicce are themselves of disputed etymology: some philologists connect them through a Germanic root to ideas of concealment, waking, or divination, while others reconstruct a more shadowy pre-history; the exact deeper root is not securely settled. What matters for "wicked" is that, by the late Old English and early Middle English period, this vocabulary of sorcery fed into the formation of an adjective meaning "evil".

2. The Middle English adjective. Around 1200 the adjective wikke (also wicke) appears in Middle English meaning "bad, evil, wicked". The traditional view, reflected in Etymonline and in much of the lexicographic tradition, is that this adjective is formed from the noun wicca — on the model that a "wicked" person is literally one who deals with evil spirits, a sorcerer or one steeped in forbidden arts. An alternative hypothesis derives wikke from the Old Norse verb víkja, "to turn aside or deviate", so that the wicked person is one who turns away from the right path — a semantic parallel to the Biblical "erring" and "apostate". The Oxford English Dictionary judiciously marks the word's origin as uncertain, and it is possible that more than one strand contributed. What is certain is that by c. 1300 the extended form wikked (with a parasitic adjectival -ed, exactly like the later "dogged" or "crabbed") had crystallised as the standard strong adjective for moral evil in English.

3. The scriptural age. The great amplification of wicked came with religion. In Middle English religious prose and then overwhelmingly in the English Reformation, "wicked" and "wickedness" became key terms of sin and depravity. The King James Bible (1611) uses "wicked" hundreds of times — "The wicked flee when no man pursueth" (Proverbs 28:1), "the way of the wicked", "wicked men" — and through the unmatched cultural reach of the Authorised Version, the word entered the religious, literary, and folk consciousness of the English-speaking world as the archetypal term for the godless and depraved. John Bunyan, the Puritan sermons, and later Victorian moral writing all draw on this well of biblical gravity.

4. Fairytale and the softening of the register. From the eighteenth and especially the nineteenth century, wicked took on a second, milder life in the nursery and the folk tale. The "wicked witch", "wicked stepmother", and "wicked fairy" of collected fairy tales (the Grimms' and Perrault's stories in translation, and their Victorian retellings) made "wicked" the stock adjective of archetypal villainy — still evil, but stylised and cartoonishly clear. Alongside this, a playful extension developed for children and mischief: "a wicked little grin", "wicked clever" — a roguish, affectionate use that drains off most of the moral horror. This softening is important because it prepared the ground for the modern slang reversal.

5. The slang reversal. In the late twentieth century wicked completed a remarkable semantic turnabout. In New England, most iconically in Boston, "wicked" became an intensifier meaning "very" ("wicked cold", "wicked bad") and a term of approval ("wicked awesome") — the usage spread through American youth culture, films, and television in the 1980s and 1990s. In British youth slang of the same period "wicked!" or "that's wicked" meant "excellent, brilliant". Both are instances of the linguistic "excess → approval" flip: a word that once denoted extreme badness came to denote extreme anything, and then extreme goodness. The same inversion is visible in "bad" (US slang "good"), "sick", "ill", and "dope" ("excellent"). Since the intense, transgressive history of "wicked" made it a natural flag for "intense", the community that prized intensity adopted it as praise.

6. Technical afterlife. Finally, in 1973 the planners Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber coined the phrase "wicked problem" to describe social and policy problems that resist solution because every attempt to frame them changes them — problems with no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and no final test. This technical sense, popularised ever since across design, business, and public-policy writing, is a metaphor from the moral word: the problem is "wicked" because it is diabolically, intractably difficult to tame. It is a striking example of a moral-religious word being systematically redeployed as a technical term of complexity. In short, wicked has travelled, over a thousand years, from the suspected vocabulary of witchcraft, through the centre of biblical condemnation, into the fairy-tale nursery, and from there into the approving slang of Boston streets and the precise jargon of planning theory — a genuinely extraordinary polysemic career.
