Pre-Latin stage uncertain: Latin mātūrus 'ripe, mature, of full growth' (attested from the 3rd c. BCE) has no securely reconstructed PIE etymon — a connection to a PIE root *mā- / *meh₂- 'to ripen, become soft, good' is sometimes suggested, but remains unproven, so the secure record begins in Latin → Old French meür, meur (Modern French mûr) 'ripe, mature' (9th–12th c.) → Old French phrase de meur 'of mature years; settled, grave, steady' (literally 'of ripe [age]') → Anglo-Norman demure, demeur 'settled, grave, sober, calm' (13th–14th c.) → Middle English demure 'calm, settled, grave, serious' (attested c. 1400) → semantic shift to 'quiet, reserved, modest in bearing' (15th–16th c.) → modern English DEMURE (current form). Debated/nuanced stages: Two origins have long competed. The derivation given here — Old French de meur 'of mature [years]', from Latin mātūrus — is now generally preferred, and it explains the archaic 'settled, grave' sense perfectly. The rival theory derives the word from Old French demeurer 'to stay, remain, dwell' (from Latin dēmorārī 'to delay, linger'), which would yield 'one who stays — settled, staid'; the OED historically weighed both and favoured de meur, but uncertainty about the earliest transmission remains, and the pre-Latin origin of mātūrus itself is unknown. Both uncertainties are stated here rather than smoothed over.
