disquiet denotes a state of disturbed calm: unease, anxiety, restlessness, or worry that prevents mental peace. It is quietness undone. Where quiet suggests stillness, rest, and freedom from disturbance, disquiet names the active presence of disturbance — a low, continuing hum of concern that will not settle. It is less violent than panic or terror, less sharp than fear, and less clinical than anxiety: it is the middle register of troubled feeling, intellectual as much as emotional.

As a noun, disquiet has two closely linked senses. First, the inward feeling: mental uneasiness, foreboding, or preoccupation. One speaks of growing disquiet about an election, private disquiet, public disquiet, or a note of disquiet in an otherwise confident report. Second, the outward condition: unrest, agitation, or disturbance in a group or place, as in civil disquiet or industrial disquiet. The two senses mirror each other: a disturbed mind and a disturbed community. In De Tocqueville's usage, to note something with disquiet is to observe with thoughtful alarm rather than hysteria — a measured but genuine disturbance of confidence.

As a verb (to disquiet), it means to make uneasy, to deprive of calm: to be disquieted by news, to disquiet the mind. The past participle disquieted and adjective disquieting are both common: a disquieting silence, a disquieting resemblance, disquieting news. Here the emphasis falls on the cause of disturbance rather than the feeling itself.

  • Core sense — anxious unease: a persistent, often low-level worry about what may happen. It implies consciousness of risk without full knowledge. Example: investor disquiet before a central-bank decision.
  • Intellectual sense — troubled reflection: the scholar's or observer's unease when facts do not fit reassuring theories. De Tocqueville's disquiet belongs here: not raw fear but reflective concern that ideas alone, divorced from words, may mislead.
  • Social sense — unrest: collective unease verging on agitation. Disquiet among troops, disquiet in the provinces. This sense is formal and slightly literary today, often preferred by historians and journalists over unrest when they wish to stress mood rather than action.
  • Literary sense — spiritual restlessness: in poetry and religious writing, disquiet can name the soul's inability to rest, akin to angst or acedia. Shakespeare, Donne, and the King James Bible use disquieted for a soul stirred within itself.

Register and collocation matter. Disquiet is formal, literary, and measured. One would write of growing disquiet in an editorial, but say worry or nerves in conversation. Typical collocations include express disquiet, voice disquiet, cause for disquiet, with some disquiet, deep disquiet, quiet disquiet — the last pairing being deliberately paradoxical. Adjectival use favours disquieting over disquiet itself: a disquieting trend, a disquieting calm.

  • Disquiet vs anxiety: anxiety is broader, clinical, and often objectless; disquiet is milder, more cognitive, and usually attaches to a named concern.
  • Disquiet vs unease: near-identical, but unease is more physical and immediate, while disquiet suggests sustained mental agitation and often moral or intellectual weight.
  • Disquiet vs alarm: alarm is sudden and sharp; disquiet builds slowly and lingers.

In short, disquiet is the word for calm that has been spoiled from within — thoughtfulness shadowed by worry, peace interrupted not by noise but by knowledge.
