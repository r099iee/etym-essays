- 1580s — pusillanimous enters English from Late Latin as a theological and moral term for faint-heartedness, at home in sermons and treatises on courage.
- 17th c. — it moves into political and military writing, where it describes cowardly counsel, surrender and desertion.
- 18th–19th c. — established as a formal term of abuse in parliamentary and journalistic prose, often paired with policy and conduct.
- 20th–21st c. — survives chiefly as a high-register political insult, rarer than cowardly but sharper because its Latin weight implies a considered moral verdict.
