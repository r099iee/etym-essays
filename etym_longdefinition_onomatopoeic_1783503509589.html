onomatopoeic (adjective; also spelled onomatopoetic, earlier onomatopœic) — relating to or characterized by onomatopoeia: the formation of a word from a sound associated with what is named. An onomatopoeic word is one whose pronunciation imitates the sound it denotes — buzz, hiss, splash, cuckoo, bang, clang, murmur. The word describes both the linguistic phenomenon (a word formed by sound imitation) and, by extension, style or effect (writing that imitates sound through its own sound). CORE SENSES
  • Linguistic sense (word formation). Of a word: formed in imitation of the natural sound of the thing or action it denotes. Examples: cuckoo (the bird's call), hiss, buzz, bang, splash, clang, crunch. This is the primary, technical sense used in grammar and phonetics.
  • Stylistic / rhetorical sense. Of language, verse, or prose: containing or characterized by words that imitate sounds, so that the sound of the passage reinforces its sense — e.g. a poet's onomatopoeic words reproducing "the clash and clang of the iron battlefield" (the card's context) or Homer's descriptions of battle din.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED MEANINGS
  • Phonaesthetics and sound symbolism. In linguistics and poetics, onomatopoeic is extended to describe sound symbolism more broadly — the phonaesthetic quality of a word whose phonetic texture seems to mirror its meaning (murmur, shimmer, glisten), even where the imitation is of sensation rather than a discrete sound. Scholars distinguish true onomatopoeia (direct acoustic imitation) from phonaesthesia (suggestive sound quality); onomatopoeic strictly covers the former and is loosely used for the latter.
  • Onomatopoeic harmony. In poetry criticism, "onomatopoeic harmony" names the effect whereby clusters of vowels and consonants echo the sounds being described (e.g. Tennyson's "murmuring of innumerable bees" — the m's, r's, and sibilants imitate the insects' hum).
  • Cross-linguistic variation. Because imitation passes through a language's own phoneme inventory, the same sound is "heard" differently in different languages — cocks crow cock-a-doodle-doo in English but kikiriki in German and co-co-ri-co in French; all of these are onomatopoeic words, showing that onomatopoeia is conventional as well as natural.
  • Cultural and literary references. In comic books, onomatopoeic words (POW!, BANG!, SPLASH!) are a stock device; in linguistics the debate over the "bow-wow theory" (that language began in onomatopoeia) uses the adjective to describe the earliest hypothesized words.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE The word is a transparent Greek compound: ónoma "name" + poieîn "to make," joined as onomatopoiía (ὀνοματοποιΐα) "the making of names" — the ancient Greek rhetorical term for creating a word by imitating a sound. The sense moved from "name-making" in general (any coined word) to the specific modern sense "sound-imitative word" by narrowing: grammarians and rhetoricians used onomatopoiía for the figure in which a word is coined to echo a sound, and the adjective onomatopoeic inherited that narrowed sense. The second element -poeia is the same root as poet and poetry (poiētḗs "maker"), so the word literally means "name-making" — words that "make their own name" by sounding like what they mean.
