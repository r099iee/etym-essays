BAGGY in its literal, physical sense means loose, puffed out, or hanging like a bag: baggy trousers, a baggy sweater, baggy eyes, baggy skin. The garment or flesh has more material than its contents require, so it sags, wrinkles, and folds, failing to follow the body's line. The image is of a bag half-empty — cloth that should be taut but slumps. The register is everyday and visual, mildly disapproving when describing tailoring (smart clothes should not be baggy) but affectionate when describing comfort (students love baggy hoodies). First securely attested in 1743 for clothing, it generalises to any loose envelope: baggy sails, baggy parachutes, baggy cheeks.

In the immediate context, BAGGY is transferred from tailoring to terminology: The word predator is ______ with misuse. Just as baggy trousers no longer grip the leg, the word predator no longer grips its meaning; stretched by journalists, politicians, and casual speakers over hawks, shareholders, and sexual offenders, it sags with accumulated senses. To be baggy with misuse is to be distended by slack handling — the lexical bag overfilled with wrong contents until it loses shape. The criticism is conservationist and lexicographic at once: sloppy words license sloppy killing, because a predator that means everything means nothing, and hawks pay for the looseness.

  • Primary sense: hanging loosely like a bag (clothes, skin, sails). Baggy jeans pool at ankles, baggy jumpers slip off shoulders, baggy eyelids droop with age or fatigue, old sails go baggy and spill wind. Semantic bridge to the predator sentence is container-shape analogy: a word is a bag that should fit its referent snugly; overuse stretches the weave until definition wrinkles. The moral is tailoring as truth: precise words fit, baggy words flap.
  • Secondary sense: loosely constructed and inflated with inessential elements (prose, plots, arguments). Critics dismiss baggy novels, baggy symphonies, baggy essays — works that sprawl, digress, and need cutting. A baggy monsters work in the nineteenth-century novel (Dickens, Thackeray) is capacious but shapeless. Bridge: from sagging cloth to sagging structure via excess material; both need taking in. A word baggy with misuse is a miniature baggy novel: too many episodes stuffed into one cover.
  • Tertiary and slang senses: baggy as noun and specialised adjective. In British colloquial plural, bags or baggies mean trousers, especially wide Oxford bags of the 1920s; in nautical use a baggy sail is a blown-out sail; in botany baggy calyces puff like pouches. The comparative baggier and superlative baggiest, adverb baggily and noun bagginess, allow fine grading of looseness. Bridge: metonymy from quality to garment — baggy trousers become bags — showing how fully the image has entered dress history. The predator sentence inverts this: not garment named for quality, but quality diagnosed through garment.

Grammatically BAGGY is a regular -y adjective (bag, baggy, baggier, baggiest), predicative or attributive, often with with-phrase of cause (baggy with misuse, baggy at the knees, baggy under the eyes) or from-phrase of origin (baggy from wear). Collocations include baggy trousers, baggy clothes, baggy eyes, baggy narrative. It is gradable and frequentative: things become baggy through time, wash, or abuse, never by design — bagginess is entropy made visible. Phonology /ˈbæɡi/ keeps the short bag vowel plus diminutive -y, sounding itself slightly saggy.

For learners, contrast BAGGY with loose-fitting (neutral-technical), oversized (deliberately large), saggy (drooping from weight or age, especially flesh), floppy (lacking stiffness), and vague (mentally imprecise without textile image). Only baggy unites textile sag and semantic slack in one homely picture: a word like trousers that no longer holds its shape because too many bodies have worn it carelessly.
