Deep stage: no secure PIE reconstruction exists for the infant-bed word; standard dictionaries (OED, AHD) mark the ultimate origin as uncertain. [DEBATED — see note below] → Old English cradol (also spelled cradel, cradul) 'a small bed for a child; a supporting frame,' attested from the late 10th century CE (e.g., in Ælfric's writings and in glossaries) → Middle English cradel, cradel(e) 'a cradle; an infant's bed,' 12th–15th centuries CE → Modern English cradle 'an infant's rocking bed; a supporting framework; a place of origin,' 16th century onwards → CRADLES — the card's target form: plural of cradle, as in 'shields as cradles or dish-covers.' NOTE on the debate: several deeper connections have been proposed but none is universally accepted. One common suggestion links cradle to a Germanic word for a woven basket or crate (cf. Old High German kratto, krezzo 'basket' and Middle Dutch krade), on the plausible grounds that early cradles were basket-like; another compares Celtic forms (e.g., Welsh crud 'cradle'); a third, older proposal ties it to the Latin wickerwork word cratis (source of English crate and grate). Because the form and meaning cannot be matched with full certainty to any one of these, the responsible chain roots the word in Old English and flags everything earlier as speculative.
