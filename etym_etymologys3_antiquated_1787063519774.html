Origins in “that which lies before.” The story of antiquated begins with the Indo-European spatial root *h₂ent-, “front, forehead,” which in its locative form *h₂énti produced the sense “before, in front of, opposite.” From this root Latin built the preposition and adverb ante, “before, in front (of),” attested from the earliest Latin inscriptions of the 3rd century BCE. What makes the word interesting is that Latin then forged an adjective from ante meaning literally “situated before,” and from this plain spatial word grew the whole family of time-related vocabulary that English later borrowed: antiquus “ancient,” antiquitas “antiquity,” antiquarius “one versed in old things” (our “antiquarian”).

The legal twist: making a law old. The decisive step for antiquated was the creation of the verb antiquare in the Roman Republic. In the law-making assemblies of Rome, when a magistrate proposed a new law, citizens voted by dividing to one side or the other; a proposed statute that failed was said to be antiquata — literally “made old,” that is, consigned to the past and refused. This is the semantic bridge that explains everything later: the notion of “making something old” became a formal act of rejection. To “antiquate” a bill was to declare it old and therefore unacceptable.

Entering English. English took the words through two channels. In the 16th century “antique” arrived from Old/Middle French antique, itself from Latin antiquus, and meant simply “old” (and eventually, by the 18th century, “valued because old”). At about the same time the verb “antiquate” was taken directly from Latin, and from it the participial adjective antiquated was formed, first recorded around 1600. Early usage (“antiquated” = “grown old, ancient”) was fairly neutral, close to “old.” Shakespeare and his contemporaries could use it of a person's age without heavy criticism.

Drift toward criticism. During the 18th century, the intellectual climate of the Enlightenment — faith in reason, progress, and improvement — loaded the word with negative value. Antiquated customs, antiquated privileges, antiquated institutions became the target of reformist writers who wanted to sweep away what the past had left standing. By the 19th century the word was a standard weapon of editorialists and parliamentarians, applied to laws, taxes, and social arrangements. It also moved down in scale from institutions to ordinary objects: an “antiquated” stagecoach in the age of the railway, an “antiquated” notion of medicine in the age of germ theory.

The modern word. Today antiquated is most at home in technology and bureaucracy. Software, hardware, databases, procedures, and infrastructure that are out of date but still nominally functioning are described as antiquated; the connotation is inefficiency, incompatibility, and being overtaken by the present rather than absolute uselessness. The word remains mildly dismissive: to call something antiquated is to imply it belongs to an earlier time and should be replaced. It coexists with a cluster of near-synonyms — “outdated” (neutral), “obsolete” (stronger, no longer usable), “archaic” (technical, chiefly of language) — and its Roman ancestor, the political rejection of a law as “made old,” still quietly informs the modern sense of being consigned to the past.

  • 3rd c. BCE: Latin ante “before”; later antiquus “ancient.”
  • Roman Republic: antiquare “to make old / reject a law.”
  • 16th c.: English “antiquate” (verb) borrowed from Latin; “antique” via French.
  • c. 1600: “antiquated” first attested, meaning “grown old.”
  • 18th–19th c.: reformist criticism loads the word with negative force.
  • 20th–21st c.: dominant use in technology and institutional criticism; “behind the times” rather than “useless.”
