Stowed is the past participle of stow, a genuinely native English verb of the Germanic layer, and its story is the story of a small word for 'place' that became a verb of careful packing and then a word of the sea. The noun behind the verb is Old English stōw 'a place', attested from the earliest English records and especially in place-names — the element survives today in names like Stow-on-the-Wold, Chepstow, and Stowmarket, where stow meant a holy place, a meeting place, or simply a place of settlement. The verb is formed directly from the noun: Old English stōwian 'to place, put', recorded from the Anglo-Saxon period, from which Middle English stowen (c. 1300) continues with the general sense 'to place, put, arrange'. The further ancestry of *stōwō 'place' is uncertain: some scholars connect it to the PIE root *steh₂- 'to stand' (a 'standing-place', like English stead and stable), but the connection is not secure and no attested intermediate stages exist, so the honest record begins at the Germanic stage with *stōwō 'place'.

The participle and its suffix. The ending -ed is the ordinary past-participle of Germanic weak verbs. It descends from the Proto-Germanic suffix *-þaz/-daz, which is itself the reflex of the PIE verbal-adjective suffix *-tós 'having been …-ed' — the same ancient suffix that gave Latin -tus (as in amātus 'loved') and Greek -tos (as in philētós 'loved'). Old English spelled it -od or -ed depending on the verb class; Middle English levelled it to -ed; modern English keeps -ed, though in speech the ending is often reduced to /d/ or /t/, as in 'stowed'. The participle's meaning — completed placement — is exactly the class of meaning the ancient *-tós suffix expressed from the beginning.

Semantic drift: from 'place' to the sea. The meaning history of the verb moves from simple 'to place' to disciplined arrangement:

  • Old English – Middle English: stōwian / stowen means simply 'to place, put, station' — no special tidiness yet.
  • 16th–18th c., the age of sail: stow becomes nautical vocabulary. To stow cargo, sails, or guns is to arrange them tightly and safely into the ship's available space — a seaman's discipline of economy and security, summed up in the noun stowage (the act, the space, and the fee for it). This maritime association gives the word its characteristic flavour of neat, firm, purposeful placement.
  • 17th c.: the phrasal stow away appears in the sense of storing away, and by the 18th–19th c. it yields the noun stowaway — a person hidden aboard a ship to travel free — extending stow from cargo to people and from arrangement to concealment.
  • 18th–19th c.: the verb generalises from ships to homes and journeys — luggage stowed, provisions stowed, and (as in Tolkien) guests stowed into spare rooms. The colloquial imperative Stow it! ('stop that talk') dates from this period, using stow to mean 'put away', i.e. cease.
  • 20th–21st c.: aviation safety instructions institutionalise the word (stow your tray tables); in everyday speech the participle stowed still means packed away, tidily arranged, put into place — the orderly fitting of things and people into finite room.

Socio-cultural and literary factors. The word's two registers — maritime and domestic — both reflect the cultures that used it: the disciplined space-management of sailing ships (where a badly stowed cargo could founder the vessel) and the housekeeperly economy of homes where provisions and guests had to be fitted into limited rooms. Tolkien's use is exactly the domestic transfer: Bilbo, a bachelor hobbit suddenly hosting thirteen dwarves, must 'find room for them all' and get them 'stowed' — the seaman's word for packing applied to guests, with the narrator's gentle comedy in the mismatch between cargo-handling and hospitality. The participle form fixes the narrative moment of completion: the job is done, everyone is stowed, and the tired, not-altogether-happy host can go to bed. From a Germanic word for 'place' to the participle of a verb of tight, tidy, purposeful placement, stowed has carried its sense of deliberate arrangement for a thousand years.
