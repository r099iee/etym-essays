English fiddle is an old native instrument-word with a contested deeper origin. Old English fiðele already names a fiddle-like stringed instrument; Middle English continues as fithele / fidhel / fedele into modern fiddle. Many handbooks reconstruct Proto-Germanic *fiþulōn (or a close shape) and treat the word as Germanic cultural vocabulary for a popular bowed or stringed instrument, with close continental cousins in Old High German fidula and Old Norse fiðla.

  • The contested source. Other scholars note a possible early contact with Medieval Latin vitula / vitella, the same obscure Latin instrument-name that feeds Romance viola and eventually English viol and violin. The Latin word's own origin is debated—some connect it to vitulārī ‘to celebrate’ and a goddess Vitula, goddess of joy and revelry, which would make the fiddle etymologically a ‘festivity instrument’. The honest tree therefore shows both paths: a solid Old English–Middle English–Modern English line, and a debated Latin/Romance reinforcement or parallel, without inventing fake cognates.
  • Sound shifts. Within English the changes are modest: Old English fiðele (with a voiced dental fricative ð) → Middle English fithele / fidhel (with th- and d- spellings jostling) → early modern fiddle, with the final unstressed vowel reduced and the word settling into its modern two-syllable shape. The plural fiddles simply adds the regular English plural -s.
  • Register split with violin. Whatever the remote source, English usage stabilizes a register split. Fiddle becomes the folk, dance, and informal name; violin—an Italian diminutive path from the same viola-stem, borrowed later—becomes the classical concert name for essentially the same four-string design. In the 17th–18th centuries the pair carried a split of class and style rather than of instrument: fiddlers played dance music at fairs and taverns, violinists commanded the court and concert hall. By the 19th–20th centuries the divide hardened into repertoire and technique—a fiddler plays folk and dance with a different bow hold and often a flatter bridge—and the word bred a rich set of idioms: ‘fit as a fiddle’, ‘to fiddle about’, ‘to play second fiddle’, and, in Britain, ‘fiddle’ for petty fraud.
  • Verb senses. From the instrument grew a family of verb and idiom senses. ‘To fiddle’ is to play the fiddle; by the rapid, fidgety movement of a fiddler's hands it also came to mean to toy, fidget, or tinker (‘to fiddle with a knob’), and then, in British informal use, to falsify or cheat (‘to fiddle the books’). ‘Fiddlesticks’ and ‘fiddle-faddle’ are dismissive reduplications from the old song-and-dance sound-word.
  • Socio-cultural context. The fiddle has always been the people's instrument, carried to dances, wakes, and fairs, portable and loud enough for a room full of dancers. That social placing—popular, mobile, informal—explains both the Tolkien context and the word's warm, slightly rustic colour.
  • Literary attestations. The word is old in English literature, from Chaucer's fiddlers to the proverbs (‘who hath no good name, is better without his fiddle’). In The Hobbit, Kili and Fili's little fiddles are travel-sized folk strings pulled from bags, not Thorin's wrapped harp or the huge viols. Tolkien's word choice is exact: young dwarves, portable gear, traditional music about to fill Bag End. The card form is the plural, as in the text—multiple small bowed instruments joining the unexpected orchestra before the deeper song begins.
