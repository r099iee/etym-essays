The story of vain begins not with pride but with absence — a hollow at the centre of things. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *h₁weh₂-, a verb meaning "to leave, to abandon, to empty out." This is not the emptiness of nothingness as a philosophical abstraction but the gut-level, physical emptiness of a dwelling after its inhabitants have departed, of hands cupped to hold water that has already drained through the fingers. The root encodes a fundamental human experience: the shock of void where fullness was expected. Its reflexes across the family confirm the primitive force: Sanskrit ūná "deficient, wanting," Germanic *wanaz "lacking" (whence English wane, of the moon's decreasing light, and German Wahn "delusion," an empty imagining), and Greek εὖνις (eûnis) "bereft, robbed of." As this root entered the Italic branch it crystallised into the Proto-Italic adjective *wanos "empty, void, hollow," and Latin refined this into vānus, whose semantic range had already begun its remarkable expansion. Vānus meant not merely "empty" in the physical sense but "idle" (speech without substance), "false" (promises without foundation), "deceptive" (appearances without reality), and — crucially — "ostentatious, boastful" (display without merit). This leap from "empty vessel" to "empty boasting" is one of the great intuitive metaphors in the history of language: the person who boasts is a hollow container, all surface and no substance. Cicero wielded vānus as a term of moral condemnation, Ovid deployed it of the empty promises of lovers, and Lucretius used it to strip religious superstition of its pretensions. From vānus Latin generated a prolific family of derivatives that seeded half the modern European vocabularies of emptiness. Vānitās "emptiness, worthlessness, falsity, and eventually conceit" gave the Church Fathers a crucial term for the futility of earthly existence — a usage immortalised when Jerome translated the Hebrew hebel "breath, vapor, vanity" as vanitas in Ecclesiastes: vanitas vanitatum, omnia vanitas ("vanity of vanities, all is vanity"). This single translation choice would echo through two millennia of Western literature, giving the word its apocalyptic, cosmic register. The verb vānēscere "to pass away, come to nothing" produced, through its compounded form, the English vanish and evanesce; the adjective vācūus "empty, free" (from a related formation) gave vacant, vacate, vacation, and vacuous; and Vulgar Latin *vānitāre "to boast" gave Old French vanter and English vaunt. Into this prolific family the French adjective vain / vein entered the English language in the thirteenth century through the Anglo-Norman speech of the Plantagenet court. The borrowing arrived with both strands of the Latin meaning already fused: "empty, futile, worthless" on one side, and the rising sense of "empty pride" on the other. Middle English vein / veyn / vain quickly became one of the great moral words of the period, the natural adjective for the hollowness of worldly striving — a theme that runs from the sermons of the fourteenth century through Spenser, Shakespeare, Milton, and the English Bible. Shakespeare exploits both senses with characteristic precision: the Dauphin's "vain" boasts in Henry V are punctured as empty display, while the Sonnets wrestle with the "vain" (futile, self-defeating) desire to immortalise beauty in verse. By the seventeenth century the word had settled into the double life it leads today. The phrase "in vain" — "without success or result" — had crystallised as the canonical idiom of futility, given permanent shape by the King James Bible ("Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain") and by Milton's "in vain" they war against heaven. At the same time the conceit sense hardened: the Renaissance imported the Italianate ideal of cortegiania while condemning vain-glory, and the word became the standard moral term for excessive pride in appearance. Shelley's Ozymandias — whose "colossal Wreck" lies shattered in the desert, all ambition rendered hollow — is the Romantic culmination of the word's first sense; the "vain" socialite of the nineteenth-century novel is its second. In modern English the word runs two parallel lives, both still grounded in the idea of emptiness. The idiomatic "in vain" preserves the Ecclesiastes grandeur in daily use ("I searched in vain"), while the everyday sense of a person "vain about their looks" preserves the moral condemnation of the hollow boaster. The same root that gave Latin its word for the void gave English wane, vanish, vacant, and vain itself — a remarkable family in which one ancient verb of leaving and emptying continues, after four thousand years, to name both the futility of effort and the emptiness of pride.
