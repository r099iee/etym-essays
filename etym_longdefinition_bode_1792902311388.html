Bode means to be an omen of what is coming, to foreshadow or presage a future outcome: if something bodes well, it promises a fortunate result, and if it bodes ill, it warns of trouble ahead. The verb looks forward always, reading the present as a sign of the future.

In the source sentence Gandalf's lateness does not bode well: his absence is read as a sign, and the sign points to trouble. The speaker is not describing the delay itself but interpreting it, treating a missed arrival as a weather-vane for the quest's fortunes, which is exactly the immemorial work this verb performs.

Core meaning in detail: To bode is to carry prophetic weight without uttering prophecy: a dark sky bodes rain, a smile bodes favour, a late wizard bodes mishap. The subject of bode is the omen itself, the sign, while the coming event stays half-hidden in the adverb, well or ill, that almost always accompanies it. The construction bode well and bode ill is among the most fixed idioms in English, two thousand years of fortune-telling compressed into two short phrases.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To foreshadow or portend, to be an omen (primary modern sense): black clouds bode storms, early sales bode success; the default sense, always oriented toward the future, with well and ill as its constant companions.
  • To proclaim or announce, to foretell explicitly (archaic): prophets bode the doom to come in older texts; the bridge is the word's origin in Old English bodian, to announce or command, since an omen was first imagined as a message delivered.
  • To abide or await, past of bide converging in form (homonymic overlap): he bode his time uses bode as the old past tense of bide; the bridge is pure sound-change coincidence, two verbs wearing one coat, yet poets have enjoyed the pun, biding and boding in a single breath.
  • To dwell or remain (archaic bide sense): knights bode in halls awaiting dawn; the bridge is the same homonym, the waiting sense of bide dressed in bode spelling.
  • To command or decree (obsolete Old English sense): a lord bodes his men to battle, that is, bids them; the bridge runs through the messenger root, since the herald who announces also conveys orders.
  • To promise or threaten by aspect — weather and markets (modern extended): still air bodes thunder, falling orders bode recession; the bridge is professional forecasting, since economists and meteorologists are the licensed omen-readers of commercial society, and their models are horoscopes disciplined by statistics.

Register and connotation: Bode is solemn, watchful, and faintly archaic outside its two great idioms: nobody bodes in casual chat, but statesmen, novelists, and wizards bode constantly. Its connotation is interpretive gravity, the hush before the verdict. To say a thing bodes ill is to borrow the mantle of the seer while keeping both feet in common sense, a hedge and a warning in a single syllable.
