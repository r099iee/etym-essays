- The word slipped into English sideways in the 1500s meaning "to be dull, stupefied, sluggish" — closer to "dazed" than to sleep; a "dozing" head was a thick, slow head.
- In the early 1600s it gained the modern core sense "to sleep lightly", and for two centuries it described the nodding, half-awake sleep of the old, the idle, and the convalescent, usually with a mildly comic or contemptuous tone.
- The 18th century fixed the noun: "a doze" became a short, light sleep, and writers like Swift and Smollett used it of anyone sinking into drowsiness at odd hours.
- By the 19th century, industry and punctuality made the doze a small public sin — clerks were reprimanded for "dozing" at their desks — while invalids and grandmothers were still permitted a gentle doze by the fire.
- In modern English it has split into two registers: "doze off" (sudden, involuntary, and slightly embarrassing — as in a meeting or a lecture) and the nostalgic "a nice doze" (a warm, harmless luxury). The word still hovers, exactly like the state it names, between sleep and waking.
