• Petrarch: Similar because the lifelong friend and co-founder of Italian humanism, the other of the ‘three crowns’. Differs because Petrarch wrote Latin letters, sonnets of interiority and moral treatises in a solitary, austere voice, while Boccaccio wrote vernacular prose comedies of the crowd and the marketplace.
  • Dante: Similar as the third crown of Tuscan literature, writing in the vernacular. Differs because Dante’s Comedy is a theological epic of the afterworld, Boccaccio’s Decameron a secular, comic catalogue of this world.
  • Chaucer: Similar as a medieval storyteller who built an English frame-tale collection (the Canterbury Tales) on the Decameron’s model, with the same earthy comedy. Differs because Chaucer wrote in Middle English half a century later and mixed estates satire and courtly romance with the comic tales.
  • Apuleius: Similar as the ancient author of a framed comic novel (the Metamorphoses, or Golden Ass) with bawdy inset tales. Differs because Apuleius wrote Latin prose in the 2nd century CE, whereas Boccaccio wrote Italian prose in the 14th and shaped the modern short story.
  • The Thousand and One Nights: Similar in frame-story technique — a story of storytellers who stave off doom with narrative. Differs because the Nights is an anonymous, many-layered Arabic and Persian oral cycle, while Boccaccio is a single named author, the ‘birth of the Renaissance writer’.
  • Rabelais: Similar as a Renaissance writer of grotesque, encyclopedic comic prose. Differs because Rabelais’s French Gargantua and Pantagruel is philosophical-anarchic fantasy of giants, whereas Boccaccio’s frame keeps comedy inside the walls of a real, plague-stricken city.
