AGROCHEMICALS (noun, plural, usually treated as plural but often used attributively) denotes manufactured chemical products used in agriculture to increase yield or protect crops and livestock — principally synthetic fertilisers, pesticides (insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, nematicides), and associated adjuvants, growth regulators and soil conditioners. In the source context, organochlorine-based agrochemicals refers specifically to the persistent chlorinated insecticides such as DDT, dieldrin and aldrin that biomagnified through food chains and devastated peregrine falcons.

Core technical sense: any industrially synthesised compound applied to soil, seed, crop or stored produce. Fertilisers supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium (NPK); pesticides kill or deter pests, weeds and fungal pathogens; adjuvants improve adhesion and uptake. Agrochemical companies formulate, test and market these under trade names, subject to licensing (in the UK by the Chemicals Regulation Division; in the US by the EPA). Farmers speak of “spray programmes”, “application rates” and “pre-harvest intervals” — all agrochemical regimes.

  • Industrial-agriculture sense: the material base of the post-war Green Revolution. Without Haber–Bosch nitrogen and selective herbicides, 20th-century yield gains would have been impossible. In this neutral register, agrochemical is synonymous with crop-protection product or farm input.
  • Ecotoxicological / polemical sense: a pejorative shorthand for polluting, persistent, health-harming farm chemistry. Silent Spring, The Peregrine and modern organic discourse use agrochemicals to evoke runoff, dead rivers, eggshell thinning, pesticide residues and farmer exposure. Here near-synonyms are poisons, toxins, biocides — emotionally loaded where the industry term is neutral.
  • Commercial-collective sense: the agrochemical industry / sector / market — the half-dozen multinationals (Syngenta, Bayer-Monsanto, Corteva, BASF) that dominate seeds-plus-chemistry packages. Financial journalism writes of “agrochemical sales”, “agrochemical patents”, “agrochemical regulation”.
  • Extended attributive use: agrochemical pollution, agrochemical runoff, agrochemical exposure, agrochemical treadmill (the cycle in which pesticide resistance demands ever stronger doses). The singular agrochemical denotes one compound: “Atrazine is an agrochemical banned in the EU.”

Polysemy bridge: all senses share the compositional core agro- (field) + chemical (manufactured substance). The split between neutral and pejorative is not lexical but evaluative and historical: before 1962 the word promised abundance; after Carson and Baker it also warned of extinction. That duality explains why the same sentence can be read as technical description or indictment depending on audience.

Domain notes: in law and regulation the preferred hypernyms are pesticides and fertilisers separately, because they fall under different directives; agrochemicals is journalistic and ecological umbrella language. In science, agrochemistry is the discipline studying soil–chemical interactions. Do not confuse with petrochemical (oil-derived industrial chemical generally) or pharmaceutical (medicinal drug): agrochemicals are defined by use-site (the farm), not by chemistry alone. Organochlorine-based, organophosphate, carbamate, pyrethroid and neonicotinoid name chemical families within the class.
