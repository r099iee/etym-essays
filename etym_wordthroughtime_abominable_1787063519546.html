- 12th c. (Ecclesiastical Latin) - Latin *abominabilis* (from *ab-* "away" + *ōmen* "prophetic sign") referred specifically to ill-omened, accursed religious taboos.
- 14th c. (Middle English & Biblical theology) - Borrowed via Old French *abominable*, dominating Wycliffite and biblical texts to describe idolatry, blasphemy, and mortal sins.
- 16th c. (Renaissance false etymology) - Frequently misspelled as *abhominable* due to the folk belief that it derived from Latin *ab homine* ("away from man / inhuman"), intensifying its sense of monstrous inhumanity.
- 17th–18th c. (Gothic & Dramatic expansion) - Used by Shakespeare, Milton, and Gothic writers to denote demonic monsters, ghastly horrors, and vile sensory filth.
- 20th c. (Cryptozoology & Hyperbole) - Popularized globally in 1921 via the "Abominable Snowman" (Yeti); colloquially softened in British and Commonwealth speech to describe dreadful weather or bad manners.
