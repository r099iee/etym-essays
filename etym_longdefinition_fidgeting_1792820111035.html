Fidgeting is the present participle of fidget: making small, quick, restless movements, especially with the hands, fingers, or feet — twisting, tapping, picking, jiggling — typically without conscious purpose and often without noticing. It is the body's way of leaking excess energy, the visible static of nerves, boredom, excitement, or impatience.

In the source sentence Bilbo stands up, fidgeting with something in his pocket, while facing the unwelcome Sackville-Bagginses, and the participle does quiet character work: the respectable Hobbit, caught off guard and indisposed to see these relatives at any rate, vents his unease on whatever lies in his pocket, likely the Ring. Readers see a small restless hand-motion and infer a whole churn of feeling — embarrassment, concealment, the wish to be elsewhere — which is exactly what fidgeting always communicates.

Core meaning in detail: To fidget is to move in fragments: not the large purposeful motions of work or gesture but the tiny repetitive ones — the pen clicked, the key turned over, the foot jiggled under the table. Psychologists read it as self-regulation: the motion discharges arousal, sustains attention during boredom, or soothes anxiety by giving the hands something harmless to do. The -ing form stresses that it is ongoing and observable, a behaviour others can see and interpret, which is why parents, teachers, and interviewers comment on it and why fiction uses it as shorthand for guilt or nerves.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Nervous restless hand-motion (primary modern sense, from 1670s; source sense): fidgeting with keys, coins, or pocket-contents under social strain. The bridge is the original Middle English sense of moving restlessly, narrowed to the small hand-motions anxiety produces.
  • Bored or impatient restlessness (everyday sense): children fidgeting in church, audiences fidgeting through long speeches. The bridge is energy without outlet: the body protests enforced stillness by moving in miniature, signalling let me out without standing up.
  • Playful tinkering and fiddling (neutral sense): fidgeting with dials, settings, or gadgets, half curiosity and half habit. The bridge softens anxiety into experimentation: the same small manipulations become learning-by-touch when no stress drives them.
  • Clinical restlessness and hyperactivity (medicalised sense, 20th c.): fidgeting as a diagnostic marker in attention and anxiety conditions. The bridge is observation scaled up: what parents always noticed becomes what clinicians count, and the playground word enters rating scales.
  • Fidget toys and deliberate fidgeting (commercial sense, 2010s): spinners, cubes, and rings sold to be fidgeted with. The bridge inverts the stigma: if restless hands are inevitable, give them a designed outlet, and the vice becomes a product category.
  • Figurative restlessness of things (transferred): fidgeting markets, a fidgeting engine at idle. The bridge lends the body's jitter to systems that twitch without settling, markets and motors heard as nervous hands.

Register and connotation: fidgeting is gently disapproving but affectionate — the word of parents, teachers, and narrators rather than of judges. It implies no malice, only undiscipline or unease, and confessing to it disarms: he was fidgeting asks our sympathy for the nerves behind the motion. Only in formal settings (interviews, courtrooms, ceremonies) does it harden into criticism, where stillness reads as respect and every jiggle reads as doubt.
