- [Late 16th–Early 17th c. Elizabethan & Jacobean Eras] — Formed in Early Modern English by compounding the adverbial particle on with the agent noun looker; denoted casual bystanders or curious eyewitnesses watching street altercations, public executions at Tyburn, royal pageants, or public fairs — [perception: detached, curious witnesses to public street life and judicial spectacles]
- [18th c. Enlightenment & Georgian Era] — Flourished in the vibrant urban print culture of London and Edinburgh (e.g., Addison and Steele's The Spectator); characterized the anonymous crowds who gathered in bustling public squares, coffee house districts, and theater lobbies — [perception: emblematic urban archetype of the detached observer of commercial society]
- [19th c. Victorian & Industrial Era] — Established as a standard sociological and journalistic term in mass-circulation newspapers, vividly describing the dense working-class and bourgeois crowds observing industrial train wrecks, street demonstrations, union strikes, and civic jubilee celebrations — [perception: essential element of the modern urban cityscape, highlighting the boundary between participants and public witnesses]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global Digital Era] — Deeply integrated into social psychology (the "bystander effect" or Genovese syndrome) and mass media journalism, frequently depicting crowds of pedestrians documenting breaking news, protests, or sporting events with handheld smartphones from the perimeter — [perception: ubiquitous modern term for non-participating observers, often scrutinized for passivity in moments of crisis]
