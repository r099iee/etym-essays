The verb form bunging represents a colorful collision of medieval continental brewing technology, Celtic substrate roots, Elizabethan thieves' cant, and Victorian vernacular slang. Its historical development illustrates how technical trade vocabulary can cross-pollinate with underworld argot to generate lively, multi-layered colloquial verbs.

  • Continental Brewing Roots and the Celtic Substrate (c. 1200–1450)
    The oldest root of the word belongs to brewing and coopering. In the fifteenth century, Middle English borrowed bunge from Middle Dutch bonge or bonghe ('stopper, bung of a cask'), which was closely related to Old French bonde. Historical linguists derive the Gallo-Romance forms from a Gaulish (Continental Celtic) substrate noun *bunda, meaning 'bottom, foundation' (related to Latin fundus and Old Irish bond, 'sole, base'). In medieval cooperage, the bung was the wooden peg driven into the bunghole to seal beer, wine, and cider barrels during storage and river transport. The act of 'bunging a cask' was an essential craft operation recorded in promptoria and guild ordinances of the 1440s.
  • Elizabethan Cant: From Bungholes to Purses (1560–1650)
    During the sixteenth century, the language of the London underworld—known as Thieves' Cant or Peddler's French—adopted bung with a mischievous anatomical and criminal twist. Because a purse or pouch resembled a stoppered leather pouch, cant lexicographers like Thomas Harman (1567) and Robert Greene recorded bung as the universal cant term for a purse or pocket. A pickpocket was known across the taverns of Southwark and Cheapside as a bung-nipper. In Shakespeare's Henry IV, Part 2 (Act 2, Scene 4), Doll Tearsheet berates the rogue Pistol: 'Away, you cut-purse rascal! You filthy bung, away!'
  • Semantic Broadening: Thrusting, Bribery, and Hurling (1750–1900)
    In the eighteenth century, the cant noun gave birth to new verbal uses. To 'bung' someone meant to pass or thrust money into their pocket—initially a surreptitious transaction between criminals, and later an informal term for giving a tip, gratuity, or bribe. Simultaneously, the physical action of driving a bung into a barrel (a swift, forceful downward hammer strike) combined with the concept of rapid manual transfer to give rise to the general colloquial meaning of 'throwing, chucking, or shoving'. By the mid-nineteenth century, English sporting journals, schoolboy stories, and Victorian novels used bunging to describe throwing cricket balls, hurling stones, or tossing clothes.
  • 20th-Century Literary Flowering and Commonwealth Slang
    In the twentieth century, bunging became a beloved stylistic hallmark of British comic prose, immortalized by authors such as P. G. Wodehouse, Richmal Crompton (in the Just William series), and George MacDonald Fraser. In Wodehouse's novels, Bertie Wooster and his fellow Drones frequently spend idle mornings 'moodily bunging stones at flowerpots' or 'bunging slices of toast across the dining room'. Concurrently, the word took deep root in Australia and New Zealand, where it spawned unique regional idioms like 'going bung' (breaking down) and 'bunging it on' (putting on false airs). Today, bunging remains an exceptionally lively, characterful component of informal Commonwealth English.
