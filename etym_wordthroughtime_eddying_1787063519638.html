- 15th century: Swirling aquatic backwater or whirlpool created where a river meets an obstruction (borrowed from Scandinavian / Old Norse nautical vernacular) - Primarily a practical maritime and river-navigation term.
- 16th to 17th century: Extended to atmospheric currents, gusts of wind, and swirling smoke or dust - Entered Elizabethan poetry and natural philosophy.
- 18th century: Codified in hydrodynamic theory and romantic nature poetry - Evoked delicate, picturesque, or turbulent natural phenomena.
- 19th century: Broadened to describe swirling human crowds, urban pedestrian currents, and psychological turbulence (swirling thoughts, memories) - Became a hallmark of Victorian psychological realism and impressionistic prose.
- 20th to 21st century: Standardized in fluid mechanics (eddy viscosity, turbulent eddies) while maintaining rich metaphorical presence in literature and psychology.
