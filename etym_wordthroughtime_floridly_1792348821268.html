- [Classical Roman Era (c. 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE)] — Latin flōridus literally meant 'blooming with flowers, covered in blossoms', used by poets to describe spring meadows, youthful vigor, and flourishing physical health
- [Late Latin & Scholastic Rhetorical Era (c. 4th–12th c. CE)] — developed rhetorical applications to describe ornate, adorned oratorical style (stylus floridus) featuring decorative 'flowers of rhetoric' (flores rhetorici)
- [Renaissance & Early Modern English Era (c. 1640s–1700s)] — adopted into English as florid and floridly, first characterizing flushed, ruddy complexions and flourishing bodily states, then aesthetic ornamentation
- [18th–19th c. Musical & Literary Era] — became a standard aesthetic term for ornate Baroque/Bel Canto vocal embellishments (florid counterpoint) and lavish, sentimental literary prose
- [Modern Critical & Clinical Era (20th–21st c.)] — acquired nuanced critical usage in literary history (contrasting florid devotional poetry with classical restraint) and technical diagnostic usage in medicine (floridly psychotic, floridly symptomatic)
