A word with no ancestors. The scholarly first fact about lippity is that it has no etymology in the ordinary comparative-historical sense. It is an English imitative — more precisely phonesthetic — creation whose job is to sound like what it means: the light, rhythmic, slightly uneven hop of a small rabbit. It belongs to the class of reduplicative and sound-symbolic words (also called expressives or ideophones) that English has always been able to coin on the spot, without derivation from older roots. There is no Old English *lippitian, no Middle English trail, no Proto-Germanic or Proto-Indo-European ancestor, and no Romance or Indo-Aryan source. Attempts to ground the word in lip (the body part, Old English lippa) or leap (Old English hlēapan) are folk etymologies that fail on both sound and history.

The birth certificate: Potter, 1902. The word's fame — and for practical purposes its creation — is Beatrix Potter's. In the picture-letter of 1893 that grew into The Tale of Peter Rabbit (privately printed 1901, commercial edition 1902), Potter writes that Peter, freed from the gooseberry net and recovering his nerve, "began to wander about, going lippity—lippity—not very fast, and looking all around." The sentence teaches the reader how to hear the word: soft onset, springy medial consonants, an unstressed final -ity, and a repetition separated by dashes, so that the whole functions like a pair of hops. The reduplication, the explicit tempo note ("not very fast"), and the light phonetics all work together to paint a cautious, curious, slightly comic movement — the exact opposite of a panic flight.

The English workshop of motion-mimics. Lippity sits in a rich native tradition. English has long built such imitative adverbs and compounds by reduplication and rhyme:

  • Hippity-hop / hippety-hop — the playful hopping gait of nursery rhyme and children's song, and the closest cousin of lippity.
  • Clippety-clop — the rhythm of a horse's hooves, built on the same -ippety template.
  • Pitter-patter / pit-a-pat — light rapid beats, of rain, footsteps, or a quickening heart.
  • Tippity / tippety — a light-step variant related to tiptoe.
  • Snip-snap, tick-tock, wiggle-waggle — the wider family of vowel-alternating reduplications that English uses for sound and motion.

Within this family lippity is distinguished by its acoustic temper: it is quieter and softer than hippity-hop, less mechanical than clippety-clop, and slower than pitter-patter. The -l- onset and the light -p- give a tactile sense of soft contact — paw meeting earth — rather than the sharp beats of metal or the drumming of many feet.

Afterlife and register. After 1902 the word occasionally reappears in children's writing as a knowing echo of Peter Rabbit, but it never became a general-purpose adverb of adult English. Its linguistic biography is therefore short, honest, and complete: a phonesthetic coinage in the reduplicative manner of English nursery diction; a single canonical context that defines its meaning; and a sisterhood of other sound-symbolic gait-words against which it is measured. In register it remains firmly literary and playful, prized precisely for its un-dictionary status — an honest imitation that does one job perfectly, making a rabbit's hop audible on the page.
