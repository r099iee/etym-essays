PIE (prehistoric)
*kap-
"to grasp, take, seize"
↓
Latin (3rd c. BCE)
capere
"to take, seize, capture"
↓
Latin (2nd c. BCE)
captus
"taken, seized"
(past participle of capere)
↓
Latin (c. 200 BCE)
captivus
"taken prisoner, captive"
(cf. Plautus, "Captivi")
↓
Late Latin (3rd–4th c. CE)
captīvitās
"captivity, imprisonment, bondage"
(Christian Latin; Vulgate usage)
↓
Old French (12th c.)
captivité
"captivity"
↓
Middle English (14th c.)
captivite
"captivity; exile; bondage"
↓
English (modern)
CAPTIVITY
"state of being held against one's will"

Note: English "have" and "heave" are distant cousins via the same PIE root *kap- (through Germanic *habjaną "to have" and *hafjaną "to lift, heave"). "Caitiff" (a wretch) is a separate Old French borrowing of captivus with a pejorative twist.
