In Gibbon's first chapter, conquests is the noun that names the whole republican achievement: "The principal conquests of the Romans were achieved under the republic." The word does not point at any single battle; it names the cumulative, institutional process by which the Roman Republic annexed foreign lands and peoples — the finished work of "the policy of the senate, the active emulation of the consuls, and the martial enthusiasm of the people." It then doubles as the historical boundary-marker of the narrative: after these conquests, the emperors "for the most part, were satisfied with preserving" the dominions thus won. The tone is grand, impersonal, slightly elegiac; conquest is the republic's characteristic labour, already complete before the principate begins. Core definition. A conquest is the act of taking possession of a territory or people by armed force, or the territory so won. The plural conquests denotes a series of such acquisitions — campaigns that end in lasting possession rather than momentary victory. Polysemy and modern senses:
  • Military (primary): the act or result of conquering territory in war — "the conquest of Gaul", "the Norman Conquest of 1066" (a capitalized proper name in English history).
  • Plural-historiographical (Gibbon's sense): a people's cumulative annexations treated as an institutional achievement.
  • Figurative-challenge sense: overcoming a difficulty, fear, or obstacle — "the conquest of space", "the conquest of the summit", "conquering one's fear". Here force is replaced by persistent effort.
  • Romantic and social sense: "a conquest" is a person won over, especially in courtship — "he considered her a conquest"; "to make a conquest". The bridge is the old military image of "winning" a person.
  • Sports and games: "conquest" for winning a title, opponent, or territory in strategy games; "Conquest" modes in board and video games; capturing ground in wargames and chess.
  • Spanish American history: la conquista — the Spanish Conquest of the Americas — a term of central historical and political weight in Latin American historiography.
  • Business and markets (metaphor): "market conquest", "conquering the market" — taking over markets by competition.
  • International law: the acquisition of territory by force — historically a mode of acquiring sovereignty, restricted since the twentieth century (the UN Charter and the principle of non-acquisition by force).
Historical bridge between senses. The etymology explains the reach. Latin conquirere meant "to seek thoroughly, hunt up, procure" — an intensive of quaerere "to seek". The word moved from "search for, procure" to "win, gain", and then, under the pressure of real warfare, to "take by force". Every figurative sense — conquering a fear, making a romantic conquest, market conquest — is the same word: to obtain something difficult by persistent effort, with the military colouring softened or retained depending on context. Register and collocation. In elevated historiography the noun collocates with verbs of achievement and conservation — "achieve conquests", "acquire by conquest", "preserve the dominions acquired by conquest" — with adjectives of scale ("principal", "bloody", "territorial"), and with genitives of agent ("the conquests of the Romans", "the conquests of Trajan"). In casual speech it is replaced by "takeovers", "wins", or "victories". (Full synonym boundary analysis appears in similarWords.)
