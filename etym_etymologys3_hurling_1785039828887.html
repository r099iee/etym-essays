A word that whirrs The verb hurl is one of a small class of English words whose histories begin, as far as the record shows, in sound rather than in a reconstructable root. No Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be found for it, and no older attested Germanic form leads to it in a clean line; the OED pronounces its origin uncertain and "possibly imitative." What the word sounds like is what it does: a hurled object tears through the air with a low rush — the whirr and whistle of a heavy body — and the very phonetics of hurl (the opening fricative and the dark back vowel) seem designed to carry that noise. Around it sits a whole expressive family of Germanic hurry-and-rush words: Middle Low German hurreln "to hurl, push violently," Middle Dutch hurlen, and German hurren "to move quickly," the ultimate source of our hurry. The possibility is that the word came into Middle English from the Low Countries or northern Germany with the sailors and traders who brought Dutch and Low German words into the language in the 13th and 14th centuries; but it is equally possible that it grew independently in English as an echoic coinage. Both the borrowing and the coinage are honestly uncertain, and both would make the same point: hurl was born as the sound of something rushing. Rush and fling The earliest Middle English attestations, from around 1225–1300, already show hurlen in its double life. On one side it is intransitive: to rush headlong, to dash violently, to hurtle forward — the sense that survives today in the separate verb hurtle, a near-twin with which hurl has traded influence for centuries. On the other side it is transitive: to fling with force, to cast violently. The two senses are two sides of one image — the hurler's arm and the hurled body both move at speed — and in later Middle English and the 16th century the transitive sense steadily won, until to hurl came to mean the superlative of throwing: the cast of maximum violence and speed. Milton, in Paradise Lost, uses the word with full epic weight, and the English Bible translations give it to God's own actions, hurling the rebel angels from heaven. From the 19th century the word found two new careers: to hurl insults, abuse, or accusations — verbal missiles cast with a thrower's violence — and the name of Ireland's national game, hurling, in which the players hurl the ball (the sliotar) with a stick called a hurley. Both uses are direct extensions of the verb's core force: the first transfers the violence from the arm to the tongue, the second institutionalises the throw as sport. The giants at play Tolkien's use in The Hobbit draws on every layer of the word at once. The giants are "hurling rocks at one another for a game": the transitive violence is present, the mock-sport is present, and the imitative sound is present — the reader hears the whistle of each boulder in the lightning-flashes. The word also keeps, in its double history, the note of heedless speed: the rocks are hurled the way Middle English hurlen rushed, with the mountain's own elemental momentum. Hurl is the right verb because it is the strongest word of violent projection in the language, and because it sounds like what it names; the giants, at play with boulders, could have been described with no other verb, and the sound of the word does half the storytelling — the arc of the rock is audible before it lands. Key points
  • Origin uncertain and probably imitative; no PIE root; a possible Low German or Dutch source (hurreln, hurlen, 13th c.) is a hypothesis, not a certainty.
  • Middle English hurlen (c. 1225) already means both "to rush headlong" and "to fling with force."
  • The transitive sense won out; hurtle is the surviving near-twin of the intransitive sense.
  • "To hurl insults" (verbal missiles) and the Irish game hurling are 19th-century extensions of the same verb.
  • Tolkien exploits the sound, the force, and the sportive irony in the stone-giant episode.
