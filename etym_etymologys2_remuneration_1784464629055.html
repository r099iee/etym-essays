PIE *mei- 'to change, exchange, give in return' (reconstructed; the root of exchange and reciprocity, with cognates in the 'exchange, barter' vocabulary of the family) → Latin mūnus (genitive mūneris) 'service, office, duty; gift, present; public charge' (attested from early Latin, 3rd century BCE, e.g., in Plautus; related to the 'gift/duty' complex of communis 'common' = 'sharing in duties' and im-mūnis 'exempt from duty') → Latin mūnerāre 'to give, present, reward' (a denominative verb from mūnus; attested in post-classical and late Latin, continuing in medieval legal usage) → Latin remūnerārī 'to repay, reward, requite' (re- 'back, again' + mūnerārī 'to give'; attested in classical Latin, 1st century BCE, e.g., in Cicero) → Latin remūnerātiō 'a repaying, a reward, recompense' (noun of action; attested in classical and early imperial Latin) → Middle French rémunération 'payment, reward' (15th century CE) and/or direct learned English borrowing → English remuneration 'payment, recompense, reward for services' (attested from the late 15th century CE; the verb remunerate and adjective remunerative follow in the 16th century) → REMUNERATION — the card's target form: the formal noun for payment or reward. NOTE: the same Latin mūnus 'gift, duty, service' is the base of a large collateral family — munificent 'generous in giving,' municipal 'of a town's official duties,' immunity 'exemption from duty' — and of communis 'common' (from *ko-moini- 'sharing the burden'); none of these is a parent of remuneration, but all illuminate the gift/duty idea at the root. Do not confuse remuneration with enumeration (from Latin enumerāre 'to count out, list'), a different word of different root.
