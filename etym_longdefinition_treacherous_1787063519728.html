Treacherous has two linked senses. First, of a person: guilty of betrayal or the violation of allegiance, faithless, disloyal, traitorous — "a treacherous friend," "a treacherous ally." Second, and increasingly common, of a thing or situation: dangerously unpredictable and liable to betray one's confidence, so that what seems safe is in fact deadly — "a treacherous mountain path," "treacherous icy roads," "the sea is treacherous in winter." The unifying idea is deception of trust: a treacherous person breaks faith; a treacherous place looks trustworthy and is not.

  • Primary sense — faithless, traitorous: a treacherous person, minister, or regime betrays trust, allegiance, or friendship. "Treachery" is the noun naming such betrayal; "treacher" (obsolete) was the person who commits it.
  • Secondary sense — deceptively dangerous: of conditions, terrain, weather, or situations that appear harmless but conceal danger: treacherous currents, treacherous ice, a treacherous descent. Here "treacherous" does not require an agent who intends harm — the danger itself "betrays" the unwary.
  • Register and intensity: stronger and more emotive than "dangerous" or "unreliable," closer to "perfidious" in the personal sense but far more common; frequently heard in travel and weather reporting ("treacherous driving conditions") with no hint that a person is involved.
  • Collocations: treacherous roads/ice/pass/terrain/currents; a treacherous enemy/alliance/friend; treacherous winds; a treacherous smile (a smile that conceals deceit); treacherous weather.
  • Related nouns and verbs: treachery ("betrayal of faith or trust"), to betray; "treacherousness" (rarer). The adjective is not to be confused with "traitorous," though they overlap in sense: "traitor" descends from Latin traditor ("one who hands over"), while "treacherous" descends from Latin tricari ("to play tricks").
  • Older, broader sense: in medieval and Renaissance usage "treacherous" meant "deceitful, full of wiles," merging trickery and betrayal; this is the sense from which both modern branches grow.
  • Figurative breadth: the word now does double duty for moral betrayal and physical hazard; in both, the essence is a promised or assumed safety that proves false — trust betrayed by a person, or by the ground itself.

Semantic bridge: from "given to trickery and deceit" (14th-century English, from Old French trecherous) the word extended in the 17th–18th centuries from treacherous people to treacherous things: once the sea or a road "deceives" the traveller, it earns the same adjective. The person-sense remains, but the hazard-sense has become the everyday one. In the Tolkien sentence, "some are tricky and treacherous," the word keeps both senses in play: the tricky deception of persons and the unreliability that makes trust dangerous.
