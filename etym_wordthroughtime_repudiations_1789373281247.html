- 16th Century (c. 1540s) - Enters Early Modern English directly from Latin *repudiātiō* and French *répudiation*; primarily denotes matrimonial divorce and the formal casting off of a wife or betrothed suitor during the English Reformation.
- 17th Century - Broadens into theological, political, and philosophical treatises (Milton, Hobbes); characterizes the formal rejection of royal authority, papal supremacy, or religious heresy.
- 18th to 19th Century - Codified into common-law contract jurisprudence (anticipatory repudiation) and international public finance, gaining international notoriety through sovereign debt repudiations by American states and revolutionary governments.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Elevates into cultural criticism, psychoanalysis, and modernist aesthetics; characterizes the profound historical breaks whereby 20th-century literature and philosophy repudiate 19th-century realism, bourgeois morality, and continuous temporal narratives.
- Late 20th Century to Present - Retains its high literary and geopolitical gravitas while acquiring a precise technical role in computer cryptography and network security through the concept of "non-repudiation."
