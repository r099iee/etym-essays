Scoured is the past tense and past participle of the verb scour, which carries two main senses in modern English: to clean or polish something thoroughly by hard rubbing, and to search an area or body of material exhaustively, as if sweeping it clean. The participle freezes both actions as completed: a scoured pot is gleaming, a scoured forest has been searched to the last pocket, a scoured database has been gone through record by record. The two senses are joined by a single governing image — friction applied until nothing unwanted remains. In the example card, the rescue crew scoured every square mile of the dense pine forest, refusing to return until every lost trail had been examined: the word insists on exhaustive, fine-tooth coverage.

Polysemy and sense structure:
  • Physical cleaning sense: scoured pots, pans, decks, armour, and surfaces are rubbed with abrasive agents — sand, grit, wire wool, or scouring pads and powders — until tarnish, grease, scale, or rust yields. Unlike mere washing, scouring implies force and deep-set grime; it is the vocabulary of kitchen, forge, and dock, not of polite dusting. Domestic products (scouring pad, scouring powder, scouring cream) keep the sense current.
  • Spatial search sense: to scour a hillside, countryside, forest, or coastline is to pass over it systematically, leaving no hiding place unexamined. From the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the cleaning image was extended to territory: a scouring party 'swept' an area clean of game, rebels, smugglers, or lost persons. The rescue in the example — scouring every square mile of pine forest — is precisely this sense.
  • Investigative and data sense (the modern extension): scoured records, archives, newspapers, ledgers, and the internet. Journalism and police-procedural style favour the word when completeness is the point — millions of records scoured, every file scoured for evidence. The metaphor treats information as terrain to be swept.
  • Technical hydraulic sense: in engineering and earth science, "scour" names the erosive stripping of riverbeds, channels, or bridge foundations by flowing water. This is a specialized nominal and verbal use (scour of the bed, pier scour) that grew out of the same abrasive image and is now a standard term in hydraulics.
  • Figurative uses: scoured of (stripped bare of something — 'scoured of vegetation', 'scoured of funds'), emotional or economic stripping, and the natural image of a wind-scoured or wave-scoured landscape.
Semantic reasoning and bridges: all senses share the image of abrasive thoroughness — a tool, cloth, or force applied until the surface (physical, territorial, or documentary) gives up everything it conceals. The search sense is the cleaning sense transferred from objects to areas: to 'sweep clean' a countryside for enemies or game became, by extension, to search it exhaustively. The hydraulic sense is the original cleaning physics — water and sediment as the abrasive agent — re-specialized in engineering. The participle, in all senses, marks completion: the work of rubbing or ranging has been done.

Register and collocations: scoured ranges from domestic and technical (scoured the pans, scoured the hull) to formal and urgent (scoured the mountainside, scoured the records, scoured the web). Collocations: scoured with sand, scoured of, scoured every inch/corner, scoured clean, scour the internet, scour the countryside, bridge scour, bed scour.

Boundary work among near-synonyms:
  • Scrubbed emphasizes the rubbing motion itself and stays domestic or technical (floors, decks, surgical hands); scoured can be domestic too, but more readily suggests deep-set grime, metal polish, or landscape-scale sweep.
  • Searched is broader and can be partial or casual; scoured insists on exhaustive, abrasive thoroughness — every corner treated as if scrubbed.
  • Combed (as in 'combed the woods') shares systematic thoroughness yet feels more methodical and linear, like the teeth of a comb; scoured adds a harsher, more forceful, more military flavour of sweeping clean.
  • Ransacked overlaps in thorough movement through space but implies disorder, violence, and theft rather than disciplined search or cleansing.
  • Swept suggests broad, rapid movement; scoured implies fine-tooth, effortful coverage.
  • Rubbed / polished are gentler or surface-specific; scoured adds abrasive force and completeness.
  • Eroded is a technical relative for the hydraulic sense, domain-locked to geomorphology and engineering.
Morphology: scoured is the regular weak past tense and past participle (scour + -ed), pronounced /ˈskaʊ.əd/. The noun scour (a scouring action, or the erosion produced by water) shares the same form.
