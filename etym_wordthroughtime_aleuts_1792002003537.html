- 1740s–1750s — Coined by Russian Siberian fur-traders (promyshlenniki) following Vitus Bering's Second Kamchatka Expedition (1741), originally applied as an exonym to the inhabitants of the westernmost Near Islands (Attu and Agattu).
- 1770s–1790s — Adopted into Western European maritime exploration literature and cartography via Captain James Cook's official third voyage journals and imperial Russian administrative charts, establishing the English term Aleutian Islands and Aleutians.
- 19th c. — Institutionalized by the Russian-American Company as an all-encompassing colonial census classification, indiscriminately applied to diverse Pacific Yupik and Unangan maritime sea-otter hunters across Russian America.
- Early to Mid-20th c. — Formally integrated into United States federal administration and American anthropological literature following the 1867 Alaska Purchase; tragically emphasized during World War II when Japanese forces occupied Attu and Kiska, prompting the forced federal evacuation and internment of Aleut villagers.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Codified in federal legislation through the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (1971) and the Aleut Restitution Act (1988); concurrently re-evaluated in modern sociolinguistics as Indigenous scholars actively promote their ancestral autonym Unangax̂ / Unangan.
