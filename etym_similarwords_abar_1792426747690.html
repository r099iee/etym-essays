• Mataṅga: Similar because both are venerated forest ascetics in the Rāmāyaṇa associated with the sacred hermitage near Lake Pampā; differs because Mataṅga was the enlightened male Vedic sage and guru, whereas Śabarī was his humble female tribal disciple who remained behind to receive Rāma.
  • Guha: Similar because both are non-Aryan forest-dwellers and passionate devotees who offer hospitality to Rāma during his exile; differs because Guha was a male tribal monarch and chieftain of the Niṣāda boatmen at Śrṅgaverapura on the Gaṅgā, whereas Śabarī is an ascetic hermit woman living in the deep southern wilderness.
  • Niṣāda: Similar because both represent ancient indigenous forest communities in epic Sanskrit literature; differs because Niṣāda is a specific tribal ethnonym typically associated with fishermen and river boatmen, whereas Śabarī belongs ethnically to the inland forest-hunting Śabara tribe.
  • Anasūyā: Similar because both are highly venerated female ascetics who host and bless the royal exiles in the forest; differs because Anasūyā is of high Brahmanical sage status, the elderly wife of Sage Atri at Citrakūṭa, whereas Śabarī is a humble, solitary tribal woman in the southern Deccan.
  • Arundhatī: Similar because both are revered holy women of classical epic tradition; differs because Arundhatī is the celestial, high-caste wife of the royal preceptor Sage Vasiṣṭha, symbolizing conjugal fidelity, whereas Śabarī is an ascetic renunciant living an austere solitary forest life.
  • Gārgī: Similar because both are famous ancient Indian women celebrated for spiritual stature; differs because Gārgī Vācaknavī was an aristocratic Vedic philosopher engaged in high metaphysical debates in King Janaka's court, whereas Śabarī represents the path of silent, loving emotional devotion (bhakti).
  • Maitreyī: Similar because both are celebrated female seekers in sacred texts; differs because Maitreyī was the scholarly philosophical wife of Sage Yājñavalkya in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad pursuing the knowledge of Brahman, whereas Śabarī achieved liberation through intuitive, loving service.
  • Bhaktā: Similar because both denote a devout female practitioner; differs because Bhaktā is a generic Sanskrit grammatical substantive meaning "a female devotee," lacking the specific epic narrative, tribal heritage, and literary identity of Śabarī.
  • Tāpasī: Similar because both describe a woman undergoing austere penance; differs because Tāpasī is a generic functional noun for any female practitioner of tapas (austerity), whereas Śabarī is the specific proper name of the Rāmāyaṇa saint.
  • Yoginī: Similar because both represent spiritually accomplished women; differs because Yoginī denotes an adept in advanced physical or esoteric tantric yoga or a semi-divine deity, whereas Śabarī embodies simple, sincere, non-esoteric loving devotion.
  • Sora: Similar because of their direct historical and linguistic connection; differs because Sora (or Savara) is the modern anthropological and endonymic term for the Austroasiatic indigenous tribe of Odisha and Andhra Pradesh, rather than the epic literary figure.
  • Śramaṇī: Similar because Vālmīki explicitly calls Śabarī a śramaṇī (female ascetic); differs because śramaṇī is a general religious title denoting a renunciant in non-Brahmanical or forest ascetic orders.
