- [Middle English 'schruggen/shruggen' (14th c.): to shudder, shiver, tremble — a convulsive movement of the body from cold or fear] - [Perception: involuntary, uneasy, animal; the word lives beside 'shudder' and 'shrink']
- [15th–16th centuries: the sense narrows onto the shoulders — to draw up the shoulders in refusal, reluctance, or silent protest] - [Perception: the movement becomes voluntary and communicative; the word begins to mean exactly what the gesture means]
- [17th–19th centuries: the noun 'shrug' appears for the gesture itself; 'to shrug at' = to disdain] - [Perception: the gesture is recognized as a fixed sign of dismissal; writers use it to cut a character's contempt or helplessness in one line]
- [Late 19th century: the garment sense arrives — a short jacket that 'hugs the shoulders' is sold as a shrug] - [Perception: fashion steals the word from the body part to the clothing; 'a shrug of silk' appears in catalogues and knitting columns]
- [20th century: 'shrug off' becomes fully idiomatic — shrugging off criticism, colds, tackles] - [Perception: the word enters the emotional center of the language: unconcern as a virtue of resilience]
- [Today: 'shrug' also names the emoji 🤷 and the public gesture of institutional helplessness — 'a collective shrug'] - [Perception: in an age of shrugged shoulders, the word has become shorthand for a whole mood of resignation]
