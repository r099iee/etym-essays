PILED is the past tense and past participle of the verb pile, meaning "heaped, stacked, or accumulated into a mass or mound". In the source sentence, the feet of the great stone gates "were piled with shingles" — covered and drifted with heaps of rounded pebbles. The word is built on the noun pile "a heap, stack, or mass".

  • Primary sense (physical heaping): to place things one on top of another to form a heap; as a participle, "piled (with)" means loaded, covered, or stacked high: "a table piled with books", "shelves piled high with jars".
  • Passive and descriptive sense: "piled with" means "covered by a mass of", stressing quantity and layering rather than neat arrangement.
  • Intransitive and crowd sense: "to pile into/out of" (of people) means to move into or out of a vehicle or building in a crowd; "piled" can describe a crush or gathering.
  • Accumulation sense: "debts piled up", "problems piled on" — figurative accumulation, mounting pressure.
  • Colloquial intensifier: "piled on" (of criticism or praise) means heaped up thickly, added one after another.

Semantic bridge and reasoning: the verb descends from the noun pile, which entered English from Old French pile "a heap, stack, mass" (and the verb piler "to heap up"), ultimately from Latin pīla "a pillar, pier, or solid mass of masonry". The bridge from "pillar" to "heap" runs through the idea of a solid accumulation: a pillar is stone heaped and compacted, a pier is a mass built up; by extension any heap — of wood, grain, pebbles, or coins — came to be a pile. The participle piled therefore always implies not just presence but mass and height: things are stacked, layered, and pressing on one another.

Collocations:

  • "piled high with" — the most common idiom, suggesting deep, overflowing heaps.
  • "piled with goods/wares" (markets), "piled with snow" (roads), "piled with shingles" (the source text).
  • "piled up" — accumulated: papers, debts, corpses, work.
  • "pile on / piled on" — heaping on criticism, praise, pressure, or weight.

Secondary meanings today:

  • Accumulation of intangibles: work, evidence, or problems "pile up" — the participle extends the physical heap to abstract masses (paperwork, setbacks).
  • Movement of crowds: "we piled into the car" — a mass of people compressing into a space, from the image of objects being heaped in.
  • American football: "pile on" means players leaping onto a fallen ball carrier; "piling on" metaphorically means ganging up, and online it names joining a wave of criticism.
  • Gaming and betting (through the noun): a "pile" of chips or cards — "piled" describes stacked stakes.
  • Architecture (through the homographic noun): a "pile" is a large building ("a stately pile"), so "piled" can describe masonry constructed high.

Register and nuance: piled is common, neutral, and slightly informal. It pictures disorder and abundance rather than neat arrangement (contrast stacked). It can be flattering (abundance: "a table piled with delicacies") or negative (excess: "piled with work"). In the source text, "feet piled with shingles" conveys slow natural accumulation — drift, deposition, and the weight of time.

Grammar: regular verb; the participle piled is used (a) passively ("were piled with"), (b) in perfects ("had piled up"), and (c) adjectivally ("a piled heap", "piled carpets"). It combines with the adverbs "high" or "deep": "piled high with snow".

Contrast with near-synonyms: stacked implies neatness; heaped implies loose mounds; amassed is formal and abstract; accumulated emphasises gradual addition; laden and loaded stress burden. Piled is the everyday word for a messy, deep, layered mound.
