- [Earliest senses (late 17th c.)] - Formed from the older "mottle" (itself from "motley") meaning marked with irregular spots or blotches of different colors; applied first to animal coats, cloth, and plumage — a plain, observational word.
- [Natural-history & literary use (19th c.)] - Widely used in nature writing and novels to describe variegated bark, stone, sunlight, and skin; gains a descriptive, slightly literary register of organic, weathered variety.
- [Medical & everyday extension (20th c.)] - Also applied to skin discolored by cold, age, or rash, and to mottled shadows of light and shade; the tone becomes partly clinical, partly painterly.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still names any surface marked with irregular blotchy patches of color, from sycamore bark to marble to a bruised complexion; it keeps a plain, physical, visual register, suggesting natural wear and unevenness rather than elegance.
