MOUNTINGS is the plural of the verbal noun mounting, meaning 'ascending impulses; upward stirrings of the mind or spirit', and in this card it occurs in the opening lines of Book First of William Wordsworth's The Prelude (1805): 'Trances of thought and mountings of the mind / Come fast upon me: it is shaken off.' The word is chosen with great care. It is not a simple noun like ascent but a verbal noun in -ing, pluralized, so that it denotes repeated, spontaneous acts of rising rather than a single climb or a finished elevation. These are upward stirrings of consciousness — surges of aspiration, imaginative flight, spiritual elation — that well up unbidden and 'come fast upon' the poet, exactly as the burthen of the 'unnatural self' is being shaken off.
  • Primary sense — the act of rising (concrete): a mounting is the action of mounting — climbing up, ascending, going up a mountain or onto a horse ('the mounting of a rider', 'the mounting of the stairs').
  • Wordsworthian sense — rising of the mind (abstract): in The Prelude, 'mountings of the mind' are involuntary surges of inspiration and elevation, pluralized to suggest recurrence; the poet's release from his burden is experienced not as emptiness but as a positive influx of rising.
  • Figurative sense — steady increase: the participle mounting attaches to anything that increases in pressure or degree: 'mounting anger', 'mounting panic', 'mounting costs', 'mounting tension'.
  • Technical / mechanical sense: in engineering and photography, a mounting is a frame, bracket, or support in which something is fixed or set (a camera mounting, an engine mounting); this is the dominant modern dictionary sense.
  • Heraldic and ceremonial sense: mountings can name the platforms, steps, or base on which something (a throne, an altar, a jewel) is raised — a concrete instance of 'that which raises'.
The root is the verb mount, which carries a mountain inside it: to mount is literally to go up a mountain (Latin mons 'mountain' → Vulgar Latin *montāre 'to climb a mountain' → Old French monter 'to go up, ascend'). Because the root still carries the concrete sense of climbing a mountain or riding up onto a horse, the Wordsworthian metaphor keeps a physical ground: the mind literally climbs, mounts its own heights, rides upward on the energies of inspiration. Near-synonyms, carefully graded:
  • Ascent: a deliberate climb or a mathematical slope, neutral and directional, with no emotional charge.
  • Elevation: a state of being raised, often moral or religious (elevation of the soul), but static — it names the condition, not the motion.
  • Upsurge: a sudden, often violent increase (an upsurge of emotion or prices), more explosive and less controlled than a mounting.
  • Impulse: a driving force or urge, emphasizing the pressure behind a motion rather than the motion of rising itself.
In Wordsworth's lines, 'mountings of the mind' is paired antithetically with the 'burthen' and the 'heavy weight': the passage descends into weariness and simultaneously rises through mountings, so that release is felt as elevation rather than emptiness. The tone is ecstatic, involuntary, and sublime — these mountings are visitations, graces of the mind, not acts of will.
