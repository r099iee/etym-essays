• Germanic: Similar because both denote the Germanic family of peoples, languages and cultures and are near-perfect synonyms in linguistics; differs because "Germanic" is the modern neutral scholarly term, while "Teutonic" is archaic, literary and now carries historical or humorous colour.
  • German: Similar because both relate to the German nation; differs because "German" is the specific national adjective for modern Germany, while "Teutonic" is broader (all Germanic peoples) and older.
  • Nordic: Similar because both describe northern Germanic peoples; differs because "Nordic" is specifically Scandinavian (Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Iceland), while "Teutonic" historically covered the whole Germanic world.
  • Norse: Similar because both belong to the ancient northern Germanic world; differs because "Norse" means Old Scandinavian, especially the language and mythology of medieval Norway and Iceland, whereas "Teutonic" is the wider umbrella.
  • Scandinavian: Similar because both describe northern Germanic peoples; differs because "Scandinavian" is a modern geographic-cultural label for the Nordic countries, not a pan-Germanic or tribal term.
  • Gothic: Similar because both name ancient Germanic peoples; differs because "Gothic" refers specifically to the Goths and their East-Germanic language, and also means the medieval architectural style — a different set of associations from "Teutonic."
  • Anglo-Saxon: Similar because both name a Germanic branch and carry old racial and mythic overtones; differs because "Anglo-Saxon" is specifically the Old English people and language, and in modern usage is often applied to English-speaking culture.
  • Saxon: Similar because both are Germanic tribal names used as adjectives; differs because the Saxons are a specific tribe of north Germany and England, while "Teutonic" generalizes the whole family.
  • Frankish: Similar because both are Germanic tribal adjectives; differs because "Frankish" is specific to the Franks (and their Germanic dialect spoken in Gaul), not an umbrella for all Germanic peoples.
  • Alemannic: Similar because both are Germanic tribal and linguistic labels; differs because "Alemannic" is the dialect group of south-west Germany and Switzerland, a narrow technical term.
  • Aryan (obsolete usage): Similar because both were used in 19th-century racialist philology of a supposed ancestral "race"; differs because "Aryan" was the Indo-Iranian (Indo-European) umbrella, later discredited as a racial term, while "Teutonic" was the Germanic subset; both are now avoided in that sense.
  • Teuton (noun): Similar because both derive from the same tribal name; differs because "Teuton" is the noun for an individual ancient Germanic person, while "Teutonic" is the adjective (and can be used as a noun for the language).
  • Germanic peoples (phrase): Similar because both denote the Germanic nations; differs because the phrase is descriptive modern usage while "Teutonic" compresses it into a single archaic adjective with mythic overtones.
