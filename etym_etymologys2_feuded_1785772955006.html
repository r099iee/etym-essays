PIE *peik- "hostile, evil-minded" (reconstructed, debated; 4th millennium BCE; cf. Sanskrit píśuna- "malicious," Greek pikrós "bitter, sharp") → Proto-Germanic *faihaz "hostile, outlawed, foe-like" (1st millennium BCE–CE; cf. Old English fāh "hostile, outlawed") → Proto-Germanic abstract noun *faihithō "enmity, hostile state, feud-condition" (cf. Old English fæhþ(u) "feud, state of vengeance," recorded in Anglo-Saxon law codes; also Frankish *faihida) → Frankish *faihida "hostility, feud" → Old French fede / faide "enmity, hatred, feud" (11th c.) → Middle English fede / feude "feud, hostile state" (late 13th c.) → Modern English feud "a prolonged quarrel or private war" (spelling feud stabilized from the early 16th c.) → verb to feud "to carry on a feud" (early modern) → FEUDED "engaged in a prolonged, bitter, mutual hostility" (past tense and past participle). Note: a native parallel strand — Old English fæhþ(u) "feud, vengeance-liability" continued into Middle English and reinforced the borrowed French-derived form. The modern spelling feud was probably reshaped by the unrelated noun feud "a fief" (Medieval Latin feudum), the ancestor of feudal; the two words share no ancestry. The PIE root is debated: older handbooks also reconstruct *poik-/*peig- or related hostile sets, and no single reconstruction is universally accepted.
