tilled is the past tense and past participle of the verb till — "to cultivate (land); to plough, harrow and otherwise prepare the soil for sowing" — and it also functions as a participial adjective describing ground that has been worked: "tilled fields", "tilled soil". In Tolkien's sentence about Dale — "all the valley had become tilled again and rich" — the word carries its full ancient weight: land broken out of ruin and waste and returned to productive cultivation after desolation.

Primary sense — cultivation of the soil. To till land is to work it mechanically so that seed can take root:

  • Ploughing: turning the topsoil with a plough or ard to aerate it, bury weeds and break the surface crust.
  • Harrowing and levelling: breaking clods, smoothing and firming the seedbed after the plough.
  • General preparation: digging, weeding and manuring in smallholder and garden contexts. "Tilled" therefore implies the whole sequence of soil preparation, not a single stroke.

The word is used transitively ("they tilled the valley"), of the land itself ("the valley had become tilled"), and in the passive ("the ground was tilled in spring"). A "tilled" field is by definition productive land; its antonym set is fallow (resting but unworked), waste (unworked and wild) and barren (incapable of production).

Secondary sense — labour and provision (now archaic or obsolete). The Old English ancestor tilian meant "to strive, aim at, labour for", and Middle English tilen retained the senses "to gain, obtain, procure, provide for". These survive only in fossils and regional use — "till" meaning "to earn or get by effort" is obsolete — but they explain the word's whole character: working the land was regarded as the archetypal form of providing, so the verb narrowed to agriculture and never widened again.

Tertiary sense — figurative cultivation. Through the biblical phrase "to till the ground" (Genesis 2:5; 3:23; 4:12, KJV), the verb acquired a durable figurative resonance: tilling is honest, divinely ordained labour — the human vocation after Eden. Modern figurative uses are uncommon but real: "tilling" an audience, a market or a mind (preparing it to receive something) is a deliberate revival of the agricultural metaphor in business and self-help prose.

Register and usage today.

  • "Tilled" is standard in agriculture and horticulture (agronomy texts, farming journalism: "tilled acreage", "soil erosion on tilled land").
  • In general speech it is becoming literary: "ploughed" or "cultivated" are more common, and "till the soil" survives as an idiom with biblical and pastoral colour.
  • The noun tillage (cultivated land, or the act of cultivating) and the agent noun tiller (one who tills) share the verb's history; the unrelated tiller meaning "the bar of a rudder" is from Old French telier, "weaver's beam".
  • Homonyms to distinguish: till "until" (from Old Norse til, a preposition meaning "to, until"); till "cash drawer" (origin obscure, 15th c.); till "stiff glacial clay" (19th c., uncertain origin). None is related to the verb.

Literary and cultural echoes. Genesis makes tilling the first human occupation and, after the Fall, a labour of sweat: Adam is sent "to till the ground from whence he was taken" (3:23), and Cain "was a tiller of the ground" against his brother Abel the shepherd (4:2). The Parable of the Sower presupposes tilled ground, and the whole biblical cycle — ploughing, sowing, reaping — gives "tilled" a resonance of redemption and restoration. That is exactly the force of Tolkien's sentence: the valley "had become tilled again and rich" after the desolation of the dragon's reign — cultivation as the sign of peace, plenty and new life.
