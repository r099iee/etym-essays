The Indo-European verb "to go". The chain begins with the PIE root *h₁ey- "to go", one of the best-attested verbs of the family: Sanskrit éti "he goes", Greek eîmi "I shall go", Lithuanian eĩti "to go". Latin kept it as īre "to go" — a short, suppletive verb whose forms survive in modern English only inside compounds and loans like "exit" (Latin exīre, "to go out") and "initial" (from initium, "a going in").

The fatal compound: perīre. Joined with the preposition per "through" (PIE *per(i) "through, forward, beyond"; cf. Greek perí "around"), the verb became perīre — literally "to go through, to go to the end", hence "to pass away, be destroyed, die". The metaphor is the great Indo-European euphemism for death as a journey to its end. Plautus, the comic poet of the 2nd century BCE, makes it an everyday exclamation of comic despair — perii!, "I'm done for!" — and Roman epitaphs use the verb of the dead. The past participle stem perit- also fed the adjective peritus "experienced" (literally "having gone through" something), a separate branch that reaches English in "expert".

Through French into English: perir → perish. In Gallo-Romance the verb passed into an inchoative formation (Latin perīscere, "to begin to perish"), which is why Old French shows the stem periss- in its present forms: il pert, nous périssons. English, borrowing the verb in the 13th century, took the French stem wholesale: Middle English perishen, modern perish. The -ish ending of the English verb is therefore not native English morphology but the fossilized French inchoative suffix. Old French also had the adjective perissable "liable to perish" (12th century), and English formed its own perishable on the same material in the 17th century, alongside the native formation perishing.

The negation: imperishable is born. To the adjective perishable English added the Latin negative prefix in- (from PIE *ne, the universal Indo-European negative, also in Greek a-/an- and Germanic un-): in- + perishable = "not liable to perish". The word is first recorded around 1814 — a telling date, because it is exactly the moment of High Romanticism, when poets and philosophers were insisting that art, fame, love, and the soul outlast death. "Imperishable" was born idealistic and stayed that way: its classic collocations are imperishable fame, imperishable glory, imperishable songs. In theological prose it competes with the older biblical "incorruptible" (Latin incorruptibilis, used of the resurrection body in 1 Corinthians 15 in the King James Version of 1611), and with "immortal" for the soul; "imperishable" adds the flavour of material endurance — the thing that neither dies nor decays.

Semantic and phonological career.

  • 19th century: the word is a staple of Romantic and Victorian high diction — imperishable fame and glory for heroes, artists, and the achievements of nations; elevated, solemn, public.
  • 20th century: the practical register appears — "imperishable goods", "imperishable foods" in retail, logistics, and emergency management; the sublime word descends into the pantry while keeping its poetic life.
  • Pronunciation note: the prefix spells in- but is pronounced im- before the labial p — a spelling-pronunciation gap typical of learned Latin formations, and one reason the prefix is easily confused with im- "in, into".
  • 21st century: rarer than "everlasting" or "forever" in speech; deliberately grand, poetic, or solemn when used — as in Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), where the Master promises the dragon-slayer "many imperishable songs".

Summary of the drift. Imperishable fuses two Latin journeys — per "through" and īre "go" — into the image of passing away, negates that image with in-, and then rides the Romantic faith that some things — songs, fame, love, the soul — will not pass away. Its history is a history of the word "perish" itself: from the Latin euphemism for death as a journey to its end, through Old French and Middle English, to a 19th-century adjective for everything that outlives the journey.
