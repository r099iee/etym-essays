- [15th century: the slanting adjective] - slope enters English describing a surface that runs obliquely rather than level — the idea of slipping (the word shadows the older sense of gliding away) applied to a static slant of ground, timber, or roof; it is plain, descriptive, and morally neutral.
- [16th–17th century: the verb in motion] - “to slope” means to move obliquely or to lie at an angle, and in military drill “slope arms” fixes a musket slantwise on the shoulder; the word is at home with soldiers, carpenters, and masons, still a working verb of direction and inclination.
- [18th century: the noun solidifies] - the noun “a slope” becomes the standard term for any graduated decline or rise of ground, the language of the surveyor and the geographer; it sheds motion and names a fixed shape of terrain, graded land between the ridge and the plain.
- [19th–20th century: abstraction and formula] - slope enters mathematics as the measure of a line's gradient and hardens into fixed phrases — “slippery slope,” “ski slope”; the word grows technical and precise, prized for neutrality where earlier it had named something visibly in motion.
- [Contemporary sense] - slopes is the workmanlike, neutral term of the geographer, surveyor, and skier for gradated inclines of ground — neither a cliff's drop nor a plain's flatness — while the verbal ghost of movement (“the ground sloped away”) survives chiefly in literary use.
