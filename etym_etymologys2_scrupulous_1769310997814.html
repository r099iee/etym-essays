Single linear derivation chain for scrupulous: 1. PIE *(s)ker- / extended *(s)kerp- — "to cut, scrape, carve" (c. 4000–3000 BCE). A root of cutting and scratching; the extended shape *(s)kerp- is the traditional source given for the cutting action. DEBATE: the deeper connection of Latin scrupus to this PIE root is traditional (Pokorny, OED, Etymonline) but the exact base and ablaut are not settled; de Vaan treats the ultimate link with caution, and the chain below should be read as the accepted traditional route. 2. Latin scrupus, scrupī m. — "a sharp stone, a rough stone" (attested 1st c. BCE, in Plautus). From the cutting root: a stone with sharp edges. 3. Latin scrupulus, scrupulī m. — diminutive, "a small sharp stone, a pebble; hence a nagging doubt, a scruple; also the smallest unit of weight, 1/24 of an ounce" (attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE, Plautus, Cicero). The pebble-in-the-sandal image produces the "doubt" sense, and the minute size produces the weight sense. 4. Latin scrupulōsus — "full of little stones; hence anxious, careful, precise" (attested 1st c. CE). The adjective built on the diminutive: meticulous and uneasy. 5. Old French scrupuleux — "full of scruples, careful" (12th–13th c.). The Latin adjective passes into Gallo-Romance. 6. Middle English scrupulous (also scripulous) — "full of scruples; careful, exact" (attested mid-15th c., c. 1440) → Modern English scrupulous "careful, exact, morally conscientious" (present).
