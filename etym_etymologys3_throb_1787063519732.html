The word throb appears in English in the late 14th century in the form throbben, and it is remarkable for the shortness of its recorded history: unlike most English vocabulary, it cannot be traced to Old English, Old French, or Latin. The standard judgement of the dictionaries — the OED, the major etymological dictionaries, and the historical grammars — is that it is imitative, or echoic: the sequence of sounds th-r-o-b, with its voiced plosive and dull, resonant vowel, reproduces the heavy, muffled beat of a pulse. In linguistic terms it belongs to the small class of onomatopoeic verbs whose form is directly motivated by their meaning, alongside words like puff, thump, and thud.

The earliest recorded uses, from the late 1300s and 1400s, describe the heaving of the heart and of the body under strong emotion or exertion. In Middle English poetry and devotional prose the heart “throbbeth” in fear, in love, and in physical agony. It is a strongly bodily word from the very start: the pulse is felt in the chest, the temples, and the limbs, and the verb carries none of the elegance of the Latin-derived synonym palpitate, which enters English from French and Latin in the early 17th century.

The noun develops out of the verb around 1570, naming a single beat: “a throb of the pulse” or, in the poetry of the period, the beating of the heart in love. From the 17th to the 19th century the word spreads through two very different channels. On one side, medicine adopts throbbing as a cardinal sign of inflammation and pain: a throbbing toothache, a throbbing wound, a throbbing headache are all standard clinical descriptions from the 18th century onward. On the other, love poetry and romance make the throbbing heart its most famous image, a usage that culminates in the 20th-century noun heartthrob (first attested 1927) for a film star or singer who makes hearts beat faster.

Industrialisation brings the word to machines. By the mid-19th century steam engines, pumps, and looms “throb”, and the railway and the factory give the word its mechanical sense: a steady, felt vibration in a regular rhythm. In the 20th century the word moves into music — jazz, then rock, then electronic dance music — where “throbbing bass” describes a low-frequency pulse physically felt in the chest. In the same period the figurative use flourishes: cities, crowds, streets, and whole cultures “throb with life”, transferring the heartbeat of the individual to the pulse of a collective.

Today the word is notable for its stability and its breadth of register. Six hundred years after its first attestation, its central meaning — a steady, dull, felt pulse — has not shifted at all, even as it has acquired medical, mechanical, musical, and amorous uses that all radiate from that core. The plural and participial forms (throbs, throbbing) follow the regular English morphology, and the word remains one of the handful of common English verbs whose etymology is, as far as can be determined, purely onomatopoeic.
