Turkic origin — no PIE ancestry. The word descends from a steppe-nomad term for "royal camp, army, seat of the khan." - Old Turkic ordu "royal encampment, palace, court, seat of the khan; army" (attested in the Old Turkic (Orkhon) inscriptions, 8th c. CE) → West Turkic / Kipchak-Tatar *urda / orda "camp, encampment, armed host" (medieval steppe usage) → Polish horda "a nomadic band, a horde" (attested 16th c.; the initial h- is a characteristic Polish addition) → Middle French horde "a nomadic band, horde" (attested 16th c.) → English horde "a tribe of Asiatic nomads living in tents" (attested 1550s) → English hordes (plural) "vast, threatening, disorderly masses" (the transferred pejorative sense is attested from 1610s) → **HORDES** (Modern English). Notes and side-branches: - The route into English is sometimes given as Polish horda → German Horde → French horde → English, and sometimes as Polish horda → French horde directly; both routes agree on the key steps: Turkic origin, the Polish form with initial h-, the 16th-century French intermediary, and the English attestation in the 1550s. - The Golden Horde: the Mongol-Tatar khanate is the "Golden Camp" (Altan Ordo in Mongolian), and the Russian form orda (attested from the 14th century) was applied to the Mongol camp and host; the historical name "Golden Horde" is thus a sibling of English horde, not a stage in its chain. - The same Old Turkic ordu lives on in Turkish ordu "army, camp" and, through Persian and Hindustani, in Urdu (zabān-i-urdū, "the language of the camp," the language-name attested from 1796) — a famous side-branch of the same root.
