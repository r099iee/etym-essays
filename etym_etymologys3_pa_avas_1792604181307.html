1. Linguistic Substrate and the Acoustic World of Pre-Vedic South Asia. The etymological lineage of paṇava reflects one of the most distinctive sociolinguistic processes in the development of Old Indo-Aryan: the absorption of indigenous material culture and musical terminology into Sanskrit. In comparative Indo-European studies, the earliest stratum of the Ṛgveda contains very few terms for complex membranophones beyond the primordial earth-drum (bhūmi-dundubhi) and the bovine-hide war drum (dundubhi). As Indo-Aryan speech communities settled across the Gangetic plains during the late Vedic period, they interacted extensively with indigenous populations speaking Austroasiatic (Munda) and Dravidian languages. These indigenous societies possessed sophisticated traditions of percussion, utilizing hourglass drums, pottery resonators, and tension-laced tabors crafted from local hardwoods.

Historical phonologists, most notably Manfred Mayrhofer in his landmark Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Altindoarischen (EWAia), emphasize that paṇava lacks any verifiable Indo-European morphological pedigree. It exhibits no cognates in Old Iranian (Avestan or Old Persian), Greek, Latin, or Germanic. While indigenous grammarians attempted to link it folk-etymologically to the verbal root paṇ- ("to bargain, praise, or stake"), modern scholars recognize this as an ex-post-facto rationalization. The word belongs to a well-known cluster of musical substrate loanwords (such as mṛdaṅga, karkaṭikā, and dardura) characterized by onomatopoeic phonesthemes depicting percussive resonance—specifically the voiceless labial stop (*p*) and retroflex nasal (*ṇ*), which mimic the sharp, snapping slap of hand upon leather.

2. Textual Emergence from the Late Vedic Stratum to the Great Epics. The term paṇava enters the written record during the late Vedic and Sūtra eras (c. 1000–500 BCE), appearing in liturgical manuals and auxiliary ritual texts where sacrificial feasts began incorporating elaborate instrumental ensembles. By the time of the composition of the great Sanskrit epics—the Rāmāyaṇa of Vālmīki and the Mahābhārata of Vyāsa—the word had become fully naturalized into the elite vocabulary of Indo-Aryan poetry:

  • In the Rāmāyaṇa (particularly in the Bālakāṇḍa describing the festive capital of Ayodhyā, and the Sundarakāṇḍa describing the nocturnal palaces of Laṅkā), the paṇava appears in recurrent formulaic couplings with the dundubhi, the mṛdaṅga, and the vīṇā. In the sleeping quarters of Rāvaṇa’s palace, Hanumān observes exhausted courtly maiden musicians slumped over their instruments, with one beautiful woman embracing her paṇava like a lover, demonstrating that the instrument was compact, personal, and intimately integrated into courtly feminine life.
  • In the Mahābhārata, the soundscape shifts dramatically to martial ferocity. During the colossal clash of armies at Kurukṣetra, the poet repeatedly combines śaṅkha-dundubhi-nirghoṣa (the clamor of conches and kettledrums) with paṇavānaka-samkula (the tumultuous clatter of paṇavas and ānakas). Here, the paṇava functions as a high-frequency tactical signaling device whose rhythmic patterns directed chariot wings and foot soldiers amidst the blinding dust of war.

3. Classical Dramaturgical Codification in the Nāṭyaśāstra. The definitive theoretical and organological zenith of the paṇava occurred during the classical era with the compilation of Bharata Muni's Nāṭyaśāstra (c. 2nd century BCE to 3rd century CE). Chapter 33 of this foundational treatise details the physics, tuning, and aesthetics of percussion instruments, establishing the famous tripuṣkara system:

  • The orchestra was anchored by three primary drums: the horizontal mṛdaṅga, the upright dardura, and the braced paṇava.
  • Bharata explicitly describes the anatomical structure of the paṇava: it had a hollow wooden shell sixteen fingers (aṅgulas) in length, with drumhead apertures measuring eight fingers across, waisted at the center like a miniature hourglass. It was fitted with internal bracing hoops and tightly laced with twisted leather straps (kakṣyā) that could be gripped or compressed by the musician’s forearm to modulate pitch during performance.
  • The treatise classifies its rhythmic strokes (pāṇi) into distinct technical cadences: samapāṇi (even rhythm), avapāṇi (descending rhythm), and uparyavapāṇi (ascending cross-rhythms). The paṇava was revered as the dynamic driver of tempo, capable of matching the rapid footwork of dramatic dancers portraying furious demons, flying spirits, or charging heroes.

4. Medieval Lexicography and the Semantic Metamorphosis. As the classical period gave way to the early medieval era (c. 6th–12th centuries CE), the physical instrument underwent regional diversification. In the Amarakośa, the great Sanskrit thesaurus of Amarasimha, paṇava is cataloged under the section on music and theatrical performers (Nāṭyavarga), grouped alongside other vādya types. However, as regional languages (Apabhraṃśa and early New Indo-Aryan vernaculars) flourished, older classical Sanskrit instruments began yielding to newer vernacular designs. Instruments like the mardala, pakhawaj, and dholak gradually assumed the primary role in royal courts and devotional temples.

In this transitional medieval period, commentators on Sanskrit lexicons and musicological treatises (such as Śārṅgadeva's Saṅgītaratnākara) noted that the term paṇava was sometimes applied by extension to small hand cymbals (kāṃsyatāla) or wooden clapping instruments used to beat time in regional folk performances. This semantic drift highlights an acoustic transition: when the original hourglass tabor became archaic in everyday folk practice, the word’s semantic core shifted from the specific wooden membranophone to the functional role of timekeeping itself.

5. Modern Indological Recovery and the Anglicized Plural Paṇavas. In the 19th and 20th centuries, Western Indologists and philologists—beginning with William Jones, Henry Thomas Colebrooke, and later Sylvain Lévi—embarked on the systematic translation of the great Sanskrit epics and classical dramas. Confronted with passages cataloging ancient royal orchestras, translators chose either to leave the exotic musical names untranslated in romanized script or to anglicize them with English plural morphology. In the Critical Edition of the Rāmāyaṇa (translated by Robert P. Goldman and his team at UC Berkeley), terms like mṛdaṅgas, dundubhis, and paṇavas are preserved with diacritics and the English plural suffix -s. This lexical hybridization allows modern readers to encounter the authentic organological texture of ancient India, preserving the memory of an acoustic world where the clattering strike of the paṇava heralded the dawn of sovereign empires.
