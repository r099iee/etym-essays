To gnash is to grind or strike the teeth together violently and noisily, in a convulsive paroxysm of rage, anguish, or bitter frustration — an instinctual, quasi-animal reflex of extreme emotion, heard before it is understood. In the battle panorama of The Hobbit, Tolkien sets the word at the exact moment the wolves “yammered and gnashed their teeth” while the goblins “yelled and stamped with rage, and flung their heavy spears in the air in vain”: gnashed supplies the harsh, scraping percussion of the pack, fangs ground edge to edge in a spasm that no longer aims at flesh. The verb degrades the wolves into creatures of pure nerve and reflex, beyond language and reason, and the very phonetics of the word — the guttural gn- onset, the snapped-open vowel, the hissing -sh release — mimic the grinding they name. Gnashing is what jaws do when they cannot reach their prey: fury with nowhere to go. The past tense gnashed seals the sound as one discrete, terrible event that flashes, is heard, and is done — a single crunch of enamel in the dark of the forest clearing.

1. The core sense and its object.

The verb is used both transitively and absolutely. Transitively, one gnashes one's teeth — the teeth are the object, and they are ground or struck together edge to edge, not bitten down on anything. Absolutely, the verb alone can name the whole act: the wolf gnashes, the furious man gnashes. Unlike bite or chomp, which presume something between the teeth — food, flesh, prey — gnash operates on empty teeth, grinding nothing but itself; the action is entirely self-contained, a violence that has no target except the teeth that make the sound. That is the word's deep logic: gnashing is frustration made audible, aggression turned inward on the body's own grinding engine because there is no one and nothing to bite. The standard collocation is to gnash one's teeth, so fixed that the object is almost always present, and the idiom is completed by the biblical phrase 'weeping and gnashing of teeth'.

2. The near-synonym field.

Among its near relations the word's exact territory becomes clear. Grind is the broad, mechanical term for crushing one substance against another and carries no emotion at all: grain is ground, blades are ground. To grit one's teeth is a deliberate, controlled clamping against pain — endurance rather than spasm, the body bracing rather than exploding. Clench is a tight, silent jaw-locking that makes no sound whatsoever. Gnaw works on a surface and shreds it slowly over time. Crunch emphasizes the breaking of something brittle between the teeth. Snarl adds the curled lip and bared fang of an overt threat — a snarl is a display aimed outward, whereas gnashing grinds nothing but the teeth themselves. Chatter (of teeth) is the rapid, uncontrollable clacking of cold or fear, lighter and faster than gnashing. Gnash alone denotes the audible, violent, empty-mouthed grinding of extreme emotion — the sound of rage that cannot reach its object, of grief without outlet, of the damned in their impotence.

3. Register, idiom, and modern use.

  • The fixed idiom: 'to gnash one's teeth' — to show furious, bitter, helpless resentment; from the New Testament's 'weeping and gnashing of teeth' (Matthew 8:12, King James Version, 1611), the standard image of damnation and frustrated rage.
  • Literary and melodramatic register: gnash is a favourite of Romantic and Gothic prose and of adventure narrative — villains, the doomed, the trapped, and the enraged gnash their teeth; the word hardened into a theatrical marker of impotent fury.
  • Animal use: of wolves, bears, and other beasts, gnash names the reflex grinding of fangs before or instead of an attack — the sound of a predator that cannot reach its prey.
  • Extension by analogy (rare, figurative): 'gnashing' can name any grating, grinding sound or destructive frustration — waves gnash at a cliff, engines gnash their gears — always preserving the image of hard surfaces ground together.
  • Related family: the noun gnash (a grinding of the teeth) and the adjective gnashing are in use, but the past tense and past participle gnashed (this card's form) is the commonest, fixing the act as a single finished event.

4. Why the word fits the passage.

Tolkien sets gnashed in a sentence full of noise — yammering, yelling, stamping, the futile flinging of spears — and the word contributes the percussion of the pack. The wolves gnash because they cannot reach their prey among the trees; the goblins yell and stamp because their spears fall short; the whole scene is frustration in the dark, and gnashed is its most animal sound. The word degrades the wolves to pure reflex, and its phonetics — the guttural gn-, the harsh vowel, the hissing -sh — grind the sound out of the mouth as the teeth grind in the dark. It is the fury of the cornered and the thwarted, given its single most physical voice.
