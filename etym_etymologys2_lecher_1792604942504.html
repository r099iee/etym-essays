Note on historical debate: Historical lexicographers and Romance philologists (including the Oxford English Dictionary and Walther von Wartburg's *Französisches Etymologisches Wörterbuch* [FEW]) establish that Old French *lechier* is an undeniable Germanic borrowing adopted from Frankish **likkōn* during the Merovingian/Carolingian superstrate era. The primary semantic debate among historical linguists has centered on the vector of transmission between oral gluttony and carnal lust. While a minority of 19th-century antiquarians attempted to link the word to Latin *laecator* or Celtic roots, modern historical semantics confirms that medieval Christian theology viewed *gula* (gluttony) and *luxuria* (lust) as twin manifestations of the same bodily sin of incontinence. In medieval tavern culture, one who licked his fingers in culinary greed (*lecheor*) was viewed as the exact same person who indulged in illicit sexual debauchery, causing the noun to rapidly specialize into sexual carnality.
