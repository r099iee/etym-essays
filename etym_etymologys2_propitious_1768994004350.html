Single root word: propitious. Stage 1 — PIE *pet- "to fly, to rush, to fall": reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root (Pokorny *pet-, *pete-), attested across the family in words of flight and motion. Stage 2 — Latin petere "to seek, to aim at, to attack, to rush at": from *pet-, attested from early Latin (Plautus, Ennius, 3rd–2nd century BCE); the verb's core meaning is vigorous directed motion or pursuit. Stage 3 — Latin propitius "favorable, gracious, well-disposed": derived (traditionally) from pro- ("forward") + the root of petere, hence "rushing forward" — an omen or deity that hurries out to meet the worshiper; attested from early Latin (Plautus, Lucretius, Vergil). An alternative modern derivation links propitius instead to prope ("near"), a power that draws close; the etymology is debated (see note below). Stage 4 — Old French propicieux "favorable, propitious": inherited Romance adjective (from Latin propitius with regular Romance development), attested from the 12th century. Stage 5 — Middle English propicious "favorable, propitious": borrowed from Old French, attested from the 15th century (c. 1400–1450); the spelling later adjusted to propitious on the Latin model. Stage 6 — English propitious "favorably disposed; boding well; promising success": modern English, in continuous use from the 16th century, together with propitiate, propitiation, propitiatory, propitiousness, propitiously. Note on debate/nuance: the internal derivation of Latin propitius is genuinely disputed. The traditional analysis pro- + petere ("rushing forward") is favored by most handbooks and is attractive because it matches the augural imagery of a power flying toward the suppliant; the alternative, from prope ("near"), rests on the semantics of a god drawing close. Both derive the word from the same PIE-era stock and neither changes the attested meaning ("favorable, gracious"), but the precise base remains open. All stages from propitius onward are secure.
