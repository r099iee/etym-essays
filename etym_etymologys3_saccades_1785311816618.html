SACCADES is a rarity in the technical vocabulary of neurology: instead of being carved from classical Greek or Latin roots in the nineteenth century, it is a loanword whose long history begins in the marketplaces of the ancient Near East and whose scientific career began, astonishingly, in the riding stable. Its biography is the story of a word that travelled from a coarse cloth bag, through a violent shaking verb, into the sharp tug of a horseman’s reins, and finally into the laboratory of vision science — a journey of pure metaphor across nearly three thousand years.

The Semitic origin. The true source is a Semitic word for a coarse woven bag: Hebrew saq and Phoenician saq, ‘sackcloth’ or ‘sack,’ the rough material of the ancient mourning garment and the everyday container for grain and goods. Greek borrowed the word as σάκκος (sákkos), ‘sack, bag of coarse cloth,’ and Latin took it as saccus, from which English eventually derives sack. There is no Proto-Indo-European root behind the word at all; its ancestry is Semitic, and its route into Europe runs through Greek and Latin exactly as the routes of wine, linen, and other trade goods of the ancient Mediterranean ran. From the noun saccus, colloquial or late Latin coined a denominal verb, saccāre, ‘to shake, to jostle, to toss, to thrust violently into a sack’ — a rough, workaday verb of the market and the farm, with no elite literary pedigree. This is the classic pattern by which the name of a concrete object gives birth to a vigorous action verb: because a sack is something you stuff things into and thump about, saccāre acquired its violent, shaking sense.

Through Old French and into the stable. The verb passed into Old French as sachier or saquier, ‘to pull, to draw out, to snatch,’ the ordinary word for dragging or yanking something violently. From this vigorous verbal stock, Middle French produced the noun saccade: a sudden jerk, a sharp pull, a violent twitch — used most characteristically of the decisive tug a rider gives the reins to check, turn, or spur a horse. In the equestrian manuals of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the saccade was a precise item of horsemanship, a firm yank that imposed the rider’s will on the animal. The word was, in short, a name for sudden, irresistible pulling — exactly the sense that would later find its way into the eye.

The leap to ophthalmology. In 1893 the French ophthalmologist Émile Javal, investigating the mechanics of reading, discovered that the human eye does not glide smoothly across a line of text but moves in a rapid series of jumps and stops. He deliberately borrowed the equestrian term, calling these abrupt relocations saccades, because their jerky, ballistic character precisely matched the violent pull of a rider on the reins. The choice was a stroke of exact metaphor: like a tug on the reins, a saccade is launched in a single decisive command and cannot be corrected in mid-flight; it is an all-or-nothing movement with a measurable speed and direction. English physiology and psychology took the word over within the same decade, and by the twentieth century saccade had become a standard term of vision science, eye-tracking research, and reading research.

Sound changes and the word’s shape. Within English the word underwent no native sound change; it was borrowed in its French form with the French stress pattern (sac-CADE, with the final syllable prominent). The adjective saccadic (in saccadic suppression, saccadic eye movements) was formed inside scientific English on the model of the noun, and the diminutive compound micro-saccades — from Greek micro-, ‘small’ — was coined in the twentieth century for the tiny fixational saccades that occur even when the eye seems perfectly still. The modern scientific family (saccade, saccadic, micro-saccades, anti-saccade) is thus a twentieth-century English construction, although the oldest member of the family, the sack-word, is attested in Semitic three millennia ago.

The science of the modern sense. The modern meaning is precise and mechanistic. A saccade lasts a few tens of milliseconds and reaches peak velocities of several hundred degrees per second; during it, saccadic suppression blanks out vision so that the world does not smear across the retina; and its purpose is to bring a new target onto the fovea, the high-acuity centre of the retina. Between saccades the eye holds fixation, and during attempted fixation micro-saccades continue to fire, refreshing the retinal image and preventing neural adaptation from fading the scene. Reading, visual search, and scene perception are all choreographies of saccades and fixations, and abnormal saccade metrics are now clinical markers in neurology. The equestrian jerk has become the grammar of sight itself — and the card’s sentence, listing “micro-saccades” among the visual-processing functions to which 30-50% of the cerebral cortex is devoted, uses the word exactly in its modern scientific sense: a precise, small, ballistic refixation movement, one of the fundamental building blocks of active vision.
