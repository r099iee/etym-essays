- c. 3400 BCE — drawn pictures: Sumerian accountants scratch pictographs of jars, sheep, and grain on clay with a pointed tool.
- c. 2600 BCE — pressed wedges: the stylus turns edge-on, pictures collapse into nail-heads and strokes, and true cuneiform is born.
- 2nd–1st millennium BCE — imperial script: Akkadian, Hittite, and Elamite chanceries write everything from omens to treaties in wedges across the Near East.
- 1st c. CE onward — silence: the last dated tablet fades, and for seventeen centuries the wedges are mute marks on bricks and seals, admired but unread.
- 1700s — travellers name it: Hyde and Kaempfer print the wedge inscriptions of Persepolis and call them cuneiform, wedge-shaped, fixing the learned word.
- 1802–1857 — decipherment drama: Grotefend guesses kings names, Rawlinson climbs the Behistun rock, and the wedges speak again amid Victorian triumph.
- Today — oldest writing in the gallery: museum star, schoolbook first, anatomist adjective for foot bones, and the source sentence reminder that literacy is larger than the alphabet.
