Etymology of English maxim: a learned borrowing ultimately from the Latin superlative of 'great'.

1. PIE *méǵh₂- 'great, large' [reconstructed] — the root of 'great' across the Indo-European family.
2. Latin magnus 'great, large, important' [attested from the earliest Latin records] — continues *méǵh₂-.
3. Latin maximus 'greatest' [attested] — the irregular superlative of magnus, source of the feminine maxima.
4. Latin maxima (sc. sententia / propositio) 'the greatest proposition; a leading premise, a fundamental principle' [Scholastic / Medieval Latin usage, esp. 12th–13th c. logic] — the feminine singular used as a noun.
5. Old French / Middle French maxime 'basic principle, axiomatic saying' [attested, 13th c.].
6. Middle English / Early Modern English maxim 'a pithy general truth or rule of conduct' [attested, 15th c.].
7. Modern English maxim [this form].

COGNATES AND SIBLING LINES
  • The same PIE root *méǵh₂- yields Greek megas (mega-), Sanskrit mahā- 'great', and the Germanic words behind English much and archaic mickle.
  • The same Latin superlative gave English maximum 'greatest amount', a parallel sibling that specialized toward the quantitative extreme while maxim took the sentential / moral path.
  • Related Latin comparatives and superlatives of magnus survive in major and magnitude.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS
  • The exact route from Latin maxima to the English noun passed through Scholastic usage (maxima propositio in logic) and then through French maxime; some lexicographers emphasize the French mediation, others the direct learned Latin loan — both routes converge on the same 'greatest proposition' sense.
