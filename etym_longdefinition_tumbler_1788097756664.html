TUMBLER is a richly polysemous English noun whose several senses all radiate from the verb to tumble — to fall headlong, roll over and over, or perform acrobatic somersaults. In the source context (“in a ___ by his side”) it denotes the drinking vessel: a plain, straight-sided glass with a flat bottom and no stem, foot, or handle, typically holding about 250–300 ml (roughly eight to ten ounces) and used for water, soft drinks, or spirits. In Indian English the word specifically designates the small, cylindrical stainless-steel or glass tumbler of everyday household use, in which water is served in homes, restaurants, and temples.

Primary senses in modern English

  • Drinking glass: a flat-bottomed, stemless, handleless glass — the default water and spirit vessel of bars and homes — whose sense arose in the 1660s, when glasses were given rounded or pointed bases so that they could not be set down without spilling and the drinker had to drain the glass at one draught; empty, the vessel “tumbled” over. Modern whiskey tumblers, highballs, and rocks glasses have flat bases, the name surviving purely by tradition.
  • Acrobat: an entertainer who performs somersaults, handsprings, and ground-tumbling feats — the original sense (14th century), formed as an agent noun from tumble + -er; medieval tumblers were court jesters, fairground performers, and festival entertainers, immortalised in the folk-tale of the Tumbler of Our Lady, who offers God his only skill — his tumbling.
  • Lock part: in a lock or gunlock, a pivoting lever or pin that must be lifted (tumbled) by the key, or released by the trigger, before the bolt or hammer can move; the modern pin-tumbler cylinder lock, perfected by Linus Yale in the 1860s, still bears the name.
  • Pigeon: a short-beaked domestic pigeon, the tumbler pigeon (17th century), bred for its habit of turning backward somersaults in flight.
  • Rotating machine part: the revolving drum of a clothes dryer (tumble dryer), a rock or stone tumbler that polishes gemstones over weeks, a glass- or metal-polishing barrel, a photographic processing tank that agitates film, and a foundry test barrel used to measure the toughness of castings.

Secondary and technical senses

  • Tumbler switch: a small electrical switch (from the 1870s) whose lever flips — tumbles — over.
  • Tumbler lock: any lock whose mechanism uses levers or pins that must be lifted to exact heights; the term is used in locksmithing, safe-making, and gunnery (the tumbler of a flintlock releases the hammer).
  • Tumbler (printing): a type-form that is turned or “tumbled” for imposition in old letterpress practice.
  • Tumbler test: the standard ASTM and foundry test in which cast iron or coke is tumbled in a revolving drum to measure resistance to breaking.

The semantic bridge: every sense is reached through a single core idea — falling, rolling, or flipping over. The acrobat rolls (the agent-noun sense); the drinking glass cannot stand and tumbles when empty (behaviour → vessel); the lock lever falls back into place (function → part); the pigeon somersaults (behaviour → breed); the dryer drum rolls its contents (motion → machine). The modern insulated travel “tumbler” — a lidded, spill-proof takeaway cup, a global commodity word since the 2010s — completes the drift: the word is now a generic category of portable drinking cups, and the acrobatic origin is entirely forgotten by ordinary speakers.

Register and usage: the glass sense is neutral and everyday; the acrobat sense is literary, historical, or circus-register; the lock, firearm, and engineering senses are professional jargon; the breed sense belongs to pigeon fanciers. Familiar compounds: tumbler dryer, tumbler lock, tumbler pigeon, rocks glass (American), highball glass.
