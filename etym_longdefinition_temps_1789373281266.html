Temps is a masculine noun of French origin designating time, duration, historical epochs, meteorological weather, grammatical tense, or a distinct phase, beat, or stage within a sequenced physical movement, musical composition, or athletic maneuver. Borrowed into English both as a technical term in specialized domains (fencing, classical ballet, musicology, and literature) and appearing ubiquitously in French cultural, civic, and philosophical discourse, the word bridges the vast cosmic continuum of temporal existence with the precise, rhythmic subdivision of human action.

In the specific urban and civic context of the source sentence—traversez en deux temps—the word operates in its structural and sequential sense: "cross in two stages" or "cross in two movements." On French boulevards and complex intersections, pedestrian traffic signals instruct walkers that crossing the wide thoroughfare cannot be accomplished in a single continuous stride; rather, it is divided into two distinct temporal and spatial segments separated by a central safety island (refuge). This civic instruction brilliantly mirrors the broader metaphysical duality of the word: time is both an uninterrupted existential flow that humans must navigate and an artificially segmented sequence of discrete intervals, steps, and beats designed to make movement possible.

The term displays extraordinary polysemy and conceptual richness across numerous domains:

  • Sequential Action, Phased Movement, and Civic Navigation (Source Sense): In physical culture, military drill, athletics, and traffic management, un temps denotes a distinct phase, beat, or intermediate stage in a multi-step operation. French pedestrian crossings command traversez en deux temps, requiring pedestrians to cross the first carriageway, pause at the island, and await the next green signal before crossing the second. In military manual-of-arms commands, an action executed en deux temps, trois mouvements signifies a brisk, strictly segmented, and disciplined drill carried out in precise successive steps.
  • Cosmic Time, Duration, and Philosophical Temporality: In general philosophy, metaphysics, and daily life, le temps is the universal continuum of temporal progression—time itself. It encompasses clock time (*quelle heure est-il? / quel temps avons-nous?*), subjective psychological duration (*le temps qui passe*), and epochal historical eras (*les temps modernes*, *de temps en temps*). In French literature, it forms the supreme metaphysical axis of Marcel Proust's À la recherche du temps perdu, where lost time (le temps perdu) dissolves human identity, while time regained (le temps retrouvé) redeems it through art.
  • Meteorology and Atmospheric Conditions (Weather): Uniquely among major European languages alongside other Romance tongues (such as Spanish tiempo and Italian tempo), le temps serves as the primary everyday word for the weather, atmospheric state, and climate. Francophones ask Quel temps fait-il? ("What is the weather like?") and describe un temps pluvieux (rainy weather) or le beau temps (fine weather). This dual meaning developed because Latin tempus encompassed the seasonal fluctuations of the year.
  • Musical Rhythm, Metric Beats, and Measure: In musicology and performance, a temps is the fundamental unit of metric pulsation—a beat—within a musical bar or measure. A piece in 4/4 meter is a mesure à quatre temps; the accented downbeat is the temps fort, while the unaccented upbeat is the temps faible. The term also designates musical tempo and timing in general.
  • Fencing Terminology (Loanword into English): In the classical martial art of fencing, temps d'escrime (often referred to in English simply as a temps or fencing time) denotes the precise duration required to execute a single, simple offensive or defensive action (such as a lunge, parry, or riposte). Mastering le temps is the mark of an elite swordsman, who launches an attack during an opponent's hesitation or mid-motion (attaque sur le temps or coup de temps).
  • Classical Ballet and Dance Choreography: In classical dance, temps enters standard global ballet terminology to describe specific steps, jumps, and connecting movements executed to a single metric count. Famous examples include temps levé (a single-leg hop or spring while retaining a pose), temps lié (a fluid, connected transference of weight through open positions), and temps de flèche (an arrow-like scissor jump).
  • Linguistics and Grammatical Tense: In structural and historical linguistics, temps denotes the morphological category of verb tense (*les temps du verbe*), expressing the temporal relation between the moment of speech and the event described (e.g., *temps présent*, *temps passé*, *temps futur*).
  • Modern English Workplace Homograph / Clipped Plural: In modern Anglophone business culture, the plural noun temps serves as a familiar colloquialism and clipped form of *temporary workers* or *temporary employees*—individuals hired on short-term contracts through employment agencies. While etymologically converging on the same Latin root *tempus*, this English commercial usage has taken on its own vibrant life in modern labor economics.

The unifying semantic thread running through all these manifestations—from atmospheric weather and pedestrian crossing phases to fencing thrusts, ballet leaps, and Proustian duration—is the ancient concept of the measurable, changing portion of reality. Whether marking the season in the sky, the rhythmic beat of a dancer's foot, or the safety pause on a busy boulevard, temps designates the rhythm through which human beings experience existence.
