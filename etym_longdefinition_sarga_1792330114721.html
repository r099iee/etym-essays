sarga (Sanskrit: सर्ग, masculine noun, literally "letting loose, release, stream, emission, creative surging") denotes a primary structural canto, narrative division, or chapter in classical Sanskrit epic poetry and ornate court epics (mahākāvya). Rooted in the verbal concept of emission and creation, the term operates simultaneously in the realms of classical literary aesthetics and Hindu cosmology. In literary theory, an ornate court epic is formally classified as a sargabandha ("a poem bound together in cantos"), establishing sarga as the definitive structural unit of classical Indian narrative architecture.

Structural Rules of the Literary Canto in Sanskrit Poetics (Alaṅkāraśāstra):

  • Metrical Homogeneity and Closure: In classical poetics, as codified by master theoreticians like Daṇḍin in his Kāvyādarśa and Bhāmaha in his Kāvyālaṅkāra, a genuine sarga must adhere to strict compositional protocols. The majority of the canto must be composed in a single uniform meter (most frequently the 32-syllable śloka, or lyrical meters like upajāti or vaṃśastha). Crucially, the final one or two verses of the sarga must shift into a longer, more elaborate, and musically complex meter to signal to the listener that the canto is drawing to a close, while previewing the narrative theme of the upcoming canto.
  • Narrative Scale: A classical mahākāvya typically consists of between eight and thirty sargas. Each sarga encapsulates an episode of substantial aesthetic richness—such as the description of a mountain, the dawn, a royal council, a battle, or a season—integrated into the central hero's quest.

Polysemic Scope, Secondary Senses & Metaphysical Bridges:

  • Cosmological Emission ("Primary Creation"): In the philosophical systems of Sāṃkhya, Vedānta, and the Purāṇas, sarga is the foundational technical term for the primary creative evolution or emission of the universe. In Sāṃkhya metaphysics, it designates the outward evolution of the manifest world (the 23 cosmic evolutes) from unmanifest primordial nature (prakṛti). In the Purāṇas, it forms the first of the canonical five defining marks (pañcalakṣaṇa) of a Purāṇa: sarga (primary cosmic creation), distinguished from pratisarga (secondary re-creation after periodic dissolution).
  • The Semantic Bridge from Creation to Poetry: The semantic transition from "cosmic creation / emission" to "poetic canto" is deeply intuitive within Indian aesthetics. Just as the Supreme Being or cosmic nature emits a world of name and form (nāmarūpa) in an outward burst of creative play (līlā), so too does the master poet (kavi)—viewed as a godlike creator—emit a complete world of aesthetic flavor (rasa) and verbal imagery within each individual canto. A sarga is literally an "outpouring" or "discharge" of poetic genius.
  • Vedic Sacrificial Outpouring: In the Ṛgveda, before developing literary and cosmic senses, sarga referred to physical rushes and streams: the pouring forth or filtration of sacred Soma juice into sacrificial vats, the shooting of a volley of arrows from a bowstring, or the uncontrolled release of warhorses galloping forward at the start of a race.
  • Psychological Dispositions and Impulses: In the Bhagavad Gītā and classical ethical treatises, sarga occasionally refers to one's natural moral orientation or inherent disposition—as in the distinction between the divine nature (daivī sarga) and the demonic nature (āsurī sarga).
