twanged (verb, past tense and past participle of twang) — to have produced a sudden, sharp, ringing, vibrating sound, most characteristically the sound of a taut string that has been pulled aside and released. The word is the acoustic past of the bowstring, the guitar string, the sitar string, and the banjo string: it names a single percussive event, the instant in which stored tension is discharged as audible vibration. In the treasure-chamber passage from C. S. Lewis’s The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, Susan “gave one little pluck to the string. It twanged: a chirruping twang that vibrated through the whole room.” The clause is almost redundant on purpose — verb and noun double the noise, and the doubling is the point: a twang is by nature brief, and to say it twice is to make it ring twice. The sound is quick, ringing, and slightly metallic, and it is remarkably clean; it is the sound of a wire speaking, and it carries a sense of recovered energy, because only a string that is still taut and alive can twang at all. It is also, crucially, a key to memory: “that one small noise brought back the old days to the children’s minds more than anything that had happened yet,” because sounds are among the most primitive triggers of recollection, and a twang is small and sharp enough to cut straight through to a forgotten past.

The word’s own sound is its definition. The onset /tw-/ clusters the stop /t/ with the lip-pursed glide /w/, forcing the tongue into a small, tense snap; the short vowel /æ/ is bright and open; the coda /ŋ/ is a nasal resonance that hangs and fades. Sound-symbolists call the family “imitative” or “echoic”: the whole word is a compressed onomatopoeia of attack, pitch, and decay. That is why the verb lasts exactly as long as the noise it names — one syllable, one twang — and why the passage can say the string “twanged: a chirruping twang,” with the noun echoing the verb like the after-vibration of the wire itself. The nasal ring of /ŋ/ is what separates a twang from a mere click or tap, and it is also what allowed the word to migrate from strings to voices.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Primary verb sense: to make or emit a sharp, ringing, vibrating sound, especially from a plucked string — “the band twanged against his finger,” “the banjo twanged as he tuned it.”
  • Noun sense (the sound itself): “a twang,” a short, sharp resonance with a metallic or nasal timbre; the passage’s “a chirruping twang” makes it birdlike and bright, at once quick and joyful.
  • Adjective sense: “twangy” (from the 19th century) — having a twanging quality, applied to instruments and to voices; a “twangy” guitar is bright, treble-forward, and cutting.
  • Voice and accent sense: “a nasal twang” or “a Southern twang” — a sharp, drawn, nasal quality of speech; the metaphorical transfer runs from string to voice, because nasal speech vibrates in the taut membranes of nose and throat like a slackened wire.
  • Figurative and emotional sense: “to twang” can mean to sting or affect sharply — “his conscience twanged,” “it twanged at the heartstrings” — the image of a string plucked inside the chest; this is the oldest metaphoric extension of the verb family.
  • Music-production jargon: “twang” names the bright, treble-heavy timbre of country, rockabilly, and surf guitar (the classic “Telecaster twang”); producers speak of a guitar “with plenty of twang,” meaning a sharp, cutting, mid-forward tone that cuts through a mix.
  • Speech-pathology jargon: “nasal twang” (hypernasality) is a clinical term for a voice quality that is too bright and nasal, echoing the acoustic source of the word.
The historical bridges between the senses are all traceable. Noun to verb was nearly simultaneous in the sixteenth century: the sound-word spawned the sound-making verb by direct conversion (denominalization). Verb to adjective (“twangy”) was a routine English adjectival formation of the nineteenth century. The accent sense is the one true metaphor: a nasal voice “twangs” like a string, and because certain rural or regional dialects were heard as nasal, the word picked up a faintly rustic, comic, or provincial flavour — yet it remains descriptive rather than insulting in modern use. The emotional sense (“twanged at the heartstrings”) transfers the physical pluck to an inward string, making memory, grief, or conscience itself vibrate.

Twanged occupies a precise place on the acoustic spectrum. Pluck names the action (fingers releasing a string), whereas twanged names the resulting sound; a string is plucked, and therefore it twangs. Ping is thinner, higher, and more metallic — the sound of a thrown coin or a sonar echo — and lacks the woody, stringy depth of a twang. Thrum is a low, continuous vibratory hum, sustained rather than sudden. Ring is far broader — bells, glasses, and rails all ring — and lacks the specific string-tension quality. Buzz adds roughness and rattling fuzz, where a twang is clean and pitched. Jangle is discordant and clashing (a jangle of keys); clang is heavier and louder (a struck metal sheet or a great bell). Tang is the near twin, a metallic ring with a sharp flavour by extension. Only twang combines the sharp percussive attack, the nasal after-ring, and the sense of a taut string discharging its stored energy in a single syllable — and “twanged,” as the past form, locks that one event into the past like a photograph of a sound.
