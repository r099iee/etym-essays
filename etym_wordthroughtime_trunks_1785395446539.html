- Earliest recorded English sense: the main woody stem of a tree — the great central axis between roots and crown. Connotation: solid, vertical, structural; the thing a tree stands on.
- 15th–16th c.: transferred from trees to bodies — the human 'trunk' is the torso, and by the 16th c. the elephant's proboscis is called its 'trunk'. Connotation: both usages lean on the idea of a main projecting body; the word gains mobility and animal life.
- 16th c. onward: a 'trunk' also becomes a large storage box or travelling chest (originally a chest made from a hollowed tree-trunk), and later the luggage compartment of a car (US). Connotation: sturdy, capacious, built to hold things.
- 19th c. onward: the plural 'trunks' names swimming or sports shorts. Connotation: light, practical, athletic — a long way down the ladder from the mighty tree.
- Modern register: the tree-stem sense stays primary in British English, while the plural keeps its torso-and-shorts associations; 'trunk' now juggles tree, body, box, and car in one compact word.
