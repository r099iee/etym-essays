The history of wayfarers is two histories braided into one word: the history of way, the road itself, and the history of fare, the going upon it. Each half walked thousands of years alone before Middle English tied them together in the 1400s — and the compound has been striding ever since.

The road half — way: the stem reaches back to PIE *weǵʰ-, to go or carry, whose daughters pave half of Europe: Latin vehō (I carry, whence vehicle), Sanskrit vahati (he carries), Old Norse vegr, Gothic wigs, Old English weġ (path, road, course). Proto-Germanic *wegaz already meant both the track and the travelling — road and journey in one — and Old English kept that doubleness: Christ is the weġ (the Way) as well as the guide along it. Middle English wey widened further to manner and respect (no way, this way, by way of), but the road never left it; every English way still smells faintly of dust.

The going half — fare: PIE *per- (to go forth, cross over, lead through) is one of the great motion roots, parent of Latin portus (harbour, a place of crossing), Greek poros (passage, ford), and Germanic *faraną (to go, travel, sail). Old English faran covered all purposeful going — men fare to battle, ships fare over seas, souls fare to heaven — and its agent noun færere named the goer. The fare that now names taxi prices and airline tickets is the same word flattened by commerce: once it meant the journey, then the price of the journey, then only the price. But in wayfarer the old glory survives intact: one who fares, goes, journeys — no ticket required.

  • The marriage (15th c.): Middle English weifarere joins road to goer at exactly the moment England is fullest of both — pilgrims to Canterbury and Walsingham, pedlars between fairs, labourers between harvests; the compound gives charity a noun (alms for wayfarers) and suspicion a label (strange wayfarers about).
  • Reformation roads (16th–17th c.): religious exiles, ejected ministers, and Quaker missionaries become unwilling wayfarers, and the word absorbs suffering borne with purpose; Bunyan's pilgrim is a wayfarer theologised, and every Dissenting memoir adds another pair of boots to the type.
  • Romantic reclamation (late 18th–19th c.): Wordsworth's leech-gatherers and discharged soldiers, Stevenson's donkey journey, and the rise of Alpinism turn foot-travel from poverty into philosophy; the Wayfarer's Chapel, the wayfarer's dole at English cathedrals, and a hundred inn signs keep the medieval kindness commercially alive.
  • The motor age borrowing (20th c.): Ray-Ban's 1952 Wayfarer sunglasses transfer the romance of the road to the dashboard, proving the compound had become pure atmosphere — freedom, horizon, movement — available for licensing; meanwhile youth hostels and long-distance footpaths (the Pennine Way, 1965) return the word to actual feet.

Tolkien's use: when strange wayfarers begin appearing in the Shire, Tolkien strikes the compound's oldest chord — settled folk watching the road bring the world to their hedgerows. Dwarves going east, Rangers watching, and darker things in traveller's clothes: all fare along ways the hobbits never walk, and the plural tells readers, before any exposition does, that history is on the move. No other noun would do the work: travellers would ride, strangers would merely be unknown — only wayfarers are unknown and walking, which is what makes them fate.

Modern afterlife: today the word thrives wherever feet still rule — pilgrimage routes to Santiago, the Appalachian Trail's wayfarer shelters, fantasy novels seating cloaked wayfarers in inn corners, and games granting the Wayfarer class its staff and cloak. Cars carry drivers, planes carry passengers, but the way still carries wayfarers, exactly as the compound promised six centuries ago.
