- disengage: 16th century – 'engage' enters English through Old French (from gage, 'pledge, security'), meaning to bind by a pledge — contracts, betrothals, battle-lines; its negation 'disengage' is coined to mean the un-binding, the release from a pledge or obligation.
- 17th century – 'disengage' settles into military use — to break off contact with an enemy — and into general use for freeing oneself from an entanglement or duty; the word keeps its aura of withdrawal and retreat.
- 18th–19th centuries – mechanics claims it: to disconnect one part of a machine from another, to throw a gear out of engagement; the literal sense widens while the pledge-imagery recedes into the background.
- 20th–21st centuries – the dominant sense is the neutral act of releasing or detaching (military disengagement, a clutch disengaging), yet the ghost of the broken pledge survives in elevated and reflexive use — as when Peake's Rottcodd 'would disengage himself of his jacket', the ceremony of unbinding the social self.

- overall: 14th–16th centuries – the compound of 'over' + 'all' (Old English ofer eall) serves as adverb and adjective meaning 'over the whole extent, taken as a whole' — an eye passing over a page, a total sum.
- 17th–18th centuries – the adjectival sense consolidates in measurements and judgments: 'overall length', 'overall impression'; and in 1759 the noun 'overalls' appears — heavy protective trousers with bib and braces, the working man's garment.
- 20th century – the British singular 'an overall' names the one-piece protective working suit that covers the whole body; the noun stays humble, utilitarian, protective, while the adverb is abstracted into blanket summary ('overall, the day was quiet') — the wholeness of a garment grown into the wholeness of a judgment.

- descended: 13th–14th centuries – 'descend' enters English through Old French from Latin descendere (de- 'down' + scandere 'to climb'), literally 'to climb down'; it is used literally of moving from a higher to a lower position, and of lineage — 'descended from'.
- 16th–17th centuries – the word extends into graduated and metaphorical movement: a descending scale in music, darkness descending, property descending to an heir; the verb carries a stately, orderly gravity.
- 19th–21st centuries – 'descended' keeps its ceremonial, step-by-step register, set against the suddenness of 'fell' and the quickness of 'dropped'; Peake has Rottcodd's grey overall 'descended shapelessly to his ankles', a verb of staircases and dynasties dignifiedly applied to a tube of cloth.
