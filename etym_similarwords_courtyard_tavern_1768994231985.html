• Court: Similar because both name an enclosed open space attached to a building; differs because "court" is the older, formal, institutional word of palaces, law, and great houses, while "courtyard" is the functional, unceremonious form of modest domestic and civic buildings.
  • Yard: Similar because both name an outdoor space adjoining a building; differs because a yard is workaday, open, and often unenclosed — a place of storage, stock, or labour — whereas a courtyard is bounded by walls and reads as an architectural "outdoor room."
  • Quadrangle: Similar because both denote an enclosed, geometrically bounded open space; differs because a quadrangle is the institutional, shaped variant of a college or cloister, while a courtyard may be irregular and belongs to any building that draws a line against an outside.
  • Atrium: Similar because both are central open spaces within a building; differs because an atrium is typically roofed or glazed — a covered, sky-lit hall — while a courtyard is definitively open to the sky.
  • Patio: Similar because both are open outdoor spaces attached to a dwelling; differs because a patio is a small, paved, private terrace beside a single house, while a courtyard is larger and enclosed by walls or buildings on all sides.
  • Cloister: Similar because both are enclosed open courts; differs because a cloister is specifically the covered arcade-walk surrounding a monastic or collegiate court, carrying a religious and scholarly register.
  • Forecourt: Similar because both are open spaces attached to a building; differs because a forecourt lies at the entrance, open to the street and public, while a courtyard is recessed, hidden, and private.
  • Inn: Similar because both are houses of drink and hospitality; differs because an inn adds lodging and a respectable traveller's welcome — the exact inverse of the tavern's public arena of combat.
  • Pub: Similar because both are places where drink is sold; differs because "pub" is the modern, cosy British word stressing community and domesticity, while "tavern" is older, literary, and bleaker — a stage for fighting rather than a lounge.
  • Bar: Similar because both are establishments serving alcohol; differs because a bar is neutral, commercial, and counter-centred, while a tavern is a full public room with tables, atmosphere, and historical weight.
  • Alehouse: Similar because both are drinking establishments; differs because an alehouse was the humbler, beer-selling counterpart of the wine-selling tavern, lower in rank and often rougher.
  • Saloon: Similar because both are rowdy public drinking-houses; differs because a saloon is the American frontier variant — the swing-door, sawdust room of the Old West — while a tavern is the older European institution.
