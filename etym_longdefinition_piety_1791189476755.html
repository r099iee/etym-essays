Piety is a noun meaning devout reverence for God and faithful fulfilment of religious duties, and more broadly dutiful respect and loyalty toward parents, ancestors, traditions, or principles held sacred.

In its primary religious sense, piety denotes inward devotion expressed in outward observance: prayer, worship, obedience to divine commands, humility before the sacred. A person of piety is godly, devout, faithful — not merely believing doctrines but living reverently. Churches praise piety as virtue; saints are models of piety; pietism as a movement stressed heartfelt devotion over cold orthodoxy.

  • Devout godliness (core sense): Reverent love and obedience toward God — e.g., a life of piety, natural piety, quiet piety. Implies sincerity, humility, and habitual worship rather than single ecstatic moments.
  • Outward observance, sincere or pretended (ambivalent sense): Profession of religion and its rites — e.g., under cover of piety, cloak of piety, show of piety. Here the same acts may be honoured as holiness or denounced as hypocrisy, sanctimony, bigotry. Your Montaigne context uses this critical sense: torture justified by professed zeal.
  • Filial and ancestral duty (classical and Confucian sense): Loyal devotion to parents, family, forebears, and fatherland — e.g., filial piety, Roman pietas. In East Asian ethics filial piety (xiao) is the cardinal virtue of caring for and obeying parents and honouring ancestors. In Western classicism it recalls Aeneas carrying his father.
  • Reverence for principles or traditions (extended secular sense): Scrupulous loyalty to a cause, text, or heritage treated as sacred — e.g., piety toward the Constitution, literary piety, ecological piety. Implies dutiful conservatism rather than critical distance.

The doublet pity explains much polysemy. Both come from Latin pietas, but English split labour: pity took compassion for suffering, piety took devotion to the divine. Older texts blur them: to have piety on someone once meant to have pity. False piety is therefore doubly offensive — it claims devotion while withholding compassion.

  • Connotation: Positive when it means sincere devotion and dutiful care; sharply negative when modified by false, hypocritical, ostentatious, or when used ironically (his much-vaunted piety). Secular discourse often hears piety as old-fashioned, prudish, or performative.
  • Domain usages: In theology, piety is a gift of the Holy Spirit and fruit of faith; in Confucian ethics, filial piety is social foundation; in classics, pietas is civic virtue; in common speech, pious hopes and pious platitudes mean well-meaning but ineffectual wishes.
  • Collocations: deep piety, quiet piety, filial piety, natural piety, false piety, cloak of piety, air of piety, reputation for piety.

In short, piety moves from Roman duty to Christian devotion to universal reverence — and always carries the warning that professed reverence can cloak its opposite.
