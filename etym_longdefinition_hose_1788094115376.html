Hose in the passage from Tolkien's The Hobbit is used in its oldest English sense: a covering for the leg, here specifically the flexible mail leggings that protected a warrior's legs below the hauberk — “hose of a fine and flexible metal mesh”. In medieval armour terminology, hose of mail (later called chausses in the technical literature) were the standard defence for the lower limbs, worn over padded leg defences and laced to the belt or the lower edge of the mail shirt. The word in this register is deliberately archaic and chivalric, evoking the armoury of knights and dragons alike.

In modern English, however, hose overwhelmingly denotes something quite different: a long, flexible tube for conveying liquids or gases. The garden hose, the fire hose, the air hose of a garage, the radiator hose of a car and the hydraulic hoses of heavy machinery are all familiar objects named with this single word. A hose is typically a cylindrical conduit made of rubber, PVC, canvas, braided nylon or other reinforced material, often fitted with couplings, nozzles or spray attachments, through which water, fuel, steam or compressed air is driven under pressure. This sense, first recorded in English around 1807, has become so dominant that many speakers are unaware the word ever meant a garment.

The full semantic range of hose today includes:

  • Leg coverings (historical) — from Old English through the early modern period, “hose” named the garments that covered the legs and feet, worn by men, women and children; in the 14th–16th centuries European fashion produced trunk hose, cannon hose, nether hose and the precursors of tights, and the word gradually yielded ground to “stockings” and “tights” for knitted legwear, though “hose” survives in the collective trade term hosiery and in the modern garment “pantyhose”.
  • Armour (historical/literary) — mail hose, plate hose and quilted hose protected the legs of knights from the 11th to the 16th century; this is the sense used in the source passage and in most historical fiction about the Middle Ages.
  • Flexible conduit (dominant modern sense) — the garden, fire, air, fuel and hydraulic hose; also the verb to hose (to spray or wash with a hose, 19th century) and to hose down (to clean by drenching).
  • Slang (US, 20th century) — to hose someone means to cheat, swindle or defeat them harshly; hosed means ruined, broken or in serious trouble. The image is of being drenched and helpless, flattened by a jet one cannot resist.
  • Computing jargon (late 20th century) — a hosed system, file or configuration is one that is corrupted, unbootable or unrecoverable, an extension of the slang sense of “ruined” into technical register.
  • Technical relics — in plumbing, “hose” remains the generic word for flexible pressure tubing; in botany, “hose-in-hose” describes a double flower whose corolla appears nested inside another, likened to a leg inside a stocking.

The semantic bridges. All the garment and conduit senses flow from one image: a long, hollow, flexible cylinder that encloses something. The leg covering was a tube of cloth, leather or mail wrapped around the limb; by extension any tubular covering, and then any flexible tube, could be called a hose, and in the age of fire brigades and piped water the word attached itself to the tube that carries water. The 20th-century slang sense is a separate metaphorical leap: to be “hosed” is to be soaked, overwhelmed or washed out, hence cheated, defeated or wrecked. The word thus travels from medieval legwear to garden watering, and from watering to ruin, while never quite losing the shape of a tube.
