The history of muslin is a history of trade, cities, and empires — a fabric name that crossed the world from a Mesopotamian city to every wardrobe and kitchen, shedding its exotic origin until it became the plainest of textile labels. The city at the beginning. The word begins not with a root but with a place: Mosul, the city of northern Mesopotamia on the west bank of the Tigris, opposite the ruins of ancient Nineveh. The city's Arabic name, al-Mawṣil, is derived from the verb wasala, "to join, to connect, to be continuous," and mawṣil is the noun of place, "the junction, the linking point." Mosul is a junction twice over — geographically, where trade routes and the crossings of the Tigris come together, and historically, as a meeting point of Arab, Kurdish, Turkic, and Christian communities. The name is therefore a piece of Arabic word-formation embedded in a fabric label: the cloth is "the thing of Mosul," mawṣilī, in exactly the way champagne is the thing of Champagne or damask is the thing of Damascus. The pattern is ancient — naming a product after its place of origin — and muslin is one of its best-known European outcomes. The medieval trade. By the medieval period Mosul was celebrated for the manufacture of fine cotton textiles, and the Arabic attributive mawṣilī, "of Mosul," designated a high-quality cotton cloth. European merchants, above all the Italians whose city-states dominated Mediterranean trade in the 14th and 15th centuries, imported the fabric and the name: Italian mussolina, attested from the 15th century, is the direct transcription of the city name (Mussolo in medieval Italian) with the cloth suffix. From Italian the word passed into French as mousseline — attested around 1600–1610 — and from French into English as muslin, attested from the 17th century (c. 1670). The phonetic journey is modest: the Arabic emphatic ṣ became a plain s, the Semitic internal structure of mawṣilī was flattened into a Romance noun, and the French ending -ine became the English -in, which the pronunciation /mˈʌslɪn/ preserves while the spelling keeps the single s and the silent final e residue. Sound and form in English. English adopted the word in the 17th century at the height of the East India trade, when fine Indian cottons were flooding into Europe. The form settled quickly: muslin, with the short central vowel of "muss" or "hush," the medial cluster -sl- preserved intact, and the light final syllable -in. The spelling occasionally wavered (musseline, musselin) before the standard muslin won out. In French the word developed a double life — mousseline for the cotton and mousseuse de soie for a silk version — but English kept a single word for the cotton cloth, using compounds (book muslin, wire muslin) rather than new bases for special varieties. The colonial and fashion century. The 18th and 19th centuries made muslin one of the most consequential words in the textile vocabulary. Bengal and above all Dacca produced the legendary fine muslins — so fine that contemporary accounts claimed a length of it could be passed through a ring — and the East India Company's trade in muslin was a pillar of British imperial commerce. In Europe, white muslin became the signature dress fabric of the late 18th and early 19th centuries: the diaphanous, high-waisted "muslin gowns" of the Regency era were worn by women across the continent, and muslin's sheer quality made it the vehicle of a deliberately light, classical, almost immaterial style of dress. The word also entered the colonial interior: muslin curtains, muslin bed hangings, and — as in the Red Chamber — muslin summer-beds, where the fabric's coolness and translucency solved the problem of sleeping through a hot season. The Chinese context in the card is historically apt: by the 19th century muslin was a global household word, a marker of refined, practical domesticity in China as in Europe. Semantic drift to the plain label. As machine-spinning and power-loom weaving industrialized cotton production in the 19th century, the exotic aura of the word faded. Muslin became simply "light cotton cloth in a plain weave," a technical term of dressmaking and household use: the dressmaker's toile muslin, the kitchen straining cloth, the baby's swaddle. The trade-origin sense survives only as history — the Dacca muslin of museums and colonial memoirs — while the modern core meaning is the fabric itself, light, breathable, cheap, and endlessly useful. The word's journey is thus the inverse of words like damask or cashmere: instead of retaining its place-name glamour, muslin democratized and became ordinary, its city origin almost completely forgotten in everyday use. Conclusion. From a Semitic verb for joining, to a junction-city on the Tigris, to a medieval textile trade, to an Italian import, a French fashion, an imperial commodity, and finally a plain household weave — muslin has travelled further than almost any fabric name, carrying its Mesopotamian birthplace with it all the way to the green summer-bed in which Grandmother Jia proposes to put Miss Lin.
