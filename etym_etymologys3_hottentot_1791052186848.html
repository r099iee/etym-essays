Hottentot has no PIE root; it is a 17th-century Dutch imitative coinage that became a colonial exonym for the Khoikhoi pastoral peoples of southwestern Africa, now regarded as dated and offensive. Its history is therefore short, well-documented, and inseparable from European contact at the Cape.

Dutch East India Company ships established a refreshment station at the Cape of Good Hope in 1652 under Jan van Riebeeck. Dutch settlers encountered Khoikhoi-speaking herders whose languages contain distinctive click consonants (|, !, ǂ, ǁ) alien to Dutch ears, and whose ritual dances involved rhythmic chanting. Settlers reportedly described the speech as stammering, stuttering, hottentotteren in Dutch (to stammer, gibber). The noun Hottentot is traditionally explained as an imitation of that supposed stammer — hot- and tot- syllables mimicking clicks and repeated dance refrains like hot-entot, hottentot. Early travelogues (Olfert Dapper 1668, William Dampier, Peter Kolb 1719) repeat this: the name was given because their language sounds like stuttering and clucking to Europeans.

  • First attestations: Dutch Hottentot / Hottentotten (plural) 1650s-1660s in Company journals; English borrowing by 1670s (John Ogilby's Africa, 1670; Thomas Herbert). Initially it simply meant Cape native, herdsman, without yet the full modern slur-weight, though already condescending and outsider-imposed.
  • Extension: 18th-century naturalists (Linnaeus, Buffon, Blumenbach) adopted Hottentot as a racial type in nascent anthropology: Hottentot Venus (Sarah Baartman, exhibited in London and Paris 1810-1815), Hottentot apron (anatomical myth), Hottentot click. The word thus moved from ethnonym to specimen label.
  • Generic insult: By the 19th century in English, a hottentot could mean any crude, savage, gibbering person, or an ill-mannered child: Don't eat like a Hottentot. Dictionaries record this transferred sense. Place-names, plant-names (Hottentot fig), and medical slang followed.
  • Phonetics: Pronunciation /ˈhɒtəntɒt/ preserves Dutch spelling with English stress on first syllable. No sound shift; borrowing is direct with spelling intact.

Socio-cultural context explains both rise and fall. At the Cape, Dutch herder-farmers (Boers) progressively dispossessed Khoikhoi of pasture, reducing many to labourers. The exonym encoded incomprehension: their speech is mere stammer, not language. Nineteenth-century evolutionism ranked Hottentots as lowest human type, as in the source sentence where every Hottentot is a gentleman of leisure — meaning supposedly too idle to work except for immediate reward. That early-20th-century popular economics text (the phrasing suggests amusement, not hatred, but still stereotypes) treats the word as neutral description; today it reads as slur.

Reclamation and replacement: Khoikhoi themselves never used Hottentot; they call themselves Khoikhoi (men of men, real people) or by group names Nama, Korana, Griqua. From the mid-20th century, especially after apartheid, Hottentot was recognised in South Africa and Namibia as derogatory, comparable to Kaffir. Modern style guides (OED, South African usage) label it offensive, archaic, avoid. Scholarly and official use now says Khoikhoi or Khoekhoe, San for hunter-gatherers, Khoe-San collectively. The old term survives only in historical quotations, taxonomic relics (Hottentot fig now often sour fig), and discussions of racism itself.

Debate note: The imitative origin, though traditional and plausible, is not provable from a single utterance; some scholars note Dutch hutteren/tutteren (to stammer) as source with expressive reduplication, rather than direct mimicry of Khoikhoi. Alternative derivation from German hotte (quiver?) is rejected. No link to hot + tent. The click-mimicry story may itself be folk rationalisation by colonists, but it is the standard etymology in OED, Etymonline and Dutch etymological dictionaries, with the caveat that it reflects European perception, not Khoikhoi language.
