HAVE TO DO (verbal idiom, normally "have to do with") — to be concerned with, related to, or connected with someone or something; to have business, dealings, or association with; to be relevant to or involved in. The idiom expresses a relationship of connection, scope, or involvement, and its exact shade is fixed by the preposition: "have to do with [a person]" means "to have dealings or association with," while "have to do with [a matter]" means "to be related or relevant to."

1. Core senses and usage

  • Dealings or association with a person: "he has nothing to do with those people" — social, professional, or moral association, usually denied in the negative.
  • Relevance or relation to a matter: "the new regulations have to do with safety protocols" — the idiom reports that one thing concerns, concerns itself with, or is about another.
  • Involvement or implication: "she had a great deal to do with the success of the project" — causal or contributory connection.
  • Business or affairs to transact: the older, more literal sense: "to have to do" = "to have business or affairs to attend to" (what one "has to do" is one's kām, one's business — the sense preserved in "I have other things to do").

2. The idiom family around "have to do with"

  • Have to do with — be related to, be concerned with; have dealings with.
  • Have nothing to do with — be entirely unrelated to, or refuse any association with ("I will have nothing to do with that plan").
  • Have something to do with — be partly related or involved in ("his lateness had something to do with the traffic").
  • What does X have to do with Y? — the standard challenge to relevance, questioning the connection between two things.
  • Have to do (without "with") — the older business-sense: to have tasks, affairs, or obligations to perform.

3. Critical distinction from the modal "have to"

The idiom must not be confused with the obligative construction "have to" + infinitive meaning "must" ("I have to go"). The obligative "have to" (from "have [something] to do," i.e. an obligation held) is also an old English idiom, but it is a marker of necessity, whereas "have to do with" is a marker of relation. The presence of the preposition "with" and the idiomatic fixed form disambiguate the two: "have to do with X" = relate to X; "have to do X" = be obliged to do X.

4. Historical bridge and semantic reasoning

The idiom is a fossil of the possessive construction. Old English habban tō dōnne meant literally "to have [something] for the doing" — to possess business or tasks to perform, where tō dōnne is the inflected infinitive ("for doing"). From "to have business to perform" the phrase extended in Middle English to "to have business with someone" — that is, to have dealings — and from "dealings with a person" the meaning broadened to any relation or connection with a thing. The semantic bridge is therefore: possessed tasks → transacted business → shared concern → mere relation or relevance. By the early modern period the phrase was fully grammaticalized, and its original possessive logic is no longer felt; one says "what has this to do with that?" without any thought of holding or doing.

5. Register and usage notes

The idiom is colloquial but universal, found from the most informal speech to critical prose — the context of this card ("the original facts with which the aesthetic critic has to do") is Walter Pater's essay style, where the idiom carries the sense "that with which the critic is concerned, the critic's proper subject." It is one of the most frequent relational constructions in English, precisely because it is vague in mechanism but exact in claiming that a relation exists. In formal writing it is often replaced by "relate to," "concern," "pertain to," or "be concerned with," but it remains a native, natural, and irreplaceable way of asserting connection.
