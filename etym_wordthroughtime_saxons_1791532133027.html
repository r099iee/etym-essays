- 2nd–4th century CE: a coastal Germanic people named in Greco-Roman geography - for Rome the word means raider and pirate, a byword for menace from the northern sea.
- 5th–8th century: federates and settlers in Britain - Bede fixes the Saxons as one of three founding peoples, slightly subordinated to the Angles in the name English.
- 9th–11th century: the West Saxon royal house and its dialect dominate England - "Saxon" becomes a political and literary standard, practically synonymous with the English themselves.
- 12th–15th century: after the Norman Conquest "English" displaces "Saxon" in ordinary speech - the older word retreats into chronicles, law and antiquarian learning.
- 16th–19th century: Romantic nationalism and Teutonic-origin theory revive "Anglo-Saxon" - the word becomes a racial and constitutional banner, evoking free institutions and ancestral vigour.
- 20th–21st century: a neutral archaeological and historical term - "Saxon" denotes the early medieval settlers, while "Anglo-Saxon" is increasingly narrowed by scholars and contested as a racial shorthand.
