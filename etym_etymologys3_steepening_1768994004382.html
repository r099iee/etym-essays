Steepening is a young word built from very old material: it combines the ancient Germanic adjective steep with the equally ancient English suffix -en, and its own attested life spans barely four hundred years. Its story is thus two stories — the slow descent of steep from the heroic vocabulary of Old English poetry, and the quietly productive history of the -en suffix that turned adjectives into verbs of becoming — meeting in a word that only came into its own with modern science and finance.

Story one: steep, from tower to slope. The adjective steep belongs to the older stratum of English. Its earliest reconstructible ancestor is Proto-Germanic *staupaz, “high, lofty, towering,” a word of verticality before it was a word of inclination; the Germanic base *staup- seems to mean “to project, to rise up, to stand out,” and beyond Germanic its ancestry is obscure — some handbooks connect it with an Indo-European root *(s)teup- “to strike, knock; to cut off” (in the sense of something standing out against the sky), but the OED records the adjective’s origin as unclear, and the connection is not settled. Old English inherited *staupaz as stēap, and in the surviving poetry the word appears in exactly its Germanic sense of height and loftiness: Beowulf’s hall is a stēap hof, the sea is stēap (deep), and the hero descends the “steep stone-cliffs” (stēap stānhlīðu) to the mere. In Old English, steep meant “towering” and “deep” as much as “inclined.”

A word of caution separates two near-homophones. Old English also had the verb stīepan, “to soak, to immerse, to steep in water” (our verb to steep tea), from a different Germanic base with a different vowel; over the centuries the adjective and the verb converged in spelling, and modern dictionaries keep them as separate words. The adjective chain — *staupaz → stēap → Middle English steep → modern steep — involves no dramatic sound change; what changed was meaning. As English landscape description developed in the Middle Ages and Renaissance, steep shifted its centre of gravity from “lofty, towering” (a hill rising up) to “sharply inclined, hard to climb” (a slope falling away), until by the early modern period the dominant sense was precisely the one on this card: a surface whose angle is severe, approaching the vertical.

Story two: the -en suffix, the suffix of becoming. The -en of steepen is one of the oldest and most productive pieces of English morphology. It descends from Old English -nian, the suffix of the class II weak verbs, which itself continues Proto-Germanic *-inōn, a denominative suffix that formed verbs meaning “to make X” (causative) or “to become X” (inchoative) from adjectives and nouns. Behind *-inōn lies the Indo-European adjectival suffix *-nó-, “having the quality of X,” which appears across the family — in Latin plēnus “full,” in Greek, and in English’s own -en adjectives such as golden and wooden. From *-nó- to *-inōn to Old English -nian to Middle English -nen to modern -en, the suffix has spent three thousand years doing the same quiet work: converting a quality into a process. In Old English it produced verbs like hālnian “to heal”; in Middle English it produced hardenen, gladden, lengthen; and in early modern English it remained productive for the same small, steady set of adjectives — deep, steep, sharp, quick, thick, wide, hard, soft, dark, light, black, white, fat, mad, moist, and their kin. Shakespeare’s stage language is full of these verbs of becoming.

The meeting: steepen and its four-century career. The verb steepen, formed from steep + -en by this ancient machinery, is first recorded in the sixteenth century, and for two hundred years it lived a modest technical life, used chiefly of fortifications, embankments, and banks being made more sharply inclined — a surveying and engineering word. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, as geology became a science, the intransitive sense “to become steeper” took hold in scientific prose, and the present participle steepening became the standard way to name a gradient changing beneath the feet or across a map: a “steepening slope,” a “steepening river bed,” a “steepening gradient.” The twentieth century carried the word from the field into the chart. In finance the “yield curve” — the graph plotting interest rates against the maturity of debt — became a subject of constant metaphor, and “steepening” entered the traders’ jargon with precise directional meaning: a steepening yield curve is one in which long-term rates rise faster than short-term ones, a signal of expected growth and inflation. This is the word’s most visible modern life, a vivid export of a spatial image into an abstract quantitative domain, and it shows how a verb of becoming can be repurposed by an entire profession.

Modern state. Today steepening lives in three registers at once. In everyday and literary description it names the process of a slope growing more severe — as in the cave passage, where the participle converts the floor from a static surface into an active force tilting under the walkers’ feet. In science and engineering it names a gradient or curve increasing in severity. And in finance it is precise jargon for the shape of the yield curve. Behind all three sits the ancient machinery of the language: the Indo-European adjectival suffix, the Germanic inchoative, and the Old English adjective of towering height, all meeting in a single present participle.

  • Sound changes: OE stēap → modern steep shows the regular Middle English loss of unstressed final vowels and the later monophthongisation of the diphthong (ēa → e:); the -en suffix shows the regular reduction of OE -nian to ME -nen and modern -en.
  • Key attestations: OE stēap in Beowulf (8th–10th c.); ME steep (12th–15th c.); steepen first recorded in the 16th c.; the intransitive “become steeper” sense in 18th–19th-c. geology; “steepening yield curve” in 20th-c. finance.
  • Debated points: The pre-Germanic ancestry of *staupaz is uncertain (the link to PIE *(s)teup- is tentative); the adjective and the verb steep “to soak” are historically distinct words that converged in spelling.
