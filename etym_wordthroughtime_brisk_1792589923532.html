- [Late 16th c. (Elizabethan London)] — adapted into English from French brusque / Italian brusco, initially denoting effervescent, sparkling drinks and flashy, fashionable courtiers — [perception: perceived as a slightly flamboyant, stylish descriptor of wit, sparkle, and dapper energy]
- [17th c. (Jacobean & Restoration Era)] — broadened into maritime navigation and natural description, defining a fresh, invigorating wind that propelled ships swiftly without damage — [perception: regarded by sailors and writers as an ideal, healthy, and bracing natural force]
- [18th c. (Enlightenment & Commercial Expansion)] — embraced by London merchants, financiers, and economists (Adam Smith) to describe rapid sales turnover and high market transaction volume — [perception: celebrated as a hallmark of economic vitality, commercial success, and diligent industry]
- [19th c. (Victorian Literature & Tea Culture)] — became the quintessential descriptor of healthy, disciplined exercise (a brisk walk across the moors) and codified into tea tasting as the mark of fresh, astringent bite — [perception: respected as an indicator of robust bodily constitution, moral alertness, and sensory refinement]
- [20th–21st c. (Contemporary Standard English)] — widely used across everyday speech, weather broadcasts, and corporate communication to denote efficient, clean, and forward-moving momentum — [perception: universally valued as a positive attribute of clarity, decisive speed, and refreshing vitality]
