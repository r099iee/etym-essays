Chain for LUNGED: - PIE *dlengʰ- 'long' — the source also of Latin longus, Greek dolikhos, and English long itself. - Latin longus 'long' — the adjective of extent. - Vulgar Latin *longāre 'to lengthen, make long' — a denominative verb built on longus. - Old French alongier / allongier 'to lengthen, extend, stretch out' (from a- 'to' + long 'long') — the verb of extending the arm, leg, or stroke. - French allonge 'a lengthening, an extension' (the noun used in fencing of the extended thrust) and allonger 'to lengthen (the attack).' - English lunge (also longe), 1735: 'a sudden forward thrust in fencing,' borrowed from the French fencing vocabulary. - Modern English lunged (past tense; the -g- spelling settles by the 19th c.). Note on the merged doublet: The horse-training word 'lunge / longe' (a long rope for exercising a horse in a circle) is a separate borrowing from French longe, 'lunge, long rope,' from Latin longa (the feminine of longus). The two words — the fencing thrust and the training rope — merged in spelling in English in the 18th-19th centuries and are now treated as one; the derivation above follows the fencing sense that governs this card. Chain for FENDED: - PIE *gʷʰen- 'to strike, slay, kill' — the root of Sanskrit hanti 'strikes, slays,' Greek theinein 'to strike,' and Latin -fendere. - Latin fendere 'to strike, thrust, hit' — a verb that survives in Latin only inside compounds (defendere 'to ward off,' offendere 'to strike against, offend'). - Latin defendere 'to ward off, repel, avert, protect, defend' (1st c. BCE) — dē- 'away from' + fendere 'to strike': literally 'to strike (the blow) away.' - Old French defendre 'to defend, protect; to forbid' (9th-10th c.). - Middle English defenden 'to defend, protect' (13th c.). - English fend (14th c.) — the aphetic (clipped) form of defend; it keeps the full ancestor sense 'to ward off,' and by the 16th c. appears in 'fend for oneself' and, later, in phrasal 'fend off.' - Modern English fended (past tense). Note: The clipping 'fend' is a true aphetic form — English simply dropped the initial syllable of 'defend' — so the verb arrives at its modern shape without passing through an intermediate attested stage. The PIE root *gʷʰen- is uncontroversial; the modern distinction is purely semantic, between the intact verb 'defend' (protect, guard) and its clipped twin 'fend' (ward off, manage for oneself).
