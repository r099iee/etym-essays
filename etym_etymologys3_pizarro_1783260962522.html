THE NAME OF THE STONY PLACE Pizarro is a surname, not a concept word, and its biography must be written in two registers: the honest, unresolved story of the name's origin, and the vivid, certain story of the man who made it world-historical. On the first register, the scholarly consensus is genuinely agnostic. Basque — the only non-Indo-European language still spoken in Western Europe — is a language isolate with no known relatives, and the surname Pizarro is most plausibly explained from a Basque word for "stone" or "slate," pizar or pitzar, the name of a broken piece of rock. This is supported by the fact that the Castilian noun pizarra, "slate; blackboard," first appears in Spanish around 1500 with no Latin parent and is itself regarded by the best etymologists (notably Corominas) as of Basque provenance; and the surname is concentrated in the rocky uplands of western Spain, in León and Extremadura, precisely where such a lithonymic byname would be born. On this account, Pizarro originally meant something like "the man of the stony place," a nickname for a dweller among the slate hills that hardened into a hereditary family name. A minority theory derives it from Latin petra, "rock" (Greek pétra), with a Romance suffix, but the phonetics fit Basque better, and the honest dictionaries simply record the origin as uncertain and most likely Basque. The methodological point matters: this is an anthroponymic, pre-Roman history, and any attempt to force a Proto-Indo-European root onto the name would be an error of method — a reminder that not every European word is Indo-European. THE SECOND REGISTER: THE MAN The history that gave the name its meaning for English readers begins with Francisco Pizarro, born about 1475 or 1476 in Trujillo, in the Extremadura of the reconquest, the illegitimate son of an infantry captain and a peasant woman. He began life as a swineherd, never learned to read, and crossed to the New World as a soldier. After years of service in the isthmus and the first Caribbean ventures, he formed the famous partnership with Diego de Almagro and the priest Hernando de Luque, obtained a royal capitulación, and launched the third and fatal expedition to Peru. The enterprise of 1532–1533 is a study in audacity: with fewer than two hundred men and a handful of horses, Pizarro marched into the heart of the Inca Empire, invited the emperor Atahualpa to Cajamarca, seized him in the famous ambush, and then held him for the largest ransom in history — a room filled with gold and silver. When the treasure arrived, Pizarro had Atahualpa executed anyway, marched to Cuzco, installed a puppet ruler, and in 1535 founded Lima, "the City of the Kings," on the coast. The sequel was the civil war of the conquerors: Almagro was captured and executed in 1538, and on 26 June 1541 the Almagrist faction broke into Pizarro's palace in Lima and killed him. THE NAME IN ENGLISH PROSE The name entered English with the news of the conquest. Sixteenth-century compilations (Hakluyt) carried the Peruvian narrative; 18th-century historians such as Robertson shaped it into a moral fable; and in 1847 William Hickling Prescott published The Conquest of Peru, which made the figure of Pizarro a household name in the English-speaking world. The decisive literary fixing, however, is Macaulay's: in his History of England (1848) the sentence "the realms which Cortes and Pizarro had added to the dominions of Charles the Fifth" turns the surname into a metonym for Spanish empire — the sword that added realms and extracted gold without building anything durable, standing in implicit contrast to the British colonies that "rapidly became far mightier and wealthier" through settlement, law, and commerce. SOCIO-CULTURAL LIFE OF THE NAME The meaning of Pizarro in English has always been bound up with the moral self-image of the British empire. In the age of the Black Legend, when Protestant Europe painted Spain as the model of colonial cruelty, Pizarro was the perfect exhibit: the illiterate soldier who destroyed a civilization for gold. British historians exploited the contrast between Spanish conquest and English commerce — between Pizarro's plunder and the "opulence and martial glory" that Macaulay celebrates — and the name became a byword for imperial rapacity, used still today in political rhetoric to condemn conquest without settlement. Its trajectory, in short, is from place-name to byname to surname to world-historical epitome: the stony place of Extremadura, the swineherd who conquered Peru, and the archetype of the destroyer of civilizations that English prose has kept alive for two centuries.
