- 15th-16th centuries - The term arrives from Old French, naming the roughly triangular space left between an arch and the rectangular frame above it - the inevitable geometric leftover of masonry, present by necessity rather than choice.
- 17th-19th centuries - The word settles firmly into architectural vocabulary; builders decorate these unavoidable triangular voids with carving and fresco, turning a structural necessity into an ornament.
- 20th century - In 1979 evolutionary biologists Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin transplant the term: a biological spandrel is a trait that exists as a byproduct of other adaptive changes, not selected for itself - and one that may later be co-opted into a genuinely useful survival tool.
