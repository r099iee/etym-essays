BANNOCKS are flat, round, coarse cakes of unleavened or lightly leavened barley, oat, or wheat (historically pease) meal, baked on a griddle or directly on a hearthstone — the classic bread of the Scottish Highlands, the Hebrides, and the Celtic and Northern English countryside. In the Elves of Rivendell's song in The Hobbit, chapter III, "The faggots are reeking, / The bannocks are baking!" pairs the smoking fuel with the rising loaves to paint an almost tactile picture of a welcoming hearth: warmth, woodsmoke, and the promise of fresh bread for exhausted travellers. The word is deliberately archaic and regional; Tolkien, a medievalist steeped in Old English and Celtic lore, chose it to give Rivendell a homely, ancient, pastoral complexion rather than an exotic or courtly one. The tone is comfortable and faintly Celtic: bannocks baking are the sound of a household making ready for its guests.

Set beside its near-synonyms, BANNOCKS differ from bread (the general, usually oven-baked, leavened loaf), from scones (smaller, finer, often sweetened and oven-baked), from oatcakes (thin, crisp biscuit-cakes), from griddle cakes and pancakes (thin batter fried in a pan), and from damper (the Australian bushman's camp loaf). A bannock is distinguished above all by its hearthstone or griddle baking, its coarse whole-grain meal, and its deep roots in Gaelic and Scots domestic tradition.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings

  • Scottish and Northern English staple: the traditional bannock is the daily hearth-bread of the Highlands — barley bannocks, oat bannocks, and bannocks of pease meal, baked on a hot stone, an iron girdle, or among the embers of a peat fire; the Selkirk bannock (19th c.) is a special sweet, fruited, oven-baked loaf from Selkirk in the Borders.
  • Indigenous North American bread: bannock is also a flatbread of flour, water, and baking powder, fried in a pan or baked — brought to First Nations and Métis communities through the fur trade (via the Hudson's Bay Company ration) and now a much-loved pan-bread of Indigenous cuisine across Canada.
  • Camp and trail bread: in Scots and Canadian usage a simple bannock baked over an open fire remains the archetypal trail ration — a durable, flour-based flat loaf carried by shepherds, soldiers, and travellers.
  • Dialectal breadth: across Northern English dialects bannock can name any large round flat cake or loaf, while the rare dialectal verb to bannock meant to bake into such cakes.
  • Ceremonial and literary colour: in folk culture the bannock is the bread of hospitality — its baking signals welcome and plenty, which is exactly the resonance Tolkien exploits in the Elves' song.

The meaning has always revolved around a flat, round, hearth-baked bread of coarse meal. The word carries a strong regional and historical register: it is the sound of Gaelic and Scots domestic life, and its modern polysemy simply extends that ancient flatbread tradition across the Scottish diaspora and the Indigenous kitchens of North America.
