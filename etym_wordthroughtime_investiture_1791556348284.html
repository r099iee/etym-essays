- 11th to 12th Century: Feudal and canon law term for the symbolic conveyance of land or ecclesiastical office (pastoral ring and crosier) - Ground zero of the defining constitutional clash of medieval Christendom (the Investiture Controversy); carried explosive overtones of royal usurpation versus papal supremacy.
- 13th to 15th Century: Broadened in feudal practice across Western Europe to denote the solemn bestowal of any fief, secular title, or knighthood - Viewed as a sacred, binding social contract uniting lord and vassal through tangible ritual.
- 16th to 17th Century: Post-Reformation era; applied to monarchical and ecclesiastical rituals across the Anglican and Catholic worlds, as well as civic guild ceremonies - Perceived as a display of state majesty, divine right, and orderly hierarchy.
- 18th to 19th Century: Historical treatises (Gibbon, Hume) analyzed the medieval controversy; contemporary usage narrowed to high state ceremonies (knighthoods, royal dignities) and religious orders - Evoked solemn antiquity, pageantry, and historical continuity.
- 20th Century: Popularized globally through broadcast royal ceremonies (e.g., the 1969 Investiture of Prince Charles at Caernarfon Castle) and formalized in academic, judicial, and diplomatic inaugurations - Associated with magnificent traditional ritual, institutional dignity, and legal legitimacy.
- 21st Century: Maintained in constitutional monarchies, collegiate installations, and judicial seatings - Represents the solemn transition of institutional stewardship and public commitment to high office.
