• dock (Rumex obtusifolius and kin): Similar because it is the broad-leaved relative of sorrel in the same genus and family (Polygonaceae); differs because it is taller and coarser, with a plain, slightly bitter taste and no distinctive acidity, and is not normally eaten as a salad green.
  • wood sorrel (Oxalis acetosella): Similar because it tastes sharply sour on the tongue and is used as a wild green; differs because it is a quite unrelated creeping plant of the Oxalidaceae with clover-like leaves, sharing the oxalic bite but not the Rumex ancestry.
  • sheep's sorrel (Rumex acetosella): Similar because it is a true sorrel of the same genus and equally sour; differs because it is smaller, narrower-leaved, sharper and more bitter, and grows as a weed of poor acid ground rather than a garden or meadow plant.
  • spinach (Spinacia oleracea): Similar because it is a leafy green cooked as a pot-herb and used like sorrel in soups; differs because it is a domesticated, mild, non-acidic plant of the amaranth family, with sorrel's sharpness bred out of it.
  • French sorrel / buckler sorrel (Rumex scutatus): Similar because it is a cultivated sorrel with a lemony, acidic flavour; differs because it is rounder-leaved and milder than common sorrel, a garden rather than a wild plant.
  • rhubarb (Rheum rhabarbarum): Similar because it is a member of the dock family whose stalks are eaten for their sour, oxalic tang; differs because it is a large-leaved plant of which only the stalk is eaten, and its sourness is sweeter and jammier than sorrel's leaf-acidity.
  • watercress (Nasturtium officinale): Similar because it is a sharp-tasting wild green eaten raw in salads; differs because its heat is a mustard-pepper pungency from a different (cress) family, not the lemony oxalic sourness of sorrel.
  • dandelion (Taraxacum officinale): Similar because it is a common foraged wild green with a bitter edge, often paired with sorrel in country cooking; differs because its flavour is a plain, milky bitterness rather than a bright acid sharpness, and it is a daisy-family plant.
  • nettle (Urtica dioica): Similar because it is a widely foraged wild green cooked as a pot-herb; differs because it is stinging, spinach-like, and bland rather than sour, with none of sorrel's lemony acidity.
  • sorrel (the roselle, Hibiscus sabdariffa): Similar because its tart calyces are also called "sorrel" and brew a sharp, festive drink; differs because it is a tropical hibiscus of a wholly different family, and the name is a transferred homonym rather than a botanical kin.
