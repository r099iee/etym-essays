The harbour began as a roof over soldiers. Its certain half is the sheltering element: PIE *bhergh-, to hide and protect, which gives German bergen (to rescue, to salvage), and Old English beorg (refuge, defence) — the same root that buries the dead (to bury is to hide in earth) and raises hills (barrow, iceberg, names for mounds that hide things within). Compounded in Old English with here, an army or host, it made herebeorg: army-shelter, quarters, lodgings — the billets and barns where a moving host slept. No salt water anywhere: the first harbours were inland barns.

The sea entered with the Vikings. Old Norse herbergi meant lodging too, but Norse crews lodged from ships, so their word already leaned over the gunwale toward the water; during the Danelaw centuries the two cousins reinforced each other, and by Chaucer's day Middle English herberwe sheltered pilgrims on land and ships at sea with equal ease. Spelling wobbled for centuries — herber, herbore, harborow — before settling on harbour in Britain (American spelling reform later stripped the u across the Atlantic, giving harbor).

Stages of the journey:

  • PIE hiding places (before 2000 BCE): *bhergh- gives the family of hiding and height — to bury, to shelter, mounds and bergs — protection imagined as covering over.
  • Germanic host-shelter (c. 500 BCE–500 CE): *bergaz (shelter) compounds with *harjaz (army, host) wherever war bands need quarters — the seed of the English word.
  • Old English billets (9th–11th c.): herebeorg in charters and chronicles means lodgings for men and horses — straw, roof, and no questions.
  • Norse reinforcement (9th–11th c.): herbergi lends the word sea legs; sailors' lodgings blur into the sheltered waters the sailors lodge from.
  • Middle English haven (14th–15th c.): herberwe and harbour now name anchorages from Bristol to Bergen — and, in devotional writers, the soul's refuge in heaven.
  • Engineering age (18th–19th c.): great breakwaters and dredged channels manufacture harbours where nature offered only surf; the word fills with granite and tariffs.
  • Metaphor everywhere (20th c.–today): harboured grudges in psychology, safe-harbour provisions in finance and data law, tax havens offshore — the army lodging now shelters feelings, funds, and fugitives, and, in Tolkien's far west, the last ships of the elves.

Attestation runs from Anglo-Saxon charters granting herebeorg to thegns, through Shakespeare's harbours of refuge and Milton's heavenly havens, to Admiralty charts pricked with anchors and modern statutes full of safe harbours. Few words have travelled so far while keeping their first promise: wherever bodies or boats or even balance sheets need to lie safe from weather, there — the word still insists — shall be harbour.
