blazoned — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *bʰleh₁- “to shine, burn, blow” (reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE) — the base proposed for the Germanic “torch / bright” and “to blow / trumpet” words (debated in exact branch; see note). 2. Frankish / Germanic *blas- “torch, burning brand” or “bright” (reconstructed, c. 5th–8th c. CE) — the Germanic source proposed for the Romance shield-word; some scholars instead start from a Germanic root meaning “to blow, proclaim.” 3. Medieval Latin blaso (genitive blasōnis) “torch” (attested in Carolingian-era glossaries, 8th–9th c.) — the intermediate Latinisation of the Germanic “torch” word; its connection to the shield is the point of dispute. 4. Old French blason “shield, buckler” (attested 12th c.), later “coat of arms, armorial bearings” (heraldic sense, 13th–14th c.); derived verb Old French blasonner “to describe or paint a coat of arms” (14th c.). 5. Middle English blasoun, blason “shield, coat of arms” (borrowed from Old French, 14th c.); Middle English verb blasonen / blasounen “to describe or paint armorial bearings” (14th–15th c.). 6. English blazon (verb) “to describe or paint a coat of arms; to proclaim publicly” (16th c.) → past participle / adjective blazoned “emblazoned, displayed with armorial colour; proclaimed abroad” (16th c.). Debate note: the etymology of Old French blason is genuinely disputed. The traditional view derives it from a Germanic “torch” word (via medieval Latin blaso), with the shield perhaps borrowing the sense of the shining boss; an alternative derives it from a Germanic verb “to blow, proclaim,” making the shield the thing that is trumpeted. Neither path is fully proven, and the OED marks the origin as uncertain — both routes are recorded here for completeness.
