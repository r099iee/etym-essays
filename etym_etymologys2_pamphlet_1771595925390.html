The word descends from a Greek personal name, not from a single PIE root; its two Greek components are given first, then the unified chain from the name to the English word. No PIE stage is asserted for either component. GREEK COMPONENT 1: pān (πᾶν, neuter of pâs, πᾶς) "all, every, whole" — a word with no certain Indo-European etymon; Beekes entertains a Pre-Greek origin, and the older link to a PIE root is not secure. No PIE reconstruction is asserted. GREEK COMPONENT 2: phílos (φίλος) "loving, dear, friend" — also of disputed origin; Beekes suspects a Pre-Greek substrate, and the older proposed PIE link *bhil- "to love" is uncertain. No reliable PIE reconstruction is asserted. UNIFIED CHAIN (attested stages): Greek pān "all" + phílos "loving, dear" → Greek personal name Pámphilos (Πάμφιλος) "beloved of all" (pân + philos), a common ancient Greek name borne by several early martyrs. → Latin Pamphilus (Latinized form of the Greek name). → Medieval Latin poem title Pamphilus, seu de Amore "Pamphilus, or Concerning Love" — a popular comic-didactic Latin poem composed c. 1180, long falsely attributed to Ovid; it circulated in cheap, small, unbound gatherings. → School slang Pamphilet "little Pamphilus" — the diminutive of the title-name, transferred from the poem to the little unbound copy in which it circulated. → Old French / Anglo-Norman pamflet, panflet "a small unbound copy (of the poem); any short unbound treatise". → Middle English panflet (first recorded c. 1387) "a small unbound book or treatise". → English PAMPHLET "a short unbound printed work, especially a polemical one" (in continuous use since the 16th century) — our card. NOTE on the debate: the derivation of English pamphlet from the name Pamphilus via the medieval poem is the standard, well-attested account (OED; Etymonline). The genuinely debated element is the deeper origin of the two Greek components pan- and phil-, for which a Pre-Greek substrate has been argued by Beekes; that uncertainty is stated here, and no PIE root is forced onto either component. The final stage from object-name to genre is a documented metonymy.
