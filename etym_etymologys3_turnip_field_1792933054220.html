The compound turnip-field joins a borrowed root to a native field, and each half tells a different migration before they meet at the stout gate.

Turnip begins in Roman kitchen gardens as Latin napus, the navew or wild turnip, passing through Old French navel into Middle English nepe. English then prefixed its own syllable: turnepe appears in the 15th and 16th centuries, usually explained as turn- for the root rounded shape, as if turned on a lathe, though some scholars hear only an intensive prefix. Either way the folk logic stuck, and turnip drove out nepe entirely, giving the modern vegetable a name half Latin and half English guesswork.

Field is pure Germanic inheritance. Proto-Germanic *felthuz meant open flat land, surviving in Old English feld for unenclosed pasture and forest clearing as opposed to towns and woods. Enclosure transformed both the land and the word: by Middle English feld increasingly meant the hedged arable plot itself, and modern compounds like cornfield, hayfield, and turnip-field each name one fenced parcel under one crop.

Agricultural revolution made the compound famous:

  • Medieval roots: nepes grew in gardens and small peasant strips, boiled in pottage, never the glory of the manor wheat.
  • Norfolk breakthrough: Townshend drilled turnips in rows on light Breckland soils around 1730, hoed them clean, and fed the roots to cattle through winter, ending the immemorial fallow year.
  • Four-course glory: wheat, turnips, barley, clover rotated endlessly, the turnip-field manured by folded sheep and the wheat following fat on their droppings, doubling rents and population.
  • Language follows the plough: surveyors, novels, and seed catalogues spread turnip-field as a standard term, and dialect gives turnip-hoeing, turnip-fly, and turnip-face for the labour, pest, and rustic alike.

Sound shifts are modest. Latin napus loses its ending in French mouths, English stresses the new first syllable, and the Great Vowel Shift rounds the vowels toward modern turnip, spelled with an intrusive i that never quite settles, giving American turnep variants. Field keeps its Germanic consonants intact, merely smoothing Old English feld into the modern spelling with its silent i. Cultural memory then fixed the compound in contrasting tones: pride in reclaimed heath and fat Christmas beef, comedy in turnip-head rustics and hollowed Hallowe'en lanterns. Tolkien, loving capable countrysides, reaches for the proud sense: his huge turnip-field and its stout gate promise farmers who hoe, fold, and fasten, the old rotation still turning honestly under autumn skies. Tithe maps and farm diaries still record the very root breaks where flocks folded two centuries past, and seed catalogues list white, yellow, and purple-topped kinds for table and fold, keeping the compound alive in hedges, gates, and parish memory.
