Note on semantic evolution and morphological continuity: The Latin base prōsternere is a vivid physical compound: prō- ("forward") combined with sternere ("to scatter, spread flat upon the ground"). The root *sterh₃- is ubiquitous across Indo-European languages as the fundamental term for laying something flat, yielding Latin strātum ("a paved road / layer", source of English street and stratum), Greek stornymi ("to spread out"), Sanskrit stṛṇāti ("he scatters, spreads sacred grass"), and Germanic *strawją (source of English straw and strew). In Roman military annals, prosternere was brutally martial: an army prostrated its enemies by throwing them face down into the dirt and slaughtering them. Through Christian monastic translation in the 4th century CE, this violent martial overthrow was re-conceptualized as a voluntary, interiorized spiritual discipline: the monk voluntarily "throws himself flat into the dirt" before God to slay his worldly ego. By the 19th century, medical science secularized the concept once again to describe systemic somatic exhaustion.
