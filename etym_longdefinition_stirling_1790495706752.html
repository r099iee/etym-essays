Stirling is the city in central Scotland whose castle on a volcanic crag guards the lowest crossing of the River Forth — the brooch that clasps Highlands and Lowlands. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita completing his pair of disappointments — Tantallon was there; but no Archibald of Angus: — Stirling, but no Knight of Snowdoun means the boy found the second castle as empty as the first: Stirling Castle stood, but Scott's Knight of Snowdoun, James FitzJames revealed as James V in The Lady of the Lake, did not step from its guard-room. Snowdoun is Scott's romantic name for Stirling's rock (from Snowdon-like white fort, or the Snowdoun of old romance); for the child the real town and the poem's place were one, and both required a king. The capital S marks a proper noun; the word has no common-noun meaning except by transfer to surname and title.

Geographically and historically Stirling is the key of Scotland. The crag — dolerite sill left by glaciers — rises 76 metres above the carse; the castle on it combines a 14th-16th-century palace (James IV's Great Hall, James V's Palace with its Stirling Heads) with 18th-century barracks, still headquarters of the Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders' museum. Below lie the bridge and the field of battles: Stirling Bridge (1297, Wallace), Bannockburn two miles south (1314, Bruce), Sauchieburn (1488), Sheriffmuir nearby (1715). The town itself — Old Town wynds climbing to the castle, Victorian suburbs, the Wallace Monument on Abbey Craig, the university at Bridge of Allan — is now Scotland's smallest city (granted 2002). To say Stirling to a Scot is to say independence, narrow defiles held against odds, and the view north to the Highland line.

  • Head sense: the city and castle (source sense): e.g. to visit Stirling, Stirling Castle, the Stirling Heads, the guard-room at Stirling where FitzJames reveals himself. Scott's Canto 6 denouement — Behold the King! ... James, the monarch of the glen — is staged there, and tourists still ask for the spot. Ruskin's boyhood expectation was literal: kings should live in castles.
  • Battle-sense: Stirling as shorthand for Scottish arms: e.g. Stirling Bridge, Bannockburn near Stirling, to hold Stirling. Military histories use the name for the whole Forth-crossing position. Who held Stirling held access to north or south; Edward I, Edward II, Cromwell, and the Jacobites all learned it.
  • Administrative sense: Stirling council, shire, and bridge: e.g. Stirling Council, the Stirling constituency, the new Stirling Bridge. The former Stirlingshire, the present council area stretching from Loch Lomond to the Forth, and the parliamentary seat all borrow the town's name.
  • Onomastic sense: Stirling as surname and title: e.g. James Stirling the mathematician, Archibald Stirling of the SAS, the Earl of Stirling (a dormant Nova Scotian peerage), Stirling Moss (whose surname comes from the town via family). The surname means of Stirling; the earldom (1600s, William Alexander) recalls the Darien and Nova Scotia schemes.
  • Romance sense: Snowdoun-Stirling: Scott deliberately archaicises the castle as Snowdoun (Knight of Snowdoun, Stirling's romantic double) to lift it into Arthurian time. Antiquaries debate whether any medieval form Snowdoun ever named Stirling; Scott's authority made it stick for poetry if not for charters.
  • Modern sporting-institutional echoes: Stirling Albion, University of Stirling, Stirling Prize (for architecture — named for James Stirling the architect, himself named for the town). Each reuse trades on centrality, solidity, and Scottishness.

Linguistically Stirling is first recorded in the 12th century as Strivelin, Strivelyn, Stryvelyne, and its origin is debated and unresolved. Proposals include Brittonic *ystrevelin (place of strife, from ystrev- to strive, seized upon by Victorian historians who liked battles), Gaelic srib-linn (pool of the river, stream-pool), and a pre-Celtic river-name *str- (to flow) plus suffix. None commands assent; the standard place-name dictionaries mark Stirling as of uncertain, probably Brittonic or Gaelic, origin. Pronunciation is /ˈstɜːlɪŋ/ with first-syllable stress and a dark r in Scotland. Spelling stabilised as Stirling in the 15th-16th century; earlier Striveling, Sterling (whence the sterling confusion, which is unrelated) survive in archives.
