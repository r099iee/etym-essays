Proto-Indo-European *trem- "to tremble, shake, trip, stamp with the feet in agitation" (reconstructed; attested through Greek τρέμω trémō "I tremble, quake," Latin tremere, and related Italic and Hellenic formations in the trembling / fear-motion field) → Latin tremere "to tremble, quake, shake" (thematic verb, attested from early Latin, e.g. Plautus and Lucretius) → Latin tremulus "shaking, trembling, quivering, tremulous" (adjective derived with the suffix -ulus; attested in classical Latin, e.g. of aged limbs and vibrating objects) → Medieval Latin maintenance of tremulus in medical and descriptive Latin, with Romance parallels (Middle French trémuleux, Italian tremulo, Spanish trémulo) → English TREMULOUS (borrowed as a learned adjective in the early 17th century, first in natural-philosophical and medical contexts). Note on parallel loans: English acquired the everyday verb tremble much earlier through Old French trembler (ultimately from the same Latin verb), so English holds two layers from one Latin family — a vernacular, naturalised verb and a bookish Latinate adjective. The related Latin noun tremor gave English tremor, and Latin tremendus ("to be trembled at") gave English tremendous, whose meaning drifted far toward slang intensity.
