HEURISTICS descends from a single Greek verbal root with an inchoative present suffix; the chain below runs from the reconstructed Indo-European base to the modern plural noun. CHAIN — ‘find’ element: - PIE *wreh₁- ‘to find, hit upon, perceive, discover, search out’ (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Ancient Greek εὑρίσκω (heurískō) ‘to find, discover, hit upon, invent, devise’ — a present formed with the inchoative suffix *-sk- added to the root *heur- (attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE; the initial h- reflects an original initial *w-, i.e. digamma) → Ancient Greek εὑρετικός (heurētikós) ‘inventive, clever at finding out, conducive to discovery’ — a derivative adjective built on the stem εὑρε- of εὑρίσκω with the suffix *-τικος (attested in Plato and Aristotle, 5th–4th c. BCE) → New Latin heuristicus ‘serving to discover, aiding inquiry’ — a scholarly coinage (early 19th c.) for the philosophical and pedagogical ‘method of discovery’ → English heuristic (adjective) ‘serving to discover, conducive to finding out’ (1820s) and (noun) ‘a practical rule of thumb that aids discovery or decision’ (19th c.) → HEURISTICS (plural noun) ‘practical rules of thumb; the study and techniques of such shortcuts’ (established as a technical term in decision science and AI from the mid-20th c.) NOTE ON DEBATE: the Greek formation involves a debated detail — the root *wreh₁- combines with *-sk-, and the precise vowel grade of the root in εὑρίσκω (where the sequence appears as εὑρ-) is discussed; the initial digamma (*w-) is inferred from the rough breathing (h-) in Greek orthography, but the internal root structure remains partly reconstructed. The English adjective is first attested in the 1820s in discussions of pedagogy and logic, and the path Greek εὑρετικός → New Latin heuristicus is the standard account; the parallel borrowings into French (heuristique) and German (heuristisch) followed in the 19th century.
