Stinker is Germanic nose-history. Its base, Old English stincan, strong verb (stanc, stuncon, stuncen), originally meant simply to emit a smell — and early glosses use it for sweet incense as well as foul middens. The strong-verb ablaut already hints at ancient Germanic pedigree: Proto-Germanic *stinkwan (to push, thrust, knock, and by extension to erupt with odour) is attested across West Germanic — Old Saxon stinkan, Old High German stinchan, Old Norse støkkva (to leap, squirt) in wider cognate sets. Whether push-to-smell is true etymology or folk linkage is debated; what is secure is that by Middle English stinken had narrowed pejoratively: to stink is to smell bad, and Chaucer and Langland already moralise it — flattery stinks, sin stinks, hell stinks.

The agent suffix -er (Old English -ere, as in baker, speaker) turns the verb into a noun of source: a stinker is that which stinks. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century uses are literal and domestic — Shenstone's stinking kennels, Pepys's stinking chimneys, sailor slang for cheap tobacco and bad breath. The spelling stinker stabilises after 1600; pronunciation follows regular Germanic /stɪŋk/ plus vocalic -er /ər/, RP /ˈstɪŋ.kər/ with short i and syllabic r-colouring.

  • From nose to character: smell-morality is pan-human, and English accelerates it after 1700. To call conduct stinking is to claim it offends a higher nose — God's, society's, decency's. By Victorian school and club slang a stinker is a fellow whose conduct stinks: cheating at cards, sneaking to masters, jilting, cadging. The Wodehouse generation inherits this ready-made: Bertie's circle calls each other stinkers the way later generations say jerk — half-serious, easily retracted, preserving friendship while venting.
  • From person to problem: parallel extension rates things by offensiveness. A stinker of a question (recorded in exam slang by the 1860s), stinker of a cold, stinker of a match, and theatrical stinker (a play that stinks, i.e. flops — American vaudeville by 1900s) all map nasal disgust onto difficulty, pain, or failure. The source phrase to come a stinker belongs here, blending to come a cropper (to fall heavily) with stinker-as-foul-outcome: love's frail craft wrecks and the wreck smells.
  • Socio-cultural context: nineteenth-century sanitation reform literalised stink as danger (miasma theory: bad air breeds cholera), giving smell-words unusual moral-medical force. Muscular public-school ethics then transferred that force to character: a stinker was unhygienic in soul. Post-war affluence softened it: you lucky stinker and poor old stinker show affectionate, even envious uses, while critics keep stinker for pans — a safe, printable insult when stronger words are libellous.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare has stinking as intensifier; Swift delights in stinkers literal and political; Dickensian clerks fear stinkers of tasks; Kipling's schoolboys brand sneaks as stinkers; Wodehouse makes stinker a versatile comic counter — Right Ho, Jeeves, Joy in the Morning, and the Glossop-Tuppy-Angela tangle where love comes a stinker; mid-century film reviews enshrine stinker as the gentleman's pan.

Phonetically the word keeps its punch because Germanic consonants do: initial st-, medial -nk-, final -er produce a snarl-to-grumble arc that feels like smelling something bad and growling. That sound-symbolism explains why stinker survived euphemism treadmills that killed cad and bounder: it still smells true. Whether denoting Angela's ruined romance, a brutal exam, or a rotten cheese, it reports the same judgement — this offends the nose, and the nose knows.
