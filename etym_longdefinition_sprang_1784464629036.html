Sprang is the strong (irregular) past tense of the verb spring: it means leaped, jumped, or burst upward or forward suddenly, with a release of stored energy. "All the dwarves sprang up, knocking over the table" records a single explosive collective motion — song, toast, and conspiracy converted into vertical bodies and flying crockery. The word is kinetic and instantaneous: not a slow standing-up, but the snapping open of coiled readiness. Tolkien uses the strong past to freeze the exact moment the party becomes a scramble. Because sprang is an inflection of spring, its meanings are the meanings of that verb applied to a completed, sudden action in the past. The central sense is physical and elastic: rising from rest in one bound. From this root-image the same verb has radiated a wide family of uses, and the past form sprang appears in all of them. Primary senses of the verb in this past form.
  • To leap or jump up suddenly: the core physical sense — "the dog sprang across the lawn," "he sprang to his feet" — a single bound from rest.
  • To burst forth or emerge suddenly: water "springs" from the earth; a trap "springs"; an idea "springs to mind" — sudden emergence rather than a human jump.
  • To move or act with sudden release of force: "he sprang into action," "the door sprang open" — elastic or explosive onset.
  • To start or set off: "sprang a trap," "sprang a surprise on someone," "sprang a leak" — the older transitive sense of releasing a mechanism.
Morphology note. Spring / sprang / sprung is one of the oldest patterns in English, the strong-verb ablaut inherited from Germanic. In careful traditional usage, sprang is the simple past and sprung the past participle; colloquial speech sometimes levels sprung into the past slot, but the literary form Tolkien writes — and this card preserves — is sprang. Usage and differentiation. Sprang emphasizes sudden elastic motion from a position of rest: a release, a burst, an unfurling. It is more vivid than jumped, more precise than rose, and distinct from bolted (flight) and bounded (repeated vigorous strides). On this card it must remain the completed strong past of that collective leap — the dwarves' one explosive rise that topples the table. The context in full. The sentence "All the dwarves sprang up, knocking over the table" caps a burst of excitement in which the party, fired by song and ale and the closing of a bargain, is converted from seated guests into upright, shoving conspirators in a single instant. Every word does work: all insists on the collective, simultaneous motion; up fixes the vertical direction of the leap; knocking over the table shows that the motion is energetic enough to topple furniture. The verb sprang is the fulcrum — one strong past tense carrying a dozen bodies. It is not a stately rising (that would be rose) nor a mere standing (got up); it is the abrupt, elastic release that the strong verb has encoded since Old English. Tolkien chooses the traditional literary form sprang rather than the colloquially leveled sprung, a choice in keeping with his deliberately old-fashioned, northern, epic-adjacent narrative voice. Grammar and usage notes. As the simple past of spring, sprang is used for a completed action at a definite past time ("the dog sprang across the lawn," "he sprang to his feet"). It belongs to the small but ancient class of strong verbs that English retains (compare begin/began, ring/rang, swim/swam), and its correctness in careful writing is a matter of inflectional tradition, not of a fixed "rule": sprung is the past participle ("the trap has sprung"), used after have/had. Because the verb's senses are many, sprang can also appear where the motion is inward or figurative — a suspicion sprang to mind, a leak sprang in the hull — but in every case the core image of sudden, elastic release is the same.
