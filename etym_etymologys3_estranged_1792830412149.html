The participle estranged carries Rome's whole theory of belonging inside it. Latin divided the world into the household and everything outside it; the outsider had a name, extraneus, and that name travelled through French courts and English marriages to become our word for love grown foreign. To trace estranged is to watch an adverb meaning out harden into a noun for the foreigner, soften into a verb for cold treatment, and settle into a participle for every broken closeness from divorce courts to the wars of Middle-earth.

Outside, and the outsider: PIE *eghs meant out — motion outward, position without. Latin built on it the most productive little family in the language: ex (out of), extra (outside), externus (outer), and, with the adjective suffix -aneus, extraneus — the one from outside, the foreigner, the stranger to the house. Roman law needed the category constantly: the extraneus could not inherit, could not witness certain rites, stood beyond the hearth's protection. Strangeness was not a feeling but a status, written into tablets. That legal coldness never left the word; estranged still sounds like a verdict even when it describes a marriage.

French courts and English hearts: Old French inherited extraneus as estrange, foreign or strange, and — crucially — made a verb of it: estrangier, to treat as a stranger, to hold at a distance. Norman clerks brought both to England, where the verb estraunge appears in the 1400s for alienating land (transferring it to outsiders) and, in the same breath, alienating minds and hearts. The Tudor century completed the domestication: Shakespeare's characters estrange wives, friends, and followers, and the legal sense of transferring property split off to alienate, leaving estrange the feelings. From then on the word's home was the household quarrel, scaled upward whenever history needed it — estranged colonies, estranged churches, estranged peoples.

  • Sound shifts to notice: Latin ex- before t simplified in French, with the x melting into the spelling es- (extraneus to estrange); English then softened the initial vowel toward e- and voiced the s toward z in speech; the verb ending -ier levelled to -e, and the participle -ed voices to -jd after the soft g.
  • Semantic drift, stage by stage: out and outside (PIE) → out of the house (Latin prepositions) → the foreigner as legal status (classical extraneus) → to treat as a stranger (Old French verb) → to transfer away and to alienate hearts (Middle English) → domestic and political coldness (Tudor onward) → the dignified sorrow of modern use, from family courts to epic histories of Men and Elves.
  • Literary attestations: chronicles record barons estranged from kings; Shakespeare estranges Lear from Cordelia and Prospero from his dukedom; Gibbon estranges provinces from emperors; Austen estranges sisters by pride and letter-writing; Tolkien, with philological precision, measures the Second Age by who was and was not estranged from the Elves — fellowship itself as the diagnostic of history.

Why foreignness hurts: estranged endures because it names a pain no blunter word reaches — the pain not of enemies but of ex-intimates, people who know exactly how to wound because they once knew how to comfort. Separated can be an accident of miles; hostile can be honest war; estranged is the chill after warmth, strangeness where fluency was. Latin law knew the outsider as a category; English feeling knows him as a loss. When the history says not all Men were estranged from the Elves, it pays the Faithful the highest compliment the word allows: across centuries of fear and distance, they never became strangers.
