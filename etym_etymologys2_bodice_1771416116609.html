Single root word; one linear chain: PIE *bhew- "to be, exist, grow, become" (reconstructed; unattested; the root's exact vocalism is debated, sometimes written *bheu-) → Proto-Germanic *budagaz "body, trunk" (reconstructed; etymology debated — handbooks compare "that which is, that which has grown," deriving the noun from the being/growing root; attested only in West Germanic) → Old English bodig "body, trunk, torso" (attested c. 1000) → Middle English bodi "body, corpse, trunk" (13th century) → Modern English body "the physical frame" → the tailor's phrase "a pair of bodies" "a two-part laced upper garment" (16th century, England) → the plural bodies reinterpreted as a singular noun → bodice "the laced upper garment; the fitted torso of a dress" (1560s–70s; spelling fixed by the 18th century) → Modern English bodice. DEBATED POINTS: - The etymology of Proto-Germanic *budagaz / Old English bodig is genuinely obscure. Many handbooks derive it from the root "to be, grow" (the body as "that which exists"), which is the derivation adopted here, but others note that the word has no clear cognates outside West Germanic and caution that the connection, while traditional, cannot be proven from attested forms. - The shift from "bodies" to "bodice" is a reanalysis of a plural as a singular and is not a regular sound change; the spelling with -ice was conventionalized by the eighteenth century and does not reflect an original Latin or French suffix.
