1. The root: law as order (PIE to Latin). Behind justly stands Latin iūstus, "just, upright, lawful", itself formed from iūs, "law, right, justice". The earlier history of iūs is one of the classic puzzles of Latin etymology. Most scholars derive it from a reconstructed PIE stem *h₂yewos-, "set of rules, right order", pointing to Sanskrit yóḥ "welfare, health, good fortune" and Avestan forms connected with ritual purity; the semantic arc runs from "well-ordered condition (of the community)" to "that which is established as rule". Other theories connect the word to Indo-Iranian notions of binding or swearing. Whatever the ultimate root, the direction of meaning is agreed: iūs names the order that holds a community together, and the adjective iūstus names the person or act in conformity with that order.

2. Rome and the jurisprudence of the word. In classical Latin, iustus was already a double word: on one side "conforming to law" (a iustum bellum, a just war), on the other "morally upright, equitable" (a iustus vir, a man of justice). Roman jurisprudence, from the Twelve Tables to the jurists of the Empire, made ius the spine of a technical vocabulary — iurisprudentia itself — while Cicero gave iustitia, "justice", its philosophical definition as the virtue that gives each his due (suum cuique). The adverb iuste carried the same double life: "lawfully" and "fairly".

3. Through French into English (11th–14th c.). With the Norman Conquest, Anglo-Norman and Old French juste entered English alongside the new legal order: just appears in Middle English by the early 14th century, both as law-term and as moral adjective. The derived adverb justly follows within about a generation (c. 1300), and Chaucer uses it in both senses — of lawful action and of exact, precise correspondence. As English law matured, justly became the workhorse adverb of the courtroom and the moral treatise.

4. The critical age: justly as the critic’s seal. From the 18th century onward, justly increasingly characterises judgements rather than deeds. Johnson, Addison, and the reviewers use justly celebrated, justly censured; the adverb signals that the verdict is proportionate — the very gesture of fair-minded criticism. Matthew Arnold’s and Walter Pater’s prose inherits this habit: to say an aim "has been justly said" is to honour it while standing above it. By the 19th century the "exact, precise" sense (justly so) begins to retreat, its work taken over by the clipped adverb just.

  • Semantic summary: action-fairness (≠ law) → judgement-fairness (≠ deservedness) → precision (now archaic).
  • Key witnesses: Plautus (iūstus), Cicero (iustitia), Chaucer (žyūstelīche/of justice), Johnson’s Dictionary definitions, Arnold’s and Pater’s critical prose, modern legal drafting ("justly entitled").
  • Modern standing: formal written English; in speech rightly and fairly dominate; precision sense obsolete except in fixed phrases.

Today justly is a survivor of the moral-legal vocabulary: precise, dignified, and slightly weighty, it still names the exact point where law, ethics, and deservedness coincide.
