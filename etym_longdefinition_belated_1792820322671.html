Belated describes what comes too late for its moment: the birthday card that arrives on Tuesday for a Saturday birthday, the apology offered years after the offence, the rain that falls on the harvested field. Built as be- + late + -ed, it literally means made late, and it carries a built-in sigh — things should have been otherwise, and everyone knows it.

In the source sentence the host revives himself with a belated cup of tea: the tea round has been delayed by the chaos of unexpected visitors, locked doors, and battering-ram jokes, so the cup arrives later than comfort requires. The adjective does comic work here — tea delayed is a small tragedy in a hobbit household, and belated grants the cup the honours of a ship arriving after a storm. Notice too the tact: nobody's lateness is blamed; the cup itself is belated, as if time, not any person, had slipped.

Core meaning in detail: Belated always measures arrival against expectation. A thing is belated only relative to a timetable — spoken or silent — that it failed to keep: belated thanks, a belated rescue, a belated realisation. The word therefore implies a story: something hindered, something forgotten, something slower than hope. Unlike late, which can be neutral (the late train through no fault of yours), belated admits the failure openly and often apologetically — belated Happy Birthday confesses on the envelope.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Delayed past the proper or expected time (primary modern sense, from 17th c.): a belated apology, belated birthday wishes, a belated spring. The bridge from late is the prefix be-, which completes or intensifies: not merely late but overtaken by lateness.
  • Overtaken by night, benighted (original Shakespearean sense, c. 1600): the belated traveller groping home in the dark. The bridge is darkness as time's visible form: to be belated was to have the night catch you on the road — memorably in Lear's belated wanderers and in Milton's evening scenes.
  • Coming as an afterthought, tardily recognised (mental sense): a belated realisation, his belated suspicion that he was lost. The bridge internalises the timetable: the mind had its appointment with the truth and missed it, arriving panting after the fact.
  • Posthumous or end-of-life honours (formal extended sense): a belated pardon, belated recognition for the forgotten veteran. The bridge is justice delayed: the honour arrives, but its recipient waited decades, and the word keeps the reproach alive inside the praise.
  • Behind the season or schedule (natural and institutional sense): belated blossoms after a hard winter, a belated report laid before parliament. The bridge generalises the timetable to nature and bureaucracy alike — anything with a season can miss it.
  • Archaic — made late at night, lingering (obsolete fringe): belated peasants in 17th-century verse, kept late by drink or labour. The bridge is the benighted sense domesticated: night makes everything it touches belated.

Register and connotation: belated is gentle, rueful, and faintly literary — the apology-word par excellence. Where overdue scolds (an overdue bill) and tardy reports (a tardy pupil), belated smiles sheepishly and hopes to be forgiven; whole greeting-card aisles depend on that smile. It flatters the latecomer by dignifying delay with Latinate length: late is a fact, belated is a confession with charm. Tolkien's belated tea is the word at its cosiest — delay as domestic epic.
