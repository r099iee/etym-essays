• Custodian: Similar in being charged with the care of something; differs because custodian (from Latin custos, 'guard') stresses guarding against loss or theft, while curator makes care itself — cura — the essence of the office.
  • Keeper: Similar in holding custody of a collection; differs because keeper is an everyday word for physical custody, without the scholarly dignity and curatorial selectivity of the museum office.
  • Caretaker: Similar in being responsible for a place or things; differs because caretaker emphasises routine maintenance and physical upkeep, while curator implies knowledgeable, selective management of a collection.
  • Steward: Similar in managing what belongs to another; differs because steward implies administering resources on someone's behalf, with overtones of the estate and the table, whereas curator is specifically the keeper of a collection.
  • Guardian: Similar in being responsible for protection; differs because guardian implies legal or moral protection of persons or rights, while curator is the care of objects, collections and data.
  • Conservator: Similar in being professionally responsible for objects; differs because conservator specifically restores and preserves the physical integrity of artefacts, while curator manages, interprets and displays the collection.
  • Warden: Similar in being an official charged with oversight; differs because warden is institutional and disciplinary, of prisons, parks and colleges, while curator is scholarly and caring.
  • Overseer: Similar in supervising what is in one's charge; differs because overseer implies managerial supervision of work, with no connotation of care for objects, while curator implies devoted custody of a collection.
  • Librarian: Similar in caring for a collection; differs because librarian is confined to books and library services, while curator ranges across museums, galleries, zoos and digital content.
  • Archivist: Similar in preserving and organising materials; differs because archivist specialises in historical records and documents and their provenance, while curator manages a collection of objects or content for display and study.
  • Curate (the parish priest): Similar through the shared root cura, 'care'; differs because curate (from Medieval Latin curatus, 'entrusted with the care of souls') cares for a congregation, while curator cares for a collection — care of persons versus care of things.
  • Content manager / digital curator: Similar in selecting and ordering content; differs because the digital curator's object is a stream of information rather than physical objects, a 21st-century extension of the museum office.
