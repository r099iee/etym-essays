Splayed is the past tense and past participle of the verb splay, meaning “to spread out, extend apart, or flatten — most characteristically of limbs, hands, or fingers, which are forced outward and pressed down so that their flat surfaces come to rest against something.” In the literary context — “He seemed to be crouched right down with his flat hands splayed on the floor, and his head thrust out, nose almost to the stone” — the word describes a feral, quadrupedal posture: the hands are not merely placed but deliberately spread wide and pressed flat, fingers parted, the whole body braced upon them like a beast’s paws. The nuance is of breadth, flatness, and a creature’s close, crawling contact with the ground: splayed carries a faint grotesquerie, suggesting something animal, spider-like, or inhuman, and it heightens the menace of the figure that hugs the floor like a predator. It is a precise, tactile verb — we feel the spread fingers against the cold stone. The tone is descriptive, almost clinical, and quietly disturbing: the posture it names is exaggeratedly open, palms exposed, the body spread out upon the earth instead of standing upright upon it. The verb’s history begins in Old French despleier “to unfold, spread out,” and its root sense is “to unfold from a folded state, to spread open.” Modern splay is an aphetic form — the unstressed initial syllable de- of display was dropped in late Middle English, so that splay and display are historical doublets, two descendants of the same French verb that diverged in English. Over the centuries the word specialised in three directions: outward-spreading limbs and fingers, the architectural widening of openings, and the giving-way of structures. Core senses and usages:
  • Limbs and fingers spread wide and flat (the primary modern sense): splayed hands, splayed feet, splayed legs — the extremities forced outward and pressed down or apart, as of a body braced on all fours or fallen with limbs flung wide (“he lay on the grass with arms and legs splayed out”).
  • Architectural term (technical jargon): in building, a splay is the outward sloping or widening of a wall opening, window jamb, or embrasure, cut at an angle so the opening flares outward and admits more light; splayed walls, splayed windows, splayed reveals — the word entered the mason’s lexicon in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
  • Anatomical and medical use: splayfoot (a flat, turned-out foot) and splay-legged describe limbs whose joints or stance turn outward; orthopaedic writing uses the adjective of congenital or acquired deformities of stance.
  • Structural failure (engineering sense): of timbers, beams, or buildings giving way — “the hut’s timbers splayed outward” — the frame spreading apart and collapsing outward rather than inward.
  • Heraldic and display use: an eagle “splayed” or “displayed” in heraldry has its wings spread wide — the oldest unfolding sense preserved as a technical term.
  • Transitive verb: to splay = to cause to spread apart or flatten (“the weight of the load splayed the cart’s wheels”); the participle splayed as an adjective describes the resulting state.
  • Figurative extensions: anything spread wide and flat — a splayed book, splayed papers, a hand splayed in warning — keeps the core image of opening outward and lying flat.
Polysemy and historical bridge: all the modern senses descend from the single French verb despleier “to unfold,” and the bridge between them is the act of opening something folded into wide extension. When English dropped the de- of display (aphesis), the resulting splay specialised in the literal, physical half of the family’s meaning — the actual spreading of wings, limbs, and masonry — while display kept the figurative half, “to show, exhibit.” The architectural sense of angled widening, the anatomical sense of turned-out limbs, and the structural sense of collapsing timbers are three applications of the same image: things forced apart and flattened, opening outward from their folded or upright state. In the passage, the participle’s descriptive flatness — the hands splayed on the floor, nose almost to the stone — makes the creature’s posture at once animal and architectural, as if the body itself were an opening flared outward against the ground, and the word’s heraldic and structural histories quietly reinforce the menace. Among near-synonyms, spread is the broad, neutral term for any extension over an area, with no implication of flattening or of contact with a surface; outstretched suggests a limb deliberately reaching toward something, with direction and intention; extended is the colourless general word; fanned implies an ordered radial arrangement, as of fingers or cards; and sprawled describes the whole body fallen or collapsed in disorder, not a deliberate posture of the hands. Splayed alone combines wide extension, flatness against a surface, and a faint animal or structural grotesquerie — the body opened and pressed down like a spread paw or a flared jamb — which is precisely the posture Tolkien gives the crouching figure at the goblin-guarded door.
