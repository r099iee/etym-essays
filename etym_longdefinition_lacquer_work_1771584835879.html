LACQUER-WORK is a compound noun that names, first and foremost, objects—or the finished decorative surface of objects—coated in lacquer: a hard, lustrous, resinous varnish applied in many thin layers, each coat dried and polished before the next, so that the final surface is deep, mirror-smooth, and semi-translucent. In the sentence from The Story of the Stone, a “low rose-shaped table of coloured lacquer-work” stands at each side of the room, and the term does exact atmospheric work: lacquer-work is the signature luxury of the East Asian art of interiors, an object produced by slow, repeated, almost monastic labour—dozens of coats, weeks or months of curing, fine abrasive polishing—and it therefore signals time as well as wealth. The qualifier “coloured” is not idle: lacquer can be pigmented (the classic cinnabar red, black, gold, or green), and a “coloured” piece announces a polychrome, jewel-like finish rather than a plain clear varnish. In a single phrase the table is established as art, not furniture: a lustrous, almost breathing surface of accumulated craft. Polysemy and the full range of senses:
  • Objects coated with lacquer as art or decoration—the primary sense on this card: lacquer boxes, trays, cabinets, screens, and ornamental wares whose decorated surface is the point.
  • The craft or technique itself—lacquer-work names the process: preparing the base, applying coat after coat, curing, and polishing; a discipline as much as a product.
  • The finished surface or finish—the deep, glossy, semi-translucent film of dried resin considered as a material quality, as in “the lacquer-work of the cabinet glowed”.
  • East Asian tree-sap lacquer—by extension from the original insect-resin lacquer of India, the word also covers the Chinese and Japanese craft made from the sap of the lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), cured in humid air to a rock-hard film; this is the sense behind most “Chinese lacquer-work”.
  • European imitation: japanning and French polish—in the 18th century, shellac-based “japanning” mimicked Asian lacquer on European wood and metal, so European lacquer-work often refers to these baked, varnished wares rather than to the genuine Eastern craft.
  • Modern extensions of the root word—hair lacquer (hair spray), nail lacquer, and automotive lacquer all descend from the same word, though lacquer-work itself stays tied to objects and craft rather than to these industrial products.
The semantic bridges that connect these senses are instructive. The word travels from a specific substance (the resin of the lac insect, Kerria lacca) to the coating made from it, to the objects covered by the coating, to the craft of making them, and finally to any glossy layered finish that resembles the original. “Lacquer” itself must be kept distinct from “varnish” (the generic clear coating laid on in a coat or two for protection and sheen), from “enamel” (pigmented glass fused to metal), from “glazing” (a thin transparent coat over paint), and from “gilding” (the application of gold leaf). The compound “-work” here functions like the second element of “needlework”, “woodwork”, or “stonework”: it makes the noun name both the activity of making and the thing made, which is why lacquer-work can be at once a craft, a set of objects, and a surface quality. Register, tone, and metaphor: the term is opulent, connoisseurial, and faintly exotic, and it carries the perfume of the old trade routes that moved such objects across Asia—the word itself passed from Sanskrit through Persian, Arabic, and Portuguese into English. Its metaphors run to “mirror-smooth”, “jewel-like”, “sealed”, and “accumulated craft”; to call something “lacquered” is to say it has been polished to a gloss, superficially perfected, its true base concealed beneath many shining coats. In modern usage the word is traditional and rather bookish, evoking museum cases and cabinets of curiosities, but it remains the precise term for the greatest decorative finish of the East Asian interior.
