• Mournful: Similar because both name sorrow-laden affect and are often interchangeable in loose prose; differs because mournful readily covers deep bereavement, funeral gravity, and heavy grief without implying a melodic or pleading contour, while plaintive more strongly suggests an audible, almost musical appeal — a cry that petitions the listener rather than mere weight of grief.
  • Melancholy / melancholic: Similar as words of sadness shading into art and mood; differs because melancholy names a reflective, inward, lingering sadness (and melancholic a temperament or prolonged mood), whereas plaintive typically qualifies a particular sound or utterance that actively laments. One is melancholic by disposition; a single note is plaintive.
  • Wistful: Similar in gentle sadness and frequent collocation with looks, tones, and memories; differs because wistful centres nostalgic yearning and bittersweet desire for what is past or absent, not an active lament. A wistful smile need not complain; a plaintive cry does. Wistful is inward and reflective; plaintive is expressive and outward.
  • Doleful: Similar as a somewhat literary marker of sadness in face, voice, or scene; differs because doleful stresses gloom, dreariness, and cheerlessness — sometimes with a faintly archaic overtone — whereas plaintive keeps pathos and lyrical appeal. Doleful weather versus a plaintive melody shows the boundary: atmosphere of misery versus shaped lament.
  • Lugubrious: Similar as elevated vocabulary for an exaggerated sorrowful manner; differs because lugubrious implies overdone, theatrical gloom, often mildly pejorative, whereas plaintive is usually sympathetic and delicate. Calling a speech lugubrious mocks it; calling a melody plaintive praises its pathos.
  • Pathetic: Similar as a word for sorrow that moves pity and historically shared the field of affecting grief; differs because pathetic now primarily means 'arousing pity for helplessness' or colloquially 'pitifully bad', while plaintive keeps the dignity of a lament and carries no pejorative force.
  • Querulous: Similar as a word for complaining expression; differs because querulous implies peevish, discontented, nagging complaint, whereas plaintive implies sorrowful entreaty without annoyance. A plaintive plea entreats; a querulous complaint carps.
  • Plangent: Similar as a rare, high-register word for a sound of sorrow (and, at root, the same Latin family as plaintive); differs because plangent stresses the loud, resounding, beating quality of grief (the Latin sense of beating the breast) and is far more literary and rare, whereas plaintive is the standard word for a soft, pleading, mournful sound.
