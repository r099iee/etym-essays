Hail (noun / verb; in context, capitalized as a personified proper noun) — Precipitation in the form of pellets, balls, or irregular lumps of hard, compacted ice and snow (hailstones) produced within the violent updrafts of cumulonimbus storm clouds. In Oscar Wilde’s fairy tale The Selfish Giant, Hail is personified alongside the North Wind, the Snow, and the Frost as an uninvited, boisterous guest who dresses in grey and rattles furiously upon the castle slates for three hours each day, embodying the relentless, punishing destructiveness of winter in the Giant’s selfish garden.

Primary meteorological sense — frozen precipitation. Scientifically, hail forms when strong convective updrafts in severe thunderstorms carry raindrops upward into extremely cold atmospheric layers below freezing (often above 10,000 to 20,000 feet). The supercooled water freezes upon contact with condensation nuclei (such as dust or ice crystals). Repeated cycles of ascending in updrafts and descending accumulate concentric layers of clear and opaque ice before the heavy stone falls under gravity. Hail is distinguished from sleet (ice pellets) by its larger diameter (at least 5 mm / 0.2 inches up to several inches), layered internal structure, and exclusive generation within cumulonimbus clouds during warm-season convection.

Figurative and metaphorical extensions:

  • A barrage or overwhelming shower of projectiles — By vivid visual and acoustic analogy to falling ice stones, a hail of denotes a rapid, dense, and dangerous succession of incoming missiles, blows, or gunfire: "a hail of bullets", "a hail of arrows", "a hail of stones".
  • A torrent of verbal criticism or inquiries — Figuratively applied to intense intellectual, legal, or journalistic pressure: "a hail of questions from the press", "a hail of abuse", "a hail of criticism".
  • Meteorological verb (impersonal) — To precipitate hailstones: "it hailed for twenty minutes", "it began to hail heavily during the storm".
  • Transitive and intransitive action verb — To shower, pelt, or rain down rapidly and forcefully like hailstones: "debris hailed down upon the pavement", "the enemy hailed shells upon the fortress".

The homographic greeting and acclamation (Historical Bridge and Distinction). In Modern English, the meteorological word hail is orthographically and phonetically identical to the verb and interjection hail (meaning to salute, greet, acclaim, or summon, as in "hail Caesar", "to hail a taxi", "critics hailed the film as a masterpiece", or "to hail from a place"). Historically, these represent two entirely distinct etymological words that coalesced in Middle English:

  • The weather word descends from Old English hægl (Proto-Germanic *haglaz, "frozen hail").
  • The greeting word descends from the Old Norse adjective heill ("healthy, whole, prosperous, of good omen"), borrowed via the greeting formula ves heill ("be healthy!"), which yielded Middle English wæs hæil (wassail) and the salutation hail!. Over centuries of shared pronunciation, speakers established conceptual bridges linking the vigorous exclamation of greeting to energy and arrival, but their genealogical origins remain completely separate.

Literary and symbolic significance. Throughout world literature and mythology, hail represents divine wrath, atmospheric violence, and agricultural ruin (as in the Seventh Plague of Egypt in Exodus 9). In Wilde’s allegory, Hail’s relentless pounding upon the castle roof translates meteorological fury into psychological externalization: the frozen, unyielding state of the Giant’s ungenerous heart.
