The word foothill is a compound made in America out of two very old Germanic words, and its story is really two stories — one about foot, one about hill — that finally converge in the nineteenth century. The first half descends from Proto-Indo-European *ped- / *pod-, "foot," a root of tremendous productivity across the family: Sanskrit pada, Greek pous (stem pod-, source of tripod and podiatry), Latin pes (stem ped-, source of pedal and pedestrian). In the Germanic branch, Grimm's Law turned the initial *p into *f, giving Proto-Germanic *fots and Old English fot, modern English foot. Crucially, this is not merely an anatomical word: from antiquity speakers of many Indo-European languages used "foot" for the lower end or base of a mountain — Latin pes montis, Greek podos orous, Sanskrit pada — an anthropomorphic transfer that pictures a mountain range as a giant body standing on feet. Old English fot had exactly this extended sense, "the bottom or base of something," so the mountain sense of "foot" is many centuries older than the compound that uses it. The second half descends from Proto-Indo-European *kel-, "to rise, project, tower," which likewise scattered across the family: Latin collis "hill," culmen "summit" (source of culminate), columna "pillar" (source of column), and — in the Germanic branch, again by Grimm's Law (k to h) — Proto-Germanic *hulliz and Old English hyll, modern English hill. So by the time English existed, both halves of the future compound were already in place, each carrying an ancient landscape-as-body metaphor. The compound itself is young:
  • First recorded in American English in the 1850s (c. 1856), as surveyors, settlers, and journalists of the westward expansion needed a precise word for the low hills standing at the foot of a mountain range.
  • Its rapid success tells a social story: the great topographic surveys and the flow of emigrants into the Rockies, Sierras, and Cascades made the geographic vocabulary of the American West urgent, and foothill answered the need with perfect transparency.
  • By the late 19th and 20th centuries it is a settled standard of geography and travel writing, and its plural — the foothills — becomes the normal form, naming the whole golden belt of low, rolling, often farmable or vine-covered hills before a range.
In Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men the singular form is used attributively — "the golden foothill slopes curve up to the strong and rocky Gabilan mountains" — a characteristically precise deployment of the term to set the Salinas Valley against the Gabilans. Finally, the word has grown a modern figurative branch: "the foothills of a career" or "of a recovery" means the early, preliminary, beginner stages of something large — you are at the foot of a mountain you have not yet begun to climb. Anthropomorphic foot, ancient hill, American compound, figurative foothills: the word is a small museum of how landscape vocabulary is made and reused.
