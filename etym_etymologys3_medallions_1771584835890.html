The biography of medallion is really two biographies, and keeping them apart is essential to accuracy. The name itself does not come from any word for metal; it descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *medhyo-, "middle", one of the great spatial roots of the family, which also produced Sanskrit madhya, Greek mesos, and Latin medius. From medius, "middle, mid", Latin built a vast English constellation - medium, median, mediate, immediate, Mediterranean, "the middle of the earth", and meridian, "midday". But the branch that matters here is a small one: within Late Latin the adjective produced medalia, a small coin "worth half a denarius", literally something cut in the middle or of middle value. This half-coin passed into Italian as medaglia, where it broadened from "half-coin" to "coin" generally, and then to the specific struck commemorative piece we call a medal.
  • From coin to medal: in the Renaissance, the struck portrait medal - the invention of Antonio Pisanello and the humanist courts of Italy - transformed the old currency piece into an art object, a round bronze or gold relief celebrating a prince or a scholar. The Italian augmentative suffix -one enlarged medaglia into medaglione, "a large medal", and the word acquired its glamour: a medallion, unlike a coin, was a thing of art.
  • Into French and English: French borrowed medaglione as medaillon, and English took the word in 1658, the year of the earliest OED record. At first it meant simply "a large medal" - the honour enlarged.
  • The decorative shift: because medals always carry a framed portrait or emblem on their face, the word's next move was from numismatics into the decorative arts. A "medallion" came to mean any round or oval framed motif containing a design - carved, moulded in plaster, woven, embroidered, painted, or printed - and repeated across a ceiling, a carpet, a textile, or a building facade. The framed emblem of a prince became the framed emblem of a dragon on a bolster; the word travelled from the Renaissance medal to the drawing-room pattern without changing its essential shape.
  • The kitchen: by the nineteenth century the shape-principle reached cookery, where a "medallion" of meat is a round cut sliced across the grain into a coin-like disc - the coin idea preserved in the dining room.
  • The modern spread: today the word is at home in architecture (ceiling and wall medallions), in fashion and jewellery (medallion pendants and necklaces), in heraldry and stamp-collecting, and in descriptive prose of any refinement.
Socio-culturally, the medallion's history is the history of framing as a claim to significance. To enclose a symbol in a roundel is to announce that the symbol matters, that it deserves its own bounded field; and because the first medallions were medals of princes, the framed emblem carried a residue of honour and hierarchy wherever it went. That is why Hawkes's "medallions in the form of tiny dragons" is so telling a choice: it lets a Chinese household's bolster claim the same framed, emblematic dignity as a Renaissance court's portrait medal. The dragon inside the medallion is imperial; the medallion that frames it is cosmopolitan. The false friend metallon completes the picture by contrast. Greek metallon, "a mine, a quarry, a metal", is probably a pre-Greek substrate word of the Aegean world, with no secure Indo-European ancestry - a reminder that Greek absorbed an older world of miners and smiths. Latin borrowed metallum, Old French metal gave Middle English metal, and modern English metal, metallic, and metallurgy all descend from it. Because medals are made of metal, the two families have always been pulled together by folk etymology; but historically they are unrelated, and medallion owes nothing to metallon. The word on this card is, in the end, a monument to the Indo-European habit of making meaning out of spatial position: a coin "of the middle", enlarged, framed, and set to decorate the world.
