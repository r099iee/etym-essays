- Late Middle English - slop begins as a word of wetness and mess: soft mud, slush, spilled or stale liquid, the soggy refuse of the table.
- 16th–17th c. - the plural slops settles into the household as the name for liquid waste and waste water - dishwater, the soiled wash of kitchen and sickroom - carried out by servants, with no euphemism attached.
- 17th–18th c. - two homographs diverge: at the sickbed slops are the basin-water and fouled waste emptied from beside the patient, while among sailors 'slops' come to mean the loose, ready-made breeches and garments of the navy's stores.
- 18th–19th c. - in domestic and nursing writing the word keeps its flat, physical register; the plural marks the daily, unending recurrence of intake and evacuation round a convalescent's bed - food enters, waste leaves, week after week.
- 20th–21st c. - institutional terms (sewage, wastewater) push it to the margins of public speech; slops survives as a deliberately blunt, literary and dialect word - and as the thin liquid food of invalids - so that choosing it today, as this author does, is a choice of unglamorous honesty.
