Phalanx is, in its founding sense, a dense rectangular formation of heavily armed infantry advancing shoulder to shoulder with shields locked and spears levelled — the battle-tactic that made Macedon and the Greek city-states masters of the ancient battlefield.

In the source sentence Tolkien borrows the military word for poetry: Gandalf's fireworks become ‘a phalanx of flying swans’ — the birds climbing in a disciplined, serried flock, rank on rank, exactly as hoplites once marched. The metaphor works because a phalanx means not just a crowd but an ordered crowd, moving as one body.

Core meaning in detail: The classical phalanx (Greek phálanx) packed men eight or more ranks deep, each shield guarding his neighbour's flank, long spears (sarissae in Macedon, up to 6 metres) projecting in a hedge of points. Its power was collective and psychological: no individual heroics, just an irresistible moving wall. The plural is phalanxes or, classically, phalanges — the latter now reserved mostly for the anatomical sense.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Dense infantry formation (primary historical sense, from 1550s in English): the Greek and Macedonian battle order; by extension any massed body of troops in close order.
  • A united, formidable group of people (figurative): ‘a phalanx of lawyers’, ‘a phalanx of supporters’. The bridge is the formation's solidarity — individuals fused into one disciplined front, whether with spears or subpoenas.
  • A compact flock or array in motion (poetic, as in Tolkien): ‘a phalanx of flying swans’. The bridge is visual and kinetic: birds in tight formation look and move like a marching block of men.
  • Finger and toe bones (anatomy, plural phalanges, 17th c.): each finger has three phalanges, each thumb two. The bridge is the rank-like row: the small bones stand in line like soldiers, and the Greek word already meant ‘log, roller, round piece of wood’ — short cylindrical things in a row.
  • A utopian communal association (Fourierist, 19th c.): Charles Fourier's phalanstère communities were called phalanxes. The bridge is the disciplined collective: workers marshalled into a harmonious social formation, battle-order turned to agriculture and craft.

Register and connotation: phalanx is learned and weighty — history books, editorials, anatomy labs. It flatters the group it names with discipline and strength (‘a phalanx of admirers’) but can menace (‘a phalanx of riot police’). The military and anatomical senses live side by side without confusion, distinguished by context and by plural (phalanxes of soldiers, phalanges of fingers).

English never found a native synonym with the same punch: ‘block’, ‘mass’, and ‘throng’ all lack the drilled, shoulder-locked menace that makes phalanx the headline writer's favourite for any disciplined crowd.
