The rare and delightfully eccentric English colloquial word beroofen (most commonly encountered in the full idiomatic exclamation oom beroofen or unberufen) represents an anglicized phonetic borrowing of an ancient Germanic and Yiddish apotropaic charm. Its ultimate etymological foundation resides in the Proto-Indo-European onomatopoeic verbal root *krohb- or *ker-, signifying "to cry out, scream, call aloud, or crow." In the Germanic branch, this root developed into Proto-Germanic *hrōpaną ("to shout, call, cry out", the source of Dutch roepen, German rufen, and Old English hrōpan, which gave archaic English roop / roup, meaning to shout or cry hoarsely).

In Old High German, combining the intensive prefix *bi- with *hrōpaną yielded biruofan ("to call upon, appeal to, shout at"). By the Middle High German period (c. 1050–1350), the verb beruofen developed a dark, superstitious folk connotation: it came to mean not merely shouting, but "to bewitch, charm, or attract malevolent supernatural attention to someone by calling out their name or praising their virtues aloud." In medieval European folklore, speaking too highly or openly of one's good health, beautiful children, or bountiful harvest was believed to alert envious demons, witches, or the "evil eye" (German böser Blick, Yiddish ayin hara), inviting immediate disaster.

To neutralize this superstitious peril, speakers in the German-speaking lands and Ashkenazi Jewish communities across Central and Eastern Europe developed the apotropaic formula unberufen (or Middle High German unberoufen, literally "uncalled-for, unsummoned, unbidden"). Whenever someone mentioned good fortune (e.g., "the child hasn't had a fever all winter"), they would immediately spit three times or add "unberufen!"—signifying "may no evil spirit consider itself summoned or called upon by my boast!" In Yiddish, this was pronounced um berufn or unberufen and became an indispensable daily phrase.

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as Jewish immigration from Central and Eastern Europe enriched British cultural and linguistic life in London, several colorful Yiddish and German expressions entered upper-class and theatrical British slang. In the mouths of English speakers who did not know German grammar, the prefix un- or the Yiddish preposition um was phonetically corrupted into oom, and the past participle berufen was rendered phonetically as beroofen, giving rise to the comic aristocratic catchphrase oom beroofen (functioning exactly like "touch wood" or "absit omen").

The preservation and fame of beroofen in English literature is almost entirely credited to the master humorist P.G. Wodehouse (1881–1975):

  • Wodehousian Comic Masterpiece: In novels such as Right Ho, Jeeves, The Code of the Woosters, and various short stories, Bertie Wooster, his formidable Aunt Dahlia (as in the source context: "‘Oom beroofen,’ said Aunt Dahlia"), and other aristocratic characters routinely utter "oom beroofen" whenever they feel they have incautiously tempted destiny by acknowledging that things are going well.
  • Social Satire & Register: Wodehouse used the phrase to brilliant comic effect, contrasting the grand, superstitious gravity of an ancient Continental demonological charm with the trivial domestic crises of the English upper classes—such as winning a bet on a horse race, securing a prized French chef, or avoiding marriage to an overbearing debutante.

Thus, beroofen stands as a fascinating linguistic artifact—an ancient Germanic incantation transformed through Yiddish folklore and British comedic genius into a whimsical charm against tempting fate.
