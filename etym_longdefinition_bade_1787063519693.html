Bade is the past tense of the verb bid, used in the senses “to command”, “to say (a greeting or farewell)”, and (archaically) “to ask, entreat”. It is pronounced /beɪd/ (rhyming with “made”) in traditional RP, or /bæd/ (rhyming with “bad”) in the modern common pronunciation. In the source context from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, “the Master and his councillors bade them farewell” — that is, they spoke a farewell to the departing company, formally bidding them goodbye. The word is now literary and old-fashioned, surviving mainly in narrative prose and in the fixed formulas “bade farewell”, “bade good night”, “bade welcome”, and “bade him do such-and-such”.

Primary senses

  • Commanded; ordered. The oldest and weightiest sense: “he bade them stay” = he commanded them to stay. This use is now distinctly literary or archaic; everyday English says “told them to stay” or “ordered them to stay”.
  • Bade farewell / good night / welcome. The living survival: “bade them farewell” = “said farewell to them”. Here “bade” frames the act of speaking the greeting or farewell; it is the verb of address, not the greeting itself. This is the sense in the Tolkien sentence.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Asked; entreated. An archaic sense inherited from Old English biddan “to ask, pray”: “he bade him for mercy”. This sense is essentially extinct in modern English except in historical or biblical pastiche.
  • Announced; proclaimed. Through the conflation with Old English bēodan “to announce, proclaim, offer”, “bid” and its past “bade” could carry the sense of announcing or offering. Today the surviving reflex is the auction sense of “bid” (offer a price) — but there the past tense is normally “bid”, not “bade”.
  • Invited. In older English “bade to a feast” meant “invited to a feast”; the word still carries a faint invitation sense in “bidden guest” and in the formula “bade them to the wedding”.

Grammar and forms

  • Bade is a strong (irregular) past tense. The full paradigm of modern “bid” is bid – bade (or bid) – bidden (past participle). The participle “bidden” survives mainly in “bidden guest” and in the sense “commanded”.
  • Two pronunciations coexist: the traditional /beɪd/ and the levelled /bæd/. Both are acceptable in RP; /beɪd/ is the older, more conservative form preferred in British dictionaries, while /bæd/ is very common in American and now widespread.
  • Because “bid” now means primarily “to offer a price” in auctions, “bade” can sound odd or even wrong to modern speakers in that context; the auction past is normally “bid”. “Bade” is reserved for the command and greeting senses.
  • It is always transitive in the command and greeting senses, with an object and often a following infinitive: “bade them (to) depart”, “bade her good morning”.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge

The history of “bade” is really the history of a merger. Old English had two verbs: biddan “to ask, pray, entreat; to command” (past bæd, participle beden) and bēodan “to offer, proclaim, announce, command” (past bēad, participle boden). During Middle English the two paradigms collapsed into a single verb “bid”, and the modern irregular past “bade” continues Old English bæd, the past of biddan. The greeting sense (“bade farewell”) is a semantic fossil of the old “speak, utter” and “announce” senses: one “bids” a farewell just as one “bids” a command — the verb meaning “to say by way of command, greeting, or announcement”. Once the ordinary sense “ask/tell” was taken over by other verbs, “bade” retreated into formula and literature, where it now signals solemnity, ceremony, and a deliberate, old-fashioned dignity — exactly the tone Tolkien wants for the formal farewell of the Master of Lake-town.

Register and stylistic nuance

  • In contemporary English, “bade” is perceived as poetic, biblical, or historical; it is rare in speech and uncommon even in most modern prose.
  • It flourishes in fantasy and historical fiction precisely because it is archaic: it instantly dates the register of a farewell and lends it weight. Tolkien's use in The Hobbit is a deliberate archaism of this kind.
  • Fixed formulas (“bade farewell”, “bade good night”, “bade welcome”) keep the word alive far more than free syntax does.
