SLEEPETH is the archaic third-person singular present-indicative form of the verb sleep: the exact equivalent of modern 'he/she/it sleeps.' It is built from the stem sleep- plus the Early Modern English ending -eth, one of the two competing third-person singular endings of the period (the other, -s, ultimately won in every spoken register). For southern speakers of Middle and Early Modern English, 'sleepeth' was an unmarked, everyday sentence-form; for speakers today it is a deliberate archaism with a strongly biblical, poetic, or mock-heroic flavour.

1. Grammatical identity.

  • Function: present indicative, third person singular. The full paradigm in Early Modern English ran I sleep / thou sleepest / he sleepeth (he sleeps) / we sleep / ye sleep / they sleep. The -eth form was never a plural, imperative or subjunctive marker; it was strictly the 'he-she-it' present ending.
  • Phonology: pronounced /ˈsliːpɪθ/ in the traditional reading pronunciation of scripture and hymnody, with the unstressed final syllable often reduced to /əθ/; the final -th is voiceless, unlike the /s/ of modern 'sleeps.' In verse the extra syllable was prosodically valuable: 'sleepeth' supplied a convenient weak (feminine) rhyme and an unstressed ending where 'sleeps' would not scan.
  • Register: neutral in the sixteenth century; sacred or deliberately antique thereafter. It survives in the King James Bible, hymnals, epitaphs ('here sleepeth…'), historical fiction, fantasy prose, and parody, where it signals pompous solemnity.

2. The full semantic field of 'sleep' carried by the form. Because SLEEPETH is only the inflected shell, all the polysemy of the verb sleep attaches to it:

  • Primary — natural rest: the periodic state of unconsciousness and reduced metabolism that restores the body: 'the wanderer sleepeth by the road' = he is resting in sleep.
  • Dormancy and latent power (metaphor): a sleeping volcano, a sleeping giant, a sleeping partner, Sleeping Beauty. The bridge: suspended activity that can abruptly awaken.
  • Death (euphemism): 'he sleepeth' = he has died, especially in Christian idiom, where the dead 'sleep in Christ' until resurrection. The bridge is the oldest of metaphors — death as the long, still rest from which God alone wakes us. This is the sense canonized in John 11:11 (KJV): 'Our friend Lazarus sleepeth,' which the disciples initially misunderstand as ordinary sleep.
  • Numbness (medical and vernacular): a limb 'goes to sleep' when compressed nerves cut sensation; the bridge is the temporary paralysis of sleep.
  • Sexual euphemism (archaic): 'to sleep with' someone — the shared bed standing for intercourse, common in the translation prose of the period.
  • Inattention and negligence: to 'sleep on' a matter, or modern slang 'don't sleep on X' (do not underestimate): sleep as a failure of vigilance.
  • Static attributive uses of the participle: sleeping car, sleeping bag, sleeping sickness, sleeping policeman (British for speed bump) — items defined by their relation to sleep.

3. The family of forms around it. The verb's core (Old English slǣpan, a strong verb of class VII in its old phase, later tending to weak inflection) generated a tight set: sleep, sleeps, sleepeth, sleeping, slept, sleepest. Middle English preserved the southern -eth ending beside the northern -es; Chaucer's London English still used -eth freely, and the Great Vowel Shift, which moved the vowel of sleep to /iː/, left the ending phonologically untouched.

4. Why the form survives. The King James Bible (1611) adopted the conservative southern -eth system wholesale, freezing forms like sleepeth, saith, goeth, cometh in the most reprinted book in English history. Every later generation has met 'sleepeth' first in scripture, which is why it still reads as hallowed rather than merely old. In the twentieth century, fantasy prose (Tolkien and his heirs) deliberately imitated the KJV register to give invented elder cultures a sacred, antique voice, keeping 'sleepeth' in living literary use.

5. Practical nuance. 'Sleepeth' is almost never used of animals or machines in modern writing except for comic effect; its natural homes are theology, epitaphs, verse and pastiche. Using it of a person outside those contexts is either poetic elevation or deliberate wit — never an accident a writer makes twice.
