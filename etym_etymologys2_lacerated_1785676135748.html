LACERATED descends from Latin, in a chain that is well attested from Classical Latin onward; only the deepest, pre-Latin stage is debated. The chain: 1) (Pre-Latin origin, debated) — Latin lacer "torn, mangled, rent to pieces" (adjective, attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE; the family's root form). Its origin beyond Latin is not securely known: some scholars connect it with Greek lákos "tattered garment, rag" and lakeîn "to burst, crack," pointing to a PIE root *lek- / *(s)leh₂g- "to tear, split"; others treat lacer as an isolated Latin adjective of unknown prehistory. See the note below. 2) Latin lacerāre "to tear to pieces, mangle, rend, lacerate" (verb, attested from the 1st c. BCE; a regular denominative verb built on lacer; used of torn flesh, torn garments, and torn bodies) — the direct source of the English verb. 3) Latin lacerātus "torn, mangled" (past participle of lacerāre, attested) — the participle on which the English word is formed. 4) English lacerate (verb) "to tear roughly, mangle, rend jaggedly" (borrowed from the Latin participle stem lacerāt-, late 16th c.); lacerated (past tense and past participle, and adjective "torn with jagged edges") — LACERATED. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The Latin chain lacer → lacerāre → lacerātus → English lacerate is secure and fully attested. (2) The deeper origin of lacer is the only contested point. The connection with Greek lákos "tattered garment, rag" and lakeîn "to burst, crack" is widely repeated and plausible, but the correspondence is not established with certainty, and no universally accepted PIE etymon exists; the standard dictionaries therefore present the pre-Latin origin as unknown or uncertain rather than fabricating a root. (3) The English borrowing date (late 16th century) is the standard first-attestation date for the verb; the participle lacerated is attested in English from the early 17th century.
