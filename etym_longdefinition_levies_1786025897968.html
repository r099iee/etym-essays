LEVIES (singular levy) is a plural noun naming bodies of conscripted troops, and the taxes, duties, or compulsory contributions raised by a government, sovereign, or other authority. The word's two great senses — soldiers and taxes — share a single root image: the raising of something from below. To levy troops is to raise men from their homes and fields into an army; to levy a tax is to raise money from a population into a treasury. In the source passage, "and added their levies to Ser Criston Cole's host" uses the military sense: raw, newly raised troops, called up under a lord's banner and thrown into an army. In feudal and military history levies were precisely this — hastily assembled, locally raised forces of peasants and knights summoned by obligation, the opposite of a standing professional army.

The word descends from Latin levāre, "to raise, lift, lighten," via Old French levée, the past participle of lever, "to raise" (the same verb that gives English lever and levitate). Latin levāre is itself a verb formed on levis, "light in weight," from the Indo-European root *legʷʰ-, "light, not heavy" — the same root as English light, lung, lever, and alleviate. The noun reached English through Anglo-Norman administration after the Norman Conquest and was firmly established in English law and military practice by the 15th century.

Primary and Secondary Senses

  • 1. Conscripted troops, feudal forces (the military sense). Soldiers raised by compulsory enlistment, feudal obligation, or emergency call-up: "the baron mustered his levies." Historically these were peasant militia and regional men-at-arms, distinguished from professional standing armies.
  • 2. Taxes, duties, and compulsory payments (the fiscal sense). A sum of money imposed by authority on people, property, income, imports, or transactions: "a levy on luxury goods." The plural levies is common for multiple or repeated assessments.
  • 3. The act of raising either (the process sense). As an uncountable or singular noun, levy can name the act itself — the levying of troops or of a tax — as in the legal phrase the levy of war.
  • 4. A formal imposition or seizure (the legal sense). In legal usage, a levy is a legal seizure of property to satisfy a judgment — the sheriff levies on the debtor's goods. Here the "raising" is the raising of a legal claim into execution.
  • 5. Extended and modern senses. The verb levy is used for any systematic raising of resources — levy a fine, levy an embargo — and figuratively for mustering effort: levy one's strength.

Semantic Bridges

The two great senses were born together in medieval practice, because the same verb, lever, "to raise," named both raising soldiers from the land and raising money from the people. Old French levée was literally "a raising," used for the summoning of vassals and the collection of feudal dues alike. English inherited the double meaning intact, and for centuries the single word levy covered the whole economy of war: the lord raised (levied) men and raised (levied) the money to feed them. The military sense has become historical and literary in modern English — we read of levies in histories and fantasy rather than in news reports — while the fiscal sense has flourished and broadened, so that a modern reader encountering levies most often thinks of taxes, tariffs, and assessments. The semantic drift is a fine example of how one concrete image (the raising of a weight) can underwrite two entirely different institutions.

Register and Tone

  • Register: Formal and institutional. The military sense is historical, literary, or fantasy-register; the fiscal sense is administrative, legal, and journalistic.
  • Collocations: raise levies, fresh levies, county levies, local levies (military); tax levy, special levy, levy on imports, levy a fine (fiscal).
  • Distinctions: Levies are compulsory and governmental — unlike recruits (volunteers), tolls (use-charges), or charges (fees for services).
  • Nominal forms: The singular levy and plural levies; the verb to levy; the agent noun levier (rare).
