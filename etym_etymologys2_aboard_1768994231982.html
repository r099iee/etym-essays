Stage 1 — PIE *bherdh- "to cut" (reconstructed, per Pokorny and the standard handbooks; the Germanic 'board' is 'a board cut from a log'). No other Indo-European branch shows a securely attested reflex feeding the English word; the root is Germanic-centered. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *burdą "a board, plank; a table; the side of a ship" (reconstructed; derived from the act of cutting planks). Old English bord "board, plank; table; the side of a ship" (attested from the 8th c.). Stage 3 — Old English on bord "on board, on the side of the ship," and the contracted a- + bord "on board" (a- "on, at" + bord "ship's side"); the phrase is a native English formation parallel to Old French à bord. Stage 4 — Middle English aboord / abord "on board, at the side of a ship" (14th c.), partly reshaped under the influence of the Old French phrase à bord (the two merged in the Middle English form). Stage 5 — English aboard "on or into a ship, aircraft, or other vehicle" (modern). NOTE: The Old French influence is a matter of convergence, not of the word's origin — aboard is natively built from English a- + board, and French à bord reinforced the shape rather than supplying it.
