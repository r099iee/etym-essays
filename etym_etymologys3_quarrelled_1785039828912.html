QUARRELLED carries in its small, spiky sound the whole history of a grievance becoming a squabble. The word's biography begins in a murmur. Its earliest attested stratum is Latin queri, "to complain, lament, bewail," from a Proto-Italic *kwese- of the same sense; beyond that, scholars hypothesise a Proto-Indo-European root *kues- "to hiss, sigh," proposed to connect the family with Sanskrit svas- "to breathe, hiss, snort." The hypothesis is attractive but unproven: the deeper link is a matter of informed conjecture, and responsible dictionaries flag it as such. What is secure is the Latin chain. From queri Latin formed the noun querella, a complaint or accusation, and in Roman legal usage the word had real teeth: the querella was a formal statement of grievance brought before a court. This is the point of origin for a crucial observation — quarrel was originally a legal term, a word of the courtroom, naming the citizen's formal lament against injury or injustice. The word then moved through the languages of medieval Europe. Old French took up querele and broadened it: it meant "dispute, controversy, grievance," and even ordinary "business" or "concern." Middle English borrowed the noun in the mid-fourteenth century as querele, meaning "dispute, altercation," and also "ground for complaint." Significantly, in Middle English the word could mean armed combat — a quarrel might still be settled by a duel — so the modern meaning "angry words" is actually a retreat from an earlier, more violent sense. The verb querelen appeared around 1375 with the senses "to raise an objection, to dispute, to rebel," and by 1520 it had settled into "to contend violently, dispute angrily, fall out." The noun's decisive modern sense, "angry contention between persons," is fixed by the 1570s, and from then on the word belongs to the family, the street, and the tavern.
  • Sound and spelling. The Old French querele gave English the qu- spelling with the /kw-/ cluster. The doubled -ll- of QUARRELLED and quarrelling is purely a British orthographic habit: British English doubles a final -l- before vowel suffixes (travelled, marvelled, quarrelled), while American English writes quarreled, traveled. The spelling distinction dates from the 19th century and Noah Webster's reforms; it affects the page, not the voice.
  • Semantic drift: from legal complaint to petty squabble. The most striking feature of the word's history is its downward drift in register and seriousness. A Roman querella was a formal legal plaint; a Chaucerian querele could still be a grievance worth armed redress; a modern quarrel is a noisy disagreement over trivia. The word has been democratised and trivialised: the courtroom gave way to the kitchen. This is a classic instance of semantic pejoration driven by social context — as formal legal machinery developed other terms (complaint, suit, action), the older word was free to slide into the domestic and the petty.
  • Etymology and the homonym warning. A full account must flag the trap of the homonym. English also has quarrel meaning a square-headed crossbow bolt, which descends from an entirely different root — Latin quadrus "square," from PIE *kwetwer- "four." The bolt is named for its four-sided head. The two words are spelled and pronounced alike but have no historical connection; the confusion is purely accidental, and fusing them would be a lexicographical howler.
  • Literary resonance. Because a quarrel stops short of blows, the word has long served prose writers as the precise term for conflict conducted in language alone. Shakespeare uses it constantly; Jane Austen's Emma jokes that a quarrel between a man and his wife "is a proverbially short one." In Tolkien's The Hobbit, quarrelled performs a bathetic deflation: goblins who terrify by torture are suddenly shown bickering like children over stolen goods, and the archaic, slightly squalid ring of the word undercuts whatever dignity their malice might claim.
  • Modern register. In contemporary English the verb has settled toward petty, ill-tempered bickering — hard words, hot tempers, and fault-finding over trivia — so that to say two people quarrelled now sounds almost squalid and childish beside a feud or a fight. The word has come full circle from the Roman courtroom: what was once the citizen's formal lament is now the sound of two people squabbling over spoils.
The trajectory is thus a history of decreasing violence and decreasing dignity: from a citizen's formal lament in a Roman court, to a medieval grievance that might still be settled by combat, to the modern sound of two people — or two goblins — squabbling over treasure. In every age the word has kept one feature constant: a quarrel is conducted in words, hard words and hot tempers, and never quite becomes a fight.
