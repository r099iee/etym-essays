French abrégé descends from Latin abbreviāre, a compound of ad- "to" and breviāre "to shorten", which is itself formed from the adjective brevis "short". Brevis is one of the oldest and most productive items in the Latin lexicon, and its Indo-European ancestry is secure: it continues a stem *mregh-u- "short", whose other descendants include Greek βραχύς (brakhús) and, through Germanic, English brief by a later borrowing. The family in English is large — brief, brevity, abbreviate, abbreviation, abridge, abridgement — and all of it turns on the same metaphor of cutting length.

From Latin to French

  • Latin breviāre belongs to late, technical prose: it means "to make short", and it is the verb used of abbreviating a text or a period.
  • The prefixed abbreviāre adds direction ("to bring to shortness") and is the form that passed into Romance. In French the intervocalic -v- disappeared, giving abregier and eventually abréger, a development parallel to brevis > bref.
  • The past participle abrégé was already adjectival in the twelfth century; its use as a noun — "a shortened thing, a summary" — is recorded by the fourteenth.
  • The acute accent is a later orthographic refinement: Old and Middle French wrote abregé or abrege indifferently, and the accent was fixed by printers and grammarians in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries to mark the close vowel.

The abrégé as a genre

The noun flourished because early modern scholarship needed it. Humanist readers wanted the substance of large works without their bulk, and a market grew for authorised digests of history, law, theology and philosophy. An abrégé was not a student's crib but a respectable literary form, often written by the author of the larger work himself and prefaced with an explanation of what had been omitted. The word thus acquired its characteristic tone: learned, purposeful, slightly condescending toward the reader who needs shortening.

Hénault and the chronological abridgement

Charles-Jean-François Hénault's Abrégé chronologique de l'histoire de France (1744) is the most famous French example. Hénault was a magistrate and salon figure rather than a professional historian, and his book succeeded precisely because it was readable: dates formed the spine, but anecdotes, portraits and maxims supplied the flesh. The 1775 edition cited in the source belongs to the long series of reprints and continuations that carried the work into the last decades of the old regime. For an English reader quoting French history in the 1770s, Hénault was the obvious abbreviation to cite.

English reception

  • English writers rarely borrowed the noun; they translated the title as Chronological Abridgement, which is exactly how the phrase reads in eighteenth-century catalogues.
  • Where the French form is kept, it is italicised and accented: Abrégé chronologique. The unaccented Abrege of the source is a compositor's or transcriber's simplification.
  • The English abridge is a doublet of abbreviate: the first came through Old French, the second straight from Latin, and both are used of shortening texts.
  • In modern English the French word survives only in titles, in en abrégé, and in the vocabulary of bibliography.

What the word contributes to the passage

The title is not decorative. It tells us the anecdote of the king's illness at Metz reached the writer through a chronological digest — a book organised by year, designed for rapid reference, and itself a product of the eighteenth-century appetite for compressed knowledge. Reading Abrege as merely "a French word" misses that bibliographic signal; the word names a method of presenting history, and the passage's careful dating ("in the year 1744") is a trace of it.
