• Baa: Similar because both name the sheep's call; differs because baa is a bare imitation of the animal sound, with no extension to human weakness or complaint.
  • Whimper: Similar because both are weak, trembling cries; differs because a whimper is a small suppressed sob of pain or fear, more internal and more human than the bleat's animal tremble.
  • Whine: Similar because both are feeble, querulous complaints; differs because a whine is thin and nasal, peevish and human, while a bleat keeps its animal trembling note.
  • Squeal: Similar because both are high-pitched cries; differs because a squeal is a sharper, piercing shriek of excitement or pain, without the wavering helplessness of a bleat.
  • Mew: Similar because both are thin animal cries; differs because mew is the kitten's or cat's plaintive call, smaller, softer, and more plaintive still.
  • Moan: Similar because both express discomfort in sound; differs because a moan is low, prolonged, and mournful, lacking the bleat's high, trembling sheep-like note.
  • Cry: Similar because both are vocal expressions of distress; differs because cry is the broad, neutral term for any loud lament or call, with no animal connotation and no note of contempt.
  • Shout: Similar because both are vocal emissions; differs because a shout is loud and forceful, the very opposite of the bleat's weak, trembling character.
  • Complain: Similar because both can voice discontent; differs because complain is the neutral, general verb, while bleat adds a note of feeble, contemptible self-pity.
  • Gripe / grumble: Similar because both voice discontent; differs because they are grumbling, resentful complaint, deeper-toned and more human, not the thin plaintive cry of a bleat.
  • Low / moo: Similar because both name farm-animal cries; differs because lowing and mooing belong to cattle, deep and resonant, and are used neutrally, not of human weakness.
  • Blat: Similar because both are sheep- and goat-call words from the same imitative stock; differs because blat is now chiefly a technical or dialectal term for the animal call, without the human extension.
