Proto-Indo-European *smer- "to smear, rub on, grease" (Pokorny; an expressive root of the "dirt/smear" field) (c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *smerwą "grease, fat, ointment" and verb *smirwjaną "to smear, anoint" (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE) → Old English smierwan "to smear, anoint" and smeoru "fat, grease, tallow" (recorded from the 8th century) → Middle English smirchen / smyrchen "to soil, darken, besmear" → noun smirch "stain, dirt; an act of soiling" (14th–15th centuries) → Early Modern English be- + smirch → besmirch "to cover thoroughly with soil; to sully, to defame" (attested from the late 16th century; prominent in Shakespeare) → BESMIRCH Note on nuance: the "smear" family is sound-symbolic and expressive, so strict sound-law correspondences among its members are inherently fluid; the development of smirch itself is sometimes said to have been influenced by the neighboring words smut and smear, and the exact route from the Old English anointing verbs to Middle English smirchen is not fully documented at every link. The be- prefix is the ordinary intensive ("thoroughly, all over, make into"), as in besmear and bedaub.
