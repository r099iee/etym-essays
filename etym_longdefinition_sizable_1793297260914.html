Sizable — also spelled sizeable — means fairly large in size, extent, or amount: big enough to command attention and to matter in practical terms, while stopping well short of huge, enormous, or gigantic.

In the source sentence the word measures scholarship rather than physical bulk: a sizable literature on differences between written and spoken language is a body of published research extensive enough to fill bibliographies and sustain a whole field of inquiry, with the studies of Gumperz, Kaltman and O’Connor, 1982 or 1983, standing as landmarks within it. The choice of sizable over vast or enormous is characteristically measured academic praise: the literature is substantial and impossible to ignore, yet the claim stays sober, verifiable, and defensible.

Core meaning in detail: At its heart sizable is a quantifying adjective built transparently from size plus the suffix -able, literally suggesting ‘possessing size worth reckoning with’. It is fully gradable — a more sizable portion, the most sizable donation on record — and works both attributively, as in a sizable crowd, and predicatively, as in the crowd was sizable. Its natural habitat is the countable and the measurable: sums of money, shares of votes, quantities of data, numbers of publications, and areas of land. On spelling, American English strongly prefers sizable while British English traditionally favours sizeable; every major dictionary accepts both as fully standard, and the pronunciation is identical either way.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Considerable in amount, number, or extent (primary modern sense): a sizable income, a sizable majority, a sizable body of research. This is the dominant everyday use, applied to money, votes, audiences, and evidence. The bridge from physical size is metaphorical extension: we habitually talk about quantities as though they occupied space, so a heap of evidence becomes a ‘large’ one and a weighty argument becomes a ‘big’ one.
  • Large in physical bulk or dimensions (founding sense): a sizable fish, a sizable warehouse, a sizable parcel. The bridge is direct and literal: the adjective was coined to describe objects, creatures, and structures whose measurements impress the eye and the tape-measure alike.
  • Big-framed, said of a person (polite euphemism): a sizable gentleman, a sizable lady of a certain age. The bridge is courteous understatement: because sizable sounds neutral and almost technical, it lets speakers acknowledge heavy build without uttering fat, huge, or overweight.
  • Important or consequential, said of problems, differences, and effects (extended sense): a sizable advantage, a sizable obstacle, sizable differences between speech and writing. The bridge runs through consequence: big things push hard on the world, so ‘large’ slides naturally into ‘weighty, significant, hard to dismiss’.
  • Business and finance usage (register-specific sense): a sizable investment, sizable returns, a sizable market share. The bridge is the boardroom taste for measured optimism: sizable promises substance without the hype of massive and without the hostage to fortune that an exact figure would give.

Register and connotation: Sizable is neutral, respectable, and mildly formal — the vocabulary of reports, prospectuses, minutes, and prize citations rather than of playgrounds or poetry. It carries quiet approval: to call a donation, a majority, or a literature sizable is to grant it standing and to say it must be taken seriously. Unlike big it never sounds childish, and unlike massive it never sounds breathless or journalistic; it is bigness stated by someone mentally holding a ruler. Its shadow side is vagueness: precisely because it commits to no number, critics sometimes hear it as a polite blur, a word that admires without auditing. Used honestly, though, it marks the exact point where quantity turns into importance — the moment at which something becomes too big to overlook.
