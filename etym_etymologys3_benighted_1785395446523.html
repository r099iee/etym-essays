Evolution of "benighted." The word is a small lantern with a long history. It was coined in English in the sixteenth century as a plain, practical verb for the traveller's misfortune, being caught out of doors by the fall of night, and within a hundred years it had become one of the great moral words of the Enlightenment, the name for the darkness of unreason and the colonial vocabulary of superiority. Its history tracks the history of the idea of darkness itself.

1. The root: night. The core of the word is night, one of the oldest words in the language, descended through Proto-Germanic *nahts from PIE *nókʷts, the same root that gave Latin nox, Greek núx, and Sanskrit nák. In Old English it was niht, a word of the darkness that ends the working day, and it has come down to the modern language essentially unchanged in meaning. Night was never a neutral idea: from the earliest literatures it is the time of danger, of the unseen, of the enemy's approach, and this charge of peril is what the prefix be- will draw out of it.

2. The coinage: benight. In the sixteenth century English made a verb from the noun, benight, "to overtake or envelop with the darkness of night," using the old verb-forming prefix be-, from the preposition "by," with its intensive force of "all over, thoroughly." The formation is entirely native, the kind of word a Tudor writer could coin without apology, and it names a sudden, active misfortune: night falls on the traveller as an agent, catching him before he reaches shelter. The participle benighted, "overtaken by night," follows almost immediately, describing the traveller stranded in the dark, lost, exposed, at the mercy of the road and its dangers.

3. The figurative leap. The moral sense begins almost at once, and it is the most important event in the word's history. From "overtaken by night" to "in moral or intellectual darkness" is a short step, because the language of darkness for the state of sin and ignorance was already ancient, biblical and classical. In the 17th century benighted begins to name minds as well as travellers: benighted heathens, benighted pagans, benighted souls, people plunged into the darkness of unreason. The participle now carries both senses at once, the literal traveller's night and the moral night of the soul, and it is the moral sense that will rise.

4. The Enlightenment and the colonial turn. The 18th and 19th centuries made benighted a word of empire and progress. In Enlightenment prose the light of reason is the opposite of night, and the benighted are those who have not yet received it, the peoples, ages, and places deemed backward or superstitious. The word became the standard epithet for the colonial subject, used with a pitying, condescending warmth in missionary and administrative writing, and its tone hardened over the century toward smug superiority even while it retained an undertone of tragic helplessness. The Victorian reader of a phrase like "the benighted natives" was being told three things at once: they are in the dark, it is not entirely their fault, and it is our duty to bring them the light, whether they want it or not. The word is thus a perfect fossil of the Enlightenment's self-image.

5. Tolkien and the literary revival. In The Hobbit (1937) Tolkien uses the word in its literal, narrative sense, and the effect is a deliberate archaism. The goblins "often caught people benighted near their gates": the word makes the danger active, night as a predator that delivers the traveller to the goblins, and it carries the pitying helplessness of the old sense, travellers to whom darkness has happened. Tolkien, a professional philologist, knew the word's moral history perfectly well, and his literal use is a reclaiming of the older, physical sense, the traveller's night rather than the coloniser's metaphor, made new again in the dark of the Misty Mountains.

6. The contemporary state. Today the literal sense of benighted is largely archaic, and the figurative sense survives chiefly as literary, mock-archaic, or ironic colouring. A modern writer may call a place benighted to say it is hopelessly backward, but the word's colonial history is now audible, and careful stylists use it with the shadows turned on, for humour, for period, or for the particular pathos of the traveller lost in the dark. It remains a word of completed misfortune, the darkness already fallen, and that shape, the participle of a verb that means to be overtaken by night, is what gives it its lasting power.

  • Sound change: PIE *kʷ- → Germanic *hʷ- → Old English h- in niht (Grimm's Law); the Old English spelling niht with its palatal glide gives the modern night, whose gh is silent after the Great Vowel Shift.
  • Morphology: the prefix be- (from the preposition "by") converts the noun night into the verb benight; the participial suffix -ed descends from PIE *-tó- through Germanic *-daz.
  • Semantic drift: from the traveller's literal night (16th century), to moral and intellectual darkness (17th century), to the colonial epithet of superiority (18th–19th centuries), to the literary, mock-archaic and ironic register (20th century onward); the literal sense retreats while the figurative sense rises and then becomes self-conscious.
  • Literary attestations: Tudor and Stuart prose; 17th-century religious writing; Enlightenment and missionary prose; Victorian imperial and descriptive writing; Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937); modern literary and ironic usage.
