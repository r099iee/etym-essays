Imitative base *puf- "short, quick blast of breath or air" (sound-symbolic; the labial stop /p/ + breathy fricative /f/ of blowing; no PIE reconstruction is accepted — the word is the sound itself, attested across West Germanic as an expressive formation) → Old English pyffan / *puffian (v.) "to blow with the mouth" (recorded in Anglo-Saxon glosses) → Middle English puffen (v.) "to blow in short, quick blasts" (12th–13th c.); noun puf / puffe (n.) "a short, quick blast of wind or breath" (c. 1200) → Modern English puff (n. and v.) "a short, quick blast of wind or breath; to blow out in puffs" (source of puff pastry, late 14th c.; powder-puff, 1650s; puffball; figurative "empty boast" 1560s, "flattery" 1732) → English interjection poof "the sound of a puff of breath or air" (1824) → extended sense "sudden puff of smoke; instant disappearance" ("poof! he was gone", from the 1910s, via the image of the stage-magic vanishing in a puff of smoke) → Modern English POOF (interjection; also converted noun "a poof of smoke", and, unrelated in meaning, the slang noun poof "an effeminate man", c. 1850, with Australian poofter 1910) Note on debate: poof is treated by lexicographers as a shortened, vowel-lengthened variant of puff (with the /ʊ/ of puff lengthened to /uː/), but no written intermediate form between puff and the 1824 interjection is attested; the derivation is inferred from form, sense, and the same p-f imitative cluster. The slang noun poof "an effeminate man" (c. 1850) is probably an independent dismissive formation from puff and is unrelated in meaning to the interjection. French pouf is a parallel imitative coinage, not an ancestor of the English word.
