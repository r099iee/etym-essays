- [14th–15th c. Middle English] — Emerged in Anglo-Norman and Middle English literature, originally designating physical strips, ribbons, or heraldic sashes (bendes) worn across armor to identify knights — [perception: a physical marker of feudal allegiance and chivalric rank]
- [16th c. Elizabethan Era] — Under the influence of French bande (military company), the verb to band developed the sense of assembling soldiers under a common flag; the participle banded came to describe confederated factions and militant bodies — [perception: an aggressive, disciplined muster of men united for common defense or revolt]
- [17th c. Early Modern English] — Milton poetically broadened banded in Paradise Lost ("banded against the Omnipotent"), cementing its grand, dramatic connotation of organized spiritual and cosmic solidarity — [perception: sublime, defiant corporate alignment]
- [Late 18th – Early 19th c. Romantic & Industrial Era] — Wordsworth adopted the martial term in The Prelude to describe an acoustic symphony ("banded host of harmony"), while naturalists and burgeoning geologists began applying banded to describe striped strata, agates, and zoological markings — [perception: expanding from military alliance to structural harmony and scientific classification]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Era] — Firmly established across specialized sciences (geology: Banded Iron Formations; biology: banded species) and wildlife conservation (banded migratory birds), alongside ongoing sociological usage for cooperative human networks — [perception: an indispensable technical and structural descriptor in the physical and life sciences]
