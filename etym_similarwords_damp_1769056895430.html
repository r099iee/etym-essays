• wet: Similar because it is covered or soaked with water; differs because wet is the unmarked word — saturated and neutral with no emotional colouring — while damp names the low, unpleasant end of the scale, the wetness that seeps.
  • moist: Similar because it means slightly wet; differs because moist is damp's neutral, often pleasant twin — the freshness of cake, earth, and skin — so the difference between a "moist sponge cake" and a "damp towel" is almost entirely attitudinal.
  • humid: Similar because it describes atmospheric wetness; differs because humid is climatic and air-borne — the warm mugginess of the tropics or summer — whereas damp is cool, clingy, and attached to surfaces and cellars.
  • dank: Similar as damp's closest literary cousin; differs because dank is damp plus darkness, cold, and staleness — the word for dungeons and cellars — and is always unpleasant, where damp alone can be a neutral property of a cloth or the grass.
  • clammy: Similar because it is damp and cold; differs because clammy is a subjective touch-sensation of sticky, moist coldness on the skin, whereas damp is a property of surfaces, air, or ground.
  • soggy: Similar because it is wet; differs because soggy is waterlogged and heavy — drowned ground, sodden bread — a much greater degree of wetness than the light lingering wetness of damp.
  • sodden: Similar because it is thoroughly soaked; differs because sodden is saturated and heavy with water, while damp is only slightly, lingeringly wet — the difference between a soaked sponge and a dewy wall.
  • soaked / saturated: Similar because they describe waterlogged material; differs because soaked and saturated are absolute — full of water to capacity — whereas damp is a partial, surface wetness.
  • dewy: Similar because it is slightly wet; differs because dewy is the fresh, delicate wetness of dawn on grass, positively valued, while damp is cool, stale, and slightly unpleasant.
  • muggy: Similar because it describes humid air; differs because muggy is warm, close, and oppressive summer air, whereas damp is cool clinging moisture of walls and cellars.
  • steamy: Similar because it involves airborne moisture; differs because steamy is visibly hot vapour — baths, kitchens, tropics — where damp is invisible cool humidity.
  • misty: Similar because it involves airborne moisture; differs because misty describes weather full of fine suspended droplets in the air, whereas damp is moisture clinging to surfaces and places.
  • drizzly: Similar because it is wet weather; differs because drizzly is fine, falling rain from the sky, while damp is the settled moisture that remains in a place after the weather has passed.
  • musty: Similar because it describes a damp place; differs because musty names the stale, mouldy smell that damp produces — the odour — rather than the wetness itself.
  • waterlogged: Similar because it is full of water; differs because waterlogged is extreme saturation — ground, wood, or vessels steeped in water — far beyond the light clinging wetness of damp.
