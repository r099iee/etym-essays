The noun vassals (plural of vassal) denotes, first and foremost, the noble landholders of the medieval feudal system who held land — a fief or benefice — from a lord in exchange for homage, fealty, military service, and counsel. In its primary historical sense a vassal was not a peasant but a free man, usually of knightly or noble rank, who entered into a personal, reciprocal bond with a superior lord: he swore homage (the act of becoming "his man") and fealty (the oath of loyalty), and in return received the protection of the lord and the usufruct of land sufficient to support his service. In modern usage the word has been extended far beyond the Middle Ages: figuratively, vassals can mean subordinate dependents, satellite nations, or puppet states that are nominally independent but effectively controlled by a dominant power, and, more loosely, any servile followers of a powerful master.

Sense 1 — Feudal landholding retainers (primary historical sense). In medieval law and history, a vassal held his fief by homage and fealty. The relationship was solemnized in ceremonies of homage (the vassal kneeling, placing his hands in those of the lord, and declaring himself "his man") followed by the oath of fealty (an oath on the Gospels to be faithful). The vassal owed the lord military service (typically a set number of days per year with his own equipment), counsel (consilium), and financial aid (auxilium) on defined occasions such as the knighting of the lord's eldest son or the marriage of his eldest daughter. In return the lord owed the vassal protection and the peaceful enjoyment of the fief. Vassals were thus a kind of military aristocracy bound by contract to their superiors — and lords themselves could be vassals of higher lords, creating the pyramid of medieval tenure.

Sense 2 — Subordinate or client states (geopolitical sense). Since the 19th century, international-relations writers have spoken of vassal states or satellites: nominally sovereign states whose foreign policy, economy, or security is effectively dictated by a more powerful patron. The term compares the modern empire or hegemonic power to a feudal overlord and the client state to a vassal. In the Cold War, "Soviet vassal states" described the Warsaw Pact satellites of Eastern Europe; "vassal" remains a standard pejorative in geopolitics for a state that obeys a stronger one.

Sense 3 — Servile dependents (figurative and pejorative sense). Applied to people, vassals describes followers who are wholly subservient to a master — sycophants, yes-men, obedient retainers who do a powerful person's bidding without independence. This sense is always derogatory: it drains the honor out of the feudal term and leaves only the servitude. A politician who "surrounds himself with vassals" is being accused of wanting courtiers, not colleagues.

Sense 4 — Technical feudal and legal vocabulary. In the technical vocabulary of medieval studies, vassalage names the whole institution (the status, the contract, and the obligations of vassals), vassal names the individual holder, and the adjective vassal ("vassal lords," "vassal kingdoms") marks subordination within a hierarchy. Scholars distinguish the vassal (a free retainer bound by homage and fealty) sharply from the serf or villein (an unfree peasant bound to the soil, owing labor rather than military service) — a distinction of rank, freedom, and obligation.

Sense 5 — Register and modern resonance. The word is historical in its primary sense, geopolitical or pejorative in its extensions, and always carries the flavor of hierarchy, oath, and dependency. In fantasy literature (and in the Game of Thrones-style world of this card), "vassals" are the sworn houses who answer a liege lord's call — "command our vassals" is precisely the language of a ruler assembling his feudal host. The word's durability comes from its precision: no modern word captures quite the same blend of land tenure, sworn loyalty, military obligation, and subordination.

Historical semantic bridge. The word's career is a study in social elevation and subsequent pejoration. Vassal begins in Celtic as a word for a servant or young man of low status (*wasso-), is adopted into Latin and then Old French, and is raised, under the Carolingian empire, to describe the aristocratic military retinue of the king — a word that once meant "servant boy" came to name a landholding noble. Then, as feudalism dissolved, the same word sank again into pejorative use, applied to dependent states and toadying underlings. The honor and the servitude have been in tension in the word's meaning from its beginning to the present day.
