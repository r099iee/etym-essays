• Cows: Similar because both refer to mature female bovine animals; differs because cows is the universal, modern, neutral standard plural, whereas kine is an archaic, poetic, or scriptural double-plural carrying deep pastoral and liturgical resonance.
  • Cattle: Similar because both denote bovine livestock collectively; differs because cattle is an overarching modern collective noun encompassing all genders and ages (bulls, cows, steers, calves), whereas kine historically designated dairy cows specifically and carries an elevated literary tone.
  • Oxen: Similar because both are domesticated bovine quadrupeds; differs because oxen specifically designates castrated draft animals trained for plowing, hauling, and heavy labor, while kine refers to dairy and breeding cows.
  • Beeves: Similar because both are archaic plural terms for bovine animals; differs because beeves (the plural of beef) was used historically for cattle raised specifically for slaughter and meat consumption, whereas kine emphasizes live dairy and pastoral herds.
  • Neat: Similar because both are obsolete or archaic English terms for domestic bovine livestock; differs because neat (from Old English nēat) was an archaic singular/collective term for horned cattle generally, often appearing in compounds like neat-herd or neat's-foot oil.
  • Livestock: Similar because both encompass farm animals; differs because livestock is a broad modern commercial category including sheep, pigs, goats, horses, and poultry, lacking the specific bovine focus and poetic antiquity of kine.
  • Heifers: Similar because both refer to female bovine animals; differs because a heifer specifically designates a young female cow that has not yet borne a calf, whereas kine denotes mature milking cows collectively.
  • Herd: Similar because both refer to a group of grazing animals; differs because herd is an abstract grouping noun that can apply to deer, elephants, or buffalo, whereas kine explicitly names the cows themselves.
