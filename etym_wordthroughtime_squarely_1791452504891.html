- 14th century - Square enters English from Old French as a builder's and geometer's word for a right-angled figure; the tone is technical and practical.
- 16th century - Squarely appears, meaning "in a square form, at right angles"; it belongs to carpentry, masonry and surveying.
- 16th to 17th century - Square acquires the moral sense of honest and fair, from the idea of true measure and square dealing; the adverb begins to carry that ethical colour.
- 18th to 19th century - "Fair and square" becomes a settled phrase, and squarely is used of straightforward, honourable conduct; the word sounds decent, masculine and slightly old-fashioned.
- 19th to 20th century - The adverb takes on its modern emphatic sense of "directly, without qualification", especially in blame, responsibility and confrontation; journalism adopts it as a marker of decisiveness.
- Contemporary - A formal but vigorous word, common in political and legal prose and in physical description, while the slang square for an uncool person diverges entirely.
