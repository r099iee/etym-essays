Rutted describes a road, lane, or track deeply grooved by the repeated passage of wheels, so that its surface runs in parallel channels with raised ridges between them.

In the source sentence the travellers see such a lane beyond the meadow: a rutted lane running between low well-laid hedges towards a distant clump of trees. The single adjective carries the whole history of the road: farm carts and waggons have pressed the same lines through wet clay summer after summer until the lane itself keeps their memory in mud and stone.

Core meaning in detail: A rut is a sunken track worn by wheels, typically a few inches deep, twin lines a cart-axle apart. A rutted lane therefore dictates where each wheel must go; drivers steer into the grooves rather than across them, and walkers pick the raised centre or the grassy edge. The word belongs to unmetalled roads: clay, chalk, and dirt that yield under weight and bake hard in summer, preserving the grooves for months. After rain the ruts hold water in twin mirrors; in frost they set iron-hard and jolt every wheel.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Grooved by wheels (primary literal sense, the source use, from 16th–17th c.): a rutted lane, a rutted farm track. The bridge is direct: rut plus -ed means furnished with ruts, exactly as wooded means furnished with woods.
  • Worn into fixed habit (figurative, 19th c.): a rutted routine, a rutted mind, stuck in a rut. The bridge carries the wheel-groove onto behaviour: just as carts cannot leave their grooves without jolting, people cannot leave fixed habits without effort, so the road image becomes the psychology of routine.
  • Scored or channelled generally (extended technical): rutted ice on a skating rink, a rutted ski slope, rutted bark. The bridge generalises wheels to any repeated pressure: skates, skis, and weather carve their own grooves, and each earns the same adjective.
  • In oestrus, of deer and cattle (separate homonym, rut + -ed): a rutted stag bellowing in the rutting season. The bridge here is a different river: this rut comes from Old French ruit, roaring, not from wheel-tracks, and the two words meet only by spelling. Tolkien lane is the wheel sense, never the animal one.
  • Stuck and jolting progress (narrative nuance): in travel writing a rutted road promises slow comic discomfort: bouncing carts, muddy boots, walkers choosing the hedge-side. The bridge is experiential: readers feel the grooves through the soles of the walkers, so the adjective does the work of a whole paragraph of hardship.

Register and connotation: rutted is earthy rural English, beloved of novelists, walkers, and war memoirists for its instant tactility. It praises nothing and blames nothing; it simply reports a road that remembers every cart. Against tarmac smoothness it can sound reproachful, as of neglect, but in Tolkien it is affectionate: the rutted lane between trim hedges is the honest working countryside, well-kept yet worn, leading the eye and the feet pleasantly onward toward the trees.
