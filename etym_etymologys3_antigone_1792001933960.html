The Greek personal name Antigone (Ancient Greek: Ἀντιγόνη) is a classical nominal compound constructed from two ancient Indo-European roots: the prepositional prefix ἀντί (antí) and the nominal derivative γονή (gonḗ). The name reflects sophisticated archaic naming traditions in ancient Greece, where aristocratic names often encoded familial expectations, ancestral lineages, or cosmological attributes.

The linguistic and etymological trajectory of the name is traced through its core components and semantic debates:

  • The Prefix Element (ἀντί / *h₂énti): The first constituent antí derives directly from the Proto-Indo-European locative adverb and root noun *h₂énti, meaning "facing", "in front of", "before", or "opposite" (from the root *h₂ent-, "front, forehead"). This root is the source of Latin ante ("before"), Sanskrit ánti ("near, opposite"), Hittite ḫanti ("separately, in front"), and Germanic cognates including English and (originally "thereupon, against") and answer (from Old English and-swaru, "a swear/speech against or in return"). In Greek onomastics, anti- can carry several nuanced meanings:
    • "Opposite" or "against" (as in adversary or counter-weight).
    • "Instead of" or "in place of" (as in equivalent, surrogate, or substitute).
    • "Equal to" or "resembling" (as in matching one's parents or peers).
  • The Nominal Element (γονή / *ǵenh₁-): The second constituent gonḗ derives from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ǵenh₁-, signifying "to produce", "to beget", "to give birth", or "to bring forth". This extraordinarily prolific root yielded Greek gígnomai ("to be born, become"), génos ("race, stock, family"), and gonos ("child, offspring, seed"); Latin gignere ("to beget"), genus ("birth, kind"), and natura; and English Germanic cognates kin, kind, king, and generate. In ancient Greek, gonḗ denoted "birth", "offspring", "generation", or "stock".
  • Scholarly Etymological Interpretations of the Compound: Classical philologists and onomastic scholars debate the exact semantic synthesis intended by the compound Antigónē:
    • "In place of a mother / surrogate parent": Many philologists interpret the name as "acting in place of the generation/mother" (anti- + gonē), highlighting her mythological role in caring for her blind father Oedipus and rearing her siblings after the suicide of her mother Jocasta.
    • "Worthy of her parentage / equal to her ancestors": In heroic onomastics (parallel to the masculine counterpart Antigonus, borne by one of Alexander the Great's famous diadochi generals), the name signifies "matching her forebears" or "equal in stature to those who begot her".
    • "Against generation / opposed to marriage": Tragic commentators, noting Sophocles' relentless emphasis on her childless demise and rejection of marital life with Haemon, suggest a dramatic wordplay where Antigone is literally "born against generation" or "counter-maternal"—a doomed maiden whose loyalty to the dead extinguishes her own reproductive line.
  • Transmission into Latin and Modern European Languages: In Roman antiquity, the name was Latinized as Antigona or Antigonē, appearing in Seneca's tragedies and Ovid's poetry. Through the Renaissance revival of Greek drama, the French form Antigone and English Antigone entered vernacular literature. The English pronunciation regularized with stress on the second syllable (/ænˈtɪɡ.ə.ni/), preserving the Greek rhythmic cadence.

Through its rich etymological layers, Antigone reflects both the ancient aristocratic pride of heroic ancestry and the tragic doom of an individual standing firm against the political machinery of her world.
