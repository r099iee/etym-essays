The name Romulus is one of the few great names of ancient history whose etymology leads not into the comfortable depths of Proto-Indo-European but into a contested substrate zone, because the word it is built on — Rōma, "Rome" itself — stubbornly resists Indo-European analysis. Modern historical linguists and historians of early Italy are now in broad agreement that Rōma is most plausibly of Etruscan origin: the site lies on the left, Etruscan bank of the Tiber, and Rome's earliest material culture shows deep Etruscan entanglement. The name has been connected with the Etruscan gentilic (family) name Ruma or Rumaś, attested in Etruscan inscriptions, from which the Roman family Rōmilia may descend. On this view, Romulus — formed from Rōma with the suffix -ulus, a Latin suffix that forms diminutives (as in catulus "puppy") and adjectives of belonging — means simply "little Roman" or "the Roman one," an eponymous hero invented, as the Romans themselves half-recognised, to give the city a founder of its own name. This is the account favoured in the standard modern handbooks, and it must be stressed that "founded by Romulus" is a myth, not a memory: the Palatine settlement is real, but it dates to the eighth century BC, and its founder is lost to us.

The ancient rival etymologies, all rejected. The ancient world was less scrupulous, and two rival etymologies circulated in antiquity. The first, reported by the grammarian Servius and by the school of Varro, derived the name from the old name of the Tiber, *Rumōn (cf. Latin rumon, "a river"), itself traced to the Indo-European root *sreu- "to flow"; Rome would then be "the city of the stream," and Romulus "he of the river." This is an attractive but unprovable construction, and most modern linguists doubt the PIE pedigree of a river-name from a region of heavy substrate influence. The second, offered by Plutarch in his Life of Romulus, derived Rōmē from Greek ῥώμη (rhōmē), "strength, might," making Romulus "the strong one" — a piece of Hellenizing flattery that Plutarch himself records as one theory among several and that no one defends today. Both rest on the instinct to give the founder an honourable, meaningful name; both are folk-etymology.

The name fused with the legend. Whatever its substrate origin, Rōmulus was from the beginning fused with the foundation legend: the twin brothers abandoned on the Tiber, suckled by the she-wolf, the augury over the Palatine, the murder of Remus, the founding of the city on 21 April 753 BC, the rape of the Sabine women, the first Senate, and finally the hero's apotheosis as Quirinus, carried to heaven in a storm. The Roman institution of dating "ab urbe condita" ("from the founding of the city") counted from his reign, and the Roman people called themselves, through his line, Rōmānī — "Romans," the people of Rōmulus. In literature the name carried a double register from the start: Livy retells the story with wonder and professed disbelief in the apotheosis; Ovid's Fasti makes the founder the first king whose festival, the Parilia of 21 April, kept his memory alive in the calendar.

Irony and afterlife. In late antiquity the name was revived as a personal name for would-be emperors; its last bearer was the boy emperor Romulus Augustulus, "the little Augustus," deposed in 476, whose very name ironically joins the founder's name to the founder's title and marks the end of the western empire. Into English the name came directly from Latin Rōmulus through the tradition of classical historiography and the medieval legendaries, which is why English preserves the Latin -ulus ending rather than the Romance developments (Italian Romolo, Spanish Rómulo).

The name in this card's context. In the passage from which this card's context is drawn, Macaulay writes in the sceptical spirit of the nineteenth century that Hengist, Horsa, Vortigern, Rowena, Arthur, and Mordred "must be classed with those of Hercules and Romulus" — that is, with heroes whose reality no historian now defends but whose stories still carry the whole weight of a nation's imagined beginning. The name Romulus thus operates on three levels at once: an Etruscan substratum etymology, a legendary biography, and a scholarly shorthand for "mythic founder," which is exactly the sense it bears in this context. Like Hercules, he is the measuring-rod of the mythic: what any national legend must be measured against, and what the modern historian — Macaulay included — consigns to the class of the unprovable but unforgettable.

  • 8th c. BC — the historical Palatine settlement; the founder's real name is lost.
  • 3rd–1st c. BC — the foundation legend is fixed in Roman historiography (Fabius Pictor, Ennius, Livy); the name is given its divine and moral meanings.
  • Late antiquity — Romulus Augustulus (d. after 476) ironises the name as the last of the line.
  • Medieval–Renaissance — the Latin name passes into English via historiography; the Romance forms diverge (Romolo, Rómulo).
  • Modern — the name is the type-case of the mythic founder, and a scientific afterlife: the moon Romulus of the asteroid 87 Sylvia.
