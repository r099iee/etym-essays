stricken is the old past participle of the verb strike, which has survived as a fully independent adjective and combining form meaning “hit, afflicted, overwhelmed by something harmful or shocking”. In the passage from The Hobbit, Thorin is “stricken dumb with amazement and confusion”: the sight of the returned Arkenstone — and of his own defeated claim — hits him like a physical blow, and the word pictures him struck silent, paralysed by the impact of what he sees. The verb’s blow is felt through every sense of the word: to be stricken is to have been struck, and the participle keeps the force of the blow alive in the present.

Primary sense — afflicted, overwhelmed.

  • Affected or laid low by disease, disaster, grief or grief-like emotion: “stricken with fever”, “a region stricken by famine”, “stricken with grief”, “stricken by the news”. The agent is impersonal — fate, nature, illness — and the victim is passive and helpless.
  • With a complement adjective: stricken dumb (or “stricken silent”) — rendered unable to speak by shock or amazement, as in the source sentence; stricken blind, stricken deaf — sudden deprivation of a faculty, as if by a blow.
  • Of a person’s face, voice or expression: “a stricken look”, “stricken eyes”, “a stricken voice” — showing the visible marks of shock, grief or fear.

Secondary senses — legal, archaic and compositional.

  • Legal: in Anglo-American court procedure, evidence or argument that is “stricken from the record” is ordered deleted — struck out, removed from the official transcript, as if erased by a blow of the judge’s gavel.
  • Archaic: stricken in years means “advanced in age, far gone in life” — the image is of a person bent and pressed down by the weight of years; the phrase is biblical (King James Version: “old and stricken in years”, Joshua 13:1; “well stricken in years”, Luke 1:7) and survives today only in deliberate archaism.
  • Archaic poetic: the stricken field means the battlefield — the field where men are struck down; once common in historical prose (“to carry the day on the stricken field”), now chiefly a Victorian relic.
  • Combining form: stricken is enormously productive in compounds: grief-stricken, panic-stricken, horror-stricken, awe-stricken, terror-stricken, poverty-stricken, disease-stricken, famine-stricken, drought-stricken, storm-stricken, war-stricken, conscience-stricken. In each, the image is of the named force as the striker and the person or place as its victim.

Tertiary and modern senses.

  • Journalism and disaster reporting: “the stricken area”, “stricken towns”, “stricken residents” — the participle is standard in headlines for regions and people hit by earthquakes, floods and storms; it is the adjective of catastrophe.
  • Hunting and rural usage: a “stricken deer” is a wounded deer; the word is the stock participle of game animals struck by the hunter’s shot.
  • Old sports usage: in 19th-century baseball, a batter was said to be “stricken out” (the modern form is “struck out”), showing the participle’s former generality.
  • Contrast with struck: the everyday past participle of strike is now struck (the clock struck twelve, the mine struck gold, the match was struck), while stricken has been promoted to a specialised mournful register — afflicted, wounded, silenced — which is exactly why it can still shock with force: it is the participle of being hit by something greater than oneself.

Semantic bridge. The whole family rests on one physical image: a blow. Medieval English extended the verb strike from “to stroke, rub, glide” to “to hit”; the participle stricken inherited the blow, and every modern sense is that blow seen from the victim’s side. Illness strikes, disaster strikes, grief strikes; the person is stricken — passive, damaged, changed. The evolution of the word in formal terms is a narrowing: from any struck object (a stricken tree, a stricken wall — senses once general) to living victims of catastrophe, grief and awe, and finally to the fixed compounds and phrases of today. The blow has become emotional and existential, but it is still a blow: that is the visceral force Thorin’s “stricken dumb” still carries.

Register and morphology. Stricken is the survivor of a morphological anomaly: strike – struck – stricken preserves two past participles with divided duties. It is formal and literary by register — seldom casual — and its compounds supply journalism with its standard vocabulary of catastrophe. The word is grammatically an adjective once a participle; its final -en (Old English -en participle ending) marks its strong-verb pedigree, and its short vowel distinguishes it from the long-vowel strike into which the Great Vowel Shift never reached.
