Draggled means made wet, dirty, and dishevelled — limp and bedraggled — by being dragged or trailed through mud, water, or wet undergrowth. A draggled thing has lost its crispness, its neatness, and its dignity: it hangs heavy, clings, and drips, as if the whole world had been dragged across it. The word is the past participle of the verb to draggle, 'to make wet and dirty by trailing on the ground', itself a frequentative (repetitive) derivative of to drag; in ordinary use it describes the result of a dragging motion through dampness and filth.

Core sense — wet and soiled by dragging: The primary and still dominant sense is physical: a garment hem, a sleeve, a tail, or a beard that has trailed through water, mud, slush, or long grass and is left sodden, stained, and limp. This is the sense of the literary context in which a chimney-sweep washed ashore has wet straw in his draggled beard: the beard is soaked, plastered down, filthy, and matted by the water. The word almost always carries a note of pitiable neglect — the state is the result of exposure, hardship, or mishap, never of choice or care.

  • Sense 1 — Wet, dirty, soiled from trailing: the basic physical sense ('her draggled skirts', 'a draggled plume'), emphasising moisture plus grime.
  • Sense 2 — Limp, bedraggled, dishevelled appearance: the extension to general untidiness and collapse of form — hair, feathers, fur, or clothing that hang flat and tangled, with all stiffness or freshness gone ('a draggled flower', 'a draggled little bird').
  • Sense 3 — Figurative weariness and defeat: a metaphorical extension in which a person, reputation, or mood is described as 'draggled' — exhausted, soiled by misfortune, stripped of pride and energy, brought low in spirit as in body.
  • Sense 4 — Related and regional forms: the twin form bedraggled (with the intensifying prefix be-) is far commoner today and carries the same meaning with slightly stronger emphasis; draggle-tailed (of a person, especially a woman, or of a fowl) means slatternly and untidy, literally with a trailing, soiled tail or hem.

Register and tone: Draggled is a literary, slightly old-fashioned, and highly pictorial word. It is not a word of everyday speech; it belongs to descriptive prose, where it paints a quick picture of damp misery. It is nearly always used with sympathy or disgust rather than admiration. Its emotional charge is closer to bedraggled and sodden than to the neutral wet or dirty: the word insists on the process — something has been dragged through something wet — and on the result, a state of limp, pitiable filth.

Grammar and form: As a past-participial adjective, draggled can be attributive ('a draggled beard') or predicative ('the hem hung draggled'); it can be modified by intensifiers ('utterly draggled', 'sadly draggled'); and it shades naturally into participial verbs when the focus is on the action ('her dress draggled in the mud'). The unprefixed verb to draggle and its participle are today rarer than the be-prefixed bedraggled, but both are still alive in literary English.

Polysemy summary: Draggled is a short, tight cluster of closely related senses rather than a word of far-flung meanings: the literal 'wet and dirty from dragging', the expanded 'limp and dishevelled', and the figurative 'worn down and degraded by hardship'. Each later sense is a transparent metaphor of the first — moisture pulls things down, drags them out of shape, and leaves them soiled, and exactly the same is true of hardship for a human spirit.
