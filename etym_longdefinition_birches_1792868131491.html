Birches, the plural of birch, names the slender, white-barked trees of the genus Betula — pioneers of heaths, moors, and northern forests, with papery peeling bark, trembling diamond leaves, and catkins that dangle gold in spring.

In the source sentence thin-clad birches sway above the travellers' heads, making a black net against the pale sky: Tolkien catches the tree's winter signature exactly — sparse fine twigs, nearly naked, tracing dark lacework overhead while the white trunks below have faded into the dusk.

Core meaning in detail: Britain knows two native birches, the silver birch (Betula pendula) with warty drooping twigs and diamond leaves, and the downy birch (Betula pubescens) of wetter, higher ground. Both are light-demanding colonists: their dust-fine seed rides the wind onto bare heath, felled ground, and bomb-sites, and they grow fast, die young, and nurse the oakwood that follows. The bark peels in papery scrolls marked with dark lenticel dashes; the autumn leaf turns pure gold; the winter twigs carry a purple haze tipped with tiny cones.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The birch tree itself (primary sense, Old English birce): the white-stemmed pioneer tree of heath and boreal forest. The bridge is the ancient name itself, one of the few tree-words shared across the whole Indo-European north.
  • Birch timber and bark (material sense): pale close-grained wood for bobbins, brooms, and plywood; bark for boxes, canoes, and writing — from Novgorod birch-bark letters to North American canoes. The bridge is product-for-tree: the tree's gifts are so many that its name covers them all.
  • The rod for chastisement (historical disciplinary sense): a bundle of birch twigs, the schoolmaster's and boatswain's birch. The bridge is the twig's suppleness: no switch stings cleaner or breaks less readily than birch, so the tree lent its name to the punishment.
  • Slenderness and pallor as metaphor (poetic sense): birch-white limbs, the birch-maiden of folklore. The bridge is visual: white bark and airy habit become the type of northern fairness.
  • Pioneer and renewal symbolism (ecological and cultural): birches as first trees after fire, felling, and war — the green flag of recovery. The bridge is the tree's behaviour: it is the first to return, so poets make it the emblem of return.
  • The Ogham and runic tree (esoteric sense): beith in Ogham, berkana in the runes — beginnings, birth, and purification. The bridge is calendrical: birch leafs and catkins among the first, so the tree-alphabets put it first too.

Register and connotation: birches is light, cool, and northern — silver, trembling, and faintly melancholy, the tree of Russian songs, Highland clearings, and Scandinavian midsummer poles. Unlike the oak's confidence or the yew's gloom, the birch suggests youth and resilience: frail-looking, unkillable, first back after every wound to the land. Even its disciplinary shadow has faded with the cane, leaving the white stems to poets, painters, and the designers of saunas, where birch whisks still raise the steam.
