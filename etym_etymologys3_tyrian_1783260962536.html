TYRIAN — The City of the Rock and the Purple Tyrian has no Indo-European ancestry at all, and its biography must begin with a firm statement of that fact: the word is Semitic, a member of the great Northwest Semitic (Canaanite) branch of the Afroasiatic family, and any attempt to attach it to a Proto-Indo-European root would be a scholarly fiction. Its root is the Canaanite noun *ṣūr-, "rock, cliff" — the same word that appears in biblical Hebrew as ṣūr, "rock," the refuge-language of the Psalms ("The LORD is my rock," Ps. 18:2). The ancient city of Tyre was built on a pair of rocky islands off the Phoenician coast — the modern Lebanese city of Ṣūr lies on the same site, now joined to the mainland by Alexander's causeway — and the city simply took the name of its geology: Phoenician Ṣūr, "Tyre," identical in form to the common noun "rock." This is not a metaphor but a transfer; the toponym is the ordinary word for the island's rock. The name lived on in the daughter languages — Arabic Ṣūr and Hebrew Ṣōr are the forms of the Bible, where Tyre is celebrated in Ezekiel's great lament (Ezekiel 27–28), the richest ancient description of Tyrian commerce: ships of cypress, sails of embroidered linen, and "thy merchants were the great men of the earth." The name passed into the Indo-European world as a loan, because Greek and Latin could not build it from their own roots. Greek, having no sound for the emphatic Semitic ṣ, rendered it with a dental: Týros, "Tyre," and from the city formed the adjective and ethnonym Týrios, "of Tyre." It was in this Hellenic wrapper that the name reached Rome: Latin Tyrus and Tyrius. The Latin adjective enjoyed a rich poetic career — Vergil opens the Aeneid with "urbs antiqua fuit, Tyrii tenuere coloni," "an ancient city was there, held by Tyrian settlers," referring to Carthage, Tyre's greatest colony — and "Tyrian" became the standard classical epithet for the Phoenicians, their seamanship, and their luxury. English took the word as a learned adjective in the sixteenth century, and its sense has been stable ever since. What gave the word its colour — literally — was industry. Tyre's greatest and most famous export was the purple dye extracted from the gland of the murex shellfish (Murex brandaris), a deep reddish-purple that never faded and was staggeringly expensive, because tens of thousands of shellfish were needed for a single robe. The dye became the imperial purple of antiquity: the colour of kings, emperors, and — after Constantine — of the Christian high clergy, so tightly controlled that Roman sumptuary laws made it a capital offence for a private citizen to wear it, and Diocletian's Edict on Prices set its price higher than any other commodity. Because Tyre was the source, the place-name became the label of the colour: "Tyrian" shifted from a geographic gentilic to a colour word and a byword for royalty, so that the adjective came to mean simultaneously "of Tyre," "purple," and "imperial." This is the historical bridge that the long definition relies on — a city's monopoly turned its name into a hue. In the modern languages the word survives in two registers. "Tyrian purple" remains the standard term in art history, dye history, heraldry, and the luxury trades for the historic imperial hue and its reproductions, and Tyrian survives as a poetic, evocative adjective for the ancient city, its seafaring, and its wealth. It is in this second register that Macaulay uses it in the sentence that frames this card: Britain, in the deep past, first became known to "the Tyrian mariners," the Phoenician traders who, in early tradition, first sailed the remote seas of the north-west, bringing tin and exotic goods — and their purple — to the edge of the known world. The word thus carries the full weight of antiquity in a single syllable: the rock of an island city, the dye that clothed emperors, and the ships that reached Britain before the Romans. Tyrian is the rare adjective that is at once a geography, a colour, a commodity, and an epoch.
