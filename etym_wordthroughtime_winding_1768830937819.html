- Old English (participle of 'windan') - a craft word of pure physical turning: twisting, plaiting, and coiling resistant material upon itself, the work that produced ropes, wicker, and baskets.
- Later Middle & Early Modern English - the participle migrates from the loom to the land: a winding path, road, or river that constantly deflects from a fixed line becomes a standard topographical description.
- 18th-19th century - the sense is fixed in architecture ('winding stair', a spiral or zigzag ascent) and loosened into the figurative: a winding speech or argument is roundabout, indirect, turning back on itself.
- Contemporary - a fully naturalized descriptive adjective, kinesthetic and almost organic; it still keeps one hand on the rope and loom, so a winding stair reads as a woven, hand-wrought thing, coiled into motion rather than stacked in place.
