girdle (noun and verb; plural girdles) — A belt, sash, cord, strap, or band worn around the waist or hips to secure clothing, gather fabric, support weapons, or hold pouches; by metaphorical extension, any continuous encircling band, ring, perimeter, boundary, or surrounding zone that encloses an object or space; in botany and poetry (as in Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose), the outer circular ring, corolla, or whorl of petals encircling the center of a flower; in anatomy, a bony or cartilaginous skeletal arch that encloses and supports the limbs (the pectoral and pelvic girdles); also an elastic supportive foundation garment worn to shape the torso.

Primary sense — the waist-belt and cincture. In historical dress and daily life across millennia, a girdle was the essential functional and decorative fastening worn around the tunic, robe, or gown. In ancient and medieval cultures, clothing lacked built-in pockets; thus, the girdle served as a portable suspension system for keys, daggers, purses, prayer books, shears, and inkhorns. Girdles ranged from simple twisted hemp ropes worn by mendicant friars (such as the Franciscan cord) to lavishly embroidered, jewel-encrusted silk and gilded leather sashes worn by monarchs and knights as badges of rank, honor, and chastity.

Sense 2 — botanical and poetic floral ring (The context in Wilde). In botanical descriptions and lyrical literature, girdle denotes the surrounding ring or outer perimeter of floral structures—specifically the whorl of petals (corolla) or sepals that encompasses the reproductive center, pistil, and stamen of a blossom. In Oscar Wilde's fairy tale, the newly bloomed miracle rose is described with rich chromatic imagery: "Crimson was the girdle of petals, and crimson as a ruby was the heart." Here, girdle defines the outer ring of velvety red petals standing as an exquisite protective enclosure around the rose's central ruby core.

Sense 3 — anatomical skeletal arches. In vertebrate anatomy, girdle denotes the bilateral skeletal structures that articulate the appendages with the axial skeleton. The pectoral girdle (shoulder girdle, consisting of clavicles and scapulae) supports the upper limbs, while the pelvic girdle (formed by the ilium, ischium, and pubis) anchors the lower limbs and supports the visceral weight of the abdomen.

  • Gemology and jewelry — the girdle of a faceted diamond or gemstone is the narrow perimeter or outer edge that divides the upper crown (table and upper facets) from the lower pavilion, serving as the setting edge when mounted in a ring or bezel.
  • Forestry and arboriculture — as a verb, to girdle (or ring-bark) means to completely remove a strip of bark, phloem, and cambium in a continuous ring around the circumference of a tree trunk, severing the downward transport of photosynthates and inevitably killing the tree.
  • Foundation garments and 20th-century fashion — in modern corsetry, a girdle refers to a flexible, elasticized undergarment (popular from the 1920s to the 1960s) worn around the waist, abdomen, and hips to flatten the stomach and mold the feminine figure.
  • Geography and planetary science — describes an encircling climatic or topographical zone, such as a "girdle of green forest," a "mountain girdle," or the equatorial belt.

Register and semantic resonance. Girdle seamlessly bridges medieval chivalric romance (the green girdle of Sir Gawain), sacred scripture ("girding one's loins"), precise vertebrate osteology, and lyrical floral aesthetics.
