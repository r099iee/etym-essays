- 14th to 15th Century (Late Middle English) - Existed in written records as *potage* (borrowed from Old French *potage*, literally "that which is boiled in a pot"), denoting an omnipresent peasant stew of boiled greens, beans, peas, and root vegetables thickened with grains.
- 16th Century (Tudor Era) - Undergoes a remarkable phonetic drift (rhotacism and consonantal confusion) where the intervocalic dental stop /t/ shifted to liquid /r/, transforming *potage* into *porrage* and *porridge*; simultaneously begins to narrow from vegetable stew toward thick boiled cereal grain porridge.
- 17th to 18th Century - Firmly established in Scotland and northern England as the primary dietary staple of rural and working-class families, celebrated as a source of physical vigor and defined satirically by Samuel Johnson in his 1755 Dictionary.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Popularized worldwide in children's folklore through Robert Southey's 1837 publication of *Goldilocks and the Three Bears*; concurrently institutionalized as the mandatory, unvarying subsistence diet in British prisons and workhouses.
- Mid-20th Century - Formalized in British criminal underworld argot as slang for a term of imprisonment (*"doing porridge"*), popularized internationally by the classic 1970s BBC television comedy *Porridge*.
- Contemporary Era - Reclaimed globally as a wholesome, nutrient-dense breakfast superfood, celebrated for complex carbohydrates and soluble beta-glucan fibers in modern wellness culture.
