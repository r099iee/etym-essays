Remuneration is payment for work or services — reward, recompense, compensation in money or its equivalent. The word is formal, legal-commercial, and slightly abstract: it names the return due for labour or risk rather than casual 'pay' alone. It is the noun of the verb remunerate ('to pay, reward, repay') and the abstract quality of remunerative ('profitable, worth the payment'). When Bilbo asks about 'risks, out-of-pocket expenses, time required and remuneration,' he is translating adventure into the ledger language of a respectable hobbit: what am I paid, and will I come back alive? The tone is comic bureaucratic elevation; the polysyllable sits beside map and dragon like a contract clause in a fairy-tale. The narration's gloss — 'by which he meant: "What am I going to get out of it?"' — confirms that remuneration here is pure self-interest framed as professional due. Primary senses
  • Payment for work, services, or office; the return due for labour or risk, in money or its equivalent — the central and dominant sense (a remuneration package, fair remuneration, remuneration for services rendered).
  • Formal compensation — recompense, reward, requital for effort, service, or loss; close to compensation but emphasizing the payment due for work done rather than for damage sustained.
  • Historical sense — a reward or requital in a general, sometimes non-monetary sense; in older prose remuneration could be a gift or return of any kind, though the monetary sense has dominated since the 16th century.
Polysemy and modern technical uses
  • Corporate governance and HR — in modern business English, remuneration is the standard formal term for all pay and benefits: the remuneration committee of a board of directors oversees executive pay, and a remuneration package bundles salary, bonuses, pension, and perquisites into one official aggregate.
  • Legal and contractual usage — remuneration appears in contracts, employment agreements, and statutes as the precise, enforceable word for payment due; its formality is its function, ruling out ambiguity about what is owed.
  • Executive and public-sector context — the word's official tone makes it the preferred term for discussing the pay of senior managers, directors, politicians, and officials, where 'pay' would seem too plain or too casual.
  • Etymology as semantic bridge — the word descends from Latin remūnerārī 'to repay, reward,' built on mūnus 'gift, duty, service, office.' The root fuses giving and obligation: in Roman thinking a service (munus) and its return gift were the same relation seen from two sides, and modern remuneration inherits exactly that 'due return for service' idea.
  • Related family, not synonyms — munificent (generous in giving), municipal (of a town's official duties), immunity (exemption from duty) all descend from the same mūnus base; they illuminate the root but are sister words, not substitutes for remuneration.
  • False friend — do not confuse remuneration with enumeration (a listing or counting), a different word from Latin enumerāre 'to count out,' despite the partial resemblance.
Near-synonyms and boundaries
  • Pay and wages are plainer and often regular employment money; a salary is fixed periodic pay; a fee is payment for a professional service; a reward may be a prize or recompense not strictly contractual; compensation often stresses making good a loss; recompense is close but more literary. Remuneration keeps the formal payment-for-service colour — exactly Bilbo's borrowed 'business' register.
  • The card preserves the formal noun REMUNERATION, not the plainer cut, pay, or share: the polysyllable is the whole joke, a respectable hobbit's contract word for what he hopes to get out of the quest — if he lives.
