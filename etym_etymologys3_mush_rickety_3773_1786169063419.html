THE EVOLUTION OF 'MUSH'

The history of mush is the history of American hunger and of a word that could not keep its shape. It begins with 'mash', the ancient Germanic verb for crushing into pulp: Old English māscan 'to mash, to pound soft', built on a reconstructed Proto-Germanic *maisk-, itself placed by most etymologists under the Indo-European root *meik-, 'to mix' — the same root as Latin miscēre, 'to mix', and English 'mix' itself. Mash is what you do to grain and fruit before they become food; and 'mush' is, literally, mashed. The word entered English in the 1670s as a dialectal variant of 'mash' — perhaps reinforced by the German Mus, 'soft food, porridge', that Pennsylvania's settlers brought with them — and it attached itself to the New World's great staple, maize.

  • Colonial and frontier staple: cornmeal mush — yellow cornmeal boiled in salted water or milk — fed the American soldier, settler and frontiersman for two centuries. With hoecake, johnnycake and hominy it formed the maize economy of early America. Chilled leftover mush, sliced and fried in lard, became 'fried mush', still a treasured comfort food of the American South and Appalachia.
  • The spread into matter: because mush was soft, shapeless and yielding, the noun spread in the 19th century to any wet, crushed, formless mass — spring-thaw snow, the break-up ice of rivers, rotted vegetation, mud. The word became the general name for matter that has lost all internal structure, that yields, flows and leaves a stain.
  • The dog-sled command: in the gold-rush and Yukon years of the late 19th century, 'Mush!' became the cry to a sled team — probably a corruption of French marchez!, 'walk!', 'go!', slurred into the native word. The musher, the Iditarod and the call 'mush, mush' fixed the interjection forever in the vocabulary of the North.
  • The leap into feeling: the same softness moved from matter to emotion in the 19th–20th centuries. 'Mushy' came to mean cloyingly, gushingly sentimental — 'mushy love letters', 'mushy talk' — and 'mush' as a noun condemned undemanding, soft-headed entertainment and mawkish romance. The bridge is the image of the shapeless mass: what dissolves without resistance, in the mouth or in the heart, is 'mush'. What the audience cannot chew, it is spoon-fed — and the critic calls it mush.
  • Literary attestations: the American humorists of the 19th century — from the frontier tall-tale writers to Mark Twain — served 'mush' up as the very emblem of plain pioneer fare, while modern writers of the South (from Erskine Caldwell to the Appalachian novelists) keep it alive as a word of homely nourishment and hard times.
  • Modern balance: today 'mush' is simultaneously the plainest of rustic foods, a geophysical term for break-up ice, the rallying cry of dog-sledding, and a dismissive word for sentimentality. No other English word holds porridge, polar ice, a sled command and gushing romance inside a single syllable; and in the Vance passage the word reverts to its most elemental meaning — the soft, formless food that cannot even be eaten, sliding down the creature's glazed skin.


THE EVOLUTION OF 'RICKETY'

Rickety is a medical word that escaped the clinic and colonised the furniture of the world. Its story begins in Greek anatomy. Greek rhachis means 'spine, ridge, backbone' — the bony axis of the body, and by extension any ridged structure. From it the Greek physicians built rhachitis, 'the disease of the spine', the name of an inflammation or softening of the backbone and its neighbours. When the great childhood disease of softened, bowed bones swept through the sunless cities of early modern Europe, English medicine of the 1630s took up the Greek term — and mangled it. The learned form rachitis was spoken, spelled and folk-etymologised into 'rickets', a corruption helped along by the dialect word 'wrick', 'to twist, to sprain', and by old dialect uses of 'rickets' for convulsive twitching. The very sound of the new word suggested a disease that wrenches the body out of shape.

  • The disease and its age: rickets is the deficiency of vitamin D — the sunshine vitamin — in which growing bone fails to calcify and harden. It became a mass affliction of children in the sooty, foggy industrial cities of 18th–19th century Britain, where factory children lived in permanent twilight; it was known across Europe as 'the English disease'. Its victims grew pigeon-chested, pot-bellied and bow-legged, their softened frames literally too weak to bear their own weight — the classic 'rickety' child of Victorian reform literature.
  • Birth of the adjective: 'rickety' appears in English in the 1720s meaning simply 'affected with rickets'. It is at first a purely clinical word: a rickety child is a child with the bone disease.
  • The great metaphor: by the 1840s the adjective began its escape from the clinic. Any loosely-jointed, ill-put-together, wobbling structure was now 'rickety' — a rickety chair, a rickety fence, a rickety boat, a rickety staircase, a rickety old man, a rickety government. The transfer is exact and cruel: the furniture trembles like the soft-boned child; its joints are loose like the sockets of a rachitic skeleton; it will give way under any weight it can no longer bear. The disease's whole pathology was carried into metaphor, and the word became the standard English adjective for precarious, creaking, about-to-collapse structure.
  • Modern usage: the medical sense survives in paediatric writing — 'rachitic' is the technical adjective, 'rickety' the lay one — but the common word has long been the metaphor. 'Rickety' today means shaky, unsound, liable to collapse, whether of furniture, bodies, bridges or institutions; we speak of 'a rickety economy', 'a rickety peace', a 'rickety' regime.
  • In the Vance context: when Vance writes of the homunculus's 'rickety frame', the whole pathology is latent in the word — a body whose bones cannot hold it up, the perfect complement to the mush that cannot nourish it. The creature is diagnosed in two words: soft pulp outside, weak bones inside. The word has carried its ancient spine into every wobbling chair and failing enterprise of modern English, and it has never quite forgotten the child it was named for.
