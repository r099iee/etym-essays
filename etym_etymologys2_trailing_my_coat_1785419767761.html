TRAILING MY COAT contains three root words; each is given its own complete linear chain. === ROOT 1: TRAILING === PIE *tragh- / *dhragh- "to drag, draw, pull along" (a well-established root of dragging; attested in the Germanic branch and connected with Latin trahere, though the exact PIE shape is debated — some set *tragh-, some *dhragh-) → Latin trahere "to drag, draw, pull" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Vulgar Latin *tragulare "to drag a net, sled, or trawl" (derivative of trahere via the noun trahea/tragula "a sledge"; not directly attested, hence the asterisk) → Old French trailer "to drag, tow, let trail" (attested 12th c.) → Middle English trailen "to drag, draw behind" (attested c. 1300) → English trail (verb) "to drag or draw along the ground; to hang down and sweep" (14th c.) → English trailing (present participle of trail; 14th–15th c.) — the participial form used in this idiom. Note on debate: whether the Germanic *trail- and Latin trahere share a common PIE root *tragh- is accepted in outline but debated in exact form; some authorities prefer to treat the Germanic verb as independently formed from an expressive base. === ROOT 2: MY === PIE *me- (first-person singular oblique/possessive pronoun, "me, my"; the genitive *mene is the direct ancestor) → Proto-Germanic *minaz "my, mine" (first-person singular possessive; attested by its reflexes) → Old English min "my, mine" (attested 8th–9th c.) → Middle English mi / my (unstressed form, 12th–13th c.; the form min was retained before vowels and final position) → English my (first-person singular possessive determiner; modern spelling fixed in the 16th c.) Note: this chain is entirely uncontroversial; every stage is attested. The PIE reconstruction *me-/*mene is standard (Pokorny *me-, de Vaan on meus). === ROOT 3: COAT === No PIE ancestry. The word is a Germanic loanword. Frankish *kotta "coarse woollen cloth, shaggy garment" (attested by its Romance reflexes; the Frankish form itself is reconstructed, hence the asterisk) → Old French cote "outer garment, tunic, coat" (attested 9th–10th c.) → Medieval Latin cotta "tunic, surplice" (church Latin, 9th c., a loan from the Germanic word) → Middle English cote / coat "outer garment" (attested c. 1300) → English coat "tailored outer garment" (14th c. onwards) → completes the phrase "trailing my coat" (idiomatic from the 19th c.) Note on the phrase as a whole: the idiom "to trail one's coat" (to provoke a fight by dragging a coat on the ground, daring passers-by to tread on it) is recorded from the early 19th century in Ireland and Britain; the individual etymologies of its three elements are independent and non-controversial. The participle trailing, the possessive my, and the Germanic-French coat each follow their own attested chain above.
