- 14th c. (Middle English) - Entered the English language exclusively denoting an 'infidel,' 'heathen,' or 'heretic'—someone who adhered to a false religion or rejected orthodox Christian doctrine, carrying severe ecclesiastical condemnation during the Crusades and medieval theological disputes.
- Late 16th c. (Elizabethan Era) - Underwent a major semantic shift from religious heresy to general moral depravity, used by dramatists like Shakespeare to describe base, unprincipled rascals, villains, and scoundrels whose actions offended civil order and common morality.
- 18th & 19th c. (Victorian Era) - Solidified in formal prose and judicial rhetoric as a standard term for criminals, cutpurses, rioters, and unprincipled characters, retaining a elevated, reproachful register.
- 20th c. (Modern Era) - Broadened to encompass petty offenders, vandals, juvenile delinquents, and disruptive societal nuisances, frequently appearing in newspaper reports and municipal decrees.
- 21st c. (Contemporary) - Retained primarily in formal journalism, literary commentary, and elevated prose as a colorful, slightly archaic descriptor for antisocial mischief-makers, rogue financial actors, or cyber-criminals.
