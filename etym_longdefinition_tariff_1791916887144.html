Tariff is primarily a noun, with a minor verb use, denoting a published table or system of fixed charges. Its oldest English sense is simply "an arithmetical table" or "a schedule of rates", and every later meaning grows out of that tabular image: a list first, a legal instrument second, a tax third. In the passage from the reading — "Soon a regular tariff arose, determining how much must be paid for an eye, a tooth, an arm, or a life" — the reference is to a schedule of compensations: a fixed scale converting bodily injury into payable amounts. This is the word's earliest documented semantic territory, and it is closer to a modern price list or fines schedule than to a customs duty.

The context matters because the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BCE) is famous precisely for tabulating penalties: so many shekels of silver for an eye, so many for a bone, so many for a life according to the victim's rank. A "regular tariff" in this setting marks the moment when customary revenge is replaced by a published, predictable scale — law as a rate card. English speakers later transferred the same noun to the rate cards of the state (customs), of utilities, and of professions.

Primary modern senses

  • Customs duty on imported or exported goods. The dominant modern sense: a tax levied by a government on goods crossing a frontier, usually ad valorem (a percentage of value) or specific (a fixed sum per unit or weight). It may be protective, designed to shield domestic industry, or revenue-raising, designed chiefly to fill the treasury.
  • A schedule or system of such duties. "The tariff" can mean the whole body of rates a country applies, as in "a high tariff", "tariff reform", "tariff wall" or "tariff war". Here the word denotes a policy regime, not one tax.
  • A utility's published rate scale. Electricity, gas, water and telephone companies issue tariffs: the schedule setting standing charges, unit rates and time-of-use bands. British and Commonwealth usage is especially strong here ("an economy electricity tariff").
  • A table of professional or service charges. A taxi tariff, a notary's tariff, a lawyer's tariff of fees, a hotel tariff (a rack-rate card). This is the direct descendant of the sixteenth-century "arithmetical table" sense.
  • A scale of penalties or compensations. The legal-historical sense used in the source; it survives in phrases such as "the tariff for minor offences" and, in English criminal practice, the sentencing tariff, the range of sentences normally imposed for a given offence.

Figurative and extended uses

  • Any implicit price list. Journalists speak of "the tariff" of political compromise, of diplomatic concessions, or of social obligations: what one must pay, in reputational or moral currency, for a particular course of action.
  • "Tariff of" as a catalogue of costs. "The tariff of grief" or "the tariff of reform" treats non-monetary burdens as if they were dutiable items — a natural extension of the schedule metaphor.
  • Telecom tariff. In British, Irish, Australian and Indian English a mobile tariff is a price plan, whether pay-as-you-go or contract, including allowances for calls and data. This sense continues the utility-rate meaning and often sounds old-fashioned to American ears, where plan is usual.
  • Indian administrative usage. "Tariff order", "tariff petition" and "tariff rationalisation" belong to electricity regulation; the word is ordinary bureaucratic vocabulary there, not a foreign-policy term.

Verb use

To tariff is to fix a tariff on goods, or to charge according to a tariff. It is uncommon but standard in trade and transport writing ("tariffed services", "tariffing arrangements"). The verb is denominal, formed directly from the noun, and rarely appears outside technical or administrative prose.

Register and collocations

In trade policy, tariff is neutral-technical but politically charged: "tariff barrier", "tariff escalation", "retaliatory tariff", "tariff-free access", "tariff quota". In domestic regulation it is dry and bureaucratic: "tariff structure", "tariff schedule", "tariff class". The adjective is tariff (attributive) or, rarely, tariffless. Unlike tax, the word is not used of income or sales taxes; unlike duty, it can name the whole schedule rather than a single charge.

Why the word matters here

The reading uses tariff at the exact point where law becomes numerical. A tariff turns an injury into a price and a feud into a payment; it is the technology of composition. Understanding that origin illuminates the modern customs sense too: both are instruments that publish a rate in advance, converting a discretionary confrontation into a calculable transaction.
