- 1726 — Swiftian Genesis: Jonathan Swift invents the noun Yahoo in Book IV of Gulliver's Travels to designate a filthy, savage humanoid species governed by rational horses, encapsulating human depravity stripped of moral reason.
- Mid-18th c. — Augustan Political Invective: Immediately adopted by British satirists, poets, and essayists (including Alexander Pope and Samuel Johnson) as a cutting metaphor for corrupt politicians, venal courtiers, and brutish parliamentary mobs.
- 19th c. — Generalization into a Common Noun: The capitalized racial proper noun softens into a common noun (yahoo), shifting from a specific literary monster into a general term for any rude, uncultivated, boorish, or anti-intellectual person in Victorian social discourse.
- Late 19th to Early 20th c. — American Frontier Vernacular: American colloquial English embraces yahoo for rowdy frontiersmen, rough cowhands, or loud rural figures; concurrently, the expressive exclamation ‘Yahoo!’ arises as a triumphant shout.
- Late 20th c. — Cyberculture Re-appropriation: Stanford graduate students David Filo and Jerry Yang name their internet portal Yahoo! (backronymed as ‘Yet Another Hierarchical Officious Oracle’), embracing the term's scrappy, anti-establishment hacker spirit.
- 21st c. — Ubiquitous Polemic: Continues in political journalism and cultural commentary as an indictment of demagoguery, mob rule, online trolling, and belligerent anti-rationalism.
