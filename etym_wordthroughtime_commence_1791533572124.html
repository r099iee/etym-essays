- 13th–14th c. — Middle English adoption from Anglo-Norman comencer: established as an elevated, courtly, and administrative verb, distinguishing chivalric, legal, and formal narrative from plain native beginnen.
- 14th–15th c. — Academic consolidation: adopted within medieval universities (Oxford, Cambridge, Paris) for the formal inception of a scholar into a master’s or doctor’s degree, originating the academic noun commencement.
- 16th–17th c. — Early Modern legal and liturgical formalisation: entrenched in statutory law, parliamentary procedure, and liturgical rubrics as the precise technical term for when an Act of Parliament, trial, or solemn rite took legal effect.
- 18th–19th c. — Victorian stylistic marker: widely favored in elevated prose, official despatches, and polite society; occasionally parodied by satirists as a pompous or euphemistic substitute for plain Saxon speech.
- 20th–21st c. — Institutional and legal standard: strictly delimited in contemporary English to formal ceremonies, legal contracts (e.g. “the lease shall commence”), military operations, and corporate decrees, retaining an unmistakable aura of dignity and procedural gravity.
