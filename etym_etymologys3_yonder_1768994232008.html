The biography of "yonder" is a biography of pointing. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European demonstrative stem *h₁ey- / *i-, "this, that" — the linguistic gesture of the finger extended into space. From that stem the daughters of Indo-European made their pointing words: Sanskrit ayám, "this one here," whose modern descendant is Hindi yah; Latin is, ea, id, "that, it," which gave English the psychoanalytic id; and, in the Germanic world, a new formation on the same deictic base, Proto-Germanic *jainaz, "that one over there."

The Germanic vocabulary of distance. The Germanic branch built from *jainaz a whole family of distance-words:

  • Gothic jains, "that yonder," with its adverb jaindre, "thither, over there" — the closest structural cousin of the English word, deictic stem plus a "there" ending;
  • German jener, "that one (over there)," still standard;
  • Dutch ginds, "yonder";
  • Old English geon, "that," and geond, "across, beyond, over there."

Into English. It is from geond that the English word descends. Around 1300, Middle English formed yonder by adding the adverbial or comparative suffix -er to the old adverb, producing a form whose shape matches Gothic jaindre almost morpheme for morpheme: a deictic stem plus a "thither/there" ending. At the same time geon gave the clipped yon and yond, and begeondan gave beyond, "on the far side." The semantic career of the word has been remarkably stable: from the beginning it meant "over there, in view but at a distance," and that is what it still means, even as standard English has largely let it go.

Survival and register. The word's fortunes are dialectal and literary.

  • Dialectal survival. Southern American speech keeps "up yonder" and "over yonder," often of heaven; Scots keeps it in ballads ("yont"); Yorkshire and other northern English dialects retain it.
  • Literary use. Poets and novelists reach for "yonder" precisely because it carries a flavor of distance — geographic, temporal, and moral — that a bare "there" cannot; it is the word of the glance that travels out to a far object and rests there.
  • Popular modern use. "The wild blue yonder," from the 1939 US Army Air Corps song "Off We Go Into the Wild Blue Yonder," made the phrase a colloquial name for the sky and the beyond.

In the passage. McCarthy's sentence exploits exactly that double distance. "Into that hellhole yonder" points to a visible, removed place — the tent packed with the wet and bathless — and the word's folk quality makes the pointing concrete, a man's hand and chin toward the ruin. But because "yonder" is the word of things seen across space, it also creates the moral gap between the speaker and the place he condemns: the hellhole is yonder because it is other, separate, a place to be pointed at rather than entered. The whole history of the word — the PIE gesture, the Germanic stare across the plain, the frontier dialect that kept the ancient pointing alive — is compressed into that single adverb, and in the passage it does what it has always done: it holds the world at arm's length and names the distance.
