Rohiṇī (proper noun, feminine; Sanskrit: रोहिणी, rohiṇī, literally "the red one" or "the ruddy cow") — In Hindu astronomy, classical mythology, Vedic cosmology, and Sanskrit epic poetry, the fourth of the twenty-seven (or twenty-eight) lunar mansions (nakṣatras), personified as a celestial goddess and the supreme, most beloved consort of Candra (or Soma), the Moon god. In the physical cosmos, Rohiṇī corresponds to the bright red giant star Aldebaran (Alpha Tauri) and the surrounding V-shaped cluster of the Hyades in the zodiacal constellation of Taurus (Vṛṣabha). Within classical Indian literature, Rohiṇī stands alongside Arundhatī and Sītā as an eternal cultural archetype of conjugal fidelity, beauty, maternal grace, and emotional devotion.

Primary mythological sense — the favored consort of the Moon god. According to the Purāṇas and the Mahābhārata, the twenty-seven daughters of the celestial progenitor Dakṣa were married to King Candra on the condition that he treat them all with equal affection. However, enchanted by the peerless charm, elegance, and tenderness of Rohiṇī, Candra spent his nights almost exclusively in her celestial mansion. Consumed by jealousy, her twenty-six sisters appealed to their father Dakṣa, who cursed Candra to be stricken with consumption (kṣaya), causing the moon to wither away. Only after the intervention of Lord Śiva was the curse modified, establishing the perpetual waxing and waning of the moon (the śukla and kṛṣṇa pakṣa) as Candra travels through the zodiac, eternally returning to the passionate embrace of Rohiṇī.

Epic simile and literary archetype in the Rāmāyaṇa. In Sanskrit epic poetry, particularly Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa, Rohiṇī functions as the supreme standard of comparison (upamāna) for an inseparable, devoted wife. When Princess Sītā resolutely abandons the imperial comforts of Ayodhyā to accompany Rāma into fourteen years of perilous wilderness exile, the poet repeatedly compares her to Rohiṇī following the hare-marked moon (śaśāṅka) across dark skies. The simile conveys not merely spousal companionship, but an intrinsic, cosmic harmony: just as the red asterism cannot exist independently of the lunar cycle, so the ideal wife's spiritual destiny is inextricably woven with that of her lord.

Polysemic extensions and secondary mythological applications:

  • Mother of Balarāma and Subhadrā — In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Harivaṃśa, and Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Rohiṇī is the senior wife of Vasudeva (the sister of King Yaśodā of Gokula). When King Kaṃsa imprisoned Vasudeva and Devakī in Mathurā, slaying their newborn infants, the seventh embryo (an incarnation of the cosmic serpent Śeṣa) was miraculously transferred by Yogamāyā from Devakī's womb into Rohiṇī's womb in Gokula, where she safely gave birth to Balarāma. Later, she also gave birth to Princess Subhadrā.
  • Vedic bovine and agricultural symbolism — Derived as the feminine of rohita ("red, reddish"), rohiṇī in the Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda designates a red cow, the red morning dawn (Uṣas), and the fertile earthly mother who yields milk, ghee, and life-sustaining rain. In rural agrarian ritual, Rohiṇī is invoked as a presiding deity of fertility, pastoral prosperity, and bovine health.
  • Astrological significance and Kṛṣṇa's nativity — In Vedic astrology (Jyotiṣa), the Rohiṇī asterism is ruled by Prajāpati (Brahmā), symbolizing creative potency, sensuality, material abundance, and artistic temperament. Persons born under Rohiṇī are traditionally considered charismatic, gentle, and blessed with aesthetic taste. Most crucially, Lord Kṛṣṇa took birth on the eighth day of the dark fortnight of Bhādrapada (Janmāṣṭamī) exactly when the moon entered the Rohiṇī nakṣatra at midnight.
  • Ayurvedic pharmacopeia and medical terminology — In classical Āyurveda (such as the Caraka Saṃhitā and Suśruta Saṃhitā), rohiṇī designates several medicinal plants known for their red-tinted bark, flowers, or roots (notably Picrorhiza kurroa, Terminalia chebula, and Soymida febrifuga). Furthermore, rohiṇī names a virulent, inflammatory throat disease (diphtheria-like pharyngeal ulceration) characterized by red, swollen fleshy growths that constrict the airway.

Socio-cultural register and poetic collocations. The name evokes supreme poetic elegance, astronomical reverence, and tender affection throughout South Asian languages. Frequent collocations include Rohiṇī-candra (the celestial lovers), Rohiṇī-tanaya (son of Rohiṇī, an epithet of Balarāma), and Rohiṇī-nakṣatra (the auspicious fourth asterism). In classical Sanskrit dramas (such as Kālidāsa's Mālavikāgnimitra), it symbolizes unshakeable royal preference and enduring romantic favor.
