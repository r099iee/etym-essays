In Walter Pater's aesthetic manifesto, nicety names the fine, almost surgical discrimination the critic must bring to a work of art: the capacity to 'disengage' pure aesthetic virtue from the coarser matter with which it is mixed. Pater's critic needs nicety as a chemist needs precision of assay; without it, the unique 'virtue' of the object dissolves into commoner elements and is never truly known. The tone is neither pedantic fussiness nor vague sensibility: it is exact, cultivated attentiveness—the intellectual tact that separates a picture's essential beauty from biography, fashion, medium-accident, and moralizing noise. Polysemy: the full range of senses, present and historical.
  • Fine precision; exactness of discrimination: the core modern sense—'the legal argument was constructed with such nicety that few could find any flaw in it.'
  • A subtle point or minute detail: usually plural—'the niceties of argument / etiquette / technique,' i.e., the fine points of a craft or social code.
  • Idiom 'to a nicety': meaning exactly, with perfect precision—'the shoe fits to a nicety'; a fixed adverbial phrase.
  • Delicacy, daintiness, or fastidiousness of manner: an older but still current sense of refined, slightly finicky care.
  • Obsolete sense 'folly, foolishness': the noun's earliest English meaning (Middle English nicetee), now wholly extinct; it was the noun of nice in its oldest sense 'foolish, simple.'
  • Legal and formal usage: 'legal niceties,' 'constitutional niceties'—the exact, sometimes vexatious distinctions of law and procedure.
  • Aesthetic-critical term: in Pater's prose, the refined judgment that separates essence from accident in a work of beauty; a technical-sounding word for cultivated attention.
  • Cognitive-refined sense: nicety as a fineness of judgment or wit, applied to the critic's power of discernment rather than to mere technical skill.
Semantic boundaries with near-synonyms:
  • Precision: stresses measurable exactness and may be coldly technical; nicety adds delicacy of taste and refined discrimination.
  • Subtlety: emphasizes elusive or understated quality but can imply slyness or mere cleverness; nicety is steadier and more fastidious.
  • Refinement: leans toward social polish or purification of manners; nicety is cognitive and critical, a fineness of judgment.
  • Discrimination: broader and more formal; nicety keeps the older sense of dainty, particular care over minute distinctions.
  • Scrupulousness: carries moral anxiety; nicety in this literary setting is aesthetic, not ethical.
  • Nuance: names the shade of meaning itself; nicety names the attentive act or quality of noticing it.
Collocations and register favor scholarly and belletristic prose: 'a nicety of judgment,' 'to a nicety,' 'the niceties of argument/etiquette/technique,' 'with great nicety.' Register is elevated, slightly archaic or formal in modern speech, and perfectly at home in nineteenth-century aesthetic criticism. The historical bridge is one of the most dramatic in English: Latin nescius 'ignorant' became Old French nice 'foolish,' and Middle English nice slid from foolishness through fastidiousness to delicacy and precision, so that the word for 'not knowing' ended up naming the most knowing kind of attention. Do not reduce nicety to modern 'niceness' (pleasantness); that later drift is foreign to Pater's usage.
