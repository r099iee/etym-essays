- Old English to 14th Century - The noun *piþa* (*pith*) designated the soft, spongy tissue in the center of plant stems or bone marrow, representing life force, botanical substance, and biological vitality.
- Late 14th to 15th Century - The adjective *pithy* (*pithi*) emerged first in literal botanical descriptions before rapidly taking on the figurative meaning of "physically robust, full of vital sap, muscular, and sturdy."
- 16th Century (Tudor Humanism) - Adopted by Renaissance rhetoricians and moralists to describe writing and speech that was "full of intellectual marrow, weight, and spiritual substance," praised by Shakespeare as enterprises of great pith and moment.
- 17th to 18th Century - Synthesized the dual virtues of brevity and weight under the influence of neoclassical Latin *brevitas*; a *pithy* remark was now expected to deliver maximum substance with minimum verbiage.
- 19th Century to Present - Universally acclaimed in literary criticism, political commentary, and everyday discourse as the gold standard for sharp, memorable, epigrammatic wit and concise intellectual clarity.
