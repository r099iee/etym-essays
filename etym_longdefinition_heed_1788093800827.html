Heed is both a noun meaning "careful attention, notice or regard" and a verb meaning "to pay careful attention to, take notice of, or act upon". In the source passage from Tolkien's The Hobbit, the dragon Smaug swoops "reckless in his rage, taking no heed to turn his scaly sides towards his foes": the word expresses the attentive, protective watchfulness he abandons — he pays no attention to defending himself, caring only to set the town ablaze.

The word is ancient and purely Germanic. Old English hēdan meant "to guard, protect, watch over, take care of", and is cognate with Old Saxon hōdian, Old High German huoten and modern German hüten "to guard, tend, herd". The deep Indo-European ancestry is debated and cannot be securely reconstructed; the Germanic base *hōd- is sometimes connected with a proposed root meaning "to shelter, protect", but external cognates are uncertain. The noun heed was formed from the verb in Middle English in the 13th century.

1. The noun — careful attention

  • Take heed: to be careful, watchful, cautious ("take heed lest you fall") — a formula with deep biblical resonance (Wycliffe's Bible, the Authorized Version: "take heed", "give heed").
  • Pay heed / give heed (to): to pay attention, listen, take notice ("they paid little heed to the warnings").
  • No heed / little heed: disregard, inattention ("taking no heed" — exactly the Tolkien construction).
  • Register: formal-to-neutral; in modern speech it survives mainly in fixed phrases and in literary or journalistic prose, and can sound archaic or elevated if used loosely.

2. The verb — to attend, notice, obey

  • To heed a warning / advice / a call: to take notice of it and act on it; to obey or follow ("heed the weather warning", "heed your doctor's advice").
  • It is often used in the negative or the imperative ("heed my words"), which reflects its heritage: heeding is the vigilant behaviour of a guardian, and the word most often appears where vigilance is being urged or neglected.
  • Heedful (adj.): attentive, mindful, careful; heedless (adj.): careless, inattentive, reckless; heedlessly (adv.); heedlessness (n.) — the negative forms are as old as the positive ones in English (Middle English heedles).

3. The semantic bridge — from guarding to attending

  • The Old English verb hēdan meant primarily "to guard, watch over, keep safe" (the shepherd's or sentinel's task; German hüten still means "to tend herds, watch over").
  • Guarding requires constant, focused attention; so "to guard" slides naturally into "to watch", "to observe", "to keep in view", and finally "to pay attention to, take notice of".
  • The reverse direction also holds: if you heed a person or a warning, you are in effect guarding yourself against the danger they represent — attention as self-protection.
  • By Middle English the noun "heed" (care, attention) was in use, and the modern verb had settled into its present sense of attentive regard plus compliance.

4. Modern usage notes

  • In contemporary English heed is slightly formal but fully alive: official notices ("please heed the signage"), safety campaigns, journalism ("the government failed to heed the scientists' warnings") and literary prose all use it.
  • It is not interchangeable with the merely sensory "notice": to heed is to notice and to weigh, remember or obey; heed implies consequence and care, where "notice" implies mere perception.
  • The noun is now almost confined to the set phrases "take heed", "pay heed", "give heed" and their negatives — a good example of a word whose modern life is phrase-bound.

5. Summary

Heed is attention with a bodyguard: it carries a thousand years of guarding, watching and protecting inside a short, plain Germanic word. Whether as noun or verb, it denotes the careful, consequential attention that keeps people safe — and the dragon who takes no heed is precisely the one who abandons all self-protection in his rage.
