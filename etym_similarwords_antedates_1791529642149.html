• Predates: Similar because it means “is earlier than” and is the commonest everyday synonym; differs because it is neutral and modern, lacking the archival implication of deliberate backdating that antedate can carry.
  • Precedes: Similar because it orders two events in time; differs because it is more general and can describe spatial order (“the verb precedes the object”) as well as chronology, and it does not imply dating documents.
  • Antecedes: Similar as a formal Latinate verb meaning “goes before”; differs because it is rarer, often logical or causal (“antecedent conditions”), and is not used of assigning dates.
  • Foredates: Similar because it means “dates from an earlier time than”; differs because it is chiefly Scots and legal, very rare in general prose, and stresses the date of origin rather than comparative age.
  • Foreruns: Similar because it means “goes before” and can suggest preparation; differs because it is literary and archaic, implies a herald or precursor, and never means backdating.
  • Anticipates: Similar because what comes first can seem to announce what follows; differs because it adds expectation or foresight, not mere temporal priority.
  • Foreshadows: Similar in the figurative sense of prefiguring; differs because it is literary and implies a deliberate authorial or symbolic hint, not a factual date.
  • Backdates: Similar because it specifically means assigning an earlier date; differs because it almost always implies intentional falsification and is the direct practical synonym of the documentary sense only.
  • Misdates: Similar because it concerns a wrong date; differs because the error may be in either direction and need not be earlier, whereas antedate specifies “earlier.”
  • Outdates: Similar in comparative chronology; differs because it means “makes obsolete” or “is older than and supersedes,” a different causative sense.
