Linear derivation chain for the lexical core FIELD of the compound AFIELD: • Proto-Indo-European *pelh₂- (also reconstructed *pel-, *pele-) — 'flat, to spread; broad' — the root of flat, spread surfaces. (Reconstruction; the precise PIE shape is debated, see note below.) • Proto-Germanic *felþuz — 'open land, flat land, field' — the flat, unforested expanse opposed to the wood. (1st millennium BCE; reconstruction.) • Old English feld — 'open country, unwooded ground, field' — attested from the 9th century, e.g. in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Old English glosses. • Middle English feld, feeld — 'field, open land' — 12th–15th century. • English field — the independent noun, 16th century onward. Linear derivation chain for the prefix a- of AFIELD: • Old English on — preposition 'in, on, at' — attested Old English; Germanic cognate of modern English on. • Middle English a- (unstressed, reduced from on/an) — prefix 'on, in' — seen in afoot, abed, akin, afield. (11th–15th century.) Merged compounding of the two chains: • Middle English a feld(e) / on felde — 'on the field, in the field' — the syntactically transparent phrase from which the adverb hardened. (12th–15th century.) • English AFIELD — the fused adverb 'in the field; at or to a distance from home' — modern English — AFIELD. Note on the debate: the derivation of Germanic *felþuz from a PIE root meaning 'flat' is generally accepted, but the exact PIE etymon is contested; a minority view connects field to the root *pléth₂- 'broad' (as in Greek πλατύς platús 'broad'), rather than to *pelh₂- 'flat, spread'. The fusion of a- + field, by contrast, is undisputed and is fully transparent in the Middle English phrase on felde, so the final compounding stage is certain even though the deepest PIE stage is debated.
