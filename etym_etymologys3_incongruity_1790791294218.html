The historical evolution of the word incongruity reflects a fascinating journey from Roman jurisprudence and scholastic grammar into the heights of Enlightenment aesthetic philosophy, modern cognitive science, and avant-garde literature.

1. Latin Origins and the Verb Congruere: The ultimate base of incongruity is the Latin verb congruere, meaning 'to come together', 'to meet', 'to coincide', or 'to agree'. The exact Proto-Indo-European root of congruere remains subject to scholarly debate among historical linguists (some connecting it tentatively to *gʷer- 'to fall together, rush', while others treat it as an isolated Latin formation). In Classical Latin, congruere described physical objects meeting harmoniously, geometric shapes coinciding perfectly, or political agreements arriving at mutual consensus. By prefixing the negative particle in- ('not, un-'), Latin formed the adjective incongruus, signifying 'inconsistent', 'unsuitable', 'discordant', or 'out of proportion'.

2. Late Latin Abstract Nominalization (incongruitas): During the transition from Classical to Late Latin (c. 4th–5th centuries CE), Christian patristic writers and late Roman jurists derived the abstract quality noun incongruitas from incongruus using the suffix -itas. In Late Latin legal codices and theological tracts, incongruitas denoted a failure of legal parity, an unseemly contradiction in testimony, or an improper theological formulation that lacked doctrinal symmetry.

3. Medieval Scholasticism and Middle French Incongruité: Throughout the Middle Ages, scholastic philosophers and grammarians preserved incongruitas to describe syntactic errors—phrases where nouns and adjectives failed to agree in gender, number, or case (known in medieval pedagogy as incongrua oratio). In the 15th century, the term entered Middle French as incongruité, gradually drifting from technical grammatical blunders to social faux pas, unseemly vulgarities, and violations of courtly decorum.

4. Introduction into Early Modern English: The word was assimilated into Early Modern English in the 1530s (during the reign of Henry VIII) as incongruity. Early English Renaissance writers used it across two main registers: first, in formal rhetoric and theology to point out flaws, self-contradictions, and absurdities in an opponent's doctrinal treatises; and second, in social satire to ridicule courtly upstarts whose pretentious dress or crude table manners clashed jarringly with their claimed status.

5. The 18th- and 19th-Century Philosophical Revolution: Kant, Schopenhauer, and Humor: The most dramatic conceptual elevation of incongruity occurred during the European Enlightenment and the 19th century. Philosophers moved beyond treating incongruity as a mere flaw to be eliminated, identifying it instead as the profound engine of human laughter and aesthetic experience. In the Critique of Judgment (1790), Immanuel Kant famously asserted that laughter is an affection arising from the sudden transformation of a strained expectation into nothing—a pure perception of cognitive incongruity. Arthur Schopenhauer expanded this in The World as Will and Representation (1819), arguing that the phenomenon of laughter always signifies the sudden apprehension of an incongruity between a universal concept and the real, concrete objects thought through it.

6. 20th-Century Literary Absurdism and Poststructuralism: In 20th-century literature and critical theory (exemplified by Samuel Beckett, Albert Camus, and Roland Barthes in the quote from The Pleasure of the Text), incongruity was elevated into an existential condition. In Barthes's formulation, modern consciousness embraces incongruity—refusing the terror of rigid consistency and dogmatic logic—thereby liberating human thought from the tyranny of monolithic classifications.
