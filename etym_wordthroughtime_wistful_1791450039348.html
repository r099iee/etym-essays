- 1610s: wistful is coined from wist (the old past tense of wit, “to know”) plus -ful, meaning “attentive, intent, closely observant” — a word about concentrated looking, not sadness.
- Late 17th century: the sense of earnest attentiveness shades into desire; to look wistfully is to look longingly at what one wants.
- 1710s: the modern sense “yearning, regretfully longing” is firmly attested; the word becomes a favourite of poetry and sentimental prose.
- 19th century: wistful flourishes in Romantic and Victorian literature as the adjective for tender, resigned longing — autumn, old houses, lost loves.
- 20th century: the word settles into its modern literary register, often describing smiles, eyes, and memories; wistfulness becomes a recognized aesthetic mood.
- 21st century: still current and emotionally precise, used in memoir, criticism, and advertising to evoke bittersweet nostalgia; the archaic “attentive” sense is entirely obsolete.
