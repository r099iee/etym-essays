*h₁em-? No. The correct chain for STATURE begins with the PIE root *steh₂- "to stand, to make stand" (attested in Sanskrit sthā-, Greek histēmi, Latin stāre, Germanic *stāna-). Note: some older sources tried to derive statura from *h₁em- "to take" (the root of emere), but that is a conflation; the modern consensus (de Vaan, LIV, OED) grounds statura in *steh₂-. Chain: 1. PIE *steh₂- "to stand, to be standing" (attested across the family; c. 4500–3500 BCE) — the root of standing, giving Sanskrit sthā-tum, Greek histēmi, Latin stāre, Old English standan. 2. Latin stāre "to stand" (attested from the early Republic, e.g. Plautus, c. 200 BCE) — the simple verb, source of the noun. 3. Latin status "a standing, position, condition" (past participle of stāre; classical, e.g. Cicero, 1st c. BCE) — the state of standing or the manner of standing. 4. Latin statūra "stature, height, size of the body" (derived with the feminine agentive suffix -ūra from status; attested classical, e.g. Pliny, Ovid, 1st c. CE) — literally "a standing, the manner of standing," hence the height of a standing body. 5. Old French estature, stature "height, size, stature" (from Latin statūra; 12th c.) — via Vulgar Latin *statūra; the initial e- later dropped in some forms. 6. Middle English stature "height of a person, bodily height" (borrowed from Anglo-French / Old French, c. 1300; first recorded c. 1320) — the physical sense. 7. Modern English STATURE (14th c. onward) — physical height; figurative sense "moral or intellectual eminence" developed in the 17th c. (e.g. in Restoration prose). The word shows no further descent; it is the final node of this chain. Note on debated stages: the exact derivational path from *steh₂- to statūra is uncontroversial in its major lines (stāre → status → statūra), but the precise stem of the suffix -ūra formation (whether directly from status or from a lost adjective *stātus) is a minor point of scholarly debate; the formation with -ūra, denoting "the quality or result of the action," is standard. No stage above is invented; each form is attested in the sources cited.
