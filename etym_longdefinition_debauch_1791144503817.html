The noun debauch denotes a bout, spell, or episode of unrestrained, excessive indulgence in physical or sensual pleasures — most typically eating, drinking, sexual activity, or riotous partying. In the source sentence (“a wild __________” upon finding a carcass), it means an orgy-like gorge: primitive people falling on meat in a frenzy of consumption. The word carries a moralising undertone absent from neutral synonyms like feast or celebration: a debauch is indulgence judged as intemperate, dissolute, or corrupting.

As a verb, to debauch means to corrupt, seduce, or lead someone away from duty, virtue, or loyalty — to debauch a soldier (entice him to desert), to debauch a young mind (corrupt it), or reflexively to debauch oneself (give oneself over to dissipation). The noun debauchery names the habitual practice; a debauch is a single episode, and a debauchee is the person addicted to such episodes. Do not confuse the single-bout noun with the general-condition noun debauchery, though dictionaries record overlap.

  • Core noun sense — a bout of excess: “after Lent came a wild debauch”; “a weekend debauch of pizza and films.” Always bounded in time, always excessive by the speaker’s standard.
  • Verb sense — to corrupt or seduce from duty: “he was debauched by flattery and wine”; “to debauch the soldiery.” Now literary; everyday speech prefers corrupt or lead astray.
  • Debauchery (habitual system): a life of debauchery — sustained dissolution, as of Restoration rakes or the proverbial Roman emperor. A debauch is to debauchery what a spree is to prodigality.
  • Debauchee (person): a habitual libertine; historically “an old debauchee” meant a worn-out rake, with pity and contempt mixed.
  • Light/humorous modern extension: “a chocolate debauch,” “a Netflix debauch” — self-mocking use where the ‘corruption’ is trivial and affectionate. The moral charge is bleached but still flavours the joke: pleasure framed as guilty.
  • Literary and historical register: historians of morals write of “the debauches of the Regency” or “the camp was given up to debauch”; the word colours the scene with censorious relish rather than neutral description.
  • Polysemy bridge: all senses share the idea of being led away — from the workshop (original French image), from duty, from sobriety, from restraint. The noun narrowed from ‘any seduction from duty’ to ‘a seduction by pleasure,’ then to the episode of pleasure itself.

Grammatically, the noun is countable (“debatches” is vanishingly rare; the plural is debauches, pronounced with /ɪz/). It collocates with wild, drunken, midnight, weekend, and verbs like indulge in, go on, and recover from. Stylistically it is literary rather than colloquial: tabloids prefer binge, bender, orgy; a historian or novelist prefers debauch for period flavour. Note the spelling trap: debauch (bout), debauchery (habit), debauchee (person) — the final -ee marks the victim of the action, as in payee or refugee.
