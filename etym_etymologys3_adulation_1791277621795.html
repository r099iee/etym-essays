Adulation enters English as a learned borrowing with a remarkably stable meaning, but its journey from Latin barnyard image to modern celebrity criticism reveals shifts in politics, religion, and media.

1. Latin origins: tail-wagging and emperor-worship. The verb adulārī, "to fawn upon," is already pejorative in Classical Latin, used by Cicero and Livy for the behaviour of dogs toward masters transferred to humans toward the powerful. Ancient etymologists connected it to ad- "toward" + cauda / *ul- suggestive of tail, the image of a dog wagging its tail. Modern scholars (de Vaan, Ernout-Meillet) treat this as folk etymology and list adulārī as of unknown origin with no secure PIE root, a caution observed here. From it was derived adūlātiō, -ōnis (accusative adūlātiōnem), meaning the act of fawning. Crucially, Roman political language used adulatio for divine honours decreed to living emperors, already blending flattery and worship. The moral charge was contempt: to adulate was to abase oneself and corrupt the recipient.

  • adulārī (deponent verb): to fawn, cringe, flatter like an animal.
  • adūlātiō: abstract noun for the act, also imperial cult honours.
  • No verified PIE ancestor; tail-wagging link is suggestive but unproven.
  • Phonology: ad- + ulari with long ū, stress on penult in adūlātiōnem.

2. Medieval transmission. Late Latin and ecclesiastical writers preserved adulatio as a vice in catalogues of sins, opposed to vera laus (true praise). Old French adulacion (13th century) carried it into vernacular moral satire. Middle English adulacioun is first attested in the late 14th century in Chaucerian and Lollard contexts condemning courtly sycophants and friars. Spelling wavered between adulacion, adulacioun, adulation as English gradually restored the Latin -tio as -tion /-ʃən/.

  • Late Latin moralists: adulatio as courtly and clerical vice.
  • Old French mediation softens pronunciation to /adylasjɔ̃/.
  • Middle English borrowing c. 1380s in religious and political complaint.
  • Early Modern standardisation to adulation with Great Vowel Shift intact in suffix.

3. Modern semantic expansion to the multitude. From the 18th century, with newspapers, elections, theatre stars, and later pop celebrity, adulation is increasingly predicated not of a single courtier but of "the multitude," "the public," "the press." The source sentence — "The adulation of the multitude means very little" — is typical of Victorian and Edwardian scepticism toward mass enthusiasm. The meaning shifts subtly from calculated insincerity to uncritical irrationality: crowds may sincerely adore, but sincerely and foolishly. Twentieth-century media intensified this: fan adulation, media adulation, critical adulation. The word remains formal and condemnatory, a favourite of reviewers signalling that they stand apart from the herd. Its endurance owes to that useful double edge: it accuses the giver of servility and the receiver of vanity in a single stroke.
