- OE (8th–11th c.) — sprang, past of springan "to leap, burst forth, arise"; used of warriors leaping, water bursting, and things coming into being; vivid, physical, concrete
- ME (12th–15th c.) — sprang/spronge, past of springen; extends to growing plants, spreading customs, and originating stories; stays a plain strong past
- 16th–17th c. — literary explosion of senses: sprang to his feet, sprang from, sprang up; Elizabethan drama (Shakespeare, Marlowe) uses it for sudden emotion, deceit (spring a trap), and revelation
- 18th–19th c. — mechanical and industrial extensions: a trap springs, a coach-spring recoils, a leak springs; "sprang" remains the standard literary past while American speech begins to favor "sprung" informally
- 20th–21st c. — fixed idioms dominate: sprang to mind, sprang to life, sprang into action, sprang a surprise; also "spring for" (pay); in careful writing "sprang" is still the approved past, and in modern fantasy prose (Tolkien, The Hobbit, 1937) its old vividness is consciously revived
- Present — an enduring, instantly-recognized strong past tense whose abrupt, monosyllabic shape still conveys suddenness and life
