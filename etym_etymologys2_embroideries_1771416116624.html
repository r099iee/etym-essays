Derivation chain for EMBROIDERIES (via Old French, from a Germanic source): 1. Proto-Germanic *bruzdōn — “to decorate with fringe or trimming” (reconstructed; the source of a family of Germanic words for edging, fringe, and trimmed ornament). No certain Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed; the deeper origin is debated and possibly a pre-Germanic substrate borrowing. 2. Frankish *brozdon — “to ornament, to trim with stitchery” (spoken Frankish of the 5th–9th centuries; transmitted to Gallo-Romance through contact). 3. Old French broisder / broder — “to braid, to weave, to work ornamental stitches” (by the 11th century). 4. Old French embroder — “to embroider,” formed from en- (“in, onto”) + broder (12th–13th century). 5. Middle English embrouden / enbrouderen — “to embroider” (borrowed via Anglo-French; attested late 14th century, c. 1390). 6. Middle English embrouderie — “embroidery, needlework” (abstract noun; attested early 15th century). 7. English embroideries — plural of embroidery, “works of ornamental stitching” (16th century onward). Final node: EMBROIDERIES. Note on the debate: the chain is secure from Old French onward. The Germanic source (*bruzdōn) is reconstructed on the basis of Old High German brosma (“fringe, trimming”), Middle Dutch broot, and Old Saxon brosd; its deeper etymology is unknown — some scholars have linked it speculatively to a PIE root meaning “to break, shred” (from the notion of a ragged, cut fringe edge), but this is not generally accepted, and the word may be a non-Indo-European substrate term.
