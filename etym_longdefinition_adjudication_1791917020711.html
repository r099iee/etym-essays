Adjudication is a noun naming the formal act or process by which an authorised person or body — a court, tribunal, arbitrator, adjudicator, or administrative agency — hears a dispute or claim and pronounces a binding decision upon it. It is the noun of action corresponding to the verb adjudicate, and its immediate family includes adjudicator, adjudicative, and adjudicatory. In ordinary educated English it is a legal and institutional word, almost never colloquial.

In the source context the term appears in an account of the prehistory of law. Fines and compositions paid “to avert revenge” required “some adjudication of offenses and damages” — that is, an authoritative finding of which wrongs had been committed and what each was worth. The argument of the passage is that private vengeance cannot be replaced by compensation until somebody with recognised authority fixes both the facts and the price. Adjudication is precisely that fixing: the moment a dispute stops being a matter of strength and becomes a matter of judgment.

Core legal sense

  • The whole procedure by which a competent body resolves a dispute: pleadings, evidence, argument, findings, and a binding order. In this procedural sense the word is uncountable — “the case is still in adjudication”.
  • The decision that results, especially when it disposes of a claim or determines status. Here it is countable — “the court issued two separate adjudications”.
  • The constitutional function of judging, distinguished from legislating (making general rules) and from administering (carrying rules out). Legal theorists speak of the “adjudicative function” of the state when they mean the judicial branch's power to decide particular cases.

Specialised legal and technical uses

  • Bankruptcy. In British insolvency law an adjudication is the order by which a debtor is formally declared bankrupt; a person is “adjudicated bankrupt”. American practice speaks more often of the adjudication of a petition, but the underlying idea is identical: a status is changed by official decision.
  • Administrative law. “Administrative adjudication” denotes agency decision-making that resembles a trial — social-security disability claims, immigration and asylum cases, licensing, tax appeals. Agencies employ “adjudicators”, hold “adjudicatory hearings”, and distinguish “adjudicative facts” (facts about a particular party) from “legislative facts” (general policy facts).
  • Insurance and healthcare. “Claims adjudication” is the routine assessment of a claim against policy terms, and “real-time adjudication” is the automated approval or rejection of a pharmacy claim at the counter. The word here has shed all courtroom solemnity and means little more than “assessment against rules”.
  • Construction and commerce (UK). Statutory adjudication, introduced by the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996, gives a party a fast interim decision from a named adjudicator, binding until arbitration or litigation. This specialised sense is now one of the commonest uses of the word in British professional life.
  • Juvenile and criminal justice (US). A juvenile is “adjudicated delinquent” rather than convicted, and “adjudication withheld” describes a court's decision not to enter a formal conviction on the record. Both usages preserve the word's core meaning of an official declaration of status.
  • International law. The International Court of Justice “adjudicates” disputes between states; here adjudication is carefully contrasted with negotiation, mediation, and arbitration, because it proceeds from a standing court and ends in a judgment.
  • Elections and competitions. “Adjudication of a disputed return” is the resolution of a contested election result, while music festivals, debating tournaments, and film juries publish the “adjudication” of their panels. The common thread remains an authorised panel delivering a reasoned ranking or verdict.

Figurative and extended uses

  • “Moral adjudication” is the passing of judgment on conduct or character, and is usually pejorative: critics of Victorian biography complain of its habit of moral adjudication, and commentators warn against “adjudicating” private lives.
  • In digital governance, platforms and content-moderation bodies are said to adjudicate disputes over speech, copyright, and account suspension. Scholars call this “private adjudication”, noting that it mimics courts without their publicity or appeal rights.
  • In epistemology and academic life, peer review is sometimes described as the adjudication of competing claims to knowledge — a metaphor that borrows the word's authority while dropping its enforceability.

Why the word carries the sense of an award

The Latin ancestor adiūdicātiō was narrower than the English word: it named the judge's assignment of disputed property to one litigant, the clause in a Roman formula that empowered the judge to “award” rather than merely to declare. That “award” element survives in English. Adjudication is expected to allocate something — property, status, liability, entitlement, a medal, a place on a list — and to do so definitively. It is consequently a poor synonym for “opinion” or “discussion”: an adjudication ends an argument rather than continuing it.

Register, connotation, and collocations

  • Formal, technical, and predominantly written. It belongs to judgments, statutes, textbooks, and news reports about institutions; in conversation it sounds stiff or deliberately impersonal.
  • Common collocations: adjudication process, adjudication hearing, adjudication panel, adjudication award, final adjudication, binding adjudication, adjudication of a claim, dispute, or competition, referred to adjudication, adjudication upon the evidence.
  • Connotation is neutral to bureaucratic. Because it hides the human judge behind a procedure, writers sometimes prefer it precisely to sound impartial, and critics attack it precisely for the same reason.
  • Do not confuse it with adjudgment, a rarer noun for the act of adjudging, or with adjudge, an older legal verb meaning to sentence or to award judicially (“adjudged insane”).
