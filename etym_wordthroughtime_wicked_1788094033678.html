- c. 1200: the adjective "wikke" "bad, evil" appears in Middle English, probably formed from Old English "wicca" (sorcerer, wizard) — the word begins life with the taint of witchcraft and demonic dealing
- c. 1300: the extended form "wikked" becomes the standard strong word for moral evil in preaching and religious writing
- 16th–17th c.: the King James Bible ("the wicked", "wickedness") fixes it as the great scriptural word for the godless and sinful — full Old-Testament gravity
- 18th–19th c.: the fairy-tale register crystallises ("wicked witch", "wicked stepmother"), and a playful mild sense for mischievous children appears ("a wicked little grin"); the word begins to soften at the edges
- 20th–21st c.: slang reversal — New England "wicked" becomes an intensifier and term of approval ("wicked cold", "wicked awesome"), and British youth slang uses "wicked!" for "brilliant"; the moral and the slang senses now live side by side.
