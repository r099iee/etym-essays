The deep history of ensuing is the history of following. The word's ultimate ancestor is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *sekw-, "to follow", one of the most productive roots of the language family. Its descendants span the branches: Latin sequī "to follow", Greek hepomai "I follow", Sanskrit sacate "he follows, accompanies", and — on a debated "follow with the eyes" specialization — the Germanic verbs of seeing (English see, German sehen). In Latin the simple verb sequī already covered physical following, pursuit, and metaphorical succession: that which follows another in order, time, or result. Latin's derivational system then built an extraordinary family on the root, most of which has entered English through direct loan or via French: consequī "to follow closely, to obtain" gave consequence and consequent; prosequī "to follow forth" gave pursue, prosecution, and pursuit; exsequī "to follow out, carry out" gave execute and exequies; sequēla "that which follows" gave sequel; secundus "following, second" gave second; and socius "companion, one who follows with" gave social and associate. A single root thus radiates across law, narrative, logic, and society. The immediate ancestor of ensue, however, is a particular prefixed verb. Latin insequī — in- "upon, after" plus sequī — meant "to follow upon, to come after, to pursue", and carried the sense of close, immediate succession: an enemy insequitur the fleeing army; one age follows upon another. This insequī, re-formed in the popular Latin and Romance of late antiquity, produced Old French ensuivre / ensivre "to follow after, to result", attested from the early Old French period. From French, with Anglo-Norman mediation, English took the verb in the fourteenth century as ensue(n), meaning "to follow, to come after, to result." Semantic drift in English is striking because the verb specialized. Early English ensue could still shade toward pursuit or active following — the older Romance range. But over time the word's centre of gravity moved decisively toward events that follow in time or as a result — especially dramatic, undesirable, or consequential ones. We say that chaos ensued, disaster ensued, confusion ensued; we rarely say that a person ensues another. This is a classic case of a word narrowing into a particular region of its sense-range while its family words keep the broader map: sequence keeps the general "order of following", sequel keeps the narrative "what follows", consequence keeps the logical "what follows from", and pursue keeps the active "follow after" that ensue gave up. The participial adjective ensuing then lexicalised as a fixed prenominal modifier: the ensuing X names the phase that flows from what has just been stated — the ensuing silence, the ensuing struggle, the ensuing years. As a preposed adjective it acquired a distinctive double flavour: chronological ("after") and lightly causal ("as a result") at once, without spelling out "because". This is why ensuing feels more deliberate than following and less technical than consequent. Its register is literary, formal-journalistic, and chronicle-like; in forensic summaries it chains events into cause-and-sequel, which is exactly its function in the novel's sentence: the refusal to give way is followed — ensured — by an affray, a wound, and a death. Socio-cultural notes. The word has always belonged to the prose of record: chronicles, legal reports, news summaries, and narrative history, where writers need a compact way to say "what then followed as a result". The stock phrase "the ensuing years" is ubiquitous in biography and history; "the ensuing chaos / silence" is a standard narrative device that lets a writer name the consequence without describing it. Because the word presupposes a preceding event and a consequential link, it imposes a shape on narrative: events are not merely sequential but productive, each generating the next. In the courtroom prose of the novel, "the ensuing affray" does exactly that — the fight is presented not as an accident but as the logical next act of a chain begun by mutual stubbornness. The etymology — Latin insequī "to follow upon" — is still visible in the word's effect: ensuing means following so closely that it seems to belong to what it follows. That intimate, consequential following is why the word is so well suited to tragedy, where one refusal is already the cause of the bloodshed that ensues.
