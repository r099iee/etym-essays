"Heart-bowed-downness" is a rare, nonce compound meaning dejection, sorrowfulness, or depression — the state of having one's heart bowed down by grief, disappointment, or loneliness. In the source sentence, "moments of heart-bowed-downness" are moments of bruised, melancholy feeling for which "a good go at the soap and water" (a wash, a freshening up) is offered as comic cure. The form is not a dictionary headword but a transparent occasionalism in the manner of Dickens, Wodehouse, and the King James Bible: take heart (courage, spirits), bow it down (bend it low under weight), add -ness (state), and you have a mock-abstract noun for sadness.

Morphologically it fuses three content elements: heart as seat of emotion, bowed-down as posture of submission or grief, and -ness as nominaliser for a condition. The image is postural metaphor: just as the head bows and shoulders stoop in sorrow, so the heart itself is imagined to bow. English constantly maps emotion onto posture — uplifted, downcast, low-spirited, heavy-hearted — and this coinage makes the mapping explicit and slightly absurd by its length. Its very clumsiness is expressive: a short word like gloom would be neat, but heart-bowed-downness drags, enacting the heaviness it names.

The register is whimsical-literary, half-biblical ("my heart is bowed down" echoes the Psalms) and half-comic. It is not clinical depression but a soft, sympathetic low: the blues, the dumps, a bruised spirit. It invites comfort rather than treatment, which is why soap and water — domestic, practical care — is presented as remedy.

  • Dejected-mood sense (core): Sad, dispirited feeling — e.g., "moments of heart-bowed-downness." The bridge is container-posture metaphor: the heart as person who bows the head under burden. This covers loneliness, disappointment, homesickness, and tender melancholy alike.
  • Spiritual-abasement sense (biblical overtone): Humility or contrition before sorrow — e.g., "heart bowed down in grief." The bridge is scriptural language where bowing signifies submission to God or fate. It adds a gentle moral colour: sadness as humbling, not merely unpleasant.
  • Comic nonce-formation sense (stylistic): A deliberately long, hyphenated abstraction for humorous effect. The bridge is parody of Victorian moral psychology, which loved ponderous -ness nouns (down-heartedness, heavy-heartedness). The humour lies in over-precision: naming a vague ache with pedantic exactness.
  • Transient-state sense: Moments of, not life of — the -ness marks a passing condition, not a trait. The bridge is aspectual: -ness plus plural moments presents sadness as weather that passes, curable by small comforts, unlike melancholia or despair which sound permanent.

To use the word well is to keep its tenderness: it pities without pathologising. Unlike depression, which is medical, or despair, which is final, heart-bowed-downness is domestic and hopeful — a bowed heart can be raised again, washed, cheered, and set upright by kindness and routine.
