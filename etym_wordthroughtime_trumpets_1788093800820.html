- 14th c. – "Trumpet" enters English via Old French trompette; the instrument of heralds, armies, and royal ceremony; "trumpets sounded" = the sound of authority.
- 15th–16th c. – Fixed as the military/heraldic signal; trumpeters are official officers; the plural marks ceremony and alarm.
- 1611 – The Authorised Version: "the trumpet shall sound" (1 Cor 15:52) and the seven trumpets of Revelation give the word eschatological weight.
- 17th–18th c. – Baroque natural trumpet: art music adopts the herald's voice (Bach, Handel); "blow one's own trumpet" idiom recorded.
- 19th c. – Valves (c. 1815–1830s) make the trumpet chromatic; orchestras and military bands take up the modern instrument.
- 20th c. – Jazz trumpet stars (Armstrong, Gillespie); "to trumpet" widens to any loud announcement and to the elephant's cry.
- 21st c. – "Warning trumpets" still sound in prose and idiom; the plural remains the word for alarm, glory, and summons.
