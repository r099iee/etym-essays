• nasturtiums: Similar because both terms denote the exact same genus of edible, spurred garden creepers (genus Tropaeolum) prized for their vibrant blossoms and mustard-like foliage; differs because nasturtiums is the standardized modern botanical and colloquial English plural preserving the Latin neuter base, whereas nasturtians is an archaic, rural, and dialectal British variant (popular from the 17th to early 20th century) chosen deliberately by J.R.R. Tolkien to reflect rustic Hobbit vernacular.
  • Indian cress: Similar because it is the classic historical common name for the identical South American ornamental plant (Tropaeolum majus); differs because Indian cress refers geographically to the West Indies and the Spanish Americas from which Spanish explorers first brought the species to Europe in the 16th century, emphasizing its culinary resemblance to European cress rather than its classical Latin name.
  • Tropaeolum: Similar because it represents the formal scientific genus encompassing all sixty-plus species of garden nasturtiums; differs because Tropaeolum is modern Linnaean taxonomic Latin (coined by Carl Linnaeus from Greek tropaion, "trophy," because the peltate leaves resemble round classical shields and the red spurred flowers evoke blood-stained helmets taken from defeated foes), belonging strictly to formal botanical classification.
  • watercress: Similar because both plants share the sharp, glucosinolate-driven, peppery mustard-oil kick that historically inspired the Roman designation nasturtium, and both are harvested as peppery salad greens; differs because true watercress belongs to the cruciferous mustard family Brassicaceae (specifically Nasturtium officinale), a semi-aquatic perennial native to Eurasia, whereas garden nasturtians belong to the distinct South American family Tropaeolaceae.
  • cress: Similar because both designate pungent, spicy, edible leafy greens widely consumed in salads, garnishes, and herbal tonics; differs because cress (Old English cærse) is an ancient, broad Germanic umbrella term encompassing garden cress, land cress, and rock cress, whereas nasturtian designates either the ancient Roman pungent water herb or the colorful Andean trailing vine.
  • snapdragons: Similar because both are traditional European cottage-garden flowering plants celebrated for bright, bilateral, richly pigmented flowers frequently planted in companion borders and stone walls; differs because snapdragons belong to the genus Antirrhinum (Plantaginaceae) with distinct hinged, bilabiate flowers resembling dragon jaws, and lack the climbing habit, edible peltate foliage, and peppery flavor of nasturtians.
  • trailing creepers: Similar because both describe sprawling, low-growing vegetative forms that trail across turf walls, rockeries, and fences; differs because trailing creepers is a generic morphological category encompassing any prostrate or vining plant (such as ivy, creeping thyme, or periwinkle), whereas nasturtians specifically identifies the peppery, edible, spur-bearing floral species.
