- 15th c. — Late Middle English clerical adoption: borrowed from Anglo-Norman and Latin inviolabilis into theological and chancery texts, signifying sacred vows, oaths, and ecclesiastical sanctuaries that could not be profaned without courting divine retribution.
- 16th–17th c. — Early Modern legal and diplomatic formalisation: integrated into emerging international jurisprudence (e.g. Grotius and the law of nations) to describe the absolute personal safety and immunity of parliamentary ambassadors, diplomatic couriers, and royal heralds during war.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment political philosophy: redefined by social contract theorists (Locke, Rousseau) and constitutional framers to apply to natural human rights, property, and constitutional covenants, transforming a religious taboo into a secular democratic pillar.
- 19th c. — Victorian institutional & imperial certainty: applied to the sanctity of international treaties (such as Belgian neutrality), corporate charters, and private individual conscience, viewed as an unshakeable benchmark of civilized governance.
- 20th–21st c. — Universal human rights & international humanitarian law: solidified in global governance (Geneva Conventions, UN Declaration of Human Rights) designating fundamental rights (freedom from torture, diplomatic immunity, bodily integrity) as absolute and non-derogable, even during states of national emergency or war.
