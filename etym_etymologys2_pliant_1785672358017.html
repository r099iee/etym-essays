PLIANT descends from the Latin verb "to fold" through French. The chain: 1) PIE *plek- "to plait, weave, braid, fold, twist together" (reconstructed; one of the most productive roots in the family, source of Greek plékō, Latin plicāre, and Germanic *flehtaną) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin plicāre "to fold, bend, double up" (verb, attested from the 2nd c. BCE) — the direct Latin ancestor; also the base of the -plic- family: complicāre "fold together" (complicate), explicāre "fold out" (explicate), implicāre "fold in" (implicate), applicāre "fold to" (apply), replicāre "fold back" (reply), multiplicāre "fold many times" (multiply). 3) Vulgar Latin *plīcāre "to fold" (reconstructed; the stressed-vowel shift of plicāre) — source of the Romance verbs. 4) Old French plier / pleier "to bend, fold, double up" (attested from the 9th c.; modern French plier) — the verb whose participle becomes the adjective. 5) Old French pliant (present participle of plier, used adjectivally, attested c. 1300) "bending, yielding, supple." 6) Middle English pliant (borrowed late 14th c., the age of Chaucer) "bending, flexible; easily influenced." 7) English pliant — PLIANT (modern form). NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The core chain Latin plicāre → Old French plier → pliant → Middle English pliant → English pliant is secure and uncontroversial. (2) The proposed Indo-Aryan reflex of *plek- (Sanskrit pṛc- "to mix, join") is debated and should not be pressed; it does not affect the Romance/Germanic chain. (3) The English family also includes pliable (via Old French pliable, 14th c.), ply (from plier), and the -plicit/-plex cluster (explicit, implicit, complex, duplex), all folds of the same root. (4) The late 14th-c. date for English pliant is the standard first-attestation date; earlier Anglo-French use may exist but is not securely attested.
