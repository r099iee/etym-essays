cordial (adjective and noun) — the core meaning, from Latin cor "heart", is "of the heart, from the heart". As an adjective it names warm, heartfelt, sincere friendliness; as a noun it names a sweetened drink or a restorative. All senses radiate from the heart as the ancient seat of feeling and of life. 1. Warm and friendly; heartfelt and sincere (primary adjectival sense)
  • Describes warmth of manner that comes from genuine feeling rather than mere politeness: "a cordial welcome", "cordial relations between the two countries".
  • Stronger than polite (which can be a mere form) and than amicable (which implies peaceful agreement without warmth); cordial warmth is expressed, demonstrated, heartfelt.
  • Can describe feelings as well as manners: "no very cordial feelings toward him" means a distinct absence of warmth — the adjective beautifully measures coldness by the standard of the heart.
  • In diplomatic and formal idiom, "cordial relations" is the standard phrase for friendly official dealings.
2. A sweetened drink; a liqueur (noun sense)
  • A sweet, concentrated, usually fruit-flavoured drink, served diluted with water or soda; standard in Britain and Australia ("orange cordial", "lime cordial").
  • In American usage, cordial commonly means a sweet liqueur or a sweet after-dinner alcoholic drink.
  • The noun developed from the medicinal sense: a drink that "revives the heart".
3. Historical medical sense: a heart-restoring medicine
  • From the fourteenth century, a cordial was a medicine, tonic, or stimulant thought to strengthen or revive the heart and vital spirits — a "comfort for the heart".
  • The adjective originally meant "of the heart" in a literal anatomical way and "heart-reviving, restorative".
  • The sweet alcoholic cordial drink descends from these restorative concoctions; the warm greeting and the sweet drink are both, etymologically, things that do the heart good.
4. The semantic bridge uniting the senses
  • Latin cordialis, from cor "heart", meant simply "of the heart". The heart was the ancient seat of both life and feeling.
  • Warmth of manner "from the heart" is the emotional sense; a medicine "for the heart" is the physical sense; the sweet drink "for the heart" descends from the medicine.
  • All three — the warm feeling, the restorative medicine, and the sweet drink — share one root idea: something that proceeds from or acts upon the heart.
Register and usage notes
  • Cordial in the sense "warm and friendly" is formal and slightly old-fashioned in everyday speech, where "warm" or "friendly" often replace it, but it remains standard in diplomatic and literary register.
  • Do not confuse with cardiac (from Greek kardia "heart"), which kept the purely anatomical sense; cordial kept the metaphorical and the medicinal ones.
  • The noun sense is fully alive in British and Australian usage; in North America "cordial" as a drink usually means a sweet liqueur.
