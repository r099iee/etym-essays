The verb flopped in the closing scene of Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902) carries a whole storm of physical and emotional meaning. After his escape from Mr. McGregor — drenched, stripped of his coat and shoes, and utterly spent — Peter reaches the safety of home and "flopped down upon the nice soft sand on the floor of the rabbit-hole, and shut his eyes." The word describes a collapse that is at once total and comic: the small body, suddenly freed from fear and effort, gives way all at once, falling limply and heavily onto the yielding sand. Unlike a neutral fall, a flop implies that the body offered no resistance whatsoever — every muscle went slack in the same instant, and the creature hit the soft sand with a loose, clumsy slap. The word also sounds like its meaning: the flat, soft thud of a limp object meeting a surface. Potter pairs it with "nice soft sand" so that the collapse is cushioned and even cosy, and the childlike "shut his eyes" completes the image of a small creature surrendering completely to relief and sleep. It is the exhausted, trusting collapse of someone who is finally, completely safe.

Polysemy: the family of meanings radiating from flop is remarkably wide, and flopped inherits all of them as potential senses.

  • Core physical sense — to fall, drop, sit, or lie down heavily and limply, with the body inert: he flopped onto the sofa. This is the sense in the rabbit scene.
  • Heavy clumsy motion — to move about awkwardly and heavily, often with a flapping quality: the seal flopped across the rocks, the fish flopped in the bottom of the boat.
  • Flapping / waving — an earlier sense of moving loosely to and fro, as of wings or loose cloth: a flag flopping in the breeze.
  • Noun: the sound or action itself — the flop of the heavy book on the table; also an inert or loose thing: he fell with a flop.
  • Failure (metaphoric shift) — since the 1890s, American theater slang, a flop is a play, film, or venture that fails utterly, as if collapsing on stage; the show flopped. Here the physical collapse is transferred to a career, a project, or an enterprise.
  • Failure of plans / a blunder — the deal flopped, the experiment flopped, in informal business and scientific English.
  • Belly flop — in swimming, a dive in which the body strikes the water flat, with a loud slap; a comic, ungainly stunt.
  • Derived noun: flophouse — a cheap lodging house (1890s), so called because one "flops" down on a bed for the night; and flop sweat, the anxious perspiration of a performer before a possible failure.
  • Related adjective: floppy — hanging or bending loosely (floppy hat), which in 1971 famously produced floppy disk, the removable magnetic storage medium, named for its flexible plastic casing.
  • In the verb cluster flap–flip–flop, the vowel alternation iconically marks differences in motion: flap is a broad, loose beating; flip a quick, light turn; flop the heavy, loose collapse.

The semantic bridges are straightforward but instructive. The bridge from "heavy loose collapse" to "total failure" runs through the theatre: a play that fails is imagined as a physical object dropping flat, dead, and unresisting onto the stage. The bridge to flophouse runs through the exhausted sleeper who simply drops onto a bed. The bridge to floppy runs through the shared image of limp, unresisting material. All of the modern senses thus keep the ancient icon of a body or thing that gives up its own stiffness.

Register and collocation: flopped is informal, vivid, and mildly comic — it is almost never neutral or tragic. Common collocations: flopped down, flopped onto the sofa/bed/ground, flopped exhausted, flopped over, flopped about. Its near-synonyms each shift the picture in a telling way.

  • Collapse is more dramatic and clinical, suggesting the giving-way of a structure or of a person under grave strain; Peter is not collapsing but flopping, and the lighter word keeps the scene domestic.
  • Slump implies a gradual, heavy sagging into a sitting posture, often with bowed head and loss of energy; flop is a more sudden, total, horizontal surrender.
  • Plop foregrounds the sound of a small soft object hitting a surface, often water; flop is the fuller, flatter, heavier version.
  • Sprawl stresses the limbs flung out at angles; flop is more compact — the whole body gives way at once.
  • Plump (as a verb) describes sitting or falling down abruptly and heavily, close in sound and sense but more archaic and regional.
  • Keel over is the sudden tipping collapse of someone who faints or dies; it carries danger that flop lacks.
  • Crash is violent and destructive; flop is cushioned and comic.
