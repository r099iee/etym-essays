Envisage offers a clear window on how a concrete body-part metaphor — the face — becomes a verb of abstract futurity in modern administrative English via French.

The deep root is Proto-Indo-European *weid- to see, know, one of the most prolific roots in the language, yielding Latin vidēre to see, English wit, wise, vision, visual, evident, provide, review, Greek eidos form, idea, Sanskrit veda knowledge. Seeing and knowing are everywhere fused.

  • PIE *weid- “to see, know”: visual perception as source of knowledge; suffixed forms produce words for seeing, knowing, face, appearance across Indo-European.
  • Latin videre, visus “to see, behold”: central verb of sight. Past participle visus gives nouns of appearance. Frequentative visitare to go to see gives visit.
  • Late Latin *visaticum? Vulgar Latin visum “thing seen, vision, face, appearance”: from visus, develops into Gallo-Romance words for face as what is seen. Late Latin visagium is postulated as source of Old French visage.
  • Old French visage (12th c.) “face, countenance, appearance, sight”: from vis face + suffix -age. Chanson de Roland (c. 1100) already uses vis for face. Visage extends to expression, aspect, front of a building. Prefix en- in + visage yields verb envisager.
  • French envisager (16th c.): first in Middle French c. 1530s–1580s meaning to look at in the face, to confront, to consider, to look at attentively. Montaigne uses envisager for facing an idea squarely. By 17th–18th c. Académie definitions stress considering, contemplating, examining with the mind: envisager un projet, envisager les conséquences.
  • English envisage (c. 1660s–1820s): borrowed as learned Gallicism. Earliest English use c. 1661 in the physical sense to look in the face, soon obsolete. Re-borrowed in late 18th–early 19th c. in the French mental sense under influence of Scottish Enlightenment and diplomatic French. Walter Scott (1821) writes “the consequences which the legislature must have envisaged.” Victorian administrative prose adopts it eagerly for reports and commissions.

19th-century Britain makes envisage a workhorse of empire, industry, and reform: parliamentary committees envisage railways, sanitation, education; novelists envisage futures. Carlyle, Mill, and later Wells and Shaw use it to picture utopias and dystopias. The sense “to provide for in a plan” — the Act envisages compensation — emerges from legal drafting mid-century, allowing inanimate subjects.

20th-century rivalry with envision shapes its geography. American English, after Emerson and later business writing, prefers envision (first U.S. 1870s, popularized 1920s–1930s by advertising and self-help: envision success). British English retains envisage as the standard formal term, with envision felt as Americanism. Your sentence, with its academic cadence — develop ways to envisage, imagine, conceptualize — is distinctly British-academic in flavor, stacking near-synonyms for precision.

Phonetic adaptation: French /ɑ̃vizaʒe/ with nasal vowel and final stress is anglicized to /ɪnˈvɪzɪdʒ/ with English stress on second syllable, denasalized first vowel to /ɪn-/, voiced /z/, and palato-alveolar /dʒ/ for French -g-. Spelling keeps French -age but pronunciation follows English visage, massage, prestige pattern. The noun visage itself remains a separate literary borrowing in English for a solemn face.

Semantic drift shows classic bleaching from concrete to abstract: look at a face → look hard at → contemplate mentally → imagine future → expect and plan for. The face has vanished from awareness for most speakers, but the visual promise remains: to envisage an information society is to give faceless data a face we can recognize and govern. The current information-economy context revives the original visual force — we literally need visualizations, dashboards, interfaces to envisage information.
