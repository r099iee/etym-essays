The formation of castaway is a compound creation of Middle and Early Modern English, born from the synthesis of a Scandinavian loanword with native Old English elements, which subsequently traveled across theological, maritime, and literary domains.

  • Old Norse and Old English Ancestry: The compound consists of two Germanic constituents: the verb cast and the adverb away. The verb cast entered Middle English (c. 1200) from Old Norse kasta ('to throw, fling, cast lots'), which completely displaced the native Old English verb weorpan (cognate with modern German werfen and surviving only in archaic 'warp'). The adverb away evolved from Old English onweg, a contraction of the prepositional phrase on weg ('on the way, moving onward, off'). By the thirteenth century, the phrasal verb to casten awei was well-established in Middle English, meaning literally to fling something aside, discard useless objects, or waste resources.
  • Theological Coinage in the English Reformation: In the 1520s and 1530s, the pioneering English biblical translator William Tyndale crystallized the phrasal verb into a nominal agent/participle compound: castaway. Translating Saint Paul's First Epistle to the Corinthians (1 Cor. 9:27: 'lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway'), Tyndale chose castaway to render the Greek ἀδόκιμος (adókimos), which in Classical and Hellenistic Greek denoted a coin or metal that failed the assayer's test and was rejected as counterfeit or disqualified. Tyndale's choice was preserved in the Geneva Bible (1560) and the King James Version (1611), ensuring that for generations of English speakers, a castaway was primarily understood as an unredeemed soul cast out from God's presence.
  • Maritime Shift and Colonial Navigation: During the late Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, as England expanded its maritime ventures into the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans, the term underwent an organic semantic shift toward nautical reality. Sailors who survived shipwrecks and were stranded on desolate coastlines were literally 'cast away' by the tempestuous ocean. Richard Hakluyt's *The Principal Navigations* (1589) and Samuel Purchas's *Purchas his Pilgrimes* (1625) popularized thrilling accounts of stranded mariners, firmly anchoring castaway as a concrete nautical noun.
  • Literary Canonization and the Robinsonade: The publication of Daniel Defoe's *The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe* in 1719 transformed castaway from a report of maritime misfortune into a foundational modern myth. Inspired by the real-life ordeal of Scottish privateer Alexander Selkirk on Más a Tierra island, Defoe brilliantly fused the word's two historical streams: Crusoe is simultaneously a physical castaway battling tropical nature and a spiritual castaway undergoing profound Christian repentance and inner redemption. The global success of the novel spawned the entire genre of 'Robinsonades' (such as Johann David Wyss's *The Swiss Family Robinson*), cementing castaway as one of the most resonant archetypes in world literature.
