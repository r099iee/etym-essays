Flowerishes — here preserving Kenneth Burke's spelling reproduced in Chapter 4 — is a variant spelling of flourishes, the plural of flourish, and must be understood as belonging entirely to the flourish family: decorative, flower-like embellishments of speech, writing, typography, music or bodily gesture deployed for show, emphasis or virtuosic display.

In its most literal and central modern sense, a flourish is an ornamental stroke, curve or embellishment added to handwriting, typography, illustration or design — the sweeping tail of a signature, the decorative initial of a manuscript, the typographic swash discussed in histories of print — and Burke's “flowerishes” transfers that visual idea to rhetoric: passages of language that bloom outward from the plain stem of sense into ornamental elaboration.

  • 1. Ornamental stroke in writing / typography / art: a sweeping decorative pen-stroke, curlicue, illuminated initial or typographic ornament; Burke's use points directly to this sense, where printed marks become visual display rather than transparent carriers of sense.
  • 2. Rhetorical / stylistic embellishment: an ornate passage, purple patch, bravura turn of phrase or gestural elaboration interjected for impressiveness; differs from sense 1 only in medium — language or performance rather than ink — but shares the logic of superadded bloom.
  • 3. Ostentatious display or fanfare (trumpet flourish, flourish of trumpets): a showy introductory fanfare, wave of the hand, brandishing of a sword or cloak, grand entrance; here the semantic bridge is movement that draws attention, blossoming outward to claim space.
  • 4. Verb: to thrive, prosper, bloom and to brandish: plants flourish, businesses flourish, artists flourish in a period; a speaker flourishes a letter, a conductor flourishes a baton; the intransitive thriving sense preserves the original flower metaphor, the transitive waving sense preserves display.
  • 5. Musical bravura passage: an elaborate improvised ornament, cadenza-like run or grace-note elaboration; historically notated with small notes and curves visually resembling pen flourishes, uniting sound and image.
  • 6. Extended / ironic sense: empty show, mere flowerishes: when ornament is judged excessive, the word acquires pejorative colouring — bombast, frippery, grandiloquence; Burke's critical interest lies exactly here, in how modernist typographic experiments reclaim or ironize that excess as meaning itself.

Domain-specific uses reinforce this polysemy: in palaeography and bibliography a flourish is a technical term for scribal abbreviation strokes and pen trials; in rhetoric since Quintilian and the early modern handbooks it names flores rhetorici, flowers of rhetoric, i.e. figures and tropes gathered as an anthology; in fencing, dance and heraldry it names a controlled ostentatious sweep; in botany obsolete, to flowerish meant to blossom.

Literary nuance is crucial for the present context: Burke's “flowerishes” are not mere decoration but analytic exhibits — reproduced typographic layouts in which letterforms, spacing and ornament become arguments. Mallarmé's Un Coup de Dés (1897), the Futurists and Dadaists discussed immediately afterwards, are therefore described as modern manifestations of the same impulse: the return of writing to image, of the letter to shape and sound, of flourish to sense. To call them flowerishes etymologically re-roots flourish in flower, reminding the reader that ornament was once understood organically, as natural blooming, not as extrinsic addition.

In contemporary usage flourish oscillates between admiration and suspicion: with a flourish admires confident completion; mere flourish dismisses emptiness; to add a flourish can be praise for craft or blame for affectation. Burke preserves both poles, treating typographic flowerishes as both serious semiotic acts and deliberate pranks on alphabetic reason.
