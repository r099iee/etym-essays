The evolution of caput spans four millennia from an Indo-European word for head to the organising metaphor of Western leadership, law, topography and capitalism.

Proto-Indo-European *kaput- (head, bowl, vessel — the skull as cup) is reconstructible from Latin caput, Germanic *haubud- (Gothic haubiþ, Old English heafod > head, German Haupt), and possibly Sanskrit kapala (skull, bowl). The Germanic h- reflects Grimm's Law (*k- > *h-), proving common origin: English head and Latin caput are cognates, sundered by consonant shift but united in sense. The root may itself derive from *kap- (to seize, hold, contain — the skull containing the brain), though this deeper link is debated.

  • Proto-Italic and early Latin: Italic *kaput- gives Latin caput, capitis with -ut, -itis heteroclite declension (nominative caput, genitive capitis, from *caput-s, *caput-es- with vowel weakening). Already in the Twelve Tables and early law, caput means both skull and civic life: to lose caput is to lose head, freedom or citizenship. Pastoral counting in capita (by heads) is prehistoric: Umbrian and Oscan cognates show the same head-counting of livestock and persons.
  • Classical elaboration: Republican and Imperial Latin exploit every metaphor: anatomy (Celsus), oratory (caput causae — main point), historiography (caput mundi — Rome), military (caput exercitus), geography (caput fluminis), law (capitis reus, capite censi — the head-count poor). Derivatives multiply: capitalis (of the head/chief/deadly), capitale (principal, stock), capitulum (little head → chapter, nipple, knob), capitellum (capital of column), capillus (hair of the head, originally *caput-pilus), anceps, praeceps, biceps (two-headed, head-first compounds), occiput (back-head), sinciput (half-head). The head becomes a derivational factory.
  • Sound law and cognate split: Latin preserves PIE *k- as c- /k/ and *-p- as -p-; Germanic shifts both: *k- > *h-, *-p- > *-f- in part, giving head/Haupt. Romance palatalises ca- > cha- before a in Central French (chef < caput via *capu), retains ca- in Norman-Picard and Italian (capo), lenites intervocalic -p- to -v-/-b- (cheval? no; caboche). English thus receives caput three times: natively as head/hood/hooded (Germanic cognate), learnedly as capital, decapitate, precipitate, occiput (Latin borrowing), and popularly as chief, chef, acheve, kerchief (cover-head), cattle, chattel, cape, chapter, capital (French mediation). Few roots enter one language through so many doors.
  • Medieval to modern: Old French chief (head, leader, end) gives Middle English chief, chieftain, captain (via Late Latin capitaneus — head-man), chef, kerchief, handkerchief, mischief (bad head/result), achieve (bring to a head). Learned 14th–17th c. borrowings restore Latin shapes: capital (chief, principal, 13th c.), decapitate (17th c.), precipitate, occipital, sincipital (medical), recapitulate (summarise under heads). Economics takes capital/cattle/chattel (head-count wealth); politics takes capital (city), captain, chief, chief executive; print takes capital (letter), chapter, heading, headline; geography takes cape, headland, headwaters. Each domain preserves a different Roman use of caput.
  • Cultural and literary attestations: Virgil's caput mundi, Livy's capite censi, Cicero's capitis deminutio, the Vulgate's caput anguli (head of the corner, cornerstone), medieval capitula (monastic chapter-meetings under the chapter-heading), Shakespeare's puns on head/capital, the guillotine's decapitation discourse, modern per capita statistics. The word's biography is Rome's biography: from herdsman's head-count to empire's head-city to modernity's head-of-state, head-office and human capital.

The source sentence isolates the economic twig of this vast tree: capitale (property) from caput (head, i.e. of cattle). But the same head that counts cattle commands armies, names cities, titles chapters, crowns columns and loses itself on scaffolds. Few etymologies reward study as richly: learn caput and a third of the dictionary falls into place as headed, beheaded or head-counted.
