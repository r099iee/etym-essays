- 14th c. (Late Middle English) — Borrowed from Old Norse baðask ("to bathe oneself"); originally denoted wallowing, soaking, or immersing oneself in liquid, tears, or shed blood (as in Chaucer's Nun's Priest's Tale).
- 16th–17th c. (Early Modern English) — Underwent semantic shift from wet immersion to dry thermal exposure, popularized by Shakespeare (e.g. As You Like It: "bask in the sun"), shedding its violent and morbid blood connotations.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment & Natural History) — Formalized in natural history treatises to describe animal thermoregulation (reptiles and insects warming themselves on rocks); celebrated in pastoral poetry as the ultimate image of rustic repose.
- 19th c. (Victorian Era) — Broadened vigorously into social and psychological metaphors, denoting delight in social prestige, royal favors, or maternal smiles ("basking in the sunshine of prosperity").
- 20th–21st c. — Codified in social psychology (Cialdini's "basking in reflected glory" / BIRGing) and pop-culture commentary (basking in Warholian fifteen minutes of fame before fading into obscurity).
