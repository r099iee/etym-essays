CHAIN: FROM PIE TO LIMPED (6 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *lemb- "to hang down, droop, be slack, be limp" (reconstructed; Pokorny IEW). The Germanic linkage of the walking verb to this root is probable but debated.
  2. Proto-Germanic *limpanan "to happen, befall, to turn out" (reconstructed; the meaning drifted from "to hang" toward "to come to pass, turn out", as though what happens "hangs down" or "falls out" of the unknown).
  3. Old English limpan "to happen, befall" (attested, frequently with ge-: gelimpan), alongside Old English lemphealt "halting, lame" (lemp- "limp, slack" + healt "lame"), a compound already carrying the lame sense.
  4. Middle English limpen "to happen; to fall short, to fail" (attested 13th–15th centuries; the negative sense "to fall short" is the bridge to walking lamely).
  5. English limp (verb), first recorded in the 1560s, "to walk lamely, to halt", from the "fall short" idea — a leg that comes up short with every step.
  6. LIMPED (weak past tense and past participle, regular -ed — the exact form of this card).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • Some authorities treat the walking verb limp as of unknown origin: the chain OE limpan "to happen, to fall short" → "to come up short in the walk" is the traditional route (OED cautiously compares), but the semantic bridge is inferred, not directly attested, and the verb is first recorded only in the 1560s.
  • The adjective limp "flaccid, without stiffness" (1706) is probably a separate formation from the same "slack, hanging" idea (compare Old English lemphealt), or a back-formation from the verb; the two words have converged in modern use.
  • The Indo-Aryan cognates are genuine: Sanskrit lambate "hangs down, clings, sinks", lamba- "hanging down, dangling" (whence Hindi lambā "long"), and ālamba "support, prop" all continue PIE *lemb-.
  • The Germanic family also took the favourable side of the root: Old High German gilimpan "to befit, suit" and Middle High German gelingen gave modern German gelingen "to succeed", so the same root means "to turn out well" in German and "to fall short, to limp" in English.
