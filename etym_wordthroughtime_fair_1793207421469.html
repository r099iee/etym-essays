- Roman — holy days: feriae, festivals when business stopped and countryfolk gathered — rest that would become market-day.
- Medieval — the charter fair: kings grant fair-rights to abbeys and towns; Stourbridge and St Bartholomew’s draw merchants from across Europe.
- Tudor-Stuart — hiring and horse dealing: fairs become labour exchanges and horse marts, rowdy, essential, and suspicious to magistrates.
- Cobbett’s 1820s — the country economy’s pulse: Chertsey’s horse, cattle, and pig fair is honest rural commerce, noted with approval amid the ugly villas.
- Victorian — decline and delight: railways and shops kill the trading fair, but roundabouts and freak-shows keep the pleasure fair alive.
- 1851 — the world’s fair: the Great Exhibition lifts the name to global display — industry’s cathedral under glass.
- Today — two heirs: the funfair’s lights and the trade fair’s halls, while livestock fairs survive as agricultural shows.
