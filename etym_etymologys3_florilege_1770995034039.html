The biography of florilege is the biography of a bouquet - of how two ancient roots, one of blooming and one of gathering, were married in a monastery scriptorium and produced a name for the finest books a culture could keep. The word is a compound, and its two halves must be told separately before they can be joined. The flower-half. The first element goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰleh₃-, "to bloom, blossom, flourish." It is one of the few roots the whole family shares for plant life: in the Indo-Aryan branch it produced Sanskrit phulla "blown, opened (of a flower)" and, further along, phala "fruit"; in Germanic it produced *blōmô, giving English bloom, blossom, blade, and flower (via the same root through Latin flos); in Hellenic it appears in phúllon "leaf", source of our chlorophyll. In Italy the root produced the Latin noun flos, genitive floris, "flower, blossom" - and, crucially, Latin flos had already begun to mean more than a botanical object: flos could mean "the best of its kind", the flower of an army, the flower of youth, the flower of a people. This secondary sense - flower as the best - was the seed of everything that followed. The gather-half. The second element goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *leǵ-, "to gather, collect." In Greece the root produced légein "to gather, to count, to say, to speak" and lógos "word, speech, reason" - the source of our logic and -logy. In Italy it produced Latin legere "to gather, collect, choose." The Latin verb is remarkably rich: to legere was to glean, to gather up, to choose - and, by a striking extension, to read, because reading was imagined as picking out letters from the page. From legere come collect (com- + legere), select, elect, intellect, and legend ("things to be read"). The marriage. Latin fused the two into florilegus (adjective, "flower-gathering") and florilegium (noun, "a gathering of flowers"). The compound is a perfect calque - a root-for-root translation - of the Greek anthologia, "a gathering of flowers", from ánthos "flower" + legein "to gather", the ancestor of our "anthology". In the medieval monasteries and cathedral schools, the florilegium became a genre of manuscript: a compendium that gathered the "finest flowers" of the Bible, the church fathers, and the classical authors into a single portable volume of wisdom. A monk compiling a florilegium was doing exactly what the metaphor said - moving through the vast field of sacred and secular letters and plucking only the perfect blossoms, so that a reader could hold the best of a library in one hand. The journey into English. From medieval Latin the word passed into French as florilège, where it remains a living word for "anthology". English took it in the 19th century as a learned, antiquarian doublet of "anthology", and it has never become common - one encounters florilege chiefly in literary history, bibliographic writing, and deliberately ornate prose. The card's context extends the word one step further, using it as the name of a high rank in the imperial examinations: the successful scholars are themselves the gathered flowers of the nation's intellect, "plucked" by the state as a gardener plucks blossoms, and the third position of honor is a place of distinction within that chosen bouquet. It is a remarkable modern afterlife for a word born in a scriptorium: the old monastic metaphor of gathering the finest flowers of literature applied to gathering the finest minds of a civilization.
