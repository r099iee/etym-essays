PERVERSION descends in a single unbroken line from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “to turn”, through Latin and Old French. The chain: 1) PIE *uert- / *wert- “to turn, rotate, wind” (reconstructed; one of the great turn-roots of Indo-European, reflected in Sanskrit vártate “turns, proceeds”, Latin vertere “to turn”, and, in the Germanic branch, in Old English weorϾan “to become” and wyrd “fate”) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin vertere “to turn, to rotate, to reverse” (verb, attested from the earliest Latin; the most productive verbal family in the language, giving versus “turned; a line, verse”, vertex “the turning point”, and versāre “to keep turning”) — attested from the 3rd c. BCE. 3) Latin pervertere “to overturn, topple, ruin, corrupt” (compound verb, per- “through, thoroughly; to the bad” + vertere “to turn”; the prefix turns plain “turning” into “turning the wrong way, overturning”) — attested from the 1st c. BCE. 4) Latin perversus “turned the wrong way, distorted, contrary, perverse” (past participle of pervertere; the participle on which the noun is built; also the source of English perverse) and Latin perversiō, accusative perversionem “a turning about, an overturning, a distortion, a corruption” (noun formed on the participle stem) — attested in Classical Latin. 5) Old French perversion “corruption, perversion” (learned noun, attested from the 13th c.; the Romance route by which the word also reached English) and direct Latin loans in English scholarship. 6) English perversion “the action of turning aside from the true or proper course; corruption, distortion” (noun, borrowed in the 14th c., reinforced through both the Latin and the Old French routes) — PERVERSION. NOTE ON DEBATE: The chain is well attested and uncontroversial in outline. The only points of nuance are (1) the exact shade of the prefix per- in pervertere — whether “thoroughly” or “wrongly” — which is a matter of emphasis rather than dispute; and (2) the Germanic branch of *uert- meaning “to turn” developed the separate sense “to become” (Old English weorϾan, German werden), so Germanic words like weird and worth belong to the same root but never fed the English form perversion, which came purely through Latin and French.
