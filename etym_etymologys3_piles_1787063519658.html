Pile, in the sense of a heavy stake or post driven into the ground — especially into the bed of a river, lake, or sea — is one of those engineering words that has quietly carried the same meaning for over six hundred years of English while the technology it describes has been utterly transformed.

The Latin inheritance and its uncertainty. English pile is borrowed from Old French pile, "a stake, a pier, a mole", which continues the Latin pīla, "a pillar, a pier, a mole of stone" — the kind of structure the Romans built at harbours and over marshes. The Latin word's own origin is not certainly known. It may be Etruscan, since so many Latin words for masonry and structures (like turris "tower" and perhaps taberna "shop") were borrowed from the Etruscans, or it may be an old Italic formation associated with the verb pīlāre, "to ram down, to compress", the source also of the verb "to pile (things up)". The dictionaries mark the origin of Latin pīla as "of unknown origin", and the connection with pīlāre as plausible but not proven. What is certain is that the Roman pīla was a load-bearing vertical element, and that the word descended into the Romance languages with exactly that meaning.

Into French and Middle English. Old French pile carried the senses "stake, pier, mole", and these passed into Middle English in the 14th century. In England the word was used for the timber posts driven into riverbeds and marshy ground to carry the foundations of bridges, jetties, and buildings. The famous medieval London Bridge stood on "starlings" and piles; pile-built bridges, quays, and tidal mills were the normal structures of the age. The noun was matched by the verb "to pile", meaning "to drive piles", and by the derivative "pile-driver", the great hammering engine used to sink piles, a word recorded from the early modern period and still in daily use among engineers.

The other piles. The history of the word is complicated by a set of identical-looking words that English inherited at about the same time. There is pile meaning "a heap or mass" (from French pile, "a heap, a stack", ultimately the same Latin pīla via the image of a "pillar" built up of stacked material, or from a separate Latin pīla meaning "ball"); pile meaning "the nap or soft surface of cloth or carpet" (from Latin pilus, "hair"); and the "piles" of British informal medicine — haemorrhoids, from Latin pīla "ball" because of their shape, via pīlulae, "little balls" (whence also "pill"). None of these is the same word as the engineer's pile, but all are spelled and pronounced identically, so the English plural "piles" is famously ambiguous. In the sentence "the town was built on piles", a modern reader must choose between driven stakes, heaps, and (with a smile) haemorrhoids; the engineering sense is normally dominant whenever water or building is mentioned.

From timber to concrete. Down to the 19th century, a pile was almost always a pointed timber trunk — oak, elm, or fir — driven by a pile-driver. The Industrial Revolution brought cast-iron piles, then steel H-piles, then reinforced-concrete piles, either driven or cast in place, and later screw piles and sheet piles (interlocking steel panels forming walls). The word "pile" itself, however, did not change: it still names the driven member that carries the load of a structure through weak soil or water down to firm ground. Modern engineers speak of "pile foundations", "piling rigs", "bearing piles", and "friction piles".

The word in literature. Because pile-built settlements are among the most evocative of prehistoric and medieval structures — the lake-villages of Glastonbury, the crannogs of Ireland and Scotland, and the pile dwellings of the Alps — the word carries a distinct antiquarian glow. It is this tradition that Tolkien invokes in The Hobbit when he describes Esgaroth, the Lake-town built on "huge piles made of forest trees", reached by a wooden bridge; the pile-built city is both a feat of engineering and a token of a drowned, older world, since the rotted piles of a greater town are visible along the shores in times of drought. In such writing the plain engineering word becomes a piece of ancient atmosphere.

  • Latin pīla "pillar, pier, mole" (origin uncertain, possibly Etruscan or from pīlāre "to ram").
  • Old French pile "stake, pier, mole" (11th–12th c.).
  • Middle English pile "pointed stake driven into the ground" (14th c.).
  • 16th–19th c. – timber piles and pile-drivers; "pile" enters standard building vocabulary.
  • 19th–21st c. – iron, steel, and concrete piles; "pile foundations" a technical standard; the word also keeps its literary, antiquarian echo.
