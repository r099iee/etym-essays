- Old English Period (c. 9th–11th Century) - Attested in Anglo-Saxon herbal texts and glosses as *popig* or *pæpeg* (borrowed from Vulgar Latin *papavum*); designated the wild scarlet arable weed growing in grain fields and used in folk salves for sleeplessness.
- 12th to 15th Century (Middle English) - Shifted phonetically through *popi*, *popy*, and plural *popies*; featured in Chaucer and medieval medical manuscripts as a symbol of deep sleep, oblivion, and herbal medicine.
- 16th to 17th Century (Tudor & Elizabethan Eras) - Celebrated in Renaissance poetry (Shakespeare's *Othello*: "Not poppy, nor mandragora, / Nor all the drowsy syrups of the world"); firmly established in English domestic cottage gardens alongside roses and lilies.
- 18th to 19th Century - Gained immense aesthetic resonance in Victorian literature and art (Oscar Wilde's *The Birthday of the Infanta*, 1891, where red poppies nod and beat time); concurrently became entangled in geopolitical tragedy during the Anglo-Chinese Opium Wars.
- Post-World War I (1915–1930s) - Transformed into the supreme global emblem of military sacrifice and battlefield remembrance following John McCrae's *In Flanders Fields*; the red paper poppy became a universal civic symbol across the Commonwealth.
- 21st Century - Continues to maintain its multifaceted identity: a beloved, cheerful garden annual, an enduring sacred token of wartime remembrance every November, and a crucial agricultural crop for both bakery seeds and modern pharmaceutical pain relief.
