OFT-TIMES is a compound of two English words, oft and times; each element is traced separately. CHAIN 1 - OFT:
  • PIE *upo "up from under, up, over" (c. 4000-3000 BCE) - an adverb/particle meaning "up, over, away"; extended with *-er as *uper "over, above" (source of Sanskrit upari, Greek huper, Latin super, Proto-Germanic *uber > English over), and with s-mobile as Latin sub "under".
  • Proto-Germanic *ufta "frequently, often" (c. 500 BCE-500 CE) - built on the *up- base with the notion of "going over and beyond", exceeding the ordinary count; attested across the family as Gothic ufta, Old Norse opt, Old Frisian ofte, Old Saxon ofto, Old High German ofto, and Old English oft.
  • Old English oft "often, frequently" (attested from the 8th-9th century) - the plain adverb of frequency.
  • Middle English ofte, oft "often" (c. 12th-15th c.) - the same adverb; alongside it the extended form often (oft + adverbial -en, on the analogy of selden "seldom") appears in the 14th century and gradually wins ordinary speech.
  • English oft "often" (archaic, poetic) - survives as the clipped form in verse and in fixed collocations such as oft-quoted, oft-told, and oft-repeated.
  • English oft-times, ofttimes (from Middle English ofte tymes onward) - formed on the model of sometimes and betimes, compounding oft with the adverbial plural of time.
CHAIN 2 - TIMES:
  • Proto-Germanic *timon "time, appointed period" (reconstructed; possibly connected, on a debated hypothesis, with a PIE root *da- "to divide", the idea being "an appointed portion or section of duration"; the deeper Indo-European ancestry is not securely established).
  • Old English tima "a limited space of time, a period, an occasion" (attested from the 9th century; source of the adverbial betima "at a good time" and of hit-tima).
  • Middle English tyme, time "time" (c. 12th-15th c.), with the plural tymes "occasions, periods".
  • English times, used adverbially in the plural with the meaning "occasions, instances" - as in sometimes, betimes, and oft-times (the adverbial plural of time, formed from the 13th century onward).
  • Compound: OFT-TIMES (the form on this card, a hyphenated spelling of the archaic adverb ofttimes, "on many occasions").
Debated / nuanced points: the reconstruction *timon itself is widely accepted for the Germanic stage, but its deeper Indo-European connection is uncertain - linking time with a root meaning "to divide" is a plausible but debated hypothesis, and some scholars prefer to leave the ancestry of *timon unexplained. On the oft side, an older theory attached the family to the PIE root *h1epi "near, at, upon" (Greek epi, Sanskrit api, Latin ob); that is a genuine but parallel root, and the frequency sense of oft comes from the "over and above" of *upo, not from the "upon" of *h1epi - the two must not be merged.
