Embroidered means decorated with ornamental needlework — patterns, figures, or scenes worked into a fabric with thread, often of contrasting colour or of gold or silver metal thread. In the description of Bao-yu's costume, "a pair of ivy-coloured embroidered silk trousers were partially visible beneath his gown," the word performs two functions at once. First, it names the fact of the garment's decoration: even the trousers — hidden, "partially visible" beneath the gown — are covered in intricate needlework. Second, it establishes the novel's theme of total, unremitting luxury: in the Jia household no surface is left plain; even the second layer of clothing is a "labour of luxury", a permanent record of needlework. The ivy-coloured silk provides a naturalistic, organic background against which the stitched threads play, and the word suggests a style that is at once opulent and vividly alive, as if the embroidery had grown into the fabric. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES "Embroidered" is the past participle of the verb "embroider", and its senses fall into two families — the literal textile sense and a large, productive family of metaphor:
  • Textile sense (the primary, literal sense): ornamented with stitched patterns of thread. This includes hand embroidery (silk, cotton, crewel, gold and silver bullion thread), machine embroidery, whitework, and the many regional traditions of surface stitch-work. In this sense the word applies to clothing, linens, handkerchiefs, cushions, ecclesiastical vestments, flags, and tapestries, and it can be used of the technique, of the item ("an embroidered shirt"), or of the pattern ("an embroidered dragon").
  • Metaphorical sense (of speech and narrative): embellished with invented or exaggerated detail — "to embroider a story", "an embroidered account", "embroidered memories". Here the word means "to add colour, drama, or false detail to an account", and the bridge is transparent: just as the needleworker adds ornamental thread to plain cloth, the storyteller adds invented ornament to plain fact. The metaphor is old (attested by the 17th century) and remains fully current.
  • Extended descriptive sense: decorated, ornamented, adorned in a general way — "embroidered with wit", "embroidered with quotations" — where the word keeps the idea of fine, deliberate surface decoration without literal thread.
  • Adjectival uses of the participle: "embroidered" can describe (i) the object (embroidered cloth, an embroidered sleeve), (ii) the technique or occasion (hand-embroidered, embroidered in silk), and (iii), in transferred use, a manner of speech or writing (embroidered prose, an embroidered tale).
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The bridge between the literal and figurative senses is one of the most transparent in the language and can be traced to the late 16th and 17th centuries, when "embroider" began to be used of any kind of elaborate adornment — of gardens (embroidered with flowers), of rhetoric (embroidered with figures of speech), of prose — before settling on narrative embellishment. The movement is from a concrete craft (adding threads to fabric) to an abstract one (adding ornament to discourse), and it never fully leaves the concrete behind: an "embroidered" account is one to which the speaker has applied visible, decorative, often florid additions. The word also carries a faint moral charge in its figurative use — embroidering a story is adding falsehood or exaggeration — that the neutral word "embellish" does not always carry. SYNONYM BOUNDARIES AND REGISTER The synonym field around "embroidered" is carefully graded. "Embellished" is the general word for ornamented, decorative, but without the technical meaning of thread-stitching. "Decorated" is looser still. "Embroidered" specifically implies needle and thread. "Brocaded" describes a fabric whose raised pattern is woven into the cloth on the loom, not stitched on afterwards; embroidery is thread applied to an already-woven ground. "Beaded" and "sequinned" name specific materials (beads, sequins) rather than thread. "Tapestried" suggests large wall-hangings whose design is woven in. "Stitched" is the plain functional word for any sewing, including repairs and seams, whereas embroidery is always ornamental. Register-wise, "embroidered" is standard literary and technical English, at home in fashion writing, art history, costume description, and figurative prose; it is formal enough for scholarship and natural enough for narrative. In the novel the word is doing quiet but important ideological work: it marks the Jia family's wealth as permanent, laborious, and inherited — thread-work is slow, patient, expensive labour, and the presence of embroidery even on hidden trousers announces a household in which luxury is not merely displayed but built into the very weave of daily life.
