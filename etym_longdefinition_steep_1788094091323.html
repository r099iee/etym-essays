Steep (adjective; comparatives steeper, steepest) denotes a surface, line, or movement that rises or falls at a sharp, near-vertical angle. In the source passage, “while the river rounded a steep shoulder of land that came down upon their left,” the word describes the flank of a hillside dropping almost sheer into the water: the river must bend around the promontory because the land falls away so abruptly. That physical sense is the root of the whole family of meanings, and from it has grown an unexpectedly rich set of secondary, figurative, and informal senses.

1. Primary physical sense — sharply inclined. A steep hill, cliff, stair, roof, bank, or path ascends or descends at a high angle; the natural opposites are gentle, gradual, or (of slopes) shallow. In surveying, railway, and road engineering the idea is formalized as gradient or grade: a route is steep when its rise per unit of horizontal distance is large. In mathematics and everyday graphing, a steep curve is one whose slope is large in magnitude, in either direction. In architecture a steep-pitch roof rises at a high angle, often to shed snow; in nautical English a shore is steep-to when it drops off suddenly into deep water — exactly the “steep shoulder of land” of the source text, seen from the river.

2. Quantitative sense — sudden and large. From the late nineteenth century, steep has measured magnitudes of change: “a steep increase in prices,” “a steep decline in output,” “a steep tax,” “a steep discount.” The mental model is a graph climbing or plunging near-vertically. Crucially, the sense is direction-neutral: a steep fall is as steep as a steep rise, because steepness now means largeness of change rather than height — a genuine semantic widening, since the Old English core of the word meant “high, lofty.”

3. Informal evaluative sense — exorbitant, excessive. In informal British and American English, a price, fee, rent, demand, or requirement can be “steep,” i.e. unreasonably high: “£80 for a ticket is a bit steep.” The experiential bridge is economic: a steep price is a bill you must haul yourself up to — an ascent of the wallet as taxing as any mountain. The same extension yields “a steep request” and “rather steep conditions.”

4. Informal sense — exaggerated, hard to believe. A story, claim, or excuse that “sounds steep” (chiefly British) is implausible or over-coloured — a tall tale. The bridge is identical: what rises too far above the level of everyday reality loses credibility, just as an over-vertical path loses practicality.

5. Figurative difficulty — arduous. Shakespeare’s Hamlet (c. 1601) makes “the steep and thorny way to heaven” the emblem of moral and spiritual effort; the modern phrase “steep learning curve” (twentieth-century management and psychology) names a process demanding rapid, punishing progress. Any undertaking can be steep when the effort required resembles a hard climb.

6. Technical uses in the professions.

  • Finance: a “steep yield curve” — long-term interest rates far above short-term ones, the curve rising sharply.
  • Aviation: “steep turn,” “steep approach,” “steep descent” in flight profiles.
  • Sport: the “steeplechase” — originally (1790s) a cross-country horse race aimed at a church steeple, later an athletics event with hurdles and a water jump.
  • Mining and quarrying: a “steep seam,” “steeply dipping” rock strata in structural geology.
  • Surveying: “steep grade” on roads and railways; the gradient percentage that triggers warning signs.

7. Homographs and relatives — beware. The rare noun steep means “a steep place” (“the steep of the hill”). The verb to steep — “to soak” (steep the tea, steep the hides) — is a different word: Old English stēpan “to soak, drench,” whose family (Old Norse steypa “to dip, cast down”) carried the sense “to plunge into liquid.” Adjective and verb are at most distant etymological cousins — a textbook homograph pair that developed in tandem. The derived noun steeple (“church tower”), from Old English stȳpel, preserves the ancient “lofty” sense: a steeple is the tall, towering structure built on the same adjective.

8. The semantic bridge in one image. Every sense radiates from a near-vertical line: a surface may be steep; a graph may be steep; a price, a demand, an excuse, and a learning curve may all be steep because they rise (or fall) far and fast, demanding great effort or stretching credibility. The Old English aura of grandeur — “lofty, towering” — has diluted in modern informal use into a mild note of complaint, while the neutral geographical sense remains the dominant everyday one.
