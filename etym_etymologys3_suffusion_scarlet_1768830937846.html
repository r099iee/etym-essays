suffusion — The biography of this word is a biography of pouring. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰew-, "to pour," a verb with a strong ritual bent: in the Indo-Iranian branch it survives in the sacrificial vocabulary of Sanskrit — juhóti, "he pours the oblation," and homa, the fire-offering itself, which passed into Hindi as hom, still a word of burnt sacrifice — while in Greek it produced khéō, "I pour," with khūsis, "a pouring out," and khumós, "juice" (whence English chyme, the liquid mass of digested food). The branch that matters most for suffusion is Latin: fundere, "to pour," which in Latin already carried the extended senses "to melt" and "to cast metal." Around fundere Latin grew an enormous prefixed family, each prefix adding a direction to the pouring: effundere (pour out — effuse), infundere (pour in — infuse), diffundere (pour apart — diffuse), confundere (pour together, throw into disorder — confuse, confound), refundere (pour back — refund), and infundibulum, the vessel for pouring in, which slid through Old French fonil into English as funnel; the melting sense gave fusio, "a casting," hence fuse, fusion, and found (to cast metal). Our word sits in this family as suffundere, "to pour beneath or over," with the Late Latin noun suffusio, "an overspreading." English borrowed suffusion in the sixteenth century, first for medical description — blood suffusing the face, humours suffusing the eye — and then for any spreading of color, light, or liquid from beneath a surface. The semantic heart of the word, and the reason it works so well in the present passage, is the direction of the pour: the color is not applied from outside but rises from within, so that a face suffused with blush, or flowers suffused with crimson, are colored from the inside out, the way a blotter takes up ink. When the text says the flowers "deepened to copper, then with a suffusion of crimson," the word performs its own etymology: crimson poured beneath the flower's surface, saturating the tissue, warming through maroon toward scarlet. Key developments of suffusion:
  • Sound and form: Latin suffundere → suffusio (supine stem suffus-) → English suffusion; the prefix sub- "under" gives the internal direction that defines the word.
  • Medical origin: the earliest English uses (16th c.) are physiological — suffusions of blood under the skin, suffusions of the eye; the clinical word remains current in ophthalmology.
  • Broadening: from the body to any surface — suffusions of light, of color, of water — always retaining the sense of an upward, internal pour.
  • Figurative transfer: a scene suffused with light, a heart suffused with joy — the internal-rise image applied to emotion and atmosphere, giving the word a warm, organic intimacy.
  • Literary attestations: the word is a favorite of descriptive prose and poetry for a color or light that seems to breathe up from within a substance, exactly as in the present passage; the Victorian and Edwardian nature writers, the Romantics, and modern fantasy prose all use it for the slow, inward spread of color across a landscape or a face.

scarlet — The biography of this word is a biography of a trade route and a dye. Its root core is not Indo-European: the word begins in the luxury textile markets of the Persian world, where saqirlāt named a rich, precious cloth; Arabic took it as siqillāṭ, a fine brocade or costly garment, and the medieval Mediterranean passed it on to Europe — Medieval Latin scarlatum, Italian scarlatto, Old French escarlate. English received it in the thirteenth century as scarlet, meaning a rich red cloth of the very highest quality, the kind that sumptuary laws reserved for nobles, prelates, and magistrates. Because the cloth's most prized dye — kermes, the crimson obtained from the scale-insect of the kermes oak (a separate Arabic word, qirmiz, which gave English crimson) — was a brilliant red with an orange cast, the color sense of scarlet steadily displaced the fabric sense: by the fourteenth century the word could mean the hue itself, and the textile sense gradually faded, leaving a color word wrapped in the memory of its cloth. That is why scarlet, alone among reds, still carries a residue of rank and ceremony: cardinals wear scarlet because cardinals once wore scarlet cloth. In England the scarlet robe was the badge of the magistrate and the noble, and from the seventeenth century the famous scarlet coat of the British "redcoats" made the color the uniform of imperial power and battle. The word's later career is entirely chromatic: scarlet fever, the disease named for its rash, entered common speech in the nineteenth century; scarlet letter (from Hawthorne's novel of 1850) made the color the emblem of public shame; and scarlet woman made it the symbol of vivid, shameless display. In the present passage, where the flowers "warmed through maroon to scarlet," the word contributes the full weight of that history: the terminal hue of the scale is not just the brightest red but the costliest, the one with the deepest pedigree of luxury and intensity — the fiery upper limit of the landscape's metamorphosis. Key developments of scarlet:
  • From cloth to hue: the word named a costly fabric for a century before it named a color; the kermes-dyed red of the cloth carried the color sense into English.
  • Rank and ceremony: sumptuary laws, the church, and the army made scarlet the color of power — cardinals, magistrates, soldiers — a connotation the color word retains today.
  • The disease and the idiom: scarlet fever (19th c.), scarlet letter and scarlet woman (19th c.) extended the word from cloth and color into medicine and moral symbolism.
  • Distinctive hue: among English reds, scarlet is the brightest and most orange; it stands at the light, blazing end of the scale against crimson and maroon.
  • Literary attestations: from medieval sumptuary records and the heraldry of the red lion to Hawthorne and modern descriptive prose, scarlet is the red of blood, rank, and passion at their most visible.
