The road that ends at Tsarevitch starts in Rome with a knife. When Julius Caesar fell in 44 BCE, his family name, of uncertain earlier meaning, perhaps elephant or hairy or blue-grey-eyed, passed to his adopted heir Octavian, then to every emperor after him, until caesar no longer named a man but meant emperor itself. That promotion from surname to title is the engine of the whole story: a proper noun became a job description, and the job description travelled further than any legion. Late Latin pronounced it with a hard k, Kaisar, and the Germanic peoples, including the Goths under Ulfilas in the 4th century, borrowed it whole as kaisar, keeping the hard consonants and the imperial weight.

From the Goths the title passed to the Slavs through mission, war, and scripture. Old Church Slavonic cesarĭ, used in the 9th and 10th centuries to render Greek basileus in Bibles and chronicles, named the Christian emperor at Constantinople, and ambitious Balkan rulers, Bulgarian Simeon above all, seized it as a claim to equal empire. Russian princes waited longer: Ivan III married a Byzantine princess and flirted with the style, but it was his grandson Ivan IV, the Terrible, crowned with the cap of Monomakh in 1547, who made tsar the working title of Moscow. From that year the sovereign's sons were tsarevichi by grammar alone, tsar plus the patronymic -evich, son of, the same suffix that makes Ivan's son Ivanovich.

From Caesar to Alexei:

  • Rome to Gothia, 1st–4th c.: Caesar the surname hardens into caesar the title, then Gothic kaisar, pronounced with the original hard k that Slavonic will soften.
  • Scripture and empire, 9th–14th c.: Slavonic cesarĭ serves emperors sacred and secular; Serbian and Bulgarian tsars wear the word as a challenge to Byzantium.
  • Muscovy, 1547–1682: Ivan IV's coronation plants the title in Russia; palace records list little tsarevichi in caftans, and folklore lifts the nursery into myth as Ivan Tsarevich quests for firebirds.
  • Empire and law, 1682–1797: Peter the Great prefers imperator for himself yet keeps tsarevich for his sons, most terribly Alexei Petrovich, interrogated and dead in 1718; Paul's succession statute of 1797 then reserves cesarevich for the designated heir.
  • The last prince, 1904–1918: Alexei Nikolaevich, born a haemophiliac into a doomed autocracy, becomes the most famous tsarevitch of all, photographed in sailor suits, carried by sailors when he could not walk, and murdered with his family at Ekaterinburg.

Phonetically the journey softened and slavicised: Latin hard k palatalised through Gothic into Slavonic ts, so kaisar became cesarĭ and then Russian tsarĭ, while the patronymic -evich added its own soft consonants. English met the word late, through French travelogues and Victorian novels, and kept the French-flavoured spelling tsarevitch or czarevitch with -tch long after linguists standardised tsarevich. Semantically the title narrowed from any emperor to the Russian autocrat, then narrowed again by grammar to his son, and finally widened in English imagination to every fairytale prince east of Warsaw. The capital letter tells the tale: lowercase tsarevich is a court rank, while capitalised Tsarevitch, as in the source sentence, is a character, the boy beyond the blue sea-ocean whose sisters are tsarevnas and whose road always leads to wonder.
