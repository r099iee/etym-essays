- Early 17th century: Early Modern Natural Philosophy - Borrowed directly from Latin accrētiō into Renaissance English scientific discourse to denote physical growth or enlargement resulting from the external adhesion or addition of material particles.
- 18th century: Legal Jurisprudence & Riparian Property Rights - Codified into property and maritime law as the doctrine of accretion (or alluvion), establishing the legal principle that land gradually and imperceptibly added to riverbanks or shorelines by water action becomes the property of the riparian owner.
- 19th century: Geology, Sedimentary Stratigraphy, & Anatomy - Adopted into Victorian geology by Charles Lyell to explain the slow build-up of sedimentary strata, coastal barriers, and coral reefs, while anatomists used it for the appositional, layered growth of bones, shells, and dental structures.
- Mid-20th century: Astrophysics, Cosmology, & Stellar Evolution - Revolutionized modern astrophysics through the formulation of Bondi-Hoyle accretion and accretion disks, describing how proto-stars, neutron stars, and black holes gravitationally capture surrounding matter.
- Late 20th century to Present: Cognitive Science, Gestalt Philosophy, & Metaphorical Prose - Employed in cognitive psychology, systems theory, and humanities as a precise conceptual contrast against holistic organization, designating any mechanical, uninspired, additive aggregation of disparate parts.
