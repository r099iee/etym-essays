ravenously
- 14th century - Middle English ravenous, from Old French ravineus "rapacious, greedy, given to seizing," describes a plundering beast - a wolf, a vulture, a famished man; a word built to name the act of seizing and plundering food.
- 15th-17th centuries - the decisive transfer: the word moves from the act of seizure to the appetite that drives it; to be ravenous is to be so hungry that feeding feels like a raid, the predatory colouring becoming a vivid buried image.
- 18th-19th centuries - ravenous hardens into the standard word for extreme hunger in "ravenous appetite" and "ravenous hunger," and the adverb ravenously ("eat ravenously," "ravenously hungry") emerges as a literary, narrative formation that projects the plundering appetite onto the verb.
- Contemporary - still the strongest, most dramatic word of hunger, fusing desperate appetite with predatory seizure; its register is vivid and slightly archaic, at home in fiction rather than neutral conversation - exactly the force Lewis draws on for the famished Edmund.
