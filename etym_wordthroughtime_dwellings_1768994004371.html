- Old English - dwelling is the gerund of dwellan, "to mislead, hinder, delay"; dwellung means "a delaying, a lingering" - the word names an act, not a building, and the family's oldest senses are being stuck and led astray.
- 14th–15th c. - Middle English dwellen softens from "to tarry, to remain" into "to reside"; dwelling follows, changing from an act of lingering into a place where one stays - the gerund becomes a building.
- 16th–17th c. - dwelling is established as the plain noun of habitation, appearing in fixed compounds (dwelling place, dwelling house) and in legal and administrative prose.
- 18th–21st c. - it keeps a formal-neutral register; the plural dwellings becomes the standard ethnographic and archaeological term for places of settled residence - cave dwellings, cliff dwellings - deliberately colder than "home," a word of settlement without warmth; applied to Martian homes, it stretches human domesticity across an unknown species.
