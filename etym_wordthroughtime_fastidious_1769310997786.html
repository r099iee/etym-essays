- Causing disgust (15th c.) - Enters Middle English from Old French in the passive sense: a thing that is loathsome, repulsive, 'full of distaste,' so a fastidious disease is a disgusting one.
- Having disgust (17th c.) - The sense rotates 180 degrees: the word now describes the person who feels the distaste - excessively particular, hard to please, refined to the point of choosiness, disdainful of anything below his exacting standard.
- Modern usage - Formal and literary, almost always approving in its censure: a fastidious palate or fastidious reader suggests refined discrimination shading into impossible choosiness - the double edge Bingley playfully throws at Darcy.
