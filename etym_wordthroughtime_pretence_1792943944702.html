- Roman — to stretch before: Latin praetendere means to hold forth, allege, or spread as cover.
- Medieval Latin — the alleged plea: praetensa names claims and allegations put forward in law.
- 14th–15th c. — English borrows it: pretence enters as a legal and courtly term for claims advanced and excuses offered.
- 16th–17th c. — plots and religion: pretences of loyalty, of orthodoxy, of illness fill chronicles of conspiracy and persecution.
- 18th–19th c. — the novelist's tool: Fielding, Austen, and Dickens dissect social pretence — manners as daily camouflage.
- Today — fraud and theatre: pretence spans obtaining by false pretences in court and dropping all pretence in conversation — the word of both the charge-sheet and the confessional.
