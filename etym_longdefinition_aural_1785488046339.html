AURAL — comprehensive definition Sense in the given context. In the sentence under study, aural is the operative adjective in the phrase "no aural embodiment": the educated readers of Latin verse, we are told, read it "with a mode of pronunciation which gave no aural embodiment to its metrical structure." The word here means "belonging to the ear as an organ of perception, received by the sense of hearing." The metrical pattern existed, and was even perceived with "fine artistic precision" — but that perception was entirely intellectual, an "intellectual perception of the ordered ranks of abstractly categorised syllables," with nothing for the ear to grasp. Aural is thus the sensory pole of a pointed antithesis: aural against intellectual, ear against mind, sound against abstract classification. The tone is scholarly, dryly analytical, and precise; the adjective is chosen to insist that the Latin system, however coherent as a mental scheme, had no audible reality. Primary lexical sense. In general English, aural is an adjective meaning "relating to the ear or to the sense of hearing." It is the Latinate, technical companion of the Germanic word ear: wherever medicine, education, music, or acoustics must speak of the hearing organ as a channel of reception, aural is the standard term. It was borrowed into English in the mid-nineteenth century (first attested around 1846) from the Late Latin adjective aurālis, "of the ear," itself built on Latin auris, "ear," with the common adjective-forming suffix -ālis. Exhaustive polysemy. The word is not a single flat sense but a small family of closely related usages, all anchored to the ear:
  • Anatomical / clinical sense: aural names whatever belongs to the ear as a physical organ — aural medicine, aural surgery, aural vertigo, aural impairment, aural canal. In otorhinolaryngology the adjective coexists with the Greek-derived otic (from Greek oûs, stem ōt-); aural is the Latin-derived member of the pair.
  • Educational sense — aural skills and aural training: In music education and language teaching, "aural training" and "aural comprehension" name exercises in listening: recognising intervals, rhythms, and spoken sounds by ear. This is the sense behind the famous "aural/oral" examination (listening versus speaking), and it made the near-homophony of aural and oral a permanent feature of exam-room and clerical humour.
  • Linguistic / communication-theory sense: In studies of speech and communication, "the aural channel" is the mode of a message received through the ear, contrasted with the visual channel; aural is the receptive member of the signal system, as opposed to the productive oral (vocal) channel.
  • Musical sense — aural perception, aural analysis, aural dictation: Conservatoires teach "aural skills"; theorists speak of "aural identification" of chords and intervals; "aural theory" is a standard university subject. Here the word means "by ear" as against "by eye" or "by notation."
  • Psychological / psychophysical sense: Auditory psychophysics studies aural localisation, aural masking, and aural thresholds — the ear's processing of sound — as distinct from the physical properties of the sound wave itself, which belong to acoustics.
  • Rare homograph — aural, "of an aura": An entirely distinct adjective exists, derived from Latin aura, "a breath of air, breeze, emanation," meaning "of or pertaining to an aura." It is rare, mostly literary or esoteric, and is a different etymon that happens to coincide in spelling with the ear-word. This is a genuine lexical trap worth knowing.
Jargon and register. In modern technical registers the word remains resolutely sensory and un-metaphorical: it does not, like vision, develop a meaning of "foresight." Its one notable extension is from organ to activity — from "of the ear" to "of listening" — so that an "aural experience" is simply something taken in by hearing. Two registers now coexist: the clinical (measuring, describing the apparatus) and the pedagogical/critical (training, analysing, praising what is heard). In literary criticism the adjective appears in phrases such as "aural texture," "aural pleasure," and "aural imagery," where it insists on the sounded, physical quality of verse — exactly the insistence at work in this context's "no aural embodiment." Synonym boundaries and usage caution. The classical rival is oral, from Latin os, oris, "mouth": aural is of hearing, oral is of speech, and the two are near-homophones in most English accents — the memorable pairing is auricle (the outer ear) versus orifice (a mouth-opening). Auditory (from Latin audīre, "to hear") is the more clinical twin, used of the physiological apparatus (auditory nerve, auditory perception). Acoustic belongs not to the organ but to the physics of sound itself. Auricular, from Latin auricula, "the little ear," is the strictly anatomical term and also carries the "whispered-in-the-ear" sense of auricular confession. And otic is the Greek-derived medical term. Where aural and auditory overlap almost entirely, this passage's "aural embodiment" deliberately stresses the receptive, listening ear — the sensory organ — and sets it against the mind that "perceives" the abstract ranks of syllables.
