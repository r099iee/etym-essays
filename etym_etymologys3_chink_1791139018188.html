Chink in the Dickens sense — “every chink and keyhole,” a narrow slit or crack that lets in light, air or fog — belongs to a small, stubbornly Germanic family of crack-words whose exact pedigree is debated. The OED traces Middle English chinke, chynke (c. 1400, “cleft, fissure”) to a variant of chine “crack, chine of the back,” itself from Old English cinu “cleft, fissure, chink” (also cine “cleft in rock”), cognate with Old Norse kina “to sob, grin_split”? and Middle Low German kine “slit.” Proto-Germanic is reconstructed as *kin- / *kīnan “to split, gape, germinate” (whence also chasm? no — chine as ravine, chine of pork along the backbone cleft). Alternative 19th-century proposals linked it to chincough? or to Celtic, but modern consensus keeps it Germanic with an expressive ch- onset that mimics the act of cracking. Crucially this must be kept apart from the homonym chink n.2 / v.2 “sharp ringing sound, clink of coins or glasses” (c. 1500–1600), which is purely imitative, like clink, clank, jingle, and has no genetic link beyond convergence in form.

Historical stages:

  • Old English (c. 900–1100): cinu, cine appears in glosses for Latin rima “crack” and in herbals for clefts in bark where salves gather. The sense is architectural-natural: rock, wood, wall.
  • Middle English (c. 1300–1500): chine / chinke widens to any narrow opening — wall-crack, window-chink, “chinkes in the wall” through which lovers whisper (cf. Pyramus and Thisbe tradition). Chaucer and Lydgate use chinke for light leaking through boards.
  • Early Modern (1500–1700): the spelling hardens to chink after southern palatalisation of ch- /tʃ/. Shakespeare uses both homonyms with punning delight: wall-chinks in A Midsummer Night’s Dream (“through the chink”) and coin-chinks in Cymbeline. The King James translators prefer cranny but keep chink in Apocrypha.
  • Industrial-Victorian (1800s): chink becomes the classic draught-word: chinks in sash windows, floorboards, shutters through which fog, snow and eavesdroppers pour. Dickens’s “every chink and keyhole” pairs the generic slit with the specific lock-hole to mean total permeability — no sealing against fog.
  • Modern (1900s–present):literal building sense persists (“chink in the armour,” “chink in the wall,” log-cabin chinking — mortar daubed between logs in American English), while figurative “chink in one’s armour / argument” (vulnerable spot) dominates journalism. The sound-homonym thrives separately (“chink of glasses”).

Phonology: Old English cinu /ˈkinu/ with hard /k/ palatalised before front vowels in late Old / early Middle English to /tʃinu/ → /tʃiŋk/ with excrescent nasal + -k fortition typical of crack-words (clink, clunk, kink). The Great Vowel Shift left short i untouched, so pronunciation has been /tʃɪŋk/ since 1500. Plural chinks /tʃɪŋks/.

Socio-cultural resonance: a chink is never neutral — it is the flaw in the barrier, the peephole for voyeurs, the draught that betrays poverty (un-chinked cabins), the light of hope (“a chink of light”) or the crack in perfection. Dickens exploits the dread: fog does not merely surround the counting-house, it “pours in at every chink,” like a besieging fluid finding every moral and physical gap. Later idiom “chink in the armour” (from Achilles / Arthurian plate) makes the slit existential: invulnerability undone by a slit.

Attestations:

  • c. 1000 Ælfric glosses: cinu = rima.
  • c. 1385 Chaucer, Merchant’s Tale? chinkes for peeping.
  • 1595 Shakespeare, Midsummer I: “peep through the chink.”
  • 1843 Dickens, Carol: “every chink and keyhole” — fog invasion.
  • Modern: “chink of light in negotiations,” “chink in the armour,” log-cabin “chinking.”

In sum, chink-as-slit travelled from Old English rock-cleft to medieval love-wall, Shakespearean stage-wall, Victorian draughty office and modern metaphor for vulnerability, while its sound-twin shadowed it as pure echo. The fog that pours through Dickens’s chinks thus carries a thousand years of leaky walls with it.
