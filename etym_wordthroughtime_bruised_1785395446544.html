- Old English 'brysan' / 'brusen' - a violent, deliberate verb meaning 'to crush, pound, shatter': a pounder crushes herbs in a mortar, a smith pounds metal; not yet the accidental mark on a shin.
- c. 1300 (Middle English) - the word narrows to the flesh injury: a blunt blow ruptures small vessels beneath unbroken skin, leaving a mark that darkens slowly from red to blue to purple-green; the homely, undramatic wound that needs no surgeon.
- 17th century - the noun 'bruise' appears, naming the mottled mark itself, the visible evidence of the invisible hurt.
- 19th century - the figurative turn: 'bruised pride', 'bruised ego', 'emotionally bruised' - hurt but not broken, consciously lighter than 'wounded'.
- Modern English - firmly everyday and domestic: a kitchen word and a nursery word ('black and blue', 'a bruised apple'), whose homeliness is exactly its point in epic contexts - even dwarves are subject to bruises.
