- Late 16th–17th c. — Classical Latin borrowing: entered English as an adjective and participle (Latin conglomeratus) meaning “wound together into a ball” or “heaped into a dense rounded cluster,” predominantly applied in anatomical descriptions (e.g. conglomerate glands) and physical philosophy.
- Early 19th c. (1810s–1830s) — Geological formalisation: adopted by pioneering British geologists (such as Charles Lyell) to classify coarse clastic sedimentary rock composed of rounded, water-smoothed pebbles cemented in a fine-grained matrix, distinguishing it from angular breccia.
- Late 19th to Early 20th c. — General sociological & cultural metaphor: broadened into an elevated metaphor for any composite, heterogeneous cultural or political entity consisting of disparate ethnic, linguistic, or institutional elements bound together under a single umbrella.
- Mid-20th c. (1950s–1970s) — Corporate finance revolution: explosive redefinition on Wall Street during the postwar wave of mergers and acquisitions; used to describe a massive corporate parent acquiring totally unrelated businesses (e.g., ITT, Litton Industries, Berkshire Hathaway) to hedge market risks.
- 21st c. — Contemporary usage: firmly bifurcated between the technical geological classification and the corporate/media mega-business sense, alongside its nuanced figurative role describing rich, multi-layered composites like the English language itself.
