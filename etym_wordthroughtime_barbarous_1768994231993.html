- 15th c. - barbarous enters English (via French and Latin, from Greek barbaros) still echoing its oldest acoustic core: it names what is foreign, non-Greek, of unintelligible speech - people who seemed only to go 'bar-bar'.
- 16th–17th c. - it broadens into a full term of civilization's judgment: rude, uncultivated, pagan, outside the pale of polished custom - a word for explorers, preachers, and chroniclers measuring the world's edges.
- 18th c. - the moral sense crowds out the descriptive one: barbarous now chiefly condemns acts and men as brutal, cruel, inhuman - exceeding roughness into atrocity, as of war and punishment.
- 19th c. - the Victorian age sets 'barbarous' hard against 'civilized' in colonial and reformist rhetoric, while a quieter aesthetic sense lingers for style that is harsh, unpolished, or unreadable.
- 20th–21st c. - strongly condemnatory in ordinary speech (barbarous cruelty), it can in poetic prose revert to its primal sense - a wildness that refuses order and language, terrains that cannot be read or tamed; here it is aimed not at men but at land.
