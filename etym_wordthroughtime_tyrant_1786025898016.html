- 7th–6th c. BCE - In Ancient Greece, *týrannos* emerged to describe non-hereditary absolute rulers who seized power outside traditional constitutional aristocracies; initially neutral and often populist, celebrated for civic building programs.
- 4th c. BCE - Classical Greek philosophers (Plato, Aristotle) codified the word with severe moral opprobrium, defining a tyrant as an unlawful monarch ruling solely for selfish indulgence rather than the civic good.
- 14th–16th c. - Entering Middle and Early Modern English via Old French, *tyrant* crystallized into an absolute embodiment of political wickedness, cruelty, and unlawful violence, central to Renaissance anti-tyrannicide debates.
- 18th–19th c. - Broadened during the Enlightenment and Democratic Revolutions into a sweeping rhetorical weapon against any monarchical overreach, while simultaneously acquiring domestic and organizational senses (despotic fathers, schoolmasters).
- 20th–21st c. - Extended metaphorically to psychological obsessions (the tyrant of conscience/habit) and specialized biological nomenclature (Tyrannosaurus rex, Tyrannidae), while remaining the ultimate ethical condemnation for authoritarian strongmen.
