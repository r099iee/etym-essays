stinking (adjective and adverb) primarily describes something that emits a strong, offensive, and foul odor. Derived as the participial adjective of the native Germanic verb stink, the word is one of the oldest and most visceral sensory descriptors in the English language. In contemporary usage, stinking exhibits extraordinary semantic versatility, functioning not only as a literal olfactory designator, but also as an established botanical and zoological naming convention, an emphatic pejorative insult, and an informal colloquial intensifier expressing obscene abundance or intoxication.

The comprehensive polysemous dimensions of stinking encompass the following primary, secondary, and colloquial senses:

  • Primary Olfactory Malodor: Giving off a foul, noxious, or revolting smell. Applied across everyday environments to substances such as rotting garbage, sewage, sulfurous chemical fumes, untreated animal hides, unwashed bodies, or stale sweat. Unlike clinical terms such as malodorous or formal terms such as fetid, stinking carries a blunt, earthy force that immediately communicates physiological repulsion and social disgust.
  • Botanical, Horticultural, & Zoological Taxonomy: Utilized systematically in vernacular and historical biological naming to designate plant and animal species characterized by strong, pungent, or offensive defensive odors. Common botanical examples include stinking chamomile (Anthemis cotula), stinking hellebore (Helleborus foetidus), stinking iris (Iris foetidissima), and stinking Roger (Tagetes minuta). In zoology, historical texts refer to the stinking badger or skunk. In this domain, the modifier denotes a protective biochemical adaptation developed by organisms to deter herbivores or predators.
  • Moral Condemnation & Pejorative Insult: Employed metaphorically to denounce persons, actions, or institutions regarded as thoroughly contemptible, corrupt, or despicable. To call someone a stinking liar, a stinking hypocrite, or a stinking coward transfers the visceral revulsion inspired by a foul stench onto moral character, signaling that the subject's behavior is so disgraceful that it metaphorically offends the senses. Similarly, an exclamation like "the whole stinking system is rigged" expresses profound, totalizing cynicism.
  • Colloquial Intensive Adverb (Extreme Abundance or Excess): Serving informally as an emphatic intensifier (adverbial particle) signifying offensive, excessive, or astonishing degree. The most famous and enduring idiom in this category is stinking rich (recorded from the early 20th century), meaning possessing wealth so vast, conspicuous, and vulgar that it figuratively "stinks" of excess. Similarly, stinking drunk denotes being thoroughly and incapacitatedly intoxicated, evoking both the literal breath and sweat of heavy alcohol consumption and the total loss of self-control.
  • Lexicographical Delineation of Offensive Filth: In lexicographical synonymy, stinking functions as a defining descriptor alongside putrid within the entry for foul. Dictionaries use stinking to specify that an offensive object or environment produces an active, airborne olfactory revulsion that penetrates the surroundings, distinguishing active stench from passive surface dirt.

The semantic bridge linking sensory stench to moral condemnation and hyperbolic intensifiers lies in the phenomenon of sensory overwhelm. A foul stench cannot be ignored; it commands the attention, forces physical withdrawal, and overwhelms the senses. Consequently, English speakers readily transferred this quality of undeniable, invasive excess onto moral violations (corrupt deeds that "stink") and financial excess (wealth so massive that it commands unavoidable notice).
