*steig- (Proto-Indo-European root; prehistoric, c. 4000–2500 BCE) — “to prick, to be sharp, to stick, to pierce” → Latin stinguō / stinguere (first attested in prefixed compounds, Roman Republic, 3rd–1st c. BCE) — “to prick, to mark by pricking; to quench, to extinguish” (the simple verb is rare outside compounds) → Latin distinguō / distinguere (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero) — dis- “apart, in different directions” + stinguere; literally “to separate by pricking, to mark off, to divide”; then “to distinguish, to tell apart”; then “to set apart as eminent, to make distinguished” → Old French distinguer (attested 12th c.) — “to distinguish, to mark the difference between” → French distingué (past participle of distinguer, used adjectivally; elevated in the aristocratic salons of the 17th–18th c.) — “singled out; distinguished, refined, elegant” → English distingué (borrowed as a French adjective, first attested early 19th c.) — “having an air of distinction; dignified, elegant, or refined” NOTE (debated/nuanced): The base verb stinguere is nearly restricted to its compounds (distinguere “to mark off”, exstinguere “to quench”, instinguere “to goad on”), and the two sense-branches “to prick” and “to quench” are both standardly derived from PIE *steig- (“to prick/stick”: a fire is quenched by “sticking” or “stamping” it out), but this bridge is reconstructed rather than directly attested; a minority of scholars keep the “quench” sense apart from the “prick” sense at the Latin stage.
