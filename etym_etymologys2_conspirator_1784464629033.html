TWO SEPARATE CHAINS — CONSPIRATOR is a Latin agent noun built from con- "with, together" + spirare "to breathe" + the agent suffix -tor. The two elements have separate Indo-European histories. CHAIN 1 — SPĪRĀRE ("to breathe"), the stem of the compound: → PIE *(s)peis- / *speis- "to blow, breathe" (proposed reconstructed root of blowing/breathing; the reconstruction is debated and not universally accepted — see note) → Latin spīrāre (v.) "to breathe, blow; to live, to have breath" (classical Latin, attested from the 2nd–1st c. BCE; the source of the huge English family spirit, inspire, expire, respire, transpire) → Latin cōnspīrāre (v.) "to breathe together; to be in harmony, to agree; to plot together in secret" (con- + spīrāre; classical Latin, attested from the 1st c. BCE — used by Cicero of political agreement and of plots) → Latin cōnspīrātiō (n.) "agreement, unanimity; conspiracy, secret plot" (classical Latin) → Latin cōnspīrātor (n.) "one who conspires, a co-plotter" (agent noun, cōnspīrā(re) + -tor; Latin, attested) → French conspirateur (n.) "conspirator" (from Latin; the form that entered English) → English conspirator (n.) "one who takes part in a conspiracy" (borrowed, Middle English from the late 14th c., e.g. as conspiratour) → Modern English CONSPIRATOR — the card's target form. CHAIN 2 — CON- ("with, together"), the first element: → PIE *ḱom (preposition/adverb) "beside, near, with, together" (reconstructed; one of the most securely attested Indo-European prepositions — cognate with Hittite katta "with, down", Old Irish com-/con- "with", Greek xun/sun "with" via a by-form, and, in Latin, cum) → Latin cum (preposition) "with" → the combining form con-/com-/co- "with, together" (used before vowels and consonants in Latin compounds) → In cōnspīrāre → cōnspīrātiō → cōnspīrātor → French conspirateur → English conspirator, the prefix survives as the initial con- of conspirator. NOTE on the debate: the PIE root of Latin spīrāre is not securely established. The traditional guess connects it to *(s)peis- "to blow," comparing Germanic blowing-breathing words (e.g. Old English fīsian "to blow", Old High German fīsan "to blow"), but the cognate set is thin and many dictionaries leave spīrāre as "origin uncertain." What is certain is the attested Latin chain spīrāre → cōnspīrāre → cōnspīrātiō → cōnspīrātor, and the semantic slide inside Latin from "harmony, breathing as one" to "secret plotting against authority" — the slide that gives conspiracy its dark modern charge. English conspirator is first recorded in the late Middle English period and derives through the French form conspirateur.
