"in an unwavering, resolute manner"

Note on historical debate: Etymologists trace the verb swerve to PIE *swerbʰ- ("to turn, whirl, wipe"). In Old English, sweorfan primarily meant "to rub, polish, or file" (surviving dialectally in metalworking). In the 14th century (attested in Chaucer and the Wycliffite Bible), the sense underwent a dramatic semantic extension from the spinning friction of a polishing tool or scouring wheel to centrifugal deflection—suddenly "veering or turning aside from a straight path." The addition of the negative prefix un- produced a moralized compound signifying absolute steadfastness, which crystallized into the adverb unswervingly by the 17th century.
