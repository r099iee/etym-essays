COURTYARD — a compound of two elements, court + yard, each of which has its own full derivation chain (both, remarkably, from the same PIE root of enclosure). CHAIN 1 (the element COURT): 1. Proto-Indo-European *ǵʰer- "to enclose, fence in, grasp" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) — also the source of Latin hortus "garden" and Greek khórtos "enclosed yard, pasture." 2. Latin hortus "enclosed garden, yard" (< PIE *ǵʰor-tó-) (attested from the 3rd c. BCE). 3. Latin cohors (gen. cohortis) "enclosed farmyard; band, retinue; cohort" — from co- "together" + hort- "enclosure" (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE); the "band" sense arose because a Roman military unit shared an enclosed yard. 4. Old French cort / curt / court "royal residence; assembly; enclosed yard" (attested from the 9th c. CE). 5. Middle English court "court of a king; court of law; courtyard" (attested 12th–13th c. CE). 6. English courtyard (compound court + yard, first attested c. 1550s) — the rootWord on this card: COURTYARD. CHAIN 2 (the element YARD): 1. Proto-Indo-European *ǵʰer- "to enclose, fence in" (reconstructed; see Chain A) — specifically the *-tó- formation *ǵʰor-tó- "enclosure." 2. Proto-Germanic *gardaz "enclosure, yard, dwelling, garden" (c. 500 BCE–500 CE) — also the source of Gothic gards "house," Old Norse garðr "enclosure, yard," German Garten "garden." 3. Old English geard "enclosure, yard, fence, dwelling" (attested from the 8th c. CE). 4. Middle English yard / yerd "enclosed space, yard" (12th c. CE). 5. English yard, then the compound English courtyard (c. 1550s): COURTYARD. Note: because both elements descend from the same root *ǵʰer- "to enclose," the compound means, etymologically, "enclosure-enclosure." TAVERN: 1. Proto-Indo-European *treb- "dwelling, building, timber house" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) — also the root of English thorp "village" and Lithuanian troba "dwelling, house." 2. Latin trabs (gen. trabis) "beam, timber, plank" (attested from the 2nd c. BCE) — the building-material sense. Debate: some scholars derive the next stage directly from *treb- "dwelling" without passing through "beam"; the trabs link is traditional but not absolutely secure. 3. Latin taberna "a wooden hut, shed, shop, tavern" — traditionally explained as *traberna "a building of boards" from trabs (attested from the 1st c. BCE; cf. taberna cauponaria "tavern," tabernaculum "tent"). 4. Old French taverne "shed; tavern, drinking-house" (attested 11th c. CE). 5. Middle English taverne / tavarne "public house selling wine" (attested 13th c. CE). 6. English tavern "an establishment for the sale of drinks; a public house" — the rootWord on this card: TAVERN. Debate note: the intermediate Latin stage taberna is sometimes derived from *treb- "building" via a *-bʰ- formation, with the link through trabs "beam" (making a "board-hut") being the traditional OED account; either route rests on the secure root *treb-, whose core sense is dwelling.
