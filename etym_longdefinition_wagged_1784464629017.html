Wagged is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb wag: to move or cause to move repeatedly from side to side or up and down in a short, quick, oscillating motion. In The Hobbit, as the firelight flickers in the April dark of Bag End and the dwarves still play, “the shadow of Gandalf's beard wagged against the wall.” The verb is concrete, homely, and slightly comic: not a grand gesture but the rhythmic bob and sway of a long beard's shadow keeping time with music and breath. The past tense freezes a continuous little motion into a single observed event, grounding the epic singing in a cozy silhouette on plaster. It is the kind of detail that makes the wizard feel both powerful and absurdly domestic.

Precision about the motion. Wag implies a short, rapid, repeated oscillation — lateral or vertical — of something flexible or loosely attached: a dog's tail, a beard, a finger, a head, a tongue. Sway suggests slower, larger arcs of a tall or broad body; nod is an intentional, abrupt head movement; flutter is lighter, often air-driven, and less regular; flicker (used of the firelight in the same sentence) is irregular variation of light rather than motion of a solid mass; wobble implies instability and teetering; shake is more violent and general; oscillate is the technical, neutral word for any to-and-fro movement. Wagged keeps the short, quick, rhythmic play of a loose appendage — the exact motion of a beard-shadow keeping time with music.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings

  • Physical wag (core sense): to move or cause to move quickly to and fro — a dog's tail, a finger of reproof, a beard, a head (“he wagged his head”).
  • Gossip idiom: “tongues wag” / “to wag one's tongue”: to talk, especially to gossip; tongues “wag” like tails — a lively repeated motion transferred to speech. “To set tongues wagging” = to provoke gossip.
  • “The tail wagging the dog”: the idiom for a minor or secondary element improperly controlling a larger one (19th-century American English; popularized in 20th-century politics), from the absurd image of the dog's tail directing the dog.
  • Wag as noun — a joker: “a wag” is a witty, joking person (17th–18th century English); “to play the wag” meant to act the merry prankster. This sense grew from the image of lively, playful motion and banter, now chiefly archaic or literary.
  • British and Australian slang — truanting: “to wag” (or “to wag school,” “to wag it”) means to play truant, to skip school (19th-century British school slang, surviving especially in Australian and New Zealand English). The link is probably via “play the wag” = to play about, idle, be a truant.
  • Figurative motion in engineering and nature: any oscillating part may be said to wag; “wagging” appears in zoology of tails, in machinery of lever play, and colloquially of anything that swings rhythmically.
  • Form note: wagged is a fully regular weak past / past participle (wag + -ed); it carries no archaic or dialectal restriction — it is simply the inflected form of an everyday verb, and only the idiom-rich verb itself carries the figurative load.

Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: All the figurative senses grow out of the core physical image of a short, quick, repetitive motion. From “the tail moves to and fro” came “the tongue moves to and fro” (gossip), “the body sways with laughter” (a wag, a joker), “the schoolboy plays about instead of attending” (wagging school), and “the tail directs the dog” (inverted control). Each is a metaphor built on the same tiny mechanical image: something small and lively moving back and forth, drawing attention to itself. The past form wagged in the Tolkien context is the plain physical sense — but it sits in a sentence of firelight and song, so the little wagging shadow reads as music made visible.
