• Old wives' tale: Similar because it is the standard synonym for the idiomatic sense of "midwife's tale"—an unscientific folk belief passed down orally; differs because "old wives' tale" is the broader, more common form that does not require a midwife at all, while "midwife's tale" keeps the historical link to birth-lore and the professional figure.
  • Superstition: Similar because both denote irrational or unverifiable beliefs; differs because superstition is the general category of such beliefs (often involving fear of the unknown), while "midwife's tale" is the specific narrative vehicle—an orally transmitted story—rather than the belief itself.
  • Folk belief / folklore: Similar because both refer to traditional knowledge passed through the community; differs because folklore is a neutral, academic term for collective tradition, whereas "midwife's tale" is narrower (birth and domestic lore) and carries a dismissive or nostalgic tone.
  • Myth: Similar because both are traditional stories transmitted by word of mouth; differs because myth generally involves gods, heroes, and cosmic origins and is treated with cultural seriousness, while a "midwife's tale" is domestic, practical, and usually doubted.
  • Legend: Similar because both are traditional narratives handed down orally; differs because legend claims a historical kernel or famous personage, while "midwife's tale" makes humble, domestic claims about herbs, omens, and births.
  • Hearsay / rumour: Similar because both are unverified claims passed from person to person; differs because hearsay and rumour are short-lived contemporary gossip, whereas a "midwife's tale" is a generational inheritance of folk tradition.
  • Fairy tale: Similar because both are oral wonder-stories; differs because fairy tales are acknowledged fiction with magic and stock characters, while a "midwife's tale" is asserted as true practical knowledge, even when false.
  • Obstetrician's: Similar because both are possessives of a childbirth practitioner; differs because the obstetrician is the modern, university-trained, surgically oriented doctor who replaced the midwife in institutional medicine, so "obstetrician's" carries scientific prestige while "midwife's" carries tradition.
  • Doula's: Similar because both are possessives of a birth attendant; differs because a doula provides non-clinical emotional and physical support and does not deliver the baby, whereas a midwife is a clinical provider responsible for the birth itself.
  • Midwifery: Similar because both name the same profession; differs because midwifery is the abstract noun for the practice, while "midwife's" is the possessive of the person, pointing to a specific practitioner's belongings, role, or lore.
