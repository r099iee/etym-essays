Yelp is a rare case of a word whose modern meaning is almost the opposite of its ancient one. Its source is the West Germanic root *gelp-, 'to cry out loudly', which in Old English, as the strong verb gielpan, meant primarily 'to boast, to exult' — the cry of pride rather than the cry of pain. The noun gielp meant 'boasting, arrogance, vainglory', and Anglo-Saxon homilies condemn gielp as empty self-praise, the vaunt of the fool who trusts in his own strength; the related gilp of Old Saxon has the same colouring in the Heliand, the Old Saxon gospel-poem, where pride is condemned as the enemy of God. From the very beginning, however, the root carried both faces of loud self-assertion: to cry aloud and to cry oneself up were two forms of the same vocal boast, and the earliest records of the family show them side by side. THE ROOT ACROSS THE GERMANIC WORLD The family was never confined to English. Old Norse gjalpa meant both to brag and to yelp shrilly — the noun gjalp is boasting, bragging, a vaunting cry — and the sense survives into modern Icelandic gjalpa, 'to talk loudly or boastfully; to yelp'. Middle Low German gelpen and Middle Dutch gelpen meant 'to cry out, to boast', so no branch of the family ever quite separated the two senses until modern English did. This Germanic-wide spread matters because it shows that the 'boast' sense was not an English peculiarity but the common inheritance: wherever the root appears, loud voice and loud pride are one. There is no secure Proto-Indo-European ancestor for the root; it is attested only within Germanic, and the Indo-European look-alikes that have been proposed are rejected as coincidental, so the word's true history is short, native, and sound-symbolic — a cry-word, made to imitate a cry. MIDDLE ENGLISH: THE SLIDE FROM PRIDE TO PAIN In Middle English yelpen continued to mean 'to speak proudly, to boast', but by the 13th century it also meant simply 'to cry out, to shout', and its application to animals began in the 14th century, when hounds that gave tongue were said to 'yelp'. The turning point came in the 15th century, when the sense specialised decisively toward the short, sharp cry of a dog in pain or sudden excitement. The boast-sense faded, and by the 17th century it had vanished altogether, leaving yelp exclusively animal — a classic case of semantic narrowing from general loud vocal self-assertion to one involuntary, high-pitched vocalisation, and a remarkable one because the emotional value flipped from pride to pain. MODERN USE AND THE PARTICIPLE Modern English keeps the narrow sense: a yelp is the brief, piercing cry of a hurt or startled dog, fox, or child, and the verb is used of people only when they too are momentarily reduced to that involuntary, animal register — she yelped in pain, he yelped with surprise. Derived forms show the word's reach: yelper (a dog or person that yelps), the 'yelping jackal' (a local name for the golden jackal), and the figurative 'yelp of protest', a small sharp complaint. The present participle yelping, the form Tolkien uses, takes the momentary cry and makes it serial: in The Hobbit the burning Warg 'leaps to and fro yelping horribly', and the word's insistent, broken repetition mimes the creature's panic — each yelp a fresh spasm of pain, the sound of discipline cracking into raw nerve. It is a measure of how far the word has travelled that the same phonetic root that condemned a boastful man in an Anglo-Saxon pulpit now names the cry of a hurt animal on a burning mountain. KEY ATTESTATIONS AND TURNING POINTS
  • Old English — gielpan 'to boast, exult, cry aloud'; gielp 'vainglory', condemned in the Blickling and Vercelli homilies and by Ælfric.
  • Old Saxon — gilpan 'to boast'; the Heliand condemns gilp as the pride that defies God.
  • Old Norse — gjalpa 'to brag, to yelp'; the sense survives in modern Icelandic gjalpa.
  • 13th century — Middle English yelpen 'to cry out, to shout', the boast-sense still attached.
  • 14th century — applied to hounds: 'to give tongue, to cry shrilly'; the animal sense begins to take over.
  • 15th century — narrowed to the short, sharp cry of a dog in pain or excitement; the human boast-sense fades.
  • 17th century to the present — yelp is exclusively the brief, high-pitched, involuntary outcry of pain or fright; yelping, the serial participle, is the word in full flood.
