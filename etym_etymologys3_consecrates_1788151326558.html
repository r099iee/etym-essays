1. The sacred in Roman law and religion. The story of consecration begins with the Latin adjective sacer, whose attested range spans ‘holy’ and ‘accursed’: in the Twelve Tables (5th century BCE) the clause sacer esto declared an offender ‘set apart’ to a god — a legal device that made a man killable without pollution. Roman religion built on this root a rich vocabulary: sacrāre ‘to hallow’, sacrum ‘a sacred thing’, sacrificium ‘the making-sacred act’. Out of it grew the verb consecrāre (Cicero, Livy, the Augustan poets), ‘to consecrate, dedicate, devote’: the formal Pontifical act that attached a temple, an altar, a statue, or a piece of public property to a divinity, removing it forever from common use. Consecration was a legal-religious transfer, and its permanence — sacrum was hard to deconsecrare — underlies every later sense of the English word.

2. Consecratio in imperial cult. From Augustus onward the word gained a political meaning: consecratio named the posthumous deification of the emperor, decreed by the Senate (the apotheosis, with the eagle released from the pyre). A consecrated emperor was enrolled among the divi, given a temple and priests, and used dynastic legitimacy for his successors. This ‘consecration’ of rulers shows how the family of sacer-words could absorb the state’s own religion — a precedent the church would later re-claim for exclusively divine honor.

3. Christian Latin and the liturgy. Christian Latin (Tertullian, Ambrose, Augustine) redirected consecratio toward the altar: the consecration of the Eucharist (the words of institution over bread and wine), of churches and altars (dedication rites with anointing), of cemeteries (consecrated ground, from the 5th century), and of bishops (episcopal consecratio, the fullest form of ordination). By the time of the Gregorian sacramentaries, consecration was a technical liturgical term with precise minister, matter, and form — a permanence of setting-apart embodied in canon law.

4. Into English: the fourteenth century. The word crosses into Middle English in the late fourteenth century — consecraten, from Old French consecrer plus Latin consecratus — in the very documents that transmitted its liturgical weight: missals, pontificals, sermons, and Wyclif’s eucharistic polemics. The earliest English senses are all ritual: consecrating the host, dedicating churches, ordaining bishops. The Reformation split the usage rather than the word: Catholics kept the full doctrine of consecration at the words of institution (Trent, 1545–63), while Luther and Cranmer redefined what ‘consecration’ did (‘the consecration of the elements’ admitted varying content), and English burial law busied itself for centuries with ‘consecrated ground’.

5. Secular widening (16th–18th centuries). Early Modern English extended consecrate beyond the altar without losing its gravity: Elizabethan and Jacobean dedication pages ‘consecrate’ poems to patrons; Cromwellian and Restoration orators ‘consecrate’ swords, flags, and causes; Milton applies it to liberty and to grief. The word became available for any dedication solemn enough to deserve permanence — the semantic bridge by which ‘a life consecrated to a cause’ became normal prose.

  • Memorial culture: from the 19th century, war memorials, banners, and cemeteries are ‘consecrated’; un-consecrated ground excludes (suicides, excommunicates), a social power visible in fiction and parish documents.
  • Modern law: consecrated land remains a legal category in church administration (faculty jurisdiction), keeping the word’s technical life alive.

6. Romanticism and the source line. The Romantic period gave consecrate its most influential figurative sense. In Wordsworth’s The Prelude (1805), the young poet, describing the days of ‘active days urged on by flying hours’ — ‘days of sweet leisure, taxed with patient thought’ — asks what would ‘avail’ all this ‘but for a gift that consecrates the joy’: the gift is the poetic imagination, and its consecrating is exactly the ritual act domesticated — to lift fleeting experience out of the ordinary and make it permanently sacred. The word thus comes full circle from the Roman priesthood through the altar to the secular altar of art. Today ‘consecrates’ is a careful, high-register verb, at home in liturgy, law, and poetry — and wherever it appears, it still promises permanence: what is consecrated is set apart, and what is set apart is not lightly touched again.
