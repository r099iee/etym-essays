1. A surname of mouths and cheeks. The name Boccaccio begins not in literature but in the marketplace of medieval Tuscany. Latin bucca, originally the ‘puffed cheek’ (the classical word for a proper mouth was os), passed into the spoken Latin of Italy as bocca, ‘mouth’, still the everyday Italian word. A man nicknamed Boccaccio — ‘big mouth’, with the augmentative-pejorative -accio — gave the surname to a Florentine banking family; the poet’s father was Boccaccino di Chellino, his own name a slightly less coarse variant of the same nickname. The deep pre-Roman origin of bucca itself is beyond recovery; it belongs to the expressive, imitative stratum of Latin that resists Indo-European reconstruction — an honest boundary-mark in the word’s history.

2. The young Boccaccio: Naples and the romances. Giovanni Boccaccio, born 1313 in Certaldo or Florence, was set by his merchant father to commerce and then canon law, first in Florence and then in Naples (c. 1327–1340). Naples under King Robert of Anjou was a court of Provençal, French and Italian letters, and there Boccaccio read the chivalric romances, the French fabliaux and the classics without system but with appetite. The love story of Fiammetta (perhaps Maria d’Aquino) runs through his early works: the Filocolo, the Filostrato (source of Chaucer’s Troilus), the Teseida (source of the Knight’s Tale), the pastoral Ameto and the extraordinary Elegia di Madonna Fiammetta, a first-person amorous monologue by a woman. In these years Boccaccio forged the two things later fused in the Decameron: the courtly romance and the comic tale.

3. The Decameron (c. 1348–1353). The Black Death reached Florence in 1348. In the Decameron’s famous frame, ten young people — seven women, three men — retreat to villas outside the city and tell one hundred tales over ten days, one story per day per person, each day under a king or queen who sets the theme (fortune, wit, love, practical jokes). The materials are a mosaic of fabliaux, the Novellino, oriental tales, local Florentine scandal and pure invention — exactly the ‘borrowing from the old French fabliaux’ that Pater’s essay notes. The result was the founding text of the European novella and the first great prose masterpiece in Italian, its style a flexible, ironic Tuscan capable of tragedy, farce, sermon and innuendo in a single day.

  • Humanist turn (1350–1375): the friendship with Petrarch (from 1350) drew Boccaccio to the Latin classics; he produced the Genealogia deorum gentilium, the massive mythographic encyclopedia, the De montibus, the Latin eclogues, and the biography of Dante with the lectures he gave at Florence in 1373–4. His last years (in Certaldo, where he died on 21 December 1375) were poor by his own account but rich in correspondences.
  • Reception: the Decameron was copied, printed (c. 1470–71), translated into French and Spanish, quarried by Chaucer, Shakespeare, La Fontaine, and by every novellieri after. The Counter-Reformation expurgated it (1573), and ‘Boccaccio’ became the test-case of censored literature.
  • Modern afterlife: Pasolini’s film (1971), the Premio Boccaccio, endless scholarly editions: the name now signifies the carnivalesque resilience of narrative — the story told while the plague rages.

4. Why the name still speaks. Boccaccio stands at the hinge of European literature: behind him the fabliau and the frame-tale of the East, before him the novel of the West. The surname of a Florentine ‘big mouth’ became the generic label for storytelling that refuses to die — the very point Pater makes when he lists Boccaccio among the creatures of the Renaissance who took the outlines of their tales from the old French fabliaux. His name, like the Decameron’s ten tellers, has outlived the plague.
