TARRY has the rare distinction of being a word whose ancestry passes through a documented debate. Its standard derivation runs from Latin tardus ("slow, sluggish") to the denominative verb tardare ("to delay"), through Anglo-Norman tarier ("to delay; to vex"), and into Middle English tarien in the fourteenth century. Yet the deeper history is murkier than the neat chain suggests, and the word's biography is partly a story of how dictionaries and philologists untangle a knot that medieval scribes themselves left loose.

1. The Latin foundation and its mystery. The Latin adjective tardus "slow" is a common word of the classical language, but its origin is obscure. Latin etymologists do not agree on an Indo-European root for it: no convincing cognate exists in Greek, Germanic, or Sanskrit, so many scholars now suspect a non-Indo-European (substrate) origin or a formation isolated within Latin itself. From tardus the Romans derived the verb tardare "to delay, to slow down", used freely by Caesar and Cicero, and tardare passed into the common speech of the western empire, where the provincial forms reshaped it toward what would become French.

2. The Anglo-Norman middleman. After the Norman Conquest, the French of the English court and law carried a verb tarier into Anglo-Norman documents. Its central sense was "to delay, to tarry, to wait", but it also carried the awkward secondary sense "to vex, to irritate, to provoke". Philologists still debate whether this vexation sense is an independent development, a different verb of Germanic origin that collided phonetically with tarier, or a semantic accident of Anglo-Norman usage. What is certain is that English took over both strands: Middle English tarien in the fourteenth century meant "to delay, to linger, to wait" and, briefly, "to vex or anger". The hostile sense faded in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, leaving only the peaceful one - but old texts still contain sentences that confuse modern readers, and historical dictionaries record the ghost sense carefully.

  • 14th century: tarien is an ordinary Middle English verb for staying, delaying, and waiting, found in Chaucer and his contemporaries; the vexation sense is also live.
  • 16th-17th centuries: the vexation sense dies out; tarry becomes a common verb of waiting and lingering, and the English Bible gives it a permanent place in the national ear.

3. The King James Bible and the fixing of register. The decisive event in the word's life is its use in the King James Bible (1611) and in earlier sixteenth-century translations. Phrases such as "tarry ye here", "tarry until I come", and the repeated command "tarry not" made the verb the language of patient, reverent waiting - of Abraham at the tent door, of the disciples told to wait for the promise. The Book of Common Prayer reinforced the effect, so that by 1700 tarry carried an overlay of devotion it had never had in Chaucer. Meanwhile the proverb "time and tide tarry for no man" (recorded from the fifteenth century) preserved the temporal sense in a memorable, quotable form that is still repeated today, and it is this proverb that most modern speakers know.

4. The retreat into literature. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries tarry withdrew from conversation. Standard prose preferred stay, wait, or delay, and tarry survived chiefly in novels, poetry, and deliberate archaism - the world of Dickens's and Scott's historical pages, of hymns and homilies. The twentieth century completed the process: tarry is now a consciously literary word, chosen for the cadence of the Bible, for period colour in fantasy and historical fiction, or for a gentle, old-fashioned dignity that modern synonyms cannot supply. On the card, "but we can __________ no longer" draws on exactly this register: the archaic grandeur of the verb makes the breaking of patience feel weighty, as though a vow of waiting were being abandoned. The word's long journey - from a disputed Latin adjective, through a vexed Anglo-Norman court verb, into the Bible, and out again into poetry - is a compact history of English itself.
