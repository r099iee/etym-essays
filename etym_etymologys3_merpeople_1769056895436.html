This note contains two root words, mer- and people, and the evolution of each is told separately. === ESSAY ON mer- (the sea-element) === The first half of merpeople is one of the most venerable words in the language. It goes back to a Proto-Indo-European root *móri-, "body of water, sea, lake," a root so widely attested that it must have belonged to the common ancestor of the Italic, Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic branches alike. Latin mare gave the whole Romance family—Italian mare, French mer, Spanish mar—and a stream of Latin-derived English loans such as marine, mariner, maritime, marina, and submarine. The Germanic branch produced *mari-, which descended on the continent as German Meer and Dutch meer, and on the northern wing as Old Norse marr; and in England it gave Old English mere, "sea, lake, pool of water." In Celtic the same root yields Old Irish muir, Welsh môr, and Breton mor, while in Balto-Slavic it gives Old Church Slavonic morje, Russian more, and Lithuanian marios. Greek, significantly, lost the word, using instead the unrelated θάλασσα (thálassa), and the Indo-Aryan branch likewise has no reflex for "sea"—so the root is a classic case of a water-word shared across the western half of the Indo-European world but absent in its eastern reaches.
  • The sound-shift record: the root's initial *m- and its o-grade vowel are strikingly stable across the family—mare, mere, muir, môr, morje all keep the same m-r skeleton—so that the "sea" of Latin, English, Irish, Welsh, and Russian are one and the same ancient word, recognisably related after four thousand years.
  • The narrowing of mere: in Old English mere meant the open sea, a lake, or a pool, but as the word sea itself (from a different Germanic root) took over the "ocean" senses, mere settled into the domestic meaning of "lake, pool, fen," and in modern English it survives mainly as a poetic archaism and in the place-names Windermere, Grasmere, and Buttermere, where it still simply means "lake." The famous "mere" of Arthurian legend—the lake from which the Lady rises—is the same word.
  • The compound that saved it: its most famous survival, however, is the compound it formed in the Old English period: mere + mægden, "sea-maiden," gave mermaid, and mere + man gave merman. Mermaid is first attested in the Old English glosses of the 11th century, and for nine hundred years these two singular, gendered figures were the only widely used descendants of the sea-root in English.
  • The generalisation to a people: in the 18th and 19th centuries folklore writing began to speak of merfolk and sea-people, and the mer- element began to generalise from a single sea-maiden into a whole species. In the 20th century fantasy writers completed the process, coining the neutral singular merperson by back-formation from mermaid and merman, and with it the plural merpeople. The semantic path is remarkable: a Proto-Indo-European word for "sea" survived three thousand years of sound change, narrowed from ocean to lake, was rescued by a compound meaning "sea-maiden," and in the last century was finally liberated into a modern collective noun for a whole singing nation under the waves. Susan's "the merpeople singing in the sea" is the endpoint of that entire journey: the ancient sea-root made conscious, musical, and social.
=== ESSAY ON people === The second half of the word is the history of English people, and it begins, as far as the attested record reaches, with the Latin noun populus, "a people, a nation, the body of citizens, the populace." The word is recorded from the Republican period (from the 3rd century BCE), and it is the ordinary Latin term for the whole citizen-body as a political community—the populus Romanus, the Roman People, from whose phrase the modern idea of "the people" descends. The deeper origin of populus is uncertain: no Proto-Indo-European etymology is securely established. Some scholars have compared it with the PIE root *pleh₁-, "to fill," reading populus as "the full one, the throng, the crowd that fills the city," a comparison supported by the sense-history (a people is a full multitude) and by the near-homonym plēnus, "full"; others have proposed an Etruscan or other substrate origin, pointing out that words for "people" and "crowd" are frequently borrowed in the Mediterranean world. The debate remains open, and the honest reconstruction begins with attested Latin rather than with a reconstructed PIE form.
  • The sound-change from populus to people: in the everyday (Vulgar) Latin of the late Empire, populum underwent the regular Romance reductions: intervocalic -p- voiced toward -b- and the final -um weakened. The accusative populum emerged in early Gallo-Romance as pueble / pueple, and Old French pueple then developed further—the puep- cluster simplifying and the medial syllable falling away—until the standard form people (later peuple in modern French) was fixed. English owes its form not to the classical Latin nominative populus but to the spoken French accusative line, which is why the English word is people and not "popul."
  • The entrance into English: the word entered Middle English in the 12th century as peple or people, through Anglo-Norman, in the period when Norman French was reshaping the English vocabulary of law, government, and society. From the first it meant "a people, a nation, persons, inhabitants," and it quickly took on the collective sense that governs modern usage: the people, a people, we the people.
  • The grammar of the plural: English people is notable for its collective grammar: the word is singular in form and, when it means "a nation or an ethnic group," takes a plural (peoples); when it means "persons, the public," it is itself a plural with no singular. This is precisely why it was the ideal second element for merpeople: the compound means "the sea-folk, the sea-nation," and the very word people makes the mermaids a society—a race with songs and customs—rather than a collection of curiosities.
  • Socio-cultural weight: from populus Romanus to "we the people" to the modern demographic people, the word has always carried political and social gravity; putting it after mer- gives the hybrid sea-creatures the full dignity of citizenship. Susan's plural, "the merpeople singing in the sea," thus quietly asserts that the beings under the waves form a community with a culture of their own—the ocean made conscious and harmonious, which is exactly the emotional work the word performs in the passage.
