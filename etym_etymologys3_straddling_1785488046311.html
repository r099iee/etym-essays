Stretching out to stride. The word straddling reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European root reconstructed as *strey-, “to stretch, stretch out”, extended in the dental form *streydʰ- to mean the taking of long, stretching steps. Its direct descendant, Proto-Germanic *strīdaną, “to stride”, produced a verb found across the Germanic world: Old English strīdan (the ancestor of English stride), Old Norse stríða, Middle Dutch striden, and Old High German strītan. The meaning, however, split along national lines. In English the word stayed close to its physical root and meant walking with long steps; in German and Dutch it slid from “striding forward” to “striding forward in hostility” — so that German streiten means “to quarrel, to fight”, and der Streit is “the dispute”. The same Germanic stem even crossed into Romance: Frankish *strīdan, “to contend”, gave Old French estriver, and from it English took strive (thirteenth century) — so that English strive and stride, today so different in meaning, are in fact cousins from the same Germanic root, one reaching English directly, the other by way of France. Modern scholars note that the ultimate PIE reconstruction is debated, and some leave the root unassigned; but the Germanic family itself is solid.

The frequentative stretch. Around 1560 English produced straddle by a process typical of the early modern language: taking the verb stride and adding the frequentative suffix -le, which makes an action iterative, diminutive, or spread out — the same suffix that gives wrestle from wrest, sparkle from spark, scuttle from scut, and nestle from nest. The vowel shifted (stride → straddle), a shortening characteristic of such formations, and the new verb meant precisely “to stand astride, with the legs spread wide”. Early spellings stradle and strodle show the form still settling.

Semantic drift and specialisation. For its first two centuries the word was purely concrete — the posture of the horseman, the farmhand astride a fence, the walker bestriding a ditch. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries American English bent the image toward evasion: to “straddle the fence” is to avoid taking sides, and a “straddler” became an equivocating politician. In the twentieth century the word went professional. The options market took straddle for a position that buys both a call and a put, bestriding the market’s direction; poker took it for a voluntary blind bet that bestrides the table; artillery and surveying used it for shots or bearings that bracket a target. Each technical sense preserves the root gesture — a thing holding both sides at once.

The modern intellectual register. In academic and critical prose of the late twentieth century straddling acquired its most elegant use: a thinker “effortlessly straddling” two disciplines, one foot in each, holding them in balance without merging them. This is exactly the image of the Foreword — the author as a living bridge, the pose athletic rather than evasive, and the word, which two centuries earlier meant hedging, is here pure praise. From a horseman’s legs to a trader’s options to a scholar’s two bookshelves, the same spread-legged gesture has carried the same meaning: to command both sides at once.

  • Literary attestations: the verb straddle is recorded from the 1560s; its figurative fence-straddling use in American political writing from the early nineteenth century; its options-market sense from the mid-twentieth century; the present sentence shows the admiring intellectual register.
