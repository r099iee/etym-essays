Shaggy describes a covering of long, coarse, unkempt, and irregularly matted hair or fur, with an overtone of roughness, wildness, and neglect that is not necessarily contemptuous. Its force in The Hobbit is twofold, tactile and narratively functional: the shaggy coat is a mass of loose, dry, fibrous hair that catches flame instantly, so the adjective does real plot work by making the Warg's burning, leaping agony physically plausible — a sleek or oily coat would not flare the way a dry shaggy pelt does — while at the same time it marks the beast as wild and untamed, a creature of rough natural growth rather than of grooming, whose very hide is tinder. The word carries a visual texture of disorder, hair at odd angles, nothing sleek or uniform, and in literary use it attaches to animals, coarse cloth, unshorn faces, and unkempt brows, always implying healthy but careless abundance rather than poverty, disease, or the matted filth of real neglect. There is almost a warmth in the word — the shaggy sheepdog, the shaggy mane — an affection for the untidy that survives even in a scene of fire and pain. PRIMARY SENSE — LONG, COARSE, UNKEMPT HAIR OR FUR In its central, physical sense, shaggy applies to any covering of long, coarse, irregular hair, fur, wool, or similar growth that hangs or stands in loose, uneven masses rather than lying sleek and smooth. It is used of animals with heavy rough coats (shaggy cattle, shaggy ponies, a shaggy dog), of a person's hair or beard (a shaggy mane of hair, a shaggy beard), and of eyebrows, brows, and unshorn faces. The word does not name a single length but a texture and an arrangement: hair that is rough, dry, and disordered, growing thickly and without discipline. SECONDARY SENSE — COARSE CLOTH AND FIBROUS MATERIAL By direct extension from the shag of rough hair, shaggy applies to cloth and materials with a long, loose, tufted surface: shaggy carpet or rug (a floor covering with a long, fluffy pile), shaggy wool, shaggy homespun cloth. The bridge is the nap — the raised, fibrous surface of the fabric imitates the raised, fibrous surface of the pelt. In this textile register the word is closely tied to the noun shag, which names both the rough nap and the cloth that carries it. TERTIARY SENSES AND EXTENDED USES
  • Vegetation and growth: shaggy extends to plants and natural growth — shaggy grass, shaggy moss, a shaggy hedge, the shaggy mane of a tree — wherever growth is long, rough, and unpruned.
  • Figurative person and behaviour: a person can be called shaggy (unkempt in appearance), and shaggy can colour speech, prose, or style as loose and untidy, though this is informal.
  • The shaggy-dog story: a shaggy-dog story is a long, drawn-out anecdote that ends in a deliberately anticlimactic or absurd joke; the name (20th century) plays on the idea of an interminably tedious tale — the dog's shaggy coat is the story's endless, irrelevant detail.
  • Zoological and botanical names: several creatures and plants carry the adjective — the shaggy mane mushroom (Coprinus comatus), named for its scaly, ruffled cap, and the shaggy mane of the lion.
  • Colloquial register: 'shaggy' as an intensifier of untidiness is mild and affectionate; a shaggy-haired student, a shaggy lawn, a shaggy sweater — the word rarely condemns.
DEGREES AND DERIVATIVES The word inflects freely — shaggier, shaggiest — and has bred shaggily (adverb), shagginess (abstract noun, 17th century), and the related nouns shag (rough hair or fur; coarse cloth with a nap; coarsely cut tobacco; and, as a separate word, a vigorous dance and a verb meaning 'to chase' or, in slang, 'to copulate', from an unrelated source). The adjective is nearly always approving or neutral in tone: it praises wild abundance, health, and naturalness even while it describes disorder. REGISTER AND SEMANTIC HISTORY Shaggy is a plain, earthy, slightly old-fashioned adjective, at home in country writing, natural history, and affectionate description of animals and unkempt people. It carries a warmth that its near-synonyms lack: unkempt implies neglect and dirt, dishevelled implies disorder after disturbance, but shaggy suggests a natural, healthy carelessness. Historically the word is remarkably stable — it has meant 'having rough, coarse, unkempt hair or fur' since it was formed in the 16th century from the noun shag — and its main movement has been outward, from the head of hair and the animal's pelt to cloth, carpet, grass, jokes, and mushrooms, always keeping the image of loose, rough, natural abundance at its heart.
