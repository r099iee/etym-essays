THE CITY OF CONSTANTINE: A NAME BUILT ON STANDING AND SETTLEMENT Constantinople is a Greek compound, Konstantinoupolis, "the City of Constantine," formed from the emperor's name and the ancient Greek noun for "city." Its two elements carry the word back to two of the oldest roots of the Indo-European lexicon — one for physical standing, one for human settlement — and then forward through a millennium of imperial history. THE FIRST ELEMENT: THE NAME THAT MEANS STEADFAST The imperial name Constantinus is a Roman cognomen derived from the adjective constans, genitive constantis, "standing firm, steadfast, constant" — the present participle of the verb constare, "to stand together, hold firm," itself a compound of com- "together" and stare "to stand." Latin stare is a direct inheritance from the Proto-Indo-European root *steh2-, "to stand," whose cognates span the whole family:
  • Sanskrit stha- "to stand"; the root is among the most stable of the family;
  • Greek histemi "I stand, I set" and stasis "standing, faction";
  • Latin stare "to stand," sistere "to set," status "position";
  • Germanic *standan-, Old English standan, English stand.
The root's core meaning is pure physical standing; the semantic drift to "steadfastness" — standing firm in resolve, not falling under pressure — is the Roman moralizing extension that made constans a virtue-name. As a cognomen, Constantinus was borne by several Romans; its permanent fame was fixed by the emperor Constantine I, who ruled from 306 to 337 and remade the Roman world. The Greek East pronounced the name Konstantinos; the personal name was then ready to be turned into a toponym. THE SECOND ELEMENT: THE POLIS THAT FILLS THE WORLD The second element, polis, "city, city-state, citadel," is one of the organizing nouns of Greek civilization. Its pedigree is ancient: it appears already in Mycenaean Greek (Linear B) and in Homer, where polis names the walled citadel and its community. Its ultimate Indo-European root is debated — Beekes derives it from *plh1-, "to fill," the city as the "full settlement"; the older school, following Pokorny, preferred *pel-, "fortified height, citadel"; and a Pre-Greek element has been suggested by some scholars. Whatever its deepest origin, the noun generated the political vocabulary of the ancient world — polites "citizen," politeia "citizenship, constitution" (whence politics and polity), metropolis "mother-city" — so that "City of Constantine" was a name built from the most characteristically Greek of words. THE FOUNDATION AND THE NAMING OF A NEW ROME In 324 Constantine defeated his last rival and chose Byzantium, a modest Greek colony on the Bosporus founded c. 657 BCE, as the site of his new capital. He refounded and massively rebuilt it, dedicating the enlarged city, according to tradition, on 11 May 330. Officially it was styled Nova Roma, "New Rome," but the Greek name Konstantinoupolis, "the City of Constantine," was already in use within the fourth century and became the universal form. The naming was itself a political act: a Christian emperor's name stamped on the seat of a new, Christian Roman world. Latin writers naturalized it as Constantinopolis, and from that Latin form — through the French and English of chroniclers — the English toponym Constantinople descends, attested in Middle English from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the age of the Crusades, when the city was the great prize and wonder of the East. THE MILLENNIUM: THE QUEEN OF CITIES For over a thousand years the name accumulated the weight of an empire. Constantinople was the seat of the Byzantine emperors, the Ecumenical Patriarch, and the imperial bureaucracy; the treasure-house of Hagia Sophia and the palace; the guardian of Greek learning through centuries in which the Latin West read less Greek; and the commercial crossroads of Europe and Asia. Its own inhabitants called it simply "the City" (he Polis) — the definite article acknowledging that there was no other. Its epithets became its synonyms: "the Queen of Cities," "the New Rome," the "God-guarded" city. THE FALLS AND THE CHANGING OF NAMES The name survived two sieges that changed the world. In 1204 the Fourth Crusade, instead of reaching the Holy Land, sacked Constantinople and installed a Latin empire for half a century — the first fall. On 29 May 1453 the Ottoman Turks under Mehmed II took the city by storm, ending the Byzantine Empire; the Ottoman court used the Greek-derived name Konstantiniyye for the city as its imperial capital, while the Slavs called it Tsargrad, "Caesar's city." Over the centuries the common Greek and Turkish phrase "eis ten polin," "to the City," eroded into Istanbul, the name the modern Turkish Republic made official in 1930. The older name did not vanish: "Constantinople" remains the standard historical designation for the Byzantine millennium, the title of the Ecumenical Patriarchate's see, and — in the 1953 song "Istanbul (Not Constantinople)" — a piece of living popular memory. SOCIO-CULTURAL AND LITERARY FORCES
  • Imperial ideology: the name proclaimed a Christian New Rome, making Constantinople the second foundation of the Roman world and the natural successor of Troy, Rome, and Byzantium in one line.
  • Orthodox Christianity: the city as the seat of the patriarch and the home of Hagia Sophia made "Constantinople" almost a synonym for Orthodox Christendom and its holy city.
  • Commerce and diplomacy: as the choke-point between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, the name stood for the world's trade; Byzantine coinage, silk, and diplomacy made it the "city of the world's desire."
  • Western memory: in the medieval West, Crusade chronicles and later travel literature fixed Constantinople as the archetypal rich and polite city — precisely the "rich and polite Constantinople" of the source context — while modern historiography (Gibbon, and later Byzantinists) made it the emblem of a civilization that preserved antiquity for Europe.
CONCLUSION From an ancient Greek colony, to Constantine's Christian New Rome, to the Byzantine Queen of Cities, to Ottoman Konstantiniyye, to modern Istanbul — the name Constantinople records, element by element, the standing root of the emperor and the settling root of the polis, and with them a full millennium of Mediterranean history.
