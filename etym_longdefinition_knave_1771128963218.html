knave is a noun meaning "a dishonest or untrustworthy man; a base, contemptible, or roguish person". Its modern sense is wholly pejorative, but its original sense was entirely neutral: in Old English a cnafa was simply a "boy, youth, male child", and later a "boy-servant, menial attendant". The word's long history is a textbook case of semantic pejoration: over several centuries it slid from "boy" to "servant" to "base fellow" to "scoundrel", each step downward powered by the social contempt of a hierarchical society. In the card's context—"he would be regarded as either a hero or a knave"—the word supplies the exact opposite of heroism: a man whose failure or treachery exposes him as base, scheming, and utterly without honor.

Sense 1: The modern sense—"a dishonest, base, untrustworthy man". In contemporary English, knave denotes a person (almost always male, and now somewhat archaic or literary) who is dishonest, unprincipled, or contemptible: a scoundrel, a rogue, a trickster. It implies not merely wrongdoing but a base and scheming character—the petty, low, deceitful villain rather than the grand one. In Shakespeare and Elizabethan drama, "knave" is a staple insult: "that arrant knave", "a knave, a rascal, an eater of broken meats" (King Lear). Today the word survives mainly in historical fiction, archaizing prose, and set phrases; it is felt as old-fashioned, vigorous, and faintly theatrical.

Sense 2: The historical sense—"a boy, youth, male servant". The original meaning of knave was simply "a boy, a male child, a youth" (Old English cnafa), and then, because boys were commonly employed as servants, "a boy-servant, a menial, a groom, a kitchen-boy". In medieval and early modern English a "knave" was the lowest-ranking male servant in a household—one who ran errands, tended horses, or waited at table. This sense is now archaic/obsolete in live use but is the semantic foundation of the whole word: the modern insult literally means "base servant-boy". The same social logic produced the playing-card sense (Sense 3) and the parallel history of words like varlet (from Old French vaslet "young serving-man") and villain (originally "a farmhand, low-born rustic").

Sense 3: The card sense—"the jack in a pack of cards". In card games, the knave is the court card ranking below the queen, also called the jack. The card depicts a young man in servant's or page's dress, and its name preserves the word's old "boy-servant" meaning. In older English usage (and still in some games, e.g. Euchre), the knave is the named card: "the knave of hearts". Modern English generally prefers jack for the playing card, but knave remains a recognized technical and historical term in this sense.

Sense 4: Derivative and related forms.

  • Knavery — "dishonest or mischievous dealing, roguery" (the abstract noun).
  • Knavish — "dishonest, roguish, mischievous" (the adjective).
  • Knavishly — "in a roguish or dishonest manner".
  • Doublet relationships: knave is a cousin of German Knabe ("boy") and of knight (Old English cniht, originally "boy, young man, servant"—which then rose, while knave fell, to noble status). The pairing of knight and knave is one of the most instructive contrasts in English lexical history: two words for "boy-servant", one that climbed to honor and one that sank to dishonor.

Synonym spectrum and boundaries.

  • Rogue: Similar because both mean a dishonest, roguish man; differs because rogue can be charming, playful, and romanticized (a lovable rogue), while knave is base and contemptible without charm.
  • Scoundrel: Similar because both denote a dishonorable, unprincipled man; differs because scoundrel is more general and modern, while knave carries the archaic, class-based sense of "low-born, base, servant-born".
  • Rascal: Similar because both can mean a mischievous or dishonest person; differs because rascal is lighter and often affectionate (a rascal of a child), while knave is a harsher moral condemnation.
  • Villain: Similar because both denote a wicked or criminal person; differs because villain is grander and more serious (and itself descended from "farmhand"), while knave implies petty, sneaky baseness and retains its servant-class heritage.
  • Varlet: Similar because both began as "boy-servant" and became insults; differs because varlet is rarer, more archaic, and lighter, while knave is the better-known and harsher word.
  • Blackguard: Similar because both denote a contemptible, low fellow; differs because blackguard (originally the scullions and kitchen-knaves of a household) is more theatrical and violent, while knave stresses deceit and petty dishonesty.

Conclusion. Knave is a word that carries its social history in its spelling: it is the old word for "boy-servant" that a class-obsessed society turned into its favorite insult for the base and the deceitful. To call someone a knave is to say not merely that they have done wrong but that they are, in the deepest social sense, beneath contempt—a petty, sneaking, untrustworthy creature. In the card's hero-or-knave contrast, the word supplies the full weight of that ancient contempt.
