- Old English roots — the parts exist: up (upp) and turn (tyrnan) live side by side for centuries before ever joining.
- 16th c. — the compound is born: upturn appears as a verb (‘to turn up, invert’), and upturned follows as its participle — faces, eyes, and furrows turned skyward.
- 17th–18th c. — boats and buckets: writers extend it to capsized vessels and tipped containers; the word becomes the standard adjective for anything keel-up.
- 19th c. — the novelist's nose: ‘upturned nose’ becomes a stock charm of heroines in Austen's heirs — perky, youthful, slightly mischievous.
- 20th c. — photography and film: ‘a sea of upturned faces’ becomes the canonical crowd shot — concerts, rallies, fireworks — wonder made visible.
- Today — wonder and wreckage: upturned faces still gaze at marvels (Tolkien's hobbits, rocket launches), while upturned boats and bins mark pauses, storms, and Monday mornings.
