Single linear chain from the earliest reconstructable form to the present rootWord ivy: 1. West Germanic *ibahs- / *ebahs- (a plant-name of obscure, probably pre-Germanic substrate origin; reconstructed for the period before the Anglo-Saxon migrations, c. 1st millennium CE) — "ivy, the evergreen climbing plant." No Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be securely reconstructed; the word is not compatible with a regular IE derivation. A reported comparison with Greek ἰφύον (īphúon) is widely treated as uncertain. 2. Old English īfig (attested in glosses, c. 9th–10th century) — "ivy." The base survives with the regular reshaping of *-ahs- to -ig-. 3. Middle English ivi / yvy (12th–15th centuries) — "ivy." Loss of the final consonant; the vowel shortens and unrounds toward modern /aɪ/. 4. Early Modern and Modern English ivy /ˈaɪvi/ (16th century onward) — "ivy, Hedera helix," the exact word form and sense of this card. The denotation has been stable for a thousand years; only the compounds and transferred uses (ivied, poison ivy, Ivy League) are later additions. Note on nuance/debate: the ultimate origin of *ibahs- is unknown. Two competing views are current—(a) a local Germanic formation of obscure origin, or (b) a loan from a lost pre-Germanic substrate shared, some argue, with the Greek plant-name ἰφύον. Both views leave the attested English chain above (Old English īfig → Middle English ivi → Modern English ivy) unaffected; what is uncertain is only the word's stage before the West Germanic period, which must be stated as unknown rather than invented.
