slops — the plural substantive of "slop," used especially for liquid or semi-liquid household waste: waste water, dishwater, the soiled wash of a kitchen or sickroom, and by extension the bodily and medical refuse that accumulates around a bedridden patient. In the literary passage this card draws on — a tavern keeper's wife who "carries out his slops" while the wounded child she is nursing recovers — the word performs a deliberately unglamorous labour. It is paired with "meals" in a single breath ("She brings his meals, she carries out his slops"), and the two nouns lock the scene into an endless two-beat cycle of intake and evacuation: food enters, waste leaves, day after day, week after week. The tone is flatly physical and resolutely domestic; there is no euphemism and no sentiment, only the brute choreography of care. The plural form matters — "slops," never "slop" — because it makes the refuse multiple and recurring, the daily overflowing of a body being nursed back from the edge, and because it names a category of things rather than a single puddle. It is the visceral underside of the weeks the child spends in the cot, and its bluntness is a kind of authorial honesty. The word is genuinely polysemous, and its several senses have developed along distinct historical lines:
  • Liquid refuse; waste water (primary, card sense): the soiled water, dishwater, kitchen leavings, and — especially in old nursing and domestic usage — the excrement, urine, and used wash-basins emptied from a sickroom. This is the sense the passage uses, and it is the oldest established English sense of the plural.
  • Weak or watery food for invalids: from the 19th century, "slops" (and singular "slop") come to mean thin, semi-liquid food — gruel, broth, pap — prescribed for convalescents and children. The semantic bridge is domestic and medicinal: the same vessels and the same semi-liquid consistency move from the waste-basin to the feeding-bowl, so that in nursing language the word swings between what is brought in and what is carried out.
  • Spilled or splashed liquid (singular "slop"): a puddle, a spill, a mess of liquid on a floor or surface; hence the verb "to slop," to spill or splash carelessly, and "to slop over," to overflow. The image is of liquid losing its proper container.
  • Sailor's ready-made clothing (historical homograph): from the 17th century, "slops" names the loose, baggy breeches and ready-made garments sold from naval stores — from an earlier Middle English sense of "slop" as a loose outer garment or baggy trouser, borrowed from Middle Low German. The two "slops" are historically distinct words that happen to share form and, at bottom, the same root image of something that "slips on."
  • Careless, messy, sentimental overflow (figurative derivatives): the adjective "sloppy" moves from "wet and muddy" to "careless and slovenly," and further to "maudlin, excessively sentimental" (sloppy kisses, sloppy sentiment), while "slop" in journalistic and colloquial use can mean trite or careless writing — emotionally or intellectually watery material.
  • Recent technical/metaphoric extension — AI slop: in the 2020s "slop" has been reclaimed as a noun for low-quality, mass-generated digital content, especially machine-generated images and text that flood feeds. The bridge is the oldest one in the word's history: material produced carelessly, in bulk, and without craft is "waste" in the informational sense — excess that gets carried out.
Its near-synonyms carve up the same territory with different boundaries: swill is the kitchen food-waste kept to feed pigs, and thus carries a barnyard rather than a sickroom resonance; sewage is institutional and infrastructural, the waste carried away by pipes on the scale of a city, wholly impersonal; wastewater is the neutral technical term of plumbing and treatment plants, clinical and odourless; refuse, garbage, and trash denote solid, dry, general waste and lack any liquid character; effluent is the technical discharge of a factory or treatment works; bilge is the waste water of a ship's hold; and hogwash is swill by another name, now chiefly figurative for nonsense. What keeps slops distinct is its combination of liquidness, domestic intimacy, and recurrence: it is waste you carry, in a basin, every day, from a room where someone is being cared for — never distant machinery, never dry rubbish, always wet, always coming again.
