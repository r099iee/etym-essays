- Late 17th c. — French Court Ceremonial: English borrows levee from the French court of Versailles, where King Louis XIV's morning rising (le lever) was transformed into an elaborate state ritual of royal intimacy and aristocratic favor.
- 18th c. — British Ministerial Patronage: In Augustan Britain, levees become the bustling morning assemblies held by powerful prime ministers (Walpole, Newcastle, Oxford) packed with place-hunters, satirized by Swift, Pope, and Hogarth.
- Early 18th c. — French Louisiana Embankments: French settlers in the Mississippi River delta apply levée to raised earthen flood-dikes and steamboat landing wharves, establishing the word's primary North American civil engineering meaning.
- Late 18th to Early 19th c. — American Presidential Receptions: George Washington and John Adams host formal republican levees, which Thomas Jefferson famously abolishes as excessively monarchical and aristocratic.
- 19th c. — Steamboat Era & Civil Engineering: Levees become the central infrastructure of Mississippi commerce and flood control, featured prominently in Mark Twain's writings and American blues songs.
- 20th–21st c. — Modern Bifurcated Usage: In North America, levees immediately evokes massive flood-wall infrastructure and disaster management (such as the 1927 flood or Hurricane Katrina); in Canadian state protocol, the ceremonial New Year's levee survives as an open-house tradition.
