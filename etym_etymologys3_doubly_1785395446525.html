DOUBLY is a young English adverb built from two older layers — a Latinate base and a native Germanic suffix — and beneath both lies one of the oldest words in Indo-European speech: the numeral 'two'. The journey begins with the Proto-Indo-European cardinal *dwóh₁, 'two', ancestor of Sanskrit dvā́u, Greek δύο (dúo), Latin duo, Lithuanian du, and the Proto-Germanic *twai that gave English two, twin, twelve, and twenty. The word reaches English twice over: once natively, in our own two, and once through the classical stream that gives us double. In Latin, duo joined the element -plus, 'fold, times', descended from the PIE root *pel- 'to fold, plait', producing duplus, 'twofold' — literally 'folded in two'. It is a formation of pleasing literalness: the ancient languages imagined 'double' not as a number doubled but as a thing folded over on itself, a cloth laid twice about the body, an army drawn up in two ranks. Greek made the identical metaphor with διπλόος (diplóos), 'twofold', from δίς (dis) 'twice' and -πλόος '-fold', and from the same source English later borrowed diploma — a letter 'folded double'. As the Roman world declined and Latin passed into the Romance vernaculars, duplus became Vulgar Latin *duplum, and in the Gallo-Romance of northern Gaul it emerged as Old French doble, later double. Norman and Anglo-Norman speech then carried the word across the Channel, and Middle English had double from about 1200 — first as an adjective, then as a verb, to double, and an adverb. The Old French form itself absorbed the influence of Latin duplex, 'of twofold nature' (duo + *plek- 'to plait'), a sister formation that reinforced the same meaning in learned uses. The native half of DOUBLY is the suffix. Old English formed adverbs with -līce, built on the noun līc, 'body, form' — the same root that gives like and the archaic lich, 'a corpse'. To do a thing x-līce was to do it 'in the body-form of x'; to act cwīclīce was to act 'in a quick manner'. Middle English eroded the endings, and by early modern times -ly was the universal adverbial suffix. So DOUBLY literally means 'in a twofold manner', a formation no older than Middle English; the earliest secure evidence of the adverb belongs to the fourteenth century, when it meant 'in two ways' or 'in a twofold manner'. From the beginning the word's centre of gravity was intensity rather than repetition. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries DOUBLY settled into the sense 'to twice the degree or force', multiplying a quality instead of counting occurrences — and by the early modern period it had become the favourite emphatic of caution and consequence. 'Doubly sure', 'doubly guarded', 'doubly bound' all trade on the same logic: experience has redoubled the stakes, so the care must be redoubled too. The rhetorical device is ancient — the 'doubling' figure was taught in classical rhetoric — but the English adverb gives it a compact, idiomatic form. In Modern English the word keeps both strands: the plain arithmetic of 'twice over' and the evaluative weight of 'for two reasons', the second ground often left implicit. It is the adverb of consequence par excellence: things are done doubly because they were once done badly, and the guard on the goblin gate is the perfect instance — after Thorin's escape the watch is doubled because the peril is doubled, and the word folds both doublings into a single dry laugh. The cultural and literary record is broad:
  • 14th century — DOUBLY first appears as a plain adverb of manner meaning 'in two ways', the -ly formation spreading through Middle English.
  • 15th-17th century — the degree sense takes over; doubly good, doubly strong, doubly armed appear in prose and verse; the word becomes a staple of emphatic moral and devotional writing.
  • 18th-19th century — the evaluative use flowers: doubly sorry, doubly sure, doubly careful, where the doubled emotion or caution is proportionate to doubled circumstances.
  • 20th century to present — DOUBLY is standard and flexible, equally at home in journalistic prose ('doubly difficult') and in narrative speech, as in Thorin's bitter laugh in The Hobbit.
Phonetically the word is stable — Middle English /duːbliː/ settled to modern /ˈdʌbli/ with the Great Vowel Shift rounding the base vowel — and its formation remains transparent to every speaker: double plus the old adverb-maker -ly. It is a word whose history is exactly what it says: two layers, folded together.
