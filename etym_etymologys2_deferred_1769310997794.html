Root 1 — the postponing sense (Latin differre)
PIE *bher- “to carry, bear” (c. 4000–3000 BCE; Sanskrit bhárati “he carries”, Greek phérō “I carry”, Latin ferō) → Latin ferre “to carry, bear” (3rd c. BCE; attested in Plautus) → Latin differre “to carry apart, to scatter; to postpone, to differ” (dis- “apart” + ferre; 2nd–1st c. BCE, Plautus, Cicero) → Old French differer “to postpone, to defer” (12th c.) → Middle English differren/deferren “to put off, postpone” (14th c.; Chaucer) → English defer “to postpone” → deferred ◄ TARGET (past tense/participle).

Root 2 — the yielding sense (Latin dēferre)
PIE *bher- “to carry, bear” (same root) → Latin ferre “to carry” → Latin dēferre “to carry down, bring down; to submit, to report” (dē- “down, away from” + ferre; 1st c. BCE, Caesar, Cicero) → Old French déférer “to submit, to refer, to defer to” (13th c.) → Middle English deferren “to yield, to submit to another's opinion” (15th c.) → English defer (to) “to yield respectfully” → deferred ◄ TARGET (same spelling, different etymon).

Note on the nuance: English “defer” is a historic fusion of two distinct Latin verbs — differre (“carry apart, postpone”) and dēferre (“carry down, submit”) — which collapsed into identical spelling and pronunciation. The senses are distinguished by context and by the phrase “defer to” for the yielding sense.
