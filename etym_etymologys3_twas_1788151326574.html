1. The two ancestors: a verb of dwelling and a deictic pronoun (pre-writing to Old English). The little word ’twas is a marriage of two ancient Indo-European inheritances. The first is the verb: the past tense of “to be” across most of the Indo-European family is supplied not by the present root (*h₁es-, seen in am, is) but by a root *h₂wes- that originally meant “to dwell, to remain, to pass the night” — perfect tenses of “to be” were, in effect, “I have remained.” Germanic generalised this into the whole past paradigm, giving Old English wæs “was.” The second ancestor is the pronoun: PIE *ḱó-/kó- “this,” which produced the Old English neuter hit “it.” In Old English, “it was” was simply hit wæs, and the two words were as unglamorous as their modern equivalents.

2. Losing the h: the making of “it” (Old to Middle English). Middle English did the quiet phonological work that made the contraction possible. In unstressed positions — and pronouns are almost always unstressed — the initial h of hit weakened and dropped: hit became it by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The pronoun, now beginning with a vowel, stood immediately before was, and spoken English had discovered, long before the written language, that the sequence “it was” flows naturally together: the vowel of it, unstressed, was rapidly elided before the following sound, producing something very like the modern ’twas in all but spelling. For centuries this was pure speech-phonetics; writing kept the two words separate. The contraction was always latent in the spoken language, waiting for a scribe to dare write it.

3. The written contraction arrives (16th–17th c.). The sixteenth century, with its delight in typographic economy and its new confidence in representing speech, wrote the elision down. ’Tis appears in print early in the century; ’twas follows; and by Shakespeare’s lifetime the whole family — ’tis, ’twas, ’twere, ’twill, ’twould — is a working part of literary English. Shakespeare uses ’twas freely in dialogue and narrative verse, and the King James Bible’s prose (though it prefers uncontracted forms) coexisted with a culture in which contracted ’twas was plain, ordinary usage. At this stage the form carries no archaism whatsoever: it is simply how English speakers said “it was” when the pronoun was light, exactly as they said “I’m” and “we’ll” without a second thought.

4. The retreat into verse (18th–19th c.). The eighteenth century changed the social weather for all contractions. Prescriptive grammarians — anxious to regularise and dignify English — began to mark contracted forms as informal, and formal prose steadily abandoned them. ’Twas, however, had an asylum: poetry and song. The metres of ballad and blank verse positively demand the contraction — two syllables in one metrical position are a gift to any line — and so ’twas prospered exactly where prose let it go. Wordsworth’s Prelude (the source of this card’s context) places “’Twas autumn, and a clear and placid day” in elevated blank verse without a trace of quaintness: the form is the natural diction of early nineteenth-century poetry, still fully alive. The decisive cultural event came in 1823 with Clement Clarke Moore’s “A Visit from St. Nicholas”: “’TWAS the night before Christmas, when all through the house ...” The line made the word immortal on one specific opening, and from that moment ’twas has arrived trailing Christmas, balladry, and the past tense of good cheer.

5. The fossil (20th–21st c.). In contemporary English, ’twas exists almost entirely as quotation. Nobody says it in conversation except in jest or pathos; its habitats are Christmas poems, sea shanties, historical and fantasy fiction, and the deliberate archaism of columnists (“’twas ever thus”). Dialect survivals — the northern English and Scots ‘twas/’twos, American archaic-rustic stylisations — keep a folk thread alive. The word’s career is a perfect case study in how grammatical contraction becomes stylistic register: a natural speech process, canonised by metre, fossilised by custom, and preserved for two centuries by a single Christmas Eve. It remains the most instantly recognisable two-letter-plus-apostrophe syllable in the language: it was, wearing its archaism like a robe.
