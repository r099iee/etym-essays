• Hull: Similar because both name parts of a ship's structure; differs because the hull is the whole body and shell of the vessel, while the keel is only the central spine at its base.
  • Bottom: Similar because both denote the lower part of a vessel; differs because bottom is a vague, non-technical word, whereas keel names the specific load-bearing timber.
  • Base / Foundation: Similar because both denote what supports a structure; differs because base is general and often abstract, while keel keeps the concrete nautical spine-image.
  • Spine / Backbone: Similar because both denote the central structural axis of a thing; differs because spine is the anatomical and figurative word, while keel is the nautical engineering term from which the comparison is drawn.
  • Ridge: Similar because both denote a long raised line or edge; differs because ridge is the generic word (of mountains, roofs), while keel in science names a specific boat-like ridge of a bone, shell or flower.
  • Carina: Similar because carina is Latin for 'keel' and names the same bird-breastbone ridge; differs because carina is the technical anatomical term, while keel is the everyday nautical and transferred word.
  • Ribs / Frames: Similar because both are parts of the ship's skeleton; differs because they are the transverse timbers rising from the keel that give the hull its shape, not the spine itself.
  • Planking: Similar because both are structural timber of the hull; differs because planking is the outer covering of boards, while keel is the single deep spine to which the planks and ribs fasten.
  • Keelson: Similar because both are central lower timbers; differs because the keelson is the inner reinforcement laid on top of the keel, a distinct member above it.
  • Ballast: Similar because both affect a ship's stability and depth in the water; differs because ballast is the heavy cargo added for stability, not a structural timber — though both can make the hull 'sink deep'.
