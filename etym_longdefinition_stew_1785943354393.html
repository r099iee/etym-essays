STEW denotes a culinary dish consisting of meat, poultry, fish, or vegetables cooked slowly in a covered vessel with liquid over low heat, so that the liquid reduces and thickens into a rich gravy and tough connective tissue breaks down into tenderness. As a verb, stew means to cook ingredients slowly in that way. Beyond these culinary senses, stew functions figuratively to mean a state of prolonged anxiety, mental agitation, brooding, or flustered confusion (as in "in a stew" or "stewing over a problem"), and in technical, historical and slang registers it carries older associations with vapor baths, hot steam rooms, stoves, and even brothels. The word family is deeply connected to heat and vapor: the same root gives English stove.

In the source context ("Does the stew have carrots in it?"), the word is used in its straightforward culinary noun sense — a pot of slow-cooked food, presumably containing meat and vegetables — and the question draws attention to the ingredients and the simple domestic setting of a homely meal.

Primary Senses

  • Noun — a slow-cooked dish: meat, fish, poultry or vegetables (or a combination) cooked slowly in a small amount of liquid in a covered pot; typical examples are beef stew, Irish stew, lamb stew, fish stew, and vegetable stew. Unlike soup, a stew keeps its solid pieces large and distinct, with only enough liquid to moisten and thicken.
  • Verb (transitive) — to cook slowly in liquid: "to stew the meat until tender," i.e. to simmer gently in a covered pot, usually after browning, allowing flavors to blend.
  • Verb (intransitive) — to be cooked slowly: "let it stew for an hour" — the food itself is the subject.
  • Verb (intransitive, figurative) — to fret or worry: "he stewed over his mistake," "stop stewing about it" — to brood in prolonged, agitated anxiety, as if simmering internally.
  • Noun (count, figurative) — a state of agitation: "she's in a terrible stew" — a condition of worried fluster or exasperation.

Historical and Archaic Senses

  • Vapor bath / steam room / heated room: in Middle English a stue or stew was a heated room for sweat baths — the sense at the root of the whole family, and the direct link to stove.
  • Plural "stews" — a brothel: because medieval public bath-houses acquired a reputation as places of prostitution, the plural stews came to mean "brothels," a sense recorded from the 14th century (e.g. the licensed stews of Southwark in London).
  • Fish stew-pond (archaic): a stew or stew-pond was a small enclosed pond in which fish were kept alive for the table; the connection to the "enclosed, heated or contained place" idea is transparent.
  • Stove-like device (obsolete): a stew could be a stove or heated apparatus, reflecting the shared etymology.

Slang, Idioms and Phrasal Uses

  • "In a stew" — agitated, worried: the idiom records the figurative extension of simmering heat to emotional agitation.
  • "To stew in one's own juices" — to suffer the consequences of one's own actions: the person is left to "cook" slowly in the heat of their own making, with no rescue; first recorded in the 19th century.
  • "Let him stew" — to make someone wait in anxiety: to deliberately leave someone in suspense or discomfort.
  • "Stewed" (slang, 18th c. and later) — drunk: the inebriate is metaphorically "cooked" or "soaked," parallel to soaked, pickled, soused.
  • "Stewed tea" — over-brewed, dark and bitter tea: tea left steeping too long, an extension of the slow-boiling idea to beverages.
  • "Stew" as a verb of waiting (British, informal): to be made to wait or to endure a delay, as in "left to stew at the station."

Regional and Culinary Variants

  • Named stews abound in world cuisine: Irish stew (lamb, potatoes, onions), Lancashire hotpot (a related oven stew), bouillabaisse (a Provençal fish stew), goulash (Hungarian beef-and-paprika stew), chili con carne, Brunswick stew, and burgoo (American).
  • In American English stew contrasts with soup by chunkiness and lower liquid ratio; in British usage the same distinction holds, and "casserole" is a near-synonym for oven-baked stew.
  • The verb can be transitive or intransitive, and is used with the preposition "in": "stew in," "stew in its own juices."

Semantic Boundaries and Near-Synonym Comparisons

  • Stew vs. Soup: a stew has a smaller proportion of liquid and keeps large, distinct pieces of meat and vegetable; a soup is predominantly broth with smaller, submerged ingredients.
  • Stew vs. Braise: braising cooks large, whole cuts partially submerged after initial searing; stewing uses uniform, bite-sized pieces fully submerged throughout.
  • Stew vs. Casserole: a casserole is cooked in the oven in a covered dish, often with a topping; a stew is typically cooked on the hob in a pot, though the words overlap heavily.
  • Stew vs. Pot-au-feu / Boiled dinner: these involve large pieces boiled in abundant liquid; a stew concentrates flavor in reduced, thickened liquid.
  • Stew vs. Chowder: a chowder is a thick, creamy soup (often seafood or corn, with milk or cream); a stew relies on reduced broth without requiring dairy.
  • Stew vs. Fricassee: a fricassee is a white stew of poultry or veal cooked without browning and finished in a white sauce.
  • Stew vs. Worry / Fret: in the figurative sense, "stew" is a near-synonym of worry, fret, fuss; differs because stew carries the added image of slow, internal, simmering agitation.

Historical-Semantic Bridge

The semantic journey runs from "heated room / vapor bath" (Old French estuve, related to stove) to "place where food is slowly cooked by heat and steam," to "the dish cooked there," to "to cook slowly," and finally to "to fret as if simmering in internal heat" — a chain that links steam rooms, stoves, brothels, fishponds, and comfort food in one surprising word family.
