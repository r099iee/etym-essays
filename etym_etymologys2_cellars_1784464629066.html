CELLARS is the plural of cellar, a word inherited through French and Latin, with the plural formed by the ordinary English suffix; the linear chains follow in isolation. Chain 1 — the base cellar: PIE *ḱel- 'to cover, conceal, hide' (reconstructed; attested through cognates: Latin celāre 'to hide', Latin occulere 'to cover up', Old Irish celim 'I hide', Greek kalyptein 'to cover') → Latin cella 'a small room, store-chamber, store-room' (Classical Latin, attested; also a monk's cell) → Late/Medieval Latin cellārium 'storehouse, pantry, larder' (attested) → Old French celier / celler 'cellar, storeroom' (12th c.) → Anglo-Norman celer → Middle English celer / sellar 'cellar, storeroom' (c. 1200; the underground wine-storage sense develops) → English cellar 'an underground room for storage' (spelling reshaped after Latin cellarium; cf. salt cellar from Old French saliere, see note) → base of the plural. Chain 2 — the plural suffix -s: PIE *-ōs / *-es (plural endings of nominal stems; cf. the Indo-European nominal plural markers) → Proto-Germanic *-ōs (plural of o-stem nouns; cf. Gothic -ōs) → Old English -as (plural ending of strong masculine a-stem nouns, e.g. stānas 'stones') → Middle English -es (generalised plural ending) → modern English -s → fused with cellar → CELLARS (card form). Note on the salt-cellar complication: English salt cellar is not the same word as cellar 'underground room'. It descends from Old French saliere 'a salt box' (Latin salārium, from sāl 'salt'), which was reshaped by folk etymology into salt + cellar because the word's form suggested the familiar 'cellar'. The underground-storage cellar proper follows Chain 1.
