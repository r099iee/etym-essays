• wood: Similar because both name a tract of trees, and both can be walked through; differs because wood is the plain, general word, usually larger and less deliberately shaped, while grove is smaller, more open, and implies a kept or bounded space.
  • copse: Similar because both name a small group of trees; differs because a copse is dense, grown for cutting, and tangled, while a grove is open and passable, without thick undergrowth.
  • thicket: Similar because both name a cluster of woody growth; differs because a thicket is an impenetrable tangle of bushes and saplings, while a grove is a clean, open cluster of full-grown trees.
  • spinney: Similar because both name a small wood; differs because spinney is dialectal and archaic, confined chiefly to England and to place-names, while grove is standard English everywhere.
  • brake: Similar because both name a clump of woody growth; differs because brake is archaic or poetic for a brush thicket, and survives mainly in place-names, while grove names an open tree cluster.
  • orchard: Similar because both name a planted cluster of trees; differs because an orchard is grown specifically for fruit, while a grove need serve no purpose beyond shade, beauty, or seclusion.
  • forest: Similar because both name tree-covered land; differs because forest is vast, wild, and legally defined, while a grove is small, bounded, and easily taken in at a glance.
  • woodland: Similar because both refer to land with trees; differs because woodland is the broad, neutral ecological term for forested land of any extent, with no sense of enclosure or management.
  • stand: Similar because both name a group of trees; differs because stand is the dry technical term of forestry (a stand of pines), stripped of the grove's aesthetic, sacred, and pastoral associations.
  • coppice: Similar because both name a managed patch of trees; differs because a coppice is cut and regrown on a cycle for wood, while a grove is kept intact for shade and beauty.
  • park: Similar because both name managed, bounded green land; differs because a park is extensive open grassland, often with scattered trees, attached to a house or town, while a grove is the wooded cluster itself.
  • plantation: Similar because both name planted stands of trees; differs because plantation stresses commercial scale and single-species planting, while grove suggests a smaller, more intimate cultivated cluster.
  • holt: Similar because both are old words for a wood; differs because holt is obsolete or poetic, while grove is living, standard vocabulary.
