English imitative base *lip- / *lipp- — sound-symbolic; light consonants /l, p/ + bright front vowel /ɪ/ evoking soft repeated contact and bounce (origin unknown; purely phonesthetic; NO PIE ancestry) ↓ Reduplicated expressive adverb *lippity-lippity — built on the echoic base by the native English reduplication pattern of motion-mimics (cf. hippity-hop, clippety-clop); unattested before Potter ↓ lippity — nonce coinage fixed by Beatrix Potter in The Tale of Peter Rabbit, 1902: "going lippity—lippity—not very fast"; survives in later children's literature as a deliberate homage. DEBATED / NUANCED: There is no Proto-Indo-European, Proto-Germanic, Romance, or Indo-Aryan root for this word, and none should be invented. Folk associations with lip (Old English lippa, the body part) and leap (Old English hlēapan, to jump) are rejected: they show only superficial sound and semantic overlap, not a historical derivation. The honest scholarly label is "unknown / imitative English," with Potter as the popularizer.
