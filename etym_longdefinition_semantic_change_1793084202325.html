Semantic change is the technical term in historical linguistics for the process by which words alter their meanings over time: gaining new senses, losing old ones, drifting from the concrete to the abstract, from the noble to the base, or from the narrow to the wide. It is change in what words mean, as distinct from phonetic change (change in how words sound) and grammatical change (change in how words behave).

In the source passage the term is introduced exactly as a textbook would introduce it: after showing that expedient once meant freeing one caught by the foot and now means advantageous, and that salient once meant jumping and now means obvious, the author names the process behind both examples — semantic change — and promises that its general patterns (narrowing, widening, elevation, degradation, metaphor, metonymy) illuminate not only word analysis and vocabulary-building but the reading of older authors such as Shakespeare and Milton, where vulgar can mean common and deer can mean any animal.

Core meaning in detail: every living word sits at the mercy of its speakers, and speakers constantly stretch old words over new things rather than minting new ones. Over centuries these stretches harden into new dictionary senses while older ones fade, so that etymological meaning and current meaning can end up strangers — silly once meant blessed, nice once meant ignorant, awful once meant awe-inspiring. Semantic change is the name linguists give this universal drift, studied since Michel Bréal founded semantics as a science in the 1890s.

All senses, patterns, and neighbouring uses:

  • Change of word meaning over time (primary technical sense): the umbrella term for every diachronic meaning shift. The bridge is compositional: semantic (of meaning, from Greek sēmantikos) + change (alteration) — alteration in meaning, nothing more and nothing less.
  • Narrowing or specialisation (classic subtype): deer narrowed from any animal to one species; meat narrowed from all food to flesh. The bridge is frequent use in one context: the general word is heard so often of one referent that the rest fall away.
  • Widening or generalisation (classic subtype): holiday widened from holy day to any day off; business widened from busyness to all commerce. The bridge is the reverse habit: a special word stretched over neighbouring cases until the fence breaks.
  • Amelioration and pejoration (evaluative subtypes): nice climbed from ignorant to pleasant (amelioration); vulgar sank from common to coarse (pejoration). The bridge is social attitude: the feelings of speakers toward the referent leak into the word itself, lifting or staining it.
  • Metaphor and metonymy as engines (mechanisms): grasp moved from seizing to understanding via metaphor (ideas as objects); the crown moved from headwear to monarchy via metonymy (thing for institution). The bridge is figurative habit: live metaphors used for generations die into literal senses — the limestone of language, in the source's image, built of dead tropes.
  • Semantic change versus semantic shift (terminology note): textbooks use the two interchangeably, though some reserve shift for a single word's drift and change for the field-wide phenomenon. The bridge is mere synonymy of metalanguage — two labels for one process, and examiners accept either.

Register and connotation: semantic change is classroom and textbook English — neutral, technical, and explanatory, at home in linguistics lectures, etymology columns, and introductions to Shakespeare. It carries no praise or blame; it simply names the weather of meaning, the slow climate-shift under every sentence we utter. To invoke it is to sound the philologist's note: patient, historical, and quietly delighted that expedient once set feet free.
