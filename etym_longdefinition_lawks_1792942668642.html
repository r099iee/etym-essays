Lawks is an old-fashioned English interjection of astonishment — a broad, comic, faintly rustic cry meaning good Lord!, good heavens!, or well I never! — uttered when something startling, incongruous, or delightfully unexpected meets the eye. In the source sentence it carries exactly this peering-in surprise: Merry looks into some snug or startling interior and bursts out Lawks! — the hobbit equivalent of a countryman whistling through his teeth at a sight, half amusement and half disbelief, preserved with its opening inverted comma and exclamation as a single ejaculated syllable.

Core meaning in detail: The word is a minced oath, one of the long English line of softened blasphemies that let speakers exclaim without profaning the divine name: Lord worn down by rapid affectionate speech to law, then dressed with the exclamatory -s (as in ye gods, goodness gracious) to make lawks, often capitalised at a sentence start. Its full-dress cousins are lawks-a-mussy (Lord have mercy), lawk-a-day, and lor lumme, and its modern heirs are lor, lummy, and blimey. It has no noun or verb life at all: it exists only as pure exclamation, punctuating dialogue in Dickens, stage Cockney, Barsetshire servants, and Tolkien's most rustic hobbits, who use it to sound unmistakably homely, unlearned, and warm-hearted.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Exclamation of surprise or wonder (sole core sense, from the 18th c.; the source sense): Lawks, what a sight!, Lawks, if it isn't raining again! The bridge is euphemism by clipping: the awe once directed at heaven in Lord! is redirected at the immediate spectacle, keeping the feeling while blunting the blasphemy, so the speaker can gape respectably.
  • Comic servant and rustic stage marker (sociolinguistic sense, Victorian): put into the mouths of maids, ostlers, and good-hearted countrymen to signal class and kindliness at once. The bridge is typecasting by vocabulary: just as oaths mark the soldier and Latin tags mark the parson, lawks marks the honest low rustic whose surprise is innocent rather than knowing.
  • Affectionate disbelief or gentle protest: Lawks, you don't say!, Lawks, fancy that! The bridge is intonation: the same syllable pitched high and short expresses delight, pitched low and long expresses mock horror at the price of eggs or the neighbour's news.
  • Archaic-nostalgic colour (modern survival): used today only in period pastiche, quoted speech, and deliberately quaint exclamation. The bridge is time-travel by diction: uttering lawks now summons gaslight, aprons, and area railings as surely as prithee summons doublets, and Tolkien deploys it to age Merry's speech toward the Shire's pre-modern hearth.
  • What it never means: it is never a name, verb, or description — no one lawks, nothing is lawks, and no plural lawkses exists. The bridge is negative but instructive: pure interjections like this one live and die as whole utterances, which is why the word's entire semantics fits inside its intonation.

Register and connotation: Lawks is warm, plebeian, and unmistakably dated — the linguistic equivalent of a starched apron or a flat cap doffed in surprise. It never sneers and never blasphemes: its whole point is to let humble speakers show strong feeling while staying respectable. In upper-class or formal mouths it would sound like mockery; in Merry's mouth, looking into a warm hole full of unexpected comfort, it sounds like home — the Brandybuck at his least grand and most loveable, reduced by wonder to his nurse's vocabulary.
