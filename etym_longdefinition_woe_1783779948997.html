woe (n.; also used as an interjection) — Great sorrow, grief, or distress; an affliction; a calamity or misfortune, whether suffered personally or befalling a person, community, or people. As an interjection ("Woe!", "Woe is me!"), an archaic or literary cry of lamentation, or, in biblical style, a prophetic denunciation ("Woe unto you!").
  • Primary sense (grief and distress): deep, abiding sorrow — the condition of suffering misfortune, stronger and more solemn than ordinary sadness, and typically framed as something that befalls one. In Milton's Paradise Lost, "all our woe" is the entire tragic condition of fallen humanity brought into the world by the forbidden tree.
  • Misfortune and calamity sense: the external circumstances themselves — the disasters, adversities, and tribulations that cause grief. The word straddles inner feeling and outer circumstance: one feels woe and one suffers woe.
  • Plural idiom (modern and productive): "woes" = troubles, problems, complaints — used of personal difficulties ("she told a tale of woe"), of groups ("the woes of the working class"), and of economies and markets ("financial woes," "the economy's woes"). This pluralized, colloquial sense has softened the tragic weight into a semi-ironic journalistic usage.
  • Interjection and imprecation (archaic, biblical): "woe to/unto [someone]" — a cry of grief or a warning of impending judgment. "Woe betide you" = trouble will come to you. "Woe is me!" = alas, how unfortunate I am (an old dative construction, literally "woe [be] to me").
  • Derived and fixed forms: the adjective woeful ("wretched, deplorable": "a woeful performance"), the adverb woefully, and the compound woebegone ("sorrowful-looking," from "beset by woe"), plus the phrasings woe is me, woe betide, woe to, tale of woe, woes of the world.
  • Register: outside the fixed phrases, woe is now archaic, poetic, or scriptural; it is the grand, solemn word of epic and biblical English (Milton's Paradise Lost, the King James Bible) rather than everyday conversation. The everyday modern words for the emotion are sorrow and grief.
Etymology and the semantic bridge: the noun grammaticalizes an interjection. Woe begins as the Proto-Indo-European cry *wáy — an imitative exclamation of grief, the sound of "alas!" — which survives with remarkably little change across the family: Latin vae (vae victis, "woe to the conquered"), Ancient Greek ouai (the New Testament's "woe to you"), and the Germanic line that reaches English. In Germanic the interjection *wai was turned into a noun (a reduplicated *waiwaz in West Germanic), giving Old English wā/wēa "grief, misery," Middle English wo, and modern English woe. The bridge from cry to name: the very sound of grief became the word for grief itself — the cry hardened into the noun, and "woe is me" is simply the oldest of English cries carried into grammar. Polysemy summary:
  • Emotional sense: deep sorrow and grief — the archaic-literary core of the word.
  • Eventive sense: misfortune, calamity, affliction — the outward trouble that causes grief.
  • Interjectional sense: the cry "woe!" and the imprecation "woe to/unto you."
  • Colloquial-plural sense: "woes" = troubles — the productive modern sense of economics and personal complaint.
  • Semantic bridge for all four: the interjection of pain was nominalized; the cry of grief became the condition, then the misfortune, then simply the trouble.
