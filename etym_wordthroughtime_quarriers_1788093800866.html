- 14th century: 'quarre/quarry' = stone-pit (and the squared stone itself); workers known as 'quarrymen' or 'hewers of stone'.
- 16th–17th centuries: 'quarrier' recorded as a distinct occupation — a skilled stone-getter at the rock face, distinct from mason and miner; also an inherited surname.
- 18th–19th centuries: the industrial age multiplies quarriers — slate and freestone districts employ thousands; the word (with 'quarryman') becomes a badge of the stone trades and of early labour politics.
- 20th century: 'quarrier' retreats to dialect and history; 'quarryman' becomes standard; the verb 'to quarry' gallops ahead metaphorically (data, facts, quotations).
- Today: 'quarriers' sounds antique and grand — the epic word for stone-getters, at home in fantasy and historical prose (as with Tolkien's dwarves of old).
