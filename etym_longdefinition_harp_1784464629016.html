Harp is a plucked string instrument whose strings rise within a frame — classically a triangular or arched frame with a soundboard, a pillar, and a neck — sounded with the fingers rather than a bow or plectrum. In northern European tradition the harp is the instrument of kings, bards, hall-song, and national identity: the Celtic harp of Ireland and Wales, the frame harp of medieval minstrels, and the modern concert (pedal) harp of the orchestra. In The Hobbit, the company returns with viols and “with Thorin's harp wrapped in a green cloth”: the wrapping marks the instrument as precious baggage — a royal or heirloom object treated with care, not a casual pack-instrument. When Thorin later plays in the dark of Bag End, the harp lifts him from traveling merchant-leader to the tragic royal line of the Mountain; it is the voice of the lost dwarven halls. The tone is elevated, courtly-archaic, mythic; the green cloth and the possessive “Thorin's” make the harp status and identity, not mere band gear.

Precision in organology. A lyre is a smaller plucked instrument with a yoke (two arms joined by a crossbar) and a different classical pedigree; a zither lies flat, its strings stretched over a board or box without a standing frame; a lute is fretted, pear-shaped, plucked or strummed; a fiddle or viol is bowed; a psaltery is a flat, zither-like plucked box of the Middle Ages; a cithara is the large ancient Greek or Roman lyre-type. The harp keeps the open, standing frame with free, plucked strings — usually open (not fretted), with each string giving one note, and in the modern instrument a system of pedals that can raise or lower each string's pitch.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings

  • The instrument (primary sense): the framed, plucked chordophone — from folk and Celtic harps to the orchestral concert harp with seven pedals.
  • The verb “to harp”: to play the harp; to make harp music. Rare as a plain verb, common in figurative idiom.
  • “To harp on / harp upon”: to dwell tiresomely and repeatedly on one subject — the major figurative sense, transferred from the musician's constant re-plucking of the same strings (cf. Shakespeare's “harping on my daughter,” Hamlet; “harp on that string” = return to that theme).
  • “Harp on one string”: idiom meaning to keep reverting to a single topic, from the image of repeatedly plucking a single string.
  • National emblems: the harp is the national symbol of Ireland (on coins and the state arms) and is closely tied to Wales; hence “the harp” in political and cultural shorthand for these nations and their bardic heritage.
  • Harp seal: a northern seal (Pagophilus groenlandicus) named for the harp-shaped dark mark on the back of the adult — an example of the word applied by shape-metaphor in zoology.
  • Jew's harp (jaw harp): a small metal mouth-plucked instrument; despite the name it is functionally and etymologically distinct from the frame harp (the word's first element is probably “jaw,” not the Jewish people — the spelling is folk-etymological), so it is a homograph, not a sense of this word.
  • Harp guitar / harp lute: modern hybrid instruments that combine a guitar or lute neck with extra unstopped bass strings mounted on a frame — borrowed “harp” as a descriptor.
  • Compound terms: concert harp, pedal harp, lever harp, Celtic harp, Irish harp, frame harp, double-action harp — a rich family of technical compound nouns in organology.
  • Figurative, non-musical: “to harp” on a grievance, “the harp of” some emotion (a poetic cliché), and the biblical “harp of David” tradition — the harp as the emblem of lyric poetry and prophecy.

Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: The core meaning — a framed, plucked string instrument — has been stable in English and the Germanic languages for well over a thousand years, so the noun shows no drift at all. The verb, by contrast, developed its famous figurative sense through a simple metaphor of repetition: the harper's fingers pluck the same strings again and again, and so “to harp on” a matter came to mean to keep sounding the same note about it. That transfer (instrument → obsessive verbal repetition) is a textbook case of metaphoric extension from a physical action to a social one.
