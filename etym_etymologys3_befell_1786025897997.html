The verb form befell is the strong past tense (preterite) of the compound verb befall. It represents a native Germanic formation constructed from two ancient Indo-European components: the intensive and transitivizing verbal prefix be- (ultimately from PIE *bʰi- / *ambʰi-, meaning "around, by, about, near") and the strong Germanic root verb *fallaną (from PIE *pōl- or *bʰel-, meaning "to drop down, fall, collapse"). As a strong Class VII Germanic verb, it historically signaled its past tense through vocalic ablaut (vowel alternation) rather than a dental suffix, shifting its vowel from the present stem vowel /ɑ/ or /ɔː/ to the preterite front vowel /eː/ or /e/.

Proto-Germanic Morphology and Old English Attestations:

  • In Proto-Germanic, the prefixed verb *bifallaną functioned both concretely as "to fall upon, tumble onto, or encompass" and abstractly as "to happen to, overtake, or betide." The prefix *bi- served to convert the intransitive base verb "to fall" into a fully transitive verb capable of governing a direct accusative or dative object.
  • In Old English (c. 700–1100 CE), the verb was recorded as befeallan (third-person singular past befeol, plural past befeollon, past participle befeallen). In early Anglo-Saxon poetry and prose, befeallan carried vivid concrete meanings alongside abstract ones. It frequently denoted physical collapse or bereavement: a warrior might be befeallen (deprived of, stripped of) his armor, his hall, or his kinsmen on the battlefield.
  • In the Old English epic Beowulf and the homilies of Abbot Ælfric, the past forms befeol and befeollon were employed to describe dramatic occurrences where doom, divine retribution, or mortal sorrow fell squarely upon the shoulders of a monarch or an entire clan.

Middle English Flourishing and Narrative Formulas:

  • Following the Norman Conquest and the phonetic erosion of unstressed inflectional endings, Old English befeallan transitioned into Middle English as bifallen or befallen, with the strong past tense taking the spellings bifel, bifell, befel, or befell.
  • During the Middle English period (c. 1150–1500), the verb became one of the primary narrative engines of Middle English romance, chronicle, and devotional literature. The opening lines of Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales (c. 1387) famously showcase this impersonal narrative past tense: "Bifil that in that seson on a day, / In Southwerk at the Tabard as I lay / Redy to wenden on my pilgrymage..." (It befell that in that season on a day...).
  • In the works of John Gower, William Langland's Piers Plowman, and Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, bifel/befell was the standard vehicle for chronicling the sudden twists of fortune that governed knights-errant and royal dynasties, illustrating the medieval worldview of Rota Fortunae (the Wheel of Fortune).

Early Modern English, Dramatic Weight, & Modern Preservation:

  • In the Early Modern English of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the spelling stabilized into the modern form befell. The verb appears frequently in William Shakespeare's historical tragedies and romances, where characters demand accounts of royal catastrophes (e.g., in Hamlet, Macbeth, and Richard III, where kings and dukes anxiously ask what fortunes or murders befell their allies).
  • Similarly, the King James Bible (1611) solidified the verb's solemn theological resonance across passages detailing divine trials, historical catastrophes, and unexpected visitations of fate upon the Israelites.
  • Across the eighteenth, nineteenth, and twentieth centuries, as the conversational English language increasingly favored plain, un-prefixed verbs like happened or occurred, befell withdrew gracefully into the elevated literary register. It remains an indispensable stylistic instrument for authors wishing to evoke historical depth, dramatic foreboding, and the solemn finality of fate.
