BUTTERCUP is a transparent Modern English compound, butter plus cup, but each half has travelled far before meeting in a medieval meadow. The first element, butter, begins in the pastoral Indo-European world with PIE *gʷows, cow or ox, reflected in Sanskrit gaus, Latin bos and English cow itself. In Ancient Greek this gave boûs, cow, and by the fourth century BCE the compound boútyron, literally cow-cheese, for the churned cream that Greeks, who preferred olive oil, regarded as a barbarian food of Thracians and Scythians. The second element tūrós, cheese, remains etymologically opaque; Robert Beekes assigns it to Pre-Greek substrate, meaning the butter story touches a non-Indo-European layer at its heart.

From Greek boútyron Latin borrowed būtȳrum in the first century CE, used by Pliny and Martial for both food and ointment. With the spread of dairying the word entered West Germanic very early as *buterā, probably via Roman military and monastic contacts, giving Old English butere by the year 1000, already the familiar yellow spread. The butter-yellow colour sense was therefore available centuries before the flower name: butter had long meant rich yellow fat.

  • Cup element: Classical Latin cūpa meant a large tub, cask or vat for wine and grain. In Late Latin a diminutive or variant cuppa emerged meaning a small drinking cup, widely borrowed into Germanic as Old English cuppe, Old High German chupfa and Old Norse koppr.
  • Middle English compounding, thirteenth to fifteenth centuries: butercoppe, buttercuppe appear for the glossy yellow Ranunculus species whose bowl-shaped, butter-glossy petals suggested a tiny cup smeared or filled with butter. Alternative folk forms like butterflower and kingcup competed briefly.
  • Early modern herbalism, sixteenth to seventeenth centuries: Gerard, Parkinson and Culpeper list buttercups, distinguishing upright meadow buttercup, creeping buttercup and bulbous buttercup, warning that despite the sweet name the plants are acrid and poisonous to cattle.
  • Modern standardisation, eighteenth to twenty-first centuries: Linnaeus places them in Ranunculus acris, repens and bulbosus; buttercup becomes the affectionate popular name, immortalised in Wordsworth, Brontë and children's games of holding the flower under the chin to see if one likes butter.

Phonetically both halves are conservative. Butter retains the geminate tt from Latin tt through Germanic, with the southern English vowel lowering to u; cup retains the short u and final p from Latin cuppa with only spelling normalisation. The compound stress is firmly initial, BUTTER-cup, with secondary stress on cup, and the spelling fused from two words to one by 1500.

Semantically the compound never meant a cup for butter. It meant a cup-like flower the colour of butter, a visual simile frozen as a name. That is why Baker can write of buttercup dust: he extends the colour term from flower to dry yellow pollen-dust that coats the black shoes of plovers feet or the peat, turning a botanical name into a painterly colour adjective. The evolution from cow-cheese to dairy staple to flower-colour to pollen-dust shows how agricultural vocabulary migrates into nature writing while retaining its homely, tactile warmth.
