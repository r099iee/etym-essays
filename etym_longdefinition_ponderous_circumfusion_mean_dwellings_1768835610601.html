ponderous — slow, laborious, and clumsy because of great weight; moving or acting as if weighted down by a vast mass. The word comes from Latin ponderōsus, 'weighty', from pondus, 'weight', and its root meaning is simply 'weighing heavily'. Applied to the architecture of Gormenghast in the sentence 'taken by itself would have displayed a certain ponderous architectural quality', the word names a quality of deliberate, gravitationally serious mass — an edifice so heavy that it seems to press down on the eye and the mind, a thing that could not hurry even if it wished, and whose very slowness is a form of sombre authority.

Polysemy of ponderous.

  • Physical heaviness (primary): of great weight, massive, cumbersome; a ponderous boulder, a ponderous piece of furniture.
  • Slow, laboured movement: moving or acting heavily and clumsily, as if under a load; a ponderous gait, ponderous machinery. Bridge: weight slows motion, so the idea of heaviness slides into the idea of sluggish, laborious movement.
  • Of prose, style, or argument: laboriously heavy, dull, and solemn; a ponderous book, ponderous wit. Bridge: a heavy thing plods, and a heavy style plods too — the word transfers from the body to the manner, acquiring a hint of tedium and self-importance.
  • Literary solemnity: with positive overtones, deliberate and majestic gravity, the slow dignity of age and mass.

Against its near-synonyms, ponderous is distinct from heavy (a neutral physical adjective with no necessary slowness), from massive (size and bulk with no motion), from weighty (usually transferred to arguments and reasons of importance), and from cumbersome (awkward and hard to handle because of bulk). Ponderous alone carries the double charge of great weight and slow, laborious movement.

circumfusion — a pouring around; the act or state of being surrounded by something that spreads or flows about a central point. The word is a rare and learned formation from Latin circumfundere, 'to pour around' (circum, 'around', + fundere, 'to pour'), and English has kept it as a dignified, quasi-technical term of learned prose — in the sentence it describes the circumfusion of mean dwellings swarming like an epidemic around the walls of Gormenghast, letting the houses behave as a fluid poured around a fixed point.

Polysemy of circumfusion.

  • Physical pouring around (primary): the spreading of a liquid, gas, or light around a centre; a circumfusion of light creating a halo or aura.
  • State of being surrounded: the condition of a thing enclosed or enveloped by something that flows or spreads about it.
  • Learned/alchemical register: a rare Latinate coinage of 16th–17th-century English, used in alchemy, medicine (the circumfusion of humours), and poetry for any enveloping radiance.

Bridge: Latin fundere 'to pour' is the same root that yields fuse, diffuse, confuse, and profuse; the prefix circum- 'around' turns a simple pouring into an enveloping — the semantic step from a stream to a surrounding atmosphere.

mean — (in this sentence) poor or shabby; of low quality, rank, or condition; small and squalid. The houses 'that swarmed like an epidemic' are the mean dwellings of Gormenghast's shadow. The adjective descends from Old English gemǣne, 'shared by all, common', and its history is a long social descent: what is common to all becomes ordinary, ordinary slides into vulgar, and vulgar into inferior, so that by the early modern period 'mean' described the shabby, the low, and the squalid — the visible signature of low estate. Bridge: the aristocratic judgment equates the common with the degraded; the word's social history is written into its sense.

Polysemy of mean (three distinct words).

  • Mean 'shabby, inferior' (the sense here): Old English gemǣne 'common, shared' → low, poor, unworthy; also 'mean-spirited' in the modern American sense 'cruel, unkind' (19th century: from 'low, unprincipled' to 'nasty').
  • Mean 'to intend': a different word from Old English mǣnan 'to say, to complain, to intend', descended from a different root — the two are homonyms, entirely unrelated.
  • Mean 'average, middle': a third word, from Old French meien < Latin medianus, 'middle', from medius — the mean of mathematics and statistics, again unrelated.
dwellings — places of residence; the noun of to dwell, carrying an old, sober dignity: a dwelling is a place where people live and remain, conceived not with the warmth of 'home' but with the fixedness of a settled habitation. In the sentence, the dwellings are the small, poor houses of Gormenghast's shadow, which 'by ancient law, were granted this chill intimacy with the stronghold' — a closeness to the castle that is itself a form of social and physical servitude, the poor pressed against the wall of power. The word is deliberately flat and legal: a dwelling is a place of residence in the language of statute, survey, and chronicle, and that dry neutrality is exactly right for a sentence about ancient law.

Polysemy of dwellings.

  • Places of residence (primary): houses, flats, or shelters where people live; the functional, administrative term.
  • The gerund 'dwelling (on/upon)': the act of lingering or remaining with a thought — 'dwelling on the past' — from the verb's older sense 'to tarry, delay' (Middle English dwellen).
  • Legal/statutory register: 'dwelling-house', a building occupied as a residence; the word of surveys, chronicles, and law-courts.

Bridge: to dwell originally meant to tarry or be delayed (Old English dwellan, 'to hinder, mislead'); if you linger long enough in one place you are no longer wandering but staying — delay becomes settlement, and the dwelling is the place where you stopped.

Compared with home (warm, emotional), house (purely physical), residence (official, slightly pompous), abode (literary, temporary), and hovel (contemptuous), dwelling is the sober, administrative word — the word a law or a chronicle would use, which is exactly why Peake chooses it.
