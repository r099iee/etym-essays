- 14th c. - a “grate” is any lattice of bars: window grates, prison grates, altar rails - architectural, a simple word for barred openings
- 16th–17th c. - the word narrows to the iron frame in the hearth - domestic comfort; the grate becomes the household’s symbol of warmth, and an empty grate the sign of poverty
- Victorian age - the polished coal-grate, centrepiece of the parlour - status, cosiness, and domestic ritual (“blacking the grate”)
- 20th c. - central heating retires the hearth-grate - the word survives in “drain grate”, “floor grating”, and in literature as nostalgia
- today - the noun feels old-fashioned and fireside; the homograph verb “grate” (“to scrape; to irritate — to grate on one’s nerves”) now dominates everyday speech
