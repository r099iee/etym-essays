BOISTEROUS: Noisy, energetic, unruly, and wildly cheerful; loud, turbulent, and unrestrained — of behaviour, crowds, winds, seas, and weather. Boisterous characterizes behaviour, crowds, winds, or activities that are rowdy, exuberant, and unrestrained. When applied to people — especially children, sailors, tavern company, or victory crowds — it conveys high-spirited noisiness and rough, playful energy rather than malice: the noise is loud and physical, the mood often cheerful or at least festive, yet manners may be disorderly. In the card’s sentence the tavern is “filled with boisterous sailors singing sea shanties and clinking wooden mugs”: the word catches the rough, joyful racket of the port tavern, men loud and vigorous, singing and drinking, energy overflowing the ordinary bounds of decorum — yet without the menace of open violence. When applied to nature (boisterous winds, seas, weather), the word describes turbulent, stormy, and violent commotion with less of the playful colour. Literary tone can be affectionate or mildly disapproving depending on context: a boisterous welcome may delight or exhaust; a boisterous classroom may amuse or alarm. The word sits between celebration and chaos, capturing energy that overflows social containment without necessarily intending harm. Senses and uses (full polysemy).
  • Sense 1 — of people and crowds (the dominant modern sense): noisily energetic, rowdy, and high-spirited — boisterous children, sailors, revellers, crowds; the card’s sentence is a classic instance, where the tavern’s festive clamour is loud and physical but essentially good-humoured.
  • Sense 2 — of noise, laughter, and celebration: boisterous laughter, a boisterous welcome, a boisterous party — the audible, kinetic commotion of mirth and energy; the sense is warm or mildly critical by context, describing noise that is exuberant rather than malicious.
  • Sense 3 — of weather and natural forces: boisterous winds, seas, weather — turbulent, stormy, violent; the transferred sense, present since the early modern period, applies the rough, unrestrained energy of the human sense to the elements, so that a boisterous sea is one that is wild and hostile to ships.
  • Sense 4 — historical: “rough, coarse, clumsy, fierce” (the older sense): the Middle English ancestor boistous described coarse cloth, rude manners, and violent force, with an edge of danger or uncouthness that modern festive uses have largely softened away; the history is a classic case of amelioration.
Near-synonyms and semantic boundaries.
  • Rowdy: similar in loud, unruly behaviour; differs because rowdy more strongly suggests disorder, trouble, or roughhousing that borders on the objectionable, while boisterous often allows joyful exuberance without criminal or violent overtones.
  • Exuberant: similar in overflow of spirits and vitality; differs because exuberant need not be loud or physically rough, while boisterous insists on audible and kinetic commotion as part of the core image.
  • Rambunctious: similar, chiefly of children and animals, in playful disorder; differs because rambunctious is more colloquial (especially American), while boisterous is broader in register and also standard for winds and seas.
  • Turbulent: similar in agitated, disordered movement; differs because turbulent is serious and unstable — political turmoil, violent air — without the festive human cheer that boisterous can carry.
  • Uproarious: similar in loudness; differs because uproarious centres on hilarious laughter and uproar, while boisterous covers general energetic racket — play, song, movement — not only mirth.
Collocations and register. The word collocates with crowd, laughter, children, celebration, winds, seas, and weather, and forms the adverb boisterously and the noun boisterousness. Register is common literary and journalistic English — neither slang nor ultra-technical — and the emotional valence is often warm or tolerant, though the word can sharpen into criticism when the noise violates the norms of the setting. Its dual life — festive crowd or stormy sea — makes it a favourite of storytellers, who use it to fill a tavern with song and a bay with weather in a single stroke.
