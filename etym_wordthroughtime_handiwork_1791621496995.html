- 8th–11th c. (Old English) - Attested as handgeweorc, combining the tactile physical limb with the collective noun of action; used in heroic and religious poetry for weapons, fortifications, and God's creation of mankind; held heroic, tactile, and spiritual reverence.
- 12th–15th c. (Middle English) - Underwent phonological weakening of the prefix ge- to -i-, becoming handiwerc / handywerk; folk-etymologically reinterpreted as handy (skillful with hands) + work; widespread in guild culture and trade crafts.
- 16th–17th c. (Early Modern English) - Canonized in Tudor and Jacobean religious English, most notably in the Coverdale Psalter and King James Bible (Psalm 19:1: 'the firmament sheweth his handywork'); acquired enduring majesty and cosmic grandeur in the English cultural imagination.
- 18th–19th c. - Broadened during the Industrial Revolution into a deliberate contrast against mechanized factory output; romanticized as a mark of pre-industrial authenticity, bespoke needlework, and artistic soul.
- 20th c. to Present - Shifted towards ironic, forensic, or intellectual contexts (e.g., inspecting the messy 'handiwork' of a burglar or the abstract 'handiwork of the mind'), while retaining its traditional appreciation for artisanal craft.
