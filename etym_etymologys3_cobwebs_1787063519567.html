The word cobwebs is a historical compound noun of native Germanic origin that preserves within its modern form the fossilized remnant of an ancient Old English word for spider that has otherwise vanished from standard English vocabulary.

1. Etymological Evolution of Constituent 1 (Cob - Spider):

The first element cob- derives from Old English coppe ("spider"), which itself represents a clipped, hypocoristic form of the ancient compound ātorcoppe ("poison-head" or "venomous spider", from PIE *ait- "poison" + *gewb- "round head/vessel"). In Proto-Germanic, *kuppaz denoted a round, spherical object or bowl. Because spiders were perceived as small, round, bulbous-bodied creatures full of venom, Anglo-Saxon speakers used coppe as an affectionate or descriptive short name for the arachnid.

While the independent noun coppe became obsolete in standard English after the 14th century (supplanted entirely by spider), it was preserved permanently in compounding. During the 14th century, voicing assimilation occurred before the voiced labial consonant /w/: the voiceless bilabial plosive /p/ in coppeweb softened into the voiced bilabial plosive /b/, yielding cobweb.

2. Etymological Evolution of Constituent 2 (Web - Woven Textile):

The second constituent web descends from the highly productive Proto-Indo-European verbal root *webʰ-, meaning "to weave, plait, braid, or intertwine threads." This PIE root yielded an extensive family of cognates across Indo-European languages: in Ancient Greek it produced hymphḗ ("woven fabric, tissue") and hyphaíno ("I weave", source of biological terms like hypha), in Sanskrit it yielded ūrṇavābhi ("wool-weaver, spider"), and in Germanic it gave rise to the Proto-Germanic verb *webaną ("to weave") and noun *wabją ("woven fabric, web").

In Old English, this produced webb ("woven textile, tapestry, or spider's net") and webba ("a male weaver") alongside webbestre ("a female weaver," source of the English surname Webster). From the earliest Germanic records, the natural silken traps constructed by spiders were poetically conceptualized as textiles woven on an invisible loom.

3. Synthesis, Folk Medicine, & Modern Semantics:

In early Middle English (around 1300–1320), speakers synthesized these two components into coppeweb (literally "spider-woven textile"). The compound appeared in Middle English translations of the Bible (such as the Wycliffite Bible in Isaiah 59:5, translating Latin telas araneae as "the webbis of coppis" or "coppewebbis").

Throughout the medieval and early modern periods, cobwebs played a vital practical role in folk medicine. Because spider silk contains fibroin, pyrrolidine, and potassium nitrate, it possesses natural styptic, antimicrobial, and blood-clotting properties. Peasants and battlefield barber-surgeons applied clumps of fresh cobwebs directly to wounds to stop hemorrhaging—a practice famously referenced by William Shakespeare in A Midsummer Night's Dream.

During the 18th and 19th centuries, the word's association with dusty, uncleaned corners fostered the modern cognitive idiom "to clear the cobwebs from one's mind." In the 20th century, economists formalized the term in the Cobweb Theorem to describe oscillating supply-and-demand cycles, showcasing the enduring semantic vitality of this ancient Germanic compound.
