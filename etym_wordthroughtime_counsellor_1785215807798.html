- 13th-15th century courtly adoption - enters English from French as the name for one who gives counsel, at home in royal courts and legal assemblies; the counsellor advises the king, the judge, and the city.
- 16th-18th century intimacy - broadens from office to relationship: a counsellor is now the trusted private adviser who shares in deliberation, a figure of domestic and intellectual partnership rather than mere office.
- 19th century elevation - used in biography and homage (as of Mill's wife Harriet) for the intimate guide whose judgment is treated as law; the word carries a courtly, reverential dignity.
- Contemporary use - professionalized into school, marriage, and careers counsellors, where the sense of trusted therapeutic or vocational guidance replaces the older courtly role; the British spelling counsellor retains a formal flavour.
