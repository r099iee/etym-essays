Frail describes a state of pronounced weakness and delicacy: physically weak, thin, easily broken or damaged, and (of people, especially the elderly or ill) lacking the strength to bear ordinary burdens. In the literary context of this card (a translated scene of Dream of the Red Chamber), the word carries tragic beauty: “They also noticed the frail body which seemed scarcely strong enough to bear the weight of its clothes, but which yet had an inexpressible grace about it.” The specificity is devastating — mere cloth, the lightest of burdens, is almost too heavy for her — yet the sentence pivots from weakness to grace, and the word’s whole weight in the passage is paradoxical: fragility itself becomes beauty, the visible sign of illness, grief, and inner refinement. In the Chinese literary tradition this is the aesthetic of the “sickly beauty,” the type of Xi-shi, the “weak willow swaying in the wind,” and the translation’s frail captures that ideal precisely.

The word’s senses, concrete and transferred, may be enumerated exhaustively:

  • Physical weakness (primary): (of a person or animal) thin, weak, delicate, lacking strength and vigour — “a frail old woman”; (of a part of the body) slight and delicate — “a frail frame.”
  • Brittleness / easy damage: (of an object) easily broken, damaged, or destroyed — “a frail bridge,” “frail china” (close to, but distinguishable from, fragile).
  • Moral weakness (archaic and medieval): weak to temptation, liable to sin, spiritually or morally inconstant — the “frail flesh” of religious writing, “frailty” as the human susceptibility to sin (the sense that dominated Middle English and produced the famous Shakespearean “Frailty, thy name is woman!”).
  • Constitutional / medical sense: in modern geriatric medicine, “frail” is a near-technical term for the syndrome of declining physiological reserve in the elderly (frailty) — reduced strength, endurance, and resistance, and heightened vulnerability.
  • Abstract and transferred: (of institutions, arguments, plans, alliances, health, hopes) weak, insubstantial, easily undermined or overthrown — “a frail alliance,” “frail hopes,” “frail health”; the abstract noun frailty extends the moral and physical senses together.
  • Literary-aesthetic sense: delicacy prized as refinement — a slender, translucent, almost ghostly grace, the “frail beauty” of romantic and Victorian writing, and the aesthetic of the sickly heroine in world literature (cf. the Chinese “sickly beauty” type above).

Historically, the word entered Middle English from Old French fraile in the 14th century with the moral sense dominant — “frail” meant morally weak, prone to sin — and the physical sense (“weak, easily broken”) developed alongside and gradually overtook it. In the 16th and 17th centuries the word settled into its modern centre: bodily weakness and easy damage, with the moral sense retreating to religious and poetic usage. The 19th century added the aesthetic valorization of frailty (delicacy as beauty), and the 20th century added the medical register. English frail and fragile are doublets — both descend from Latin fragilis (“easily broken”), frail through the natural Old French development and fragile borrowed directly from Latin in the 16th century — so the pair share a root while differing in feel: fragile stresses brittleness (easily broken), frail stresses weakness (lacking strength).

Against near neighbours the distinctions are crucial. Fragile (the root-doublet) means “easily broken,” a quality of the object; frail can mean this too but centres on weakness, especially of a living creature. Weak is the broadest term, covering strength, power, and character without the connotations of thinness and delicacy. Delicate adds fineness and elegance — something can be delicate and strong, whereas frail always implies vulnerability. Brittle applies to hard objects that shatter; frail leans toward soft, thin, weakened things. Feeble is closer in register — “lacking strength” — but is often contemptuous or clinical, lacking frail’s undertone of pathos and grace. The core of frail remains: weak and delicate, easily broken — with a long halo of moral, medical, and aesthetic senses layered over that foundation.
