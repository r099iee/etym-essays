In Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince the young playwright — trying to finish a play for the Director of the Theatre, too cold to write, with no fire in the grate and only a bunch of withered violets at his side — lives in a garret: a small, often cramped room immediately beneath the sloping roof of a house. The word names the uppermost inhabited chamber of a dwelling, typically reached by a final, steeper flight of stairs and lit by a dormer ("garret") window. In ordinary modern use it is a slightly bookish synonym for "attic room" or "top-floor room", but its historical and cultural cargo is far richer: for three centuries the garret has been the stock stage-set of the poor artist, the starving writer and the romantic bohemian.

Core sense and its boundaries. A garret is strictly the room under the roof, not the whole roof-space, and it is a room that is lived or worked in, not merely stored in. The distinctions from its neighbours matter:

  • Garret vs attic: an attic is the entire storey, void or storage space under the roof; a garret is a habitable room inside that space — someone sleeps, writes or works there, and the word implies occupation.
  • Garret vs loft: a loft is traditionally an open, unpartitioned space (hayloft, organ-loft); in modern estate-agent English "loft" has been glamorised into the open-plan converted studio — the functional inverse of the tiny, partitioned garret.
  • Physical character: garrets follow the rafters, so the ceilings slope; they are lit by dormer ("garret") windows; they lose heat, gain damp, and in period houses were the cheapest rooms of all — let to servants, apprentices, students, seamstresses and the poor.

Secondary and once-technical senses. The word's polysemy is small but real, and a full dictionary entry records it:

  • Watchtower / turret (historical, Middle English): the earliest attested English sense (c. 1300) was military — a garite was a watchtower or turret of a town wall or castle, a raised place of guard beyond the reach of an enemy. As town defence gave way to private building, the small tower set on a roof kept the name, and when house design changed again, the roof-turret became the inhabitable top room: this descent from rampart to rafter is the word's central semantic bridge.
  • Slang: the head (19th century, chiefly British): Victorian slang used "garret" for the head, on the model of "upper storey"; hence "wrong in the garret" or "cracked in the garret" for someone mentally unbalanced — a jokey extension that proves how thoroughly the word had been domesticated.
  • Attributive and technical use: "garret window" (the dormer that lights a garret), "garret flat", "garret studio" survive in British building, joinery and letting vocabulary.

The cultural bridge: the romantic garret. The word's modern emotional charge comes from the nineteenth century. The Romantic and post-Romantic imagination romanticised poverty: the cold, unheated garret became the sacred workshop of the unrecognised genius — the place where the starving artist completes his masterpiece by candlelight. The topos runs through Henri Murger's Scènes de la vie de bohème (1851), Puccini's La Bohème (1896, whose first scene is literally the bohemians' garret), and countless Victorian magazine tales of the "garret-artist"; Wilde's own young playwright, shivering in his garret over an unfinished play, is the type of the figure to the life. The bridge is complete: a humble word for a roof-chamber became a symbol — poverty ennobled as artistic authenticity, the garret a badge of devotion to art.

Metaphorical extension and register today. From this core the word extends figuratively: "the garret of the mind" for the private upper workspace of thought (rare, poetic); "garret philosophy" for cranky, retired-theorist ideas; and in ironic letting prose, where "a charming garret" euphemises a cold, sloping, walk-in cupboard. In register, "garret" is now distinctly literary: an ordinary speaker says "attic" or "top room", and the word survives chiefly in fiction, in heritage descriptions, and in compounds like "garret-artist" as shorthand for the struggling creative class. Its social opposite is the penthouse — the luxurious top-floor apartment that inverts the garret's poverty into display — and its modern functional heir is the studio flat.

  • Sense 1: a small, cramped room under the roof, with the strong implication of poor, romantic occupation.
  • Sense 2 (historical): a watchtower or roof-turret; the word's original English sense.
  • Sense 3 (slang, 19th c.): the head; "wrong in the garret" = insane.
  • Sense 4 (attributive): garret window, garret flat — dormer-lit top-floor accommodation.
