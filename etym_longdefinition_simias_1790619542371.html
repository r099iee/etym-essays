Simias of Rhodes was a Hellenistic Greek poet, grammarian and innovator active around 300 BCE, remembered as one of the earliest creators of technopaegnia, poems whose lines are arranged to form a visual shape on the page or stone.

Biographical detail is thin and largely inferred from later lexicographers, the Suda, and surviving fragments: a Rhodian writing in the early Alexandrian age, contemporary with Philetas of Cos and Asclepiades of Samos, moving between Rhodes, Alexandria and perhaps Athens, participating in the learned, bookish culture fostered by the Ptolemies where brevity, erudition, riddle and formal experiment were prized over Homeric length.

  • Inventor of shape poetry: three surviving pattern poems attributed to Simias — the Pelekys (Double Axe), the Pterygia (Wings) and the Oon (Egg) — each displays metrical ingenuity in which line lengths wax and wane to draw the object named; the Axe poem, for example, was meant to be inscribed on an actual votive axe, uniting text, object and offering, and ancient commentators explicitly cite Simias as the originator whom Dosiadas, Theocritus and later Latin and Renaissance poets imitated.
  • Grammarian and polymath: beyond play, Simias compiled glossaries, wrote on dialect and is credited with works titled Months, Apollon and epigrams in the Greek Anthology; this double identity — scholar and player — explains his semantic bridge from serious philologist to virtuoso game-maker, typical of Alexandrian learned wit where erudition authorises play.
  • Proper-name sense and cultural symbol: today “Simias” functions less as a biography than as shorthand for origin-point of concrete and visual poetry; to call a poem Simian is to claim lineage from riddling Hellenistic modernism to Apollinaire’s Calligrammes and digital concrete poetry; the secondary meaning is thus emblematic rather than personal.

In literary history Simias marks the shift from oral performance to book culture: his poems only work when seen, not merely heard, presupposing papyrus rolls, figured layout and rereading. Ancient critics admired his poikilia — intricacy — while later moralists sometimes dismissed such games as trifles, a tension still present when scholars debate whether to shelve him with major Hellenistic poets or with curiosities.

Nuance for the learner: pronounce with initial stress, capitalise always, and distinguish Simias of Rhodes the poet (c.300 BCE) from Simias of Thebes, the Socratic interlocutor in Plato’s Phaedo, and from later homonyms; context — Rhodes, axe, wings, egg, shape poetry — disambiguates instantly.

For students of Greek literature Simias is also a chronological anchor: his floruit around 300 BCE places him at the hinge between Classical city poetry and Hellenistic court and book poetry, alongside Philetas, Asclepiades and the young Callimachus. Ancient testimonia credit him with treatises on glossaries and months and with epigrams on mythological rarities, suggesting a scholar who collected obscure words while composing them into puzzles, a double practice that later Alexandrians institutionalised in the Museum and Library.

  • Pedagogical and riddling use: in modern classrooms Simias exemplifies poikilia and leptotes — intricacy and fineness — used to teach dialect mixture, Aeolic and Doric forms, and quantitative metre; the bridge is exemplification, where one difficult author stands for a whole aesthetic of learned brevity.
  • Art-historical and material-text use: archaeologists and book historians cite Simias to show that Hellenistic reading was already visual and spatial, requiring centred lines, ruled margins and deliberate mise-en-page on papyrus; here the polysemy shifts from author to artefact, from who he was to what his layout proves about ancient media.
  • Comparative-modernist use: critics from Lessing to contemporary concrete poets invoke Simias to legitimise visual verse as classical rather than novelty; the semantic bridge is ancestry-claiming, where invoking a Rhodian founder dignifies experimental print and screen poetry as renewal rather than rupture.

To name Simias in context is therefore to signal more than biography: island origin, Alexandrian erudition, and the founding gesture by which Greek poetry learned to mean through shape as well as sound.
