Loath is an adjective meaning unwilling, reluctant, or strongly disinclined to do something, usually because of dislike, disgust, fear, or moral objection, as in she was loath to admit her mistake or he seemed loath to leave the fireside. It describes not mere hesitation but an active inward pulling-away, a settled aversion that makes the action feel repugnant or wrong to the person facing it.

In the source sentence the word appears inside the famous litotes nothing loath: Tsarevitch Alexei, nothing ______, dismounted and remained the guest of Maria Morevna. Here nothing loath is a double negative meaning altogether willing, glad, and eager, with no reluctance at all. The construction is deliberate archaism on the translator's part, giving the fairy tale a chivalric flavour: the prince does not merely agree to stay, he stays with a willing heart, and within two days the two have fallen deeply in love.

Core meaning in detail: Loath governs an infinitive (loath to speak) or a nominal complement (loath to change), and it almost always describes people rather than things. Its strength sits midway on the scale of refusal: weaker than refusing or opposed, stronger than hesitant or slow. To be loath is to feel the weight of dislike pressing against the act, so that doing it would cost genuine inward effort. Dictionaries often gloss it as unwilling and hesitant, catching both the aversion and the dragging foot.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Unwilling, reluctant (primary modern sense, from c. 1200): loath to disturb him, loath to spend the money. The bridge from the Old English sense of hateful, hostile runs through the person who hates the deed and therefore will not do it: what was once hateful to me becomes I am unwilling because I hate it, shifting the hatred from the object onto the subject's attitude.
  • Nothing loath (idiom, 16th c., as in the source): altogether willing, with cheerful readiness. The bridge is litotes, the rhetorical understatement by negation: denying reluctance (nothing loath) asserts eagerness more gracefully than saying very willing outright, and the antique phrasing suits romance, ballad, and fairy tale.
  • Hateful, repulsive, hostile (Old English sense, now obsolete): Old English lāð meant hateful, odious, grievous, applied to enemies, injuries, and loathsome things. The bridge to the modern sense is subject-object reversal: the loath thing (hated object) of Beowulf's age becomes the loath person (hating subject) of Middle English, keeping the hatred but moving it inside the feeler.
  • Loathhtly and loathness (derivative senses): the adverb loathly (loathly to confess) and noun loathness or lothness name the reluctance itself. The bridge is ordinary suffixation, turning the state of aversion into a quality that can be measured, confessed, or overcome.
  • Confused with loathe (modern error sense): many writers misspell the adjective as loathe (I am loathe to say), importing the verb's -e. The bridge is phonetic identity: adjective and verb sound exactly alike (/ləʊθ/ versus /ləʊð/ differ only in voicing, inaudible to most ears), so the spelling of hatred's verb bleeds into reluctance's adjective, though careful editors still correct it.

Register and connotation: loath is formal and slightly literary; everyday speech prefers reluctant, unwilling, or don't want to. Its stiffness is its charm: loath sounds principled, as if the reluctance came from conscience rather than laziness, which is why politicians are loath to comment and heroines are loath to forgive. The phrase nothing loath is pure storybook, belonging to translators of fairy tales, romances, and chronicles, where it gilds willingness with courtesy.
