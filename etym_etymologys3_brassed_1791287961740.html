Brassed here is the past tense of brass in the phrasal verb brass up, "to pay up, hand over money". Its base is the noun brass, Old English bræs, a mystery word with no known cognates beyond English. The OED and Etymonline agree: of unknown origin, not found elsewhere; comparisons with Old Swedish brasa "fire" or French brasser "to brew" are speculative and untraced. What is certain is early use: Ælfric glosses bræs as the yellow alloy, originally copper alloyed with tin (what we now call bronze), later copper alloyed with zinc (modern brass). The Romans first made true zinc-brass deliberately; English translators loosely used "brass" for any ancient copper alloy, including in Bible translations.

From metal to money is a short metonymic step. Copper and bronze were coin metals, so by 1362 brass could mean a copper coin, and by the late 16th century it meant cash in general, especially low-denomination copper coinage. Northern English preserves this best in the proverb "where there's muck there's brass", money earned in dirty industry. Farmer's Slang and Its Analogues (1890) lists brass as "a generic term for money", noting "at one time money was made of brass". From "money" to "pay money" is inevitable slang verbing, first securely attested in the Sporting Times (25 Jan 1890): "If you don't brass up I shall put you off the bus." Binstead, Pugh, and Burke carry it through the 1900s-1910s, always with a conductor, creditor, or bookie demanding coin.

  • Alloy to coin: yellow metal becomes small copper money (1362-1775); figurative hardness ("bold as brass", impudence from 1620s) branches separately and does not feed the pay sense.
  • Coin to cash: by Elizabethan times brass = ready money in slang and dialect; the northern industrial sense cements it.
  • Cash to verb: to brass up = to produce the brass, to settle. The particle up marks completion, as in pay up, cough up. Green's Dictionary of Slang records only this pay sense for brass up, with Wodehouse's 1934 Right Ho, Jeeves as the classic citation.
  • Wodehouse elevation: "He brassed up like an officer and a gentleman" adds honour to mere payment — prompt, ungrudging, gentlemanly. Later Wodehouse reuses it (Mating Season 1949, Jeeves and the Feudal Spirit 1954) to signal decent financial behaviour.
  • Modern narrowing: brass up survives as dated but lively British slang, overshadowed by fork out and cough up; military "brass up" (to bombard, from shell casings) is a separate homonym from ammunition brass.

Phonologically brass shows the northern /æ/ versus southern /ɑː/ split: RP /brɑːs/, General American /bræs/, past brassed /brɑːst/. Spelling is conservative. The scholarly nuance is the admission of ignorance: despite a continuous written record from Old English, the ultimate origin remains unknown, a rare isolate in the metal vocabulary where most terms (gold, silver, copper, iron, bronze) have clear cognates. The "officer and a gentleman" simile is itself an Army formula for honourable conduct, making Uncle Tom's payment sound not extracted but gallant — a comic contrast to the usual reluctant flavour of brass up.
