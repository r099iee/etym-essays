The name Fijians fuses an Austronesian island self-name heard through Tongan ears with a Latinate European suffix for peoples — a miniature history of Pacific contact.

  • Oceanic deep roots (1500 BCE-1000 CE): Fiji was settled by Lapita voyagers speaking Proto-Oceanic, diversifying into Central Pacific languages. The endonym is Fijian Viti (as in Viti Levu Great Fiji, Viti = Fiji itself). Proto-Central-Pacific *viti is of uncertain deeper etymology — possibly connected to *viti "east, sunrise" or simply the island name — but it is securely attested in all Fijian dialects and in Rotuman Fiti. We flag pre-Viti reconstruction as debated rather than inventing a PIE-style chain; Austronesian linguistics does not yield a neat "meaning" like "land" here.
  • Tongan filter and Cook's chart (17th-18th c.): Tongans, Fiji's powerful neighbours, pronounce Fijian /v/ as /f/ and /t/ with aspiration, rendering Viti as Fisi (spelled Fiji in early wordlists). Captain James Cook, hearing the name via Tongan informants in 1773-1774, charted Feejee. Nineteenth-century hydrographers regularised to Fiji (pronounced /fiːˈdʒiː/ in English, /viˈtʃi/ in Fijian). Thus English Fiji is an exonym twice removed: Fijian self-name filtered through Tongan phonology into English orthography.
  • Latin suffix -ian (antiquity to early modern): The adjectival/demonymic ending -ian / -an continues Latin -anus "belonging to, coming from" (Romanus Roman, Africanus), via Old French -ien/-ain and Middle English -ian (Parisian, Indian, Jamaican). It became the default English way to make New World and Pacific peoples: Tahitian, Hawaiian, Samoan, Fijian (first attested c.1830s-1840s in missionary journals: "the Feejeans / Fijians"). The plural -s is regular Germanic nominal plural grafted onto the Romance suffix.
  • Missionary-press fixation (1835-1874): Wesleyan missionaries (Cargill, Cross) created written Fijian (Bauan dialect), printed dictionaries, and in English prose invariably wrote Fijians for converts and heathen alike. Naval surveys, Williams' Fiji and the Fijians (1858), and cannibalism anecdotes — including the salty-white-flesh quip in your passage — cemented the spelling and the warrior stereotype. Pronunciation settled as /fɪˈdʒiːənz/ with penultimate stress.
  • Indenture and bifurcation (1879-1970): Britain annexed Fiji (1874) and imported 60,000+ Indian girmitiyas for sugar. Colonial census split Fijians (natives under Native Regulations, inalienable land) from Indians (leaseholders, urban traders). English compound Indo-Fijian emerged mid-20th c. to claim belonging while marking difference. At independence (1970) citizenship made all Fiji citizens, but everyday Fijian still meant Indigenous.
  • Post-coup redefinition (1987-present): Coups justified as defending Indigenous Fijian paramountcy entrenched the ethnic sense; the 2013 Constitution countered by decreeing all nationals are Fijians, with iTaukei (owners, original people) for Indigenous rights. Rugby's Flying Fijians, tourism (Visit Fiji, Fijian hospitality / bula!), and climate diplomacy now project a multiethnic civic Fijian brand. Socio-cultural perception thus travelled from 19th-century cannibal-warrior exotic to 21st-century rugby-powerhouse and climate-vulnerable democracy.

In short, Fijians joins a voyaging canoe-name (Viti → Fisi → Fiji) with a Roman imperial suffix (-anus → -ian) to label a modern Pacific people whose identity law still negotiates who the word covers.
