wielded is the past tense and past participle of the verb wield, meaning “to hold and handle (a weapon, tool, or instrument) with the hand and use it effectively,” and, by a long-established figurative extension, “to exercise or exert (power, authority, influence, control, or a faculty) with command and effect.” The word always implies skill, mastery, and active control: one wields a sword the way a commander wields authority, with a deliberate, governing hand.

In the present context the verb is applied to Harriet Taylor's influence over the career of John Stuart Mill: the circumstances under which Mill wrote his Liberty (the essay On Liberty) “are largely connected with the influence which Mrs. Taylor wielded over his career.” The metaphor is martial and sovereign: she wielded influence the way a warrior wields a blade — actively, expertly, and with decisive effect. The choice of verb quietly argues that Mill's intellectual life was not shaped by accident or passive drift but by her deliberate, commanding agency; the influence was not merely felt but exercised, handled, and controlled.

  • Sense 1 (literal, physical): “to hold in the hand and use as a weapon or tool,” e.g. to wield a sword, an axe, a hammer, a pen. The emphasis is on effective handling rather than mere possession: a farmer owns a hammer, but the blacksmith wields it.
  • Sense 2 (figurative, power): “to exercise authority, power, control, or influence,” e.g. to wield power, wield authority, wield influence, wield control, wield the veto. This is the dominant modern use in journalism and rhetoric.
  • Sense 3 (faculties and instruments of mind): “to employ a mental or rhetorical instrument to effect,” e.g. to wield language, wield a pen, wield wit, wield an argument. A writer “wields” prose as an instrument of force.
  • Sense 4 (archaic / historical): “to rule, govern, direct, control,” the original Old English meaning (wealdan), surviving in fixed phrases such as “to wield the sceptre” and in the idea of wielding dominion.
  • Distinction from near-synonyms: exert stresses the application of force or effort without the image of manual dexterity; exercise suggests habitual, systematic use of a right or faculty and lacks the energetic physicality of wield; brandish and flourish mean to wave a weapon threateningly or showily, not necessarily to use it effectively; command focuses on the authority to order others, whereas wield focuses on the effective handling of the instrument of power itself; ply suggests steady repetitive industry with a tool rather than dominant mastery.
  • Register and connotation: vigorous, forceful, slightly literary; used of both weapons and abstract power. The collocations “wield power” and “wield influence” are standard in journalism; “wield a pen” is a favourite of rhetorical prose. Its connotation is competence and force, and its opposite is to be subject to or at the mercy of power rather than to hold it.

Antonyms and contrasts: to obey, to be subject to, to bear passively, to undergo, to be dominated by. The contrast is with possession without use (holding but not handling) and with power exercised carelessly. In short, to wield is to hold and master — the word unites the literal hand on the weapon and the figurative hand on the levers of power, and its past form wielded records that act as completed and effective in the historical record, as with Mrs. Taylor's influence over Mill.
