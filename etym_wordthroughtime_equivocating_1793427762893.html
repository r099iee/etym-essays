- 14th–15th c. — Scholastic classification: equivocate enters English discourse as a technical term of Aristotelian logic, designating the use of a single word to mean two different things in disputation.
- Late 16th c. — The casuistical turn: Catholic theologians adopt the term for the doctrine of mental reservation under religious persecution, giving it a high-stakes ethical and theological dimension.
- 1606 — Jacobean treason and infamy: The trial of the Gunpowder Plot conspirators and Shakespeare’s Macbeth cement the word as a badge of dangerous, oath-breaking Jesuitical deceit.
- 18th c. — Augustan moral satire: Jonathan Swift and his contemporaries secularize the term, deploying it to chastise the hypocrisies, double-dealing, and evasions of polite European society.
- 19th c. — Parliamentary and diplomatic evasion: The word transitions into political and legal reporting, characterizing ministers and witnesses who craft evasive answers to sidestep scrutiny.
- 20th–21st c. — Universal badge of spin: In modern journalistic discourse, equivocating denotes any calculated refusal to give a straight answer, especially by politicians, corporate executives, or institutional spokespeople dodging accountability.
