• Scorched: Similar because both mean burning the surface; differs because scorching implies dry, discolouring heat that leaves a brown or black mark over a broader area, while singeing is a lighter, quicker, edge-touching burn.
  • Burned: Similar because both describe damage by fire or heat; differs because “burned” is the general and stronger term — it covers everything from a singe to total destruction, and it has far more figurative uses.
  • Charred: Similar because both mean surface-burned; differs because “charred” means blackened and carbonised, often irreparably — far more severe than a singe.
  • Sear: Similar because both mean applying heat to the surface; differs because “sear” implies a deliberate, brief, high-heat treatment (searing meat, searing a wound) and is usually intentional, whereas singeing is often an accident.
  • Frizzled: Similar because both describe heat damage to hair; differs because “frizzled” emphasises the curling and crisping of the hair ends by dry heat, while “singed” emphasises the superficial burning itself.
  • Blistered: Similar because both describe heat damage to a surface; differs because blistering forms raised bubbles of skin or paint and implies deeper tissue damage than a singe.
  • Parched: Similar because both can describe drying by heat; differs because “parched” means dried out by heat or thirst and does not involve burning at all.
  • Cauterised: Similar because both involve burning a surface; differs because cauterising is a deliberate medical or surgical burning to seal a wound — purposeful and professional.
  • Singe (as noun): Similar because the rare noun “singe” means the mark or act of singeing; differs in being a rare noun rather than the everyday participle form.
