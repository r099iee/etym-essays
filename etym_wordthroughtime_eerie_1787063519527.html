- [c. 1000–1200: Old English 'earg'] - Originally meant cowardly, timid, vile, or sluggish; describing faint-hearted or fearful individuals.
- [c. 1300–1500: Northern Middle English 'eri' / 'eerie'] - Shifted in Scottish and Northern English dialects from 'timid/cowardly' to 'fearful, superstitious, filled with dread of the supernatural'.
- [c. 1750–1800: Scottish Enlightenment & Robert Burns] - Shifted from the person feeling fear to the object/environment producing fear ('inspiring ghostly dread'); popularized in mainstream English through Scottish poetry and ballads.
- [c. 1820–1900: Victorian Gothic Literature] - Walter Scott and Gothic writers cemented 'eerie' as the preeminent term for uncanny, atmospheric supernatural unease.
- [c. 1900–Present: Modern English] - Universal descriptor for unsettling coincidences, silence, unearthly music, or alien landscapes.
