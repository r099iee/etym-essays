Toledo (proper noun) — A historic city in central Spain, capital of the autonomous community of Castile–La Mancha, set on a steep promontory almost encircled by a great bend of the river Tagus (Tajo). It served as the capital of the Visigothic Kingdom in the 6th–8th centuries, a centre of the Spanish church whose councils bore its name, one of medieval Europe's great centres for the translation of Arabic, Jewish, and Greek learning, and the home of the sword-makers whose blades made "Toledo" a byword for fine steel.

In the source context. Gibbon lists Toledo among the "German princes"' cities — Paris, Toledo, Arles, and Ravenna — where bishops were listened to reverently and the Nicene theology eagerly debated. The pairing is exact: Toledo was where the Visigothic kings — Germanic rulers like Theodoric at Ravenna — abjured Arianism and embraced Catholic (Nicene) Christianity, most famously at the Third Council of Toledo in 589 under King Recared. Toledo is thus Gibbon's emblem of the Germanic world converted to orthodox Roman Christianity, the direct antithesis of Wessex and Mercia still serving Thor and Woden.

History. Under the Roman Republic the town of Toletum, capital of the Celtiberian Carpetani, was a strategic stronghold on the Tagus; under the Empire it became a Roman municipium. In the 6th century it became the capital of the Visigothic kingdom, and its church councils (Toledo, 400–702) legislated for Spanish Christianity. After the Muslim conquest (711) it flourished as Ṭulayṭulah under the Caliphate of Córdoba and the taifa kingdoms, a celebrated city of many faiths. Alfonso VI reconquered it in 1085, and it became the capital of Castile until the court moved to Madrid in 1561. The 12th-century School of Translators, patronized by Archbishop Raimundo and later Alfonso X the Wise, made Toledo the gateway by which Arabic and Greek science, philosophy, and Aristotle entered western Europe. It was also the adopted city of El Greco (1541–1614), who painted the View of Toledo and the great altarpieces of its cathedral and churches. Its historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 1986).

Polysemy and later senses.
  • The city itself: the primary sense — a living Spanish city, UNESCO site, and seat of the primatial archbishop of Spain.
  • Toledo steel / Toledo blade: the celebrated sword- and knife-steel made in the city from the Roman and medieval periods onward; by extension "a Toledo" names a sword or a style of fine blade in English from the 16th–17th centuries.
  • American geography: Toledo, Ohio (founded and named in 1833), and other New World towns and counties named after the Spanish city.
  • The Councils of Toledo and the School of Translators: metonymic uses in church and medieval history for the institutions the city hosted.
  • Toledano / Toledan: the derived adjectives meaning "of Toledo", applied to the city, its art, and its style.

Semantic bridge. From the pre-Roman toponym Toletum — traditionally explained as a fortified or raised place on its hill above the Tagus — the name absorbed a thousand years of Visigothic, Islamic, and Castilian history, then extended metonymically to the city's most famous product, so that a place-name became, in English, a synonym for a fine sword.
