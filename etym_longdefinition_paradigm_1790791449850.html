Paradigm denotes an exemplary model, archetype, typical pattern, or foundational framework that serves as a standard or template for understanding, categorizing, or generating something. In intellectual history and contemporary discourse, it refers most powerfully to an overarching conceptual worldview, methodological framework, or set of shared assumptions that shapes how a scientific community, philosophical school, or society perceives reality and conducts inquiry.

Across science, linguistics, sociology, computing, and structuralist philosophy, paradigm operates across several vital domains:

  • Kuhnian Scientific Paradigm and Paradigm Shifts: Revolutionized by philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn in his seminal 1962 work The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. Kuhn defined a scientific paradigm as the universally recognized scientific achievements, theories, instruments, and standards that, for a time, provide model problems and solutions for a community of practitioners ('normal science'). When anomalies accumulate that the existing paradigm cannot resolve, a scientific crisis ensues, leading to a revolutionary paradigm shift (such as the shift from Ptolemaic geocentrism to Copernican heliocentrism, or from Newtonian classical mechanics to Einsteinian relativity and quantum mechanics).
  • Linguistic and Grammatical Paradigm: In traditional grammar and structural linguistics, a paradigm is the complete set of all the inflected forms of a single word, serving as a model for all other words of the same conjugation or declension class (e.g., the Latin conjugation paradigm *amō, amās, amat, amāmus, amātis, amant*). In Saussurean structural linguistics, the *paradigmatic axis* (associative relation) refers to the vertical set of linguistic units that can substitute for one another in the same structural position in a sentence, contrasted with the horizontal *syntagmatic axis* (linear sequence of words).
  • Programming Paradigms in Computer Science: In software engineering and computer science, a programming paradigm is a fundamental style, architectural philosophy, and way of structuring code and computation. Major paradigms include imperative programming, object-oriented programming (OOP), functional programming (FP), declarative programming, and logic programming—each dictating how programmers model data, handle state, and organize logic.
  • Sociological and Cultural Worldviews: In sociology and cultural theory, a social paradigm represents the shared web of values, metaphysical beliefs, and institutional norms that govern a civilization or historical era (e.g., the 'dominant Western social paradigm' based on industrial growth, technological optimism, and material progress, versus the 'ecological paradigm' centered on sustainability and interconnectedness).
  • Exemplary Model and Archetype: In general rhetoric and literary criticism, a paradigm is a supreme, archetypal exemplar that personifies a particular ideal, virtue, or phenomenon (e.g., 'she stood as the paradigm of artistic integrity').

Historical Semantic Bridges and Polysemy: Derived from ancient Greek *parádeigma* ('pattern, model, precedent', from *paradeiknynai* 'to show side by side, compare'), the word entered Western thought through Plato's theory of Forms (where eternal archetypes served as paradigms for physical reality) and classical grammar tables. In 1962, Thomas Kuhn's epistemological adaptation forever elevated the word from a modest grammatical and pedagogical term into an indispensable macro-concept for explaining how human knowledge, technology, and cultural worldviews evolve through revolutionary structural transformations.
