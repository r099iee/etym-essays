Late 13th c. — Early Middle English emergence as hedling: exclusively physical and spatial, denoting a literal headfirst tumble, dive, or fall from a height where the crown leads the torso.
- 14th–15th c. — Folk-etymological restructuring: the adverbial suffix -ling assimilated phonetically into -long by analogy with spatial orientation, stabilizing into hedlong while continuing to describe violent physical plunges.
- 16th c. — Renaissance metaphorical expansion: broadened widely under Elizabethan and Jacobean dramatists (including Shakespeare) to denote rash mental impulsiveness, uncontrolled passion, and reckless pursuit of desire or destruction.
- 17th–18th c. — Moral and philosophical condemnation: deployed in neoclassical treatises and moralizing sermons to describe the dangerous folly of those who rush into political upheavals, religious heresies, or theological vanity without sober reflection.
- 19th c. — Romantic and industrial acceleration: used to evoke thrilling kinetic speed in Romantic literature (Byronic heroes plunging into turmoil) and the breathtaking pace of modern industrial, financial, and urban expansion.
- 20th–21st c. — Stylistic consolidation in critical prose: commonly utilized for precipitate macroeconomic collapses and relentless creative, artistic, or literary pacing where kinetic momentum outstrips structural deliberation.
