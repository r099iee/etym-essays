- [15th-16th century: the grubbing sense] - "To stub" first meant to dig up or clear away tree stumps and roots (to stub up a field); it was hard, practical, agricultural labour, with no emotional colouring at all.
- [17th century: the collision sense] - The meaning shifts from clearing stumps to colliding with them: to stub one's toe against a fixed projection. The register stays homely and physical — a small, awkward accident rather than a wound.
- [19th century: truncation] - As an adjective, stubbed describes anything left short or blunt (a stubbed cigar, a stubbed tail), so the word's underlying idea of a blunt leftover fragment begins to colour the toe-collision with clumsiness.
- [20th-21st century: cigarette and domestic mishap] - "To stub out" a cigarette extends the crushing-of-a-remnant sense, while stubbing one's toe endures as the archetypal minor domestic accident, told in the same comic-pathetic, colloquial tone that rules today.
