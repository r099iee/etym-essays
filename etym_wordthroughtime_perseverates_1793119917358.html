- Ancient Rome — to persist firmly: Latin perseverare praises steady endurance through hardship, a virtue of soldiers and Stoics.
- 14th c. — English perseverance: persevere enters Middle English as a moral word for holding to faith and purpose.
- 16th–19th c. — perseverance as glory: sermons and schoolbooks celebrate the persevering student, worker, and saint; persistence is purely admirable.
- Early 20th c. — the clinic coins perseveration: German-influenced psychiatry needs a noun for pathological repetition, and English back-forms perseverate around the 1910s.
- Mid-20th c. — neurology adopts it: brain-injury and aphasia studies make perseveration a standard sign, and perseverates enters case notes.
- Today — from ward to world: the verb escapes into essays and everyday speech for minds, eyes, committees, and code that keep repeating their errors.
