No Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for the strike-rap — the verb is an imitative (onomatopoeic) coinage of English, born to match the sound of a sharp blow. The chain is therefore English-internal: - Imitative coinage: rap (verb) "to strike a sharp, quick blow" — first recorded c. 1300, in Middle English; onomatopoeic, imitating the sound of a sharp, dry impact → rap (noun) "a sharp, quick blow; a knock" — c. 1300, alongside the verb → extended senses from the blow: "a rap on the knuckles" → figurative "a reprimand" (16th–17th c.); rap out "to utter sharply" (18th c.); take the rap "to accept blame or punishment" (1904); rap sheet "a criminal record" (20th c., from rap "a charge or blame") → 20th-century sense: to rap "to talk, converse; to deliver rhythmic speech" (African-American vernacular, early–mid 20th c.) → rap music (genre name, late 1970s, c. 1979), rapper, rap battle → rap + past-tense suffix -ed → rapped (the inflected form on this card) Note (anti-merging guardrail): The homophonous rap "to hasten, to seize, to snatch" is a different word — from Old Norse hrapa "to fall, to hurry", via Middle English — and must not be merged with the strike-rap. English rapid is unrelated as well: it descends from Latin rapidus, from rapere "to seize, to tear". The strike-rap is imitative and has no family outside the English echo-cluster of sharp-impact words (tap, clap, slap, snap, flap, whap), and it has no descendants in Sanskrit, Hindi, French, Italian, or German.
