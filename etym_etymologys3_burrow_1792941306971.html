The burrow began as a hill. Its ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰerǵʰ-, high or lofty, which raised hills across half of Eurasia: Sanskrit bṛhat, lofty, Old Irish brí, hill, Slavic *bergъ, bank or mountain, German Berg, mountain, and Gothic bairgahei, hill-country. In Proto-Germanic this root settled as *bergaz, hill or mountain, whose Old English heir beorg covered every eminence from downland to grave-mound: natural hills, defensive heights where towns were founded, and the artificial mounds heaped over the dead. Modern English still keeps all three heirs side by side, sorted only by spelling: borough for the town on the height, barrow for the burial mound, and burrow for the hollow beneath the heap.

From hill to hole to hobbit-home:

  • Old English — eminences only: beorg in Beowulf and the charters means hill, cliff-spur, or mound, including burial mounds; rabbits and their holes go unrecorded in the surviving vocabulary, though the animals and their warrens were certainly known.
  • c. 1300 — the rabbit-hole appears: Middle English borow, burgh, and berw spellings record the new coupled sense: a mound and the hole dug beneath it. The metonymy is practical rather than poetic: every rabbit-hole announces itself by its spoil-heap, so heap and hollow are perceived, named, and taxed as one feature of the warren.
  • 15th–17th c. — sorting the triplets: spelling and pronunciation drift assigns borough to towns (especially parliamentary and fortified ones), barrow to grave-mounds and later to wheeled heaps, and burrow/burrough to animal earths. The verb to burrow is firmly established by the sixteenth century, applied to rabbits, foxes, and coneys in hunting manuals and husbandry books.
  • Sound shifts: the Old English eo diphthong simplifies, the velar g before rounded vowels vocalises or drops, and stress fixes on the first syllable; the -ow ending stabilises as the same unstressed vowel heard in borough and borrow. Regional spellings persist for centuries — borow, borough, burrow interchange in warreners' accounts — before Johnson's dictionary and nineteenth-century schooling fix the modern three-way split.
  • Early modern — the figurative warren opens: divines and essayists begin to burrow into consciences, scriptures, and controversies; naturalists from Ray to White of Selborne describe burrowing owls, burrowing urchins, and burrowing bees, extending the verb across classes of animal life. Sappers and miners carry it into siegecraft, where to burrow is to sap.
  • Victorian and Tolkienian — the apotheosis of snugness: children's literature makes the burrow the moral opposite of the dark wood: Mole's home in The Wind in the Willows, the rabbits' warren-warrens of Beatrix Potter, and at last the hobbit-hole, the self-consciously philological burrow whose round green door still sits in a beorg-descended bank. Tolkien, who knew the beorg triplet intimately, lets his Brandybucks recapitulate a millennium of semantics in one sentence: first they burrow like beasts, then they build like men.

So the Brandybucks' verb carries its own hill inside it. Every time a reader meets burrow, three landscapes stand overlaid: the fortified height of the borough, the solemn heap of the barrow, and the honeycombed bank of the warren — high, dead, and dug, all from one PIE height-word that learned, in one small island, to hollow itself out and move in.
