Yammering is the present participle of the verb yammer, which is to pour out a continuous, loud, repetitive stream of complaining, whining, or distressed sound — noise that goes on and on, querulous and half-formed, the voice raised not in song or eloquence but in ungovernable fuss. In The Hobbit, wolves whose coats have caught fire from the magic blaze are rolling over to put out the sparks on their backs, while those already burning run about howling and setting others alight, until their own friends chase them away and they flee down the slopes 'crying and yammering and looking for water'. The word captures the dreadful, pitiful outcry of the burning pack: a moment ago the wolves were a disciplined menace encircling the glade, and now they are reduced to a helpless, chaotic rabble, and yammering conveys the shrill, panicky, unmusical quality of their cries — not the dignified, full-throated howl of a great beast rallying its kin, but the repeated, querulous wailing of creatures in agony, breathless and unheeded. The present-participial form stresses that the sound is unbroken: it saturates the night air as the animals scatter, and the very phonetics of the word — the soft palatal y-, the slack low vowel -a-, the doubled nasal -mm-, the drawling -er-, the clipped -ing — mimic an undifferentiated, moaning drone. There is pathos here, but also a hint of the ridiculous: Tolkien's choice of yammering rather than a nobler verb keeps the beasts both pitiable and faintly absurd, creatures reduced by fire to noisy, scrambling panic. PRIMARY SENSE — CONTINUOUS COMPLAINT OR WHINE In its central modern sense, to yammer is to complain, whine, or fuss persistently and noisily: someone yammers on, yammers away, or yammers at you about a grievance. The noise is continuous, querulous, and repetitive, and the word always carries a note of irritation in the speaker who uses it — the yammerer is a nag, a moaner, or a perpetual complainer, not an eloquent advocate. The noun yammer names the sound itself: a long, undifferentiated stream of whining or complaint. SECONDARY SENSE — ANIMAL AND INANIMATE NOISE By extension the word attaches to animals and machines making a continuous, insistent, unpleasant noise: dogs and wolves yammer (whimper, wail, and yelp in a continuous jumble — exactly the burning pack in The Hobbit), and a radio, television, or engine can yammer on in the background, an endless droning that cannot be tuned into meaning. The semantic bridge is the same continuous, formless, urgent sound, whether it comes from a muzzle, a speaker, or a machine. TERTIARY SENSE — TALKING VOLUBLY AND TEDIOUSLY In American English especially, yammer has also come to mean 'to talk volubly, insistently, and at tedious length', often with the implication that nothing of value is being said: politicians yammering on the stump, pundits yammering about the news. This sense overlaps with jabber and prate but keeps the querulous, complaining colouring of the older word: the yammerer is not merely talkative but whiningly, insistently so. MODERN JARGON AND REGISTER
  • Informal register: yammering is always informal and mildly dismissive; no one yammers in formal prose, and the word is used to deflate and mock rather than to dignify.
  • Animal behaviour: in natural history and dog-training writing, 'yammer' is used of the continuous, high-pitched, distressed vocalisation of canines — distinct from the howl (long and deliberate) and the yelp (a single sharp burst) — and of the wailing of other animals in fear.
  • Sound description: in colloquial description of the modern environment, things yammer — a burglar alarm, a leaf-blower, a squealing compressor — whenever they make an unbroken, attention-grabbing, grating din.
  • Derivatives and near-homophones: 'yammery' (given to yammering) and the verb phrase 'to yammer on / away' (to talk or complain interminably) all keep the continuous-noise image.
  • Negotiation and office slang: to 'yammer' in American workplace slang is sometimes used of endless, unproductive complaining in meetings — the meeting yammers on — a modern extension of the perpetual fuss.
REGISTER, CONNOTATION AND SEMANTIC HISTORY Yammering is a word of the streets, the kennel, and the kitchen, not the pulpit or the lecture hall. Its connotation is uniformly negative but almost always mild and comic: the yammerer is a nuisance, not a menace, and even in The Hobbit, where the wolves are genuinely burning and dying, the word keeps a faint absurdity that the more solemn howling does not. Historically the word has slid steadily down the scale of dignity: its Old English ancestor gēomrian meant 'to lament, to mourn, to bemoan', a word of elegy and high grief, and over eight centuries the lamentation of a lone wanderer or a fallen lord became the querulous whine of a nagging complainer, the radio left on, and a dog that will not stop crying. The participle yammering, with its unbroken -ing, is the word at full stretch: the sound that goes on and on, half-complaint, half-whimper, and entirely impossible to ignore.
