- Antiquity — fathers rule: Greek and Roman society knows patriarchs in law and scripture, while ruling mothers exist in fact but lack the title.
- c. 1600 — a word is coined: English scholars, needing the female of patriarch, fuse Latin mater with Greek archein, and matriarch enters learned prose.
- 17th–18th c. — biblical and tribal use: commentators call Sarah and tribal queen-mothers matriarchs, dignifying ancestresses with a scholar's neologism.
- Victorian — the dowager's century: novels and memoirs enthrone great family matriarchs, from formidable widows to empire-building founding mothers.
- 20th c. — science adopts her: field biologists name elephant and whale herd-leaders matriarchs, while anthropologists debate whether true matriarchies ever ruled.
- Today — every family's crown: the word honours commanding grandmothers everywhere, half joke and half homage, and Gandalf's river-bank matriarch stands lovingly in the line.
