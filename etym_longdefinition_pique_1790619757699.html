pique centres on two intimately related verbal senses and two nominal senses that together map a psychology of the sharp point: literally to prick, figuratively to provoke. In its most familiar modern use, pique means to stimulate, excite or kindle, especially interest, curiosity or imagination, as when a strange fact piques curiosity; the image is of the mind pricked into attention rather than informed by argument. A second, older and sharper verbal sense means to wound, offend or nettle, especially by slighting pride or self-esteem, as when criticism piques someone into answering; here the prick draws blood emotionally and leaves resentment. A third, reflexive and now somewhat literary sense, to pique oneself, means to pride or plume oneself on something, as in she piqued herself on her spelling, preserving the French reflexive se piquer and implying a self-administered spur of vanity.

As a noun, pique most commonly denotes a transient but keen feeling of wounded vanity, umbrage, irritation or spite, often acted on hastily, as in to leave in a fit of pique or a pique between friends; it is lighter than enmity, sharper than annoyance, and implies injured self-love rather than moral indignation. In textile history, pique is also a stiff cotton or silk fabric with a raised corded or waffled pattern, and a fine needlepoint lace with a picoted edge, both named for their pricked or peaked surface. In cards, pique survives as the French name for spades, the suit whose pip is a pike-head, reminding us that all senses meet in the point.

The semantic bridge from physical pricking to mental provocation is direct and historically continuous: to prick flesh is to cause a sudden localised pain and alertness; to prick curiosity is to cause sudden mental alertness; to prick pride is to cause sudden moral pain that festers as resentment. Pride furnished the perfect target because early modern honour culture conceived reputation as a tender surface easily pierced. Hence to be piqued is simultaneously to be awakened and wounded, and context alone tells whether the awakening is pleasant curiosity or injured vanity, a duality already present in French piquer.

  • to excite interest or curiosity: to arouse or whet, as to pique interest; similar because it shares stimulation; differs because it is positive and intellectual rather than hostile.
  • to wound or provoke through pride: to nettle, sting or offend, as to be piqued by neglect; similar because it shares provocation; differs because it implies injured self-love and lingering ill humour.
  • to pride oneself, to plume oneself: reflexive to pique oneself on punctuality; similar because it shares self-spurring; differs because it is self-directed vanity rather than other-directed offence.
  • noun - fit of resentment, umbrage: a temporary quarrel from wounded dignity, as to depart in pique; similar because it names the resulting state; differs because it is a feeling-state rather than an act.
  • noun - pique fabric and lace, spades: corded marcella cloth, picot-edged lace, and French cartes, all pointed surfaces; similar because they preserve the literal point; differs because they are concrete technical terms with no emotion.
  • literary nuance - to pique conceptual reason: as in the source context, to goad, irritate or puncture complacent bourgeois rationality by prankish provocation; similar because it combines intellectual stimulation with hostile wounding; differs because the target is a whole structure of thought rather than a person.

In contemporary usage the curiosity sense dominates journalism and pedagogy, the resentment sense dominates narrative of social slights, the reflexive pride sense survives in formal style, and the textile and card senses survive as trade terms. Mastery of pique therefore requires holding point, prick, provocation and pride together as one pointed psychology.
