Doltish is an adjective meaning "stupid, idiotic, dull-brained", and more precisely it names a particular kind of dullness: slow, thick, stubborn, and unpolished - the sluggish head of a clod rather than the merely unintelligent one. In the verse from David Hawkes's translation of The Dream of the Red Chamber, the word is spoken in the voice of the jeering, conventional world that dismisses Bao-yu as "A harum-scarum, to all duty blind / A doltish mule, to study disinclined." Its tone is contemptuous, exasperated, and glibly conventional. The noun "mule" supplies obstinacy, for the mule is the classical animal of stubborn refusal, and "doltish" adds the accusation of slow, thick, cloddish intelligence: the boy is not merely unwilling to study but, in the world's eyes, too dull-brained to be worth teaching. The crucial literary irony is that the speaker is wrong: the whole poem is a satiric portrait painted by a hostile society, and "doltish" is the verdict of the folk who jeer; its very coarseness exposes the narrowness of a culture that can see a spiritual rebellion only as stupidity. The senses and registers of the word can be set out:
  • Core sense: slow-witted, stupid, dull - of persons, their expressions, or their behaviour; e.g. "a doltish expression", "a doltish mistake".
  • Nuance of obstinacy: because dolt descends from the same family as dull, the word often carries a hint of thick, resistant dullness - the head that cannot be taught - rather than a merely low IQ.
  • Register: blunt, rural, almost barnyard in flavour; the word is unambiguously insulting, but with a semi-humorous, country quaintness that distinguishes it from the clinical harshness of "idiotic".
  • Archaic and literary standing: doltish is now rare, surviving mainly in literary, humorous, or deliberately old-fashioned diction; it belongs to the Elizabethan-to-Victorian vocabulary of blockheads, dolts, and dullards.
  • Related formations: the noun dolt "a stupid fellow", the abstract noun doltishness, and the adverb doltishly; the family is native Germanic, with no Latin or Greek layer.
Boundary comparisons sharpen the word's peculiar force. "Stupid" is the general, neutral word for low intelligence; it is too plain and too global to capture doltish, which names a particular kind of dullness - the slow, stubborn, unpolished head rather than the merely unintelligent one. "Foolish" points at bad judgment rather than slow wits; a foolish person can be quick and brilliant yet act unwisely, so "foolish" misses the sluggishness that "doltish" insists upon. "Idiotic" is far harsher and clinically dehumanising, suggesting profound mental deficiency, and it has none of the semi-humorous, country flavour of "doltish". "Obtuse" is the intellectual's word - slow to grasp a point, often used of someone who resists understanding - but it is Latinate, polite, and can even be a compliment (deliberate obtuseness), whereas "doltish" is Germanic and unambiguously insulting. "Thickheaded" and "blockheaded" are colloquial equivalents that emphasise the skull, but they lack the archaic, poetical weight and the mule-like stubbornness that doltish carries in the verse - where the word, ironically, is the jeering world's confession of its own narrowness rather than an accurate portrait of Bao-yu.
