jigjog is a reduplicative compound of two independent English words; each root is traced in isolation, then the compounding step. ROOT 1 — jig: Frankish *gīga "a fiddle, a stringed instrument" (reconstructed West Germanic loan-word, c. 5th–6th c. CE; cf. Old Norse gígja, Middle High German gīge → modern German Geige "violin"; the word is ultimately of uncertain, possibly Byzantine-Greek, origin) → Old French gigue "a fiddle; a lively dance; also a leg or a ham" (attested 12th c. CE) → Old French giguer "to play the fiddle; to jump, leap, gambol" (attested, 12th–14th c. CE) → Anglo-Norman / Middle English gigge "a whirling toy, a top; a lively tune or dance" (attested 14th c. CE) → Middle English gigen "to hop, dance, move lightly" (attested, 14th–15th c. CE) → English jig "a lively hopping dance or tune; a light, springy, bouncing motion" (16th c. CE) ROOT 2 — jog: Middle English joggen / shoggen "to shake, jolt, nudge, push, cause to move with a heavy uneven motion" (attested c. 14th–15th c. CE; of imitative / expressive origin, no secure PIE ancestor; cf. Middle Low German schocken "to shake, rock") → English jog "to nudge or shake; to move at a slow, steady, jolting pace; (from the 20th c.) to run gently for exercise" (16th c. CE) COMPOUND STEP: English jig + jog → English jigjog "a jerky, bouncing, uneven pace or motion" (reduplicative compound, first recorded 16th c. CE) ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: Both elements are of expressive, sound-symbolic origin and neither has a secure Indo-European ancestor. The Frankish *gīga behind jig is itself a loan of uncertain ultimate origin (a Byzantine Greek word for a small stringed instrument is one hypothesis). The dance sense of jig (from Old French giguer "to gambol") and the instrument sense (from gigue "fiddle") travelled together through French and into English; modern German Geige "violin" preserves the instrument side of the family alone. The jog side is imitative Germanic with no attestation before Middle English. The compounding of the two into jigjog (16th c.) is a word-play formation, not a regular sound change.
