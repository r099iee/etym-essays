[CHAIN 1 — PRISMATIC] Stage 1: Greek πρίειν (priein) “to saw” (attested classical period; older proposals connect it to a PIE root *per-/*prei- “to strike,” but this is not securely established and no consensus exists — treat the PIE stage as unproven) → Greek πρίσμα (prisma) “something sawn; a solid with parallel, identical faces” (Euclid’s Elements, c. 300 BCE) → Latin prisma “a prism” (mathematical term, from Greek) → New Latin prismaticus “of a prism” (16th c.) → English prismatic “of or like a prism; producing the rainbow of refracted colours” (17th c.; the colour sense is fixed by Newton’s Opticks, 1704) ◄ TARGET [CHAIN 2 — MANTLE] Stage 1: origin of Latin mantellum “cloak, mantle, covering” is uncertain — no secure PIE root; a link to Etruscan or to Celtic *mantlon has been proposed but is unproven; the Latin word is attested from c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE → Old French mantel “cloak, mantle” (c. 9th–12th c.), the main Romance source of the English form (parallel Germanic route: Germanic *mantilaz “cloak” (a loan from Latin mantellum) → Old English mentel “mantle,” attested before the Norman Conquest) → Middle English mantel / mantle “a loose sleeveless cloak” (c. 12th–13th c., blended from the Old English and Old French forms) → English mantle “a loose cloak; any enveloping covering” ◄ TARGET Note on the debated etymology: because the Latin noun mantellum itself has no agreed etymology, the chain cannot be pushed back to a PIE root with any confidence; the word is a Latin isolate or a loan from a non-Indo-European source (possibly Etruscan or Celtic).
