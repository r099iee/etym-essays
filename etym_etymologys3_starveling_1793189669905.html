The strange career of starveling begins with a word that did not mean hunger at all. Old English steorfan meant simply to die — of wounds, of plague, of age, of anything — and its Germanic cousins agree: German sterben still means to die today, with no hunger in it. Somewhere in the cold north of England, death narrowed to its commonest peacetime causes, and by Middle English sterven meant above all to die of hunger or of cold. A general verb for mortality became a special verb for want — and then, with one contemptuous suffix, a noun for want's victims.

Death narrows to hunger: the semantic specialisation is easy to reconstruct from the climate. In medieval England famine and winter killed more surely than any sword, and the parish priest burying February's dead needed no finer distinction than starved — dead of want, whether the want was bread or warmth. Chaucer's age already uses sterven of hunger-death, and the northern dialects kept the cold-sense longest (‘starving with cold’, ‘clemmed’ beside ‘starved’). Southern standard English eventually assigned hunger to starve and left cold to freeze, but the old breadth survives in the King James Bible's ‘destroyed’ and ‘perished’ renderings and in Scots usage into modern times.

The -ling sneer: Old English -ling built diminutives that curdled easily into contempt: dēorling (darling) stayed sweet, but hȳrling (hireling), underling, and weakling all carry the lip-curl. Starveling, formed in the 16th century on the new hunger-verb, inherits the full curl: not merely one who starves but a characteristic starver, hunger as destiny. The suffix does double duty — smallness and scorn — so that a starveling is at once pitiable (small, wasted) and dismissible (a mere thing). Shakespeare heard both notes perfectly when he named the tailor in A Midsummer Night's Dream Starveling: the audience laughed at the proverbial leanness of tailors and winced at the hunger behind the joke.

Literary crowding: the 19th century made the word ubiquitous and almost tender. Workhouse reports, famine sketches, Dickensian waifs, Brontëan charity-school girls — starveling became the standard literary label for hunger's children, respectable enough for sermons and sentimental enough for Christmas stories. The Irish famine press of the 1840s uses it relentlessly of roadside sufferers; Mayhew's London poor are starvelings of the street trades. Each attestation widened the adjective's range — starveling portions, starveling wages, starveling hopes — until any meagreness could wear the name.

Key stages and attestations:

  • Proto-Germanic (reconstructed): *sterbaną ‘to die, perish’ — secured by Old English steorfan, Old Saxon sterban, Old High German sterban, Gothic *(ga-)staúrban; no certain cognates outside Germanic.
  • Old English (9th–11th c.): steorfan ‘to die’ generally — hunger-death is one instance among many, with no special verb for it.
  • Middle English (13th–14th c.): sterven narrows to death by hunger or cold, especially in northern texts; the cold-sense (‘starved with cold’) persists in dialect into the 20th century.
  • 16th c.: starveling first recorded as noun and adjective; Shakespeare's Starveling the tailor (c. 1595) fixes the contemptuous-pitiable tone for all time.
  • 17th–18th c.: pulpit and satire adopt it — charity sermons parade starvelings before the congregation; misers and bad masters are starveling-makers.
  • 19th c.: the humanitarian heyday — famine journalism and social novels make starveling the period's signature pity-word for underfed children and stunted labourers.
  • Modern: displaced in official use by malnourished and food-insecure, but immortal in Shakespeare, fable, and fantasy, where its instant bony silhouette still works at first sight.

Sound shifts: the Germanic consonant shift is not visibly involved here (the initial st- cluster resisted it), and the word's music comes instead from vowels: Old English steorfan (with breaking of e before r + consonant) levelled in Middle English to sterven, and the Great Vowel Shift plus dialect levelling produced modern starve with its broad northern ar. The suffix -ling is unchanged since Old English — a thousand-year-old sneer, still perfectly audible.
