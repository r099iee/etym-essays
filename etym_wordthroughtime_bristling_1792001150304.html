- Old English to Early Middle English (c. 10th – 13th c.) — Used in literal agrarian and hunting contexts to describe the coarse, stiff dorsal hair of swine (byrst) and wild boars; the emergent verb described animals raising these bristles when alarmed or combative.
- Late Middle English (c. 14th – 15th c.) — Expanded to human physiology, vividly describing human hair and beards standing upright in moments of horror, frozen terror, or mortal agony (prominent in medieval chivalric romances and Chaucerian verse).
- Early Modern English & Renaissance (16th – 17th c.) — Widely adopted in military and poetic descriptions of warfare, portraying dense infantry ranks and phalanxes of upright pikes, spears, and halberds ("a field bristling with pikes"); Shakespeare applied it both to martial arrays and fierce emotional posturing.
- 18th to 19th Century — Solidified as a psychological descriptor for individuals reacting with prickly indignation or offended dignity ("bristling at an insult"); simultaneously adopted in botanical and entomological taxonomy to classify species with rigid setae.
- 20th to 21st Century — Heavily utilized in geopolitical and military analysis ("borders bristling with heavy artillery and missile batteries") and in literary prose to evoke dangerous environments saturated with lethal, defensive capabilities.
