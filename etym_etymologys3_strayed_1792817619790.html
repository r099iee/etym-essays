Few English verbs carry their passport so openly as strayed: say it slowly and the Latin is still legible — extra (outside) fused to vagari (to wander) — wandering-outside compacted over a millennium into a single syllable's drift. The word entered the language already meaning two things at once, and it has never stopped meaning both: feet off the path and souls off the path, the lost sheep and the lost traveller always the same verb.

From Rome to the sett-side:

  • Latin foundations: classical Latin combined extra (outside, beyond — itself from ex, out of) with vagari (to roam, ramble — kin to vagus, roving, uncertain, which also gives English vague and vagabond). The compound *extravagare is Vulgar Latin, the working speech of soldiers, drovers, and pastors who needed a word for stock and persons outside their limits — the same register that gives French extravagant (wandering beyond measure) and English extravaganza.
  • Old French way-station (12th c.): estraier (to wander, roam) narrowed the Latin compound to the everyday experience of losing the road, while its past participle estraié specialised into the homeless, the forlorn, the waif — the person with no road at all. English would later borrow that participle twice over, as astray (gone astray) and as the adjective stray (a stray dog).
  • Middle English arrival (c. 1300): strayen took root with the full spread intact — cattle straying from pasture, travellers straying from the highway, Christians straying from grace. Pastoral England had daily use for the livestock sense, and the pulpit had weekly use for the moral one; the verb thrived in both weathers, and the strayed sheep of parable and sermon fixed the image of sin-as-wandering for six centuries of listeners.
  • Early modern widening (16th–18th c.): the verb spread from feet to faculties: eyes stray, hands stray, thoughts stray, discourse strays into digression. Shakespeare's age already lets arrows, hearts, and fancies stray; the Restoration lets wits and wives do it. Each extension follows the same bridge — whatever can hold a course can leave it — until straying covers every departure that is gradual rather than violent.
  • Technical strays (19th c.): the industrial age conscripted the word for whatever leaves its appointed channel: stray bullets on the battlefield, stray currents and stray capacitance in the new electrical engineering, stray shots in sport. The engineer's stray is the shepherd's stray without the shepherd — deviation measured, not mourned.
  • Tolkien's hand: the source sentence shows the verb at its subtlest — a hand straying where its owner claims it is not going. The micro-drift convicts the speaker of the intention the speech denies, and the whole menace of the scene gathers in one gentle syllable. No other near-synonym would serve: moved is neutral, went is deliberate, crept is furtive — only strayed is innocently guilty.

Sound shifts and form: the Vulgar Latin cluster extrav- collapsed in French mouths: the initial vowel dropped, x softened, and estraier emerged a full syllable lighter. Middle English borrowed it as strayen, shedding the French infinitive for the English one, and the Great Vowel Shift lifted the diphthong from medieval ah-ee to modern ay — stray now rhyming with day rather than see. The past strayed is perfectly regular, and the family it heads — astray, stray (noun and adjective), strayer — keeps the whole history of wandering-outside unpacked in plain sight.
