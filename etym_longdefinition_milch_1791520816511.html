Milch is the standard German noun for milk: the opaque white fluid secreted by the mammary glands of female mammals for the nourishment of their young, and by extension the milk of cows, goats, sheep and other domestic animals used as human food. It is a feminine noun, die Milch, with the genitive der Milch; in ordinary usage it is uncountable and has no plural, though specialist and regional texts occasionally form a plural for kinds or portions of milk. The word is one of the oldest and most stable items of the Germanic vocabulary, and its closeness to English milk — visible in the card's comparison — is a direct inheritance from Proto-Germanic, not a borrowing in either direction.

Primary sense: the substance. In its core meaning Milch denotes the mammary secretion itself, whether taken directly from the breast (Muttermilch, mother's milk) or drawn from a domestic animal (Kuhmilch, Ziegenmilch, Schafsmilch). German distinguishes the raw product (Rohmilch), the heat-treated product (pasteurisierte Milch), the fat-standardised product (Vollmilch, whole milk; Magermilch, skimmed milk), and preserved forms (Kondensmilch, condensed milk; Milchpulver, milk powder; Trockenmilch, dried milk). The word also names milk as a category of food and as a commercial commodity, so that Milchpreis (milk price), Milchquote (milk quota) and Milchwirtschaft (dairy farming) are everyday economic vocabulary.

  • Dairy products. Buttermilch (buttermilk), Sauermilch (soured milk), Dickmilch (curdled milk), Kefir and Joghurt are all defined by their relation to Milch; the fermented product preserves the base noun in its name.
  • Body and biology. Milchzahn (milk tooth, deciduous tooth), Milchgebiss (milk dentition), Milchdrüse (mammary gland), Milchfluss (lactation), Milchstau (blocked duct) and Milchschorf (cradle cap) show the word's extension to lactation and infant physiology.
  • Food and drink. Milchkaffee (coffee with milk), Milchreis (rice pudding), Milchsuppe (milk soup), Milchshake, Milchschokolade and Milchbrötchen are ordinary culinary terms.
  • Chemistry and industry. Milchzucker (lactose), Milchsäure (lactic acid), Milcheiweiß (milk protein), Milchfett (butterfat) and Milchglas (opal or milk glass) belong to food science, chemistry and manufacturing.
  • Metaphor and idiom. Milchstraße (the Milky Way), Milchbart (a downy beard, hence a greenhorn), Milchgesicht (a baby face), Milchbubi (an overgrown soft youth) and Milchmädchenrechnung (a naive, wishful calculation) show the word's drift into figurative and evaluative uses.

Secondary sense: the English adjective. English also possesses milch, pronounced /mɪltʃ/, an adjective meaning “yielding milk” and applied chiefly to animals kept for dairy: a milch cow, a milch goat. The adjective is archaic in literal farming prose but survives in the figurative milch cow, meaning a person, business or institution treated as an inexhaustible source of profit — an image that goes back to the dairy economy and carries a distinctly exploitative colour. Milch glass is opaque white glass, and the same adjective underlies the German surname Milch.

Tertiary sense: emulsion and product extension. Modern commercial German extends Milch to any milky emulsion or cosmetic lotion: Sonnenmilch (suntan lotion), Reinigungsmilch (cleansing milk), Körpermilch (body lotion). The bridge is visual and tactile: a white, opaque, smooth liquid. The same bridge explains Pflanzenmilch or Hafermilch, Sojamilch, Mandelmilch and Reismilch, plant-based drinks that imitate the appearance and culinary function of dairy. These formations are linguistically transparent but legally contested: European Union marketing rules reserve the term Milch for mammary secretions in most commercial labelling, so producers sell “Haferdrink” while consumers say “Hafermilch”. The dispute is a neat demonstration of how a word's boundaries are policed by institutions as well as by usage.

Semantic bridges and cultural weight. The literal sense “mammary secretion” generates every other sense by three routes. The first is metonymy: the product stands for the animal or the dairy economy (Milchvieh, Milchbauer). The second is metaphor based on whiteness and opacity (Milchglas, Sonnenmilch, the Milky Way). The third is biblical and literary allusion: the land “flowing with milk and honey” (ein Land, in dem Milch und Honig fließen) makes Milch a symbol of abundance, while Schiller's phrase Milch der frommen Denkart, “the milk of pious disposition”, makes it a symbol of gentle, even sentimental mildness. In German literary memory the word therefore carries both nourishment and softness, and the derived Milchgesicht can be either affectionate or contemptuous.

Register and connotation. Milch itself is neutral and domestic. Its compounds are often warm or homely (Milchkaffee, Milchreis, Abendmilch), but the derived idioms can be sharply dismissive: to call a plan a Milchmädchenrechnung is to accuse it of ignoring obvious costs, and to call a young man a Milchbart is to question his maturity. The English milch cow is more cynical still. In all these uses the original dairy reference remains faintly audible, which is why the metaphors feel concrete rather than abstract.
