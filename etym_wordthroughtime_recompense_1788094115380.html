- c. 1400 — English borrows recompensen from Old French recompenser, “to compensate, make amends”; from the start it is a word of law, courts and chivalry.
- 15th–16th centuries — noun and verb both current; the noun settles into the meaning “compensation, repayment”; the spelling wavers between “recompence” and “recompense”.
- 17th century — the King James Bible gives the word its most famous currency: “Vengeance belongeth unto me, I will recompense, saith the Lord” (Hebrews 10:30); “Say not thou, I will recompense evil” (Proverbs 20:22) — divine requital, good or ill.
- 18th–19th centuries — legal and commercial English: recompense for damages, recompense of services; the word is formal but ordinary.
- 20th century — “compensation”, “damages” and “indemnity” take over the legal and insurance registers; “recompense” retreats to literature, moral argument and formal prose.
- Modern — in Tolkien's sentence, “the recompense of our damage”, the word carries its full medieval gravity: the weighing-out of justice for widows and orphans.
