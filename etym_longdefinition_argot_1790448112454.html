argot most centrally means the specialised slang, cant, or private vocabulary used by a particular social group to mark insider identity and to exclude outsiders, historically above all the secret jargon of thieves, beggars, tramps, and other marginal underworld communities. In this core sense an argot is not merely colourful colloquial speech but a functional cryptolect: its words rename the tools of the trade, the victims, the police, punishments, and hideouts, so that accomplices can plan, warn, and boast in public without being understood. Classic French argot turned la tete into la sorbonne, the police into les cognes, and prison into la taule, exactly as English thieves cant turned a pickpocket into a file and a victim into a cull. The Wodehouse source sentence uses the word in its extended, affectionate English sense: Aunt Dahlia falls back into the hearty argot of the hunting field, meaning the bluff, horsey, slangy talk of fox-hunters, full of tally-hos, gone-aways, and cheerful abuse, which signals warmth and belonging rather than secrecy.

Beyond the underworld, modern dictionaries define argot more broadly as the jargon, lingo, or occupational slang of any closely knit group, whether criminals, soldiers, sailors, schoolboys, sportsmen, musicians, programmers, or drug users. Linguists distinguish argot from neighbouring terms with care: slang is the general informal and playful layer of a language open to all; jargon is the technical terminology needed for precision within a profession; cant is hypocritical or sanctimonious stock phraseology and, in its older sense, exactly thieves slang; while argot sits at the intersection of secrecy and solidarity, a whole parallel lexicon deliberately maintained against the outer world. In French itself the word has widened still further so that argot today covers almost any lively popular slang, from schoolyard argot scolaire to military argot militaire, whereas English keeps it slightly bookish and foreign-flavoured, often italicised in older books and chosen precisely to sound knowing.

  • Thieves cant and underworld cryptolect (original and still primary): the secret vocabulary of beggars, pickpockets, housebreakers, and prostitutes; e.g. Villon ballades in jobelin, the 1628 Histoire generale des larrons, Sue and Hugo catalogues of Parisian argot. The semantic bridge is concealment plus cohesion: renaming dangerous things protects the gang and proves membership.
  • Any close-group slang by extension: hunting-field argot, public-school argot, regimental argot, jazzmen argot, internet-gamer argot. Here secrecy weakens and identity remains: using the in-words shows you belong, as Aunt Dahlia does when she drops drawing-room English for stable-yard heartiness. Example: racing argot, football-terrace argot, newsroom argot.
  • Linguistic-technical sense: in sociolinguistics, a variety defined by deliberate lexical substitution within the same grammar, contrasted with a dialect which has its own phonology and syntax. Argot relexicalises rather than restructures: same French syntax, different nouns and verbs. Textbooks cite French verlan and English backslang as productive argot mechanisms.
  • Literary-romantic sense: after Victor Hugo chapter on argot in Les Miserables and Balzac Splendeurs et miseres des courtisanes, argot became a literary emblem of Parisian low life, energetic, metaphorical, and strangely poetic. Writers collect it, bourgeois readers thrill to it, and the word itself carries that frisson: to say argot rather than slang is already to strike a literary pose.
  • Loose journalistic sense for colourful jargon: the argot of the stock exchange, of Westminster, of Hollywood agents. Here the bridge is metaphorical: fast, opaque, slightly raffish talk likened to thieves talk. It often carries amused approval when hearty, as in Wodehouse, and disapproval when exclusionary, as in bureaucratic or teenage argot that locks parents out.
  • French contemporary nuance: in modern French argot versus argotique versus familier versus vulgaire marks a scale of acceptability in dictionaries like Larousse and Robert. An argotique label warns learners that a word is lively but not usable in formal writing, while populaire is milder. English learners meeting the French label need to grasp this cline.

In register argot is a learned, slightly showy noun in English, more formal than the thing it names. One does not normally speak argot unselfconsciously; one observes that others do, or announces with a wink that one is lapsing into it, exactly as Aunt Dahlia does. Pronunciation keeps a French trace, and spelling never anglicises to argo. Collocations are revealing: the argot of the streets, of the underworld, of the barracks, of the hunting field, thieves argot, criminal argot, professional argot, hearty argot, impenetrable argot. Verbs that go with it are to lapse into, to fall back on, to translate from, to be fluent in, to pick up argot, never simply to speak it neutrally.

For the learner the practical point is that recognising an argot matters more than memorising its ever-changing word-list. Yesterday Parisian thieves said toubib for doctor and today banlieue youths say keuf for cop; the list churns precisely to stay ahead of outsiders, police, and parents. What endures is the social function: compression, disguise, humour, and defiance. Wodehouse plays this affectionately: Aunt Dahlia hearty hunting argot of threshold, nonce, blasted, and sixes-and-sevens is not criminal at all, but it performs the same insider warmth, a momentary return from worried aunt to sporting squire, and Bertie Wooster as narrator flags it with anthropological delight.
