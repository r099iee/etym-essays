- Old English period (c. 9th–11th c.) - *rǣran* served as a robust, native Germanic verb signifying the physical setting upright of buildings, stone crosses, and timber halls, as well as the arousing of slumbering people or civic strife; perceived as an active, vigorous verb of physical elevation and kinetic awakening.
- Middle English period (c. 12th–14th c.) - *rēren* expanded from purely physical architecture to the domestic and pastoral spheres, encompassing the feeding, care, and moral rearing of children and livestock; acquired an enduring dual character as both an architectural and domestic verb of life-sustaining cultivation.
- Early Modern English period (16th–17th c.) - Stabilized into the modern spelling *rear*/*reared*; featured prominently in high literary, biblical, and dramatic registers for raising temples and noble dynasties, while simultaneously developing the specialized equestrian sense of a frightened or belligerent horse rising onto its hind hooves.
- 18th & 19th Century Classical Historiography - Widely embraced by Enlightenment historians and literary authors (e.g., David Hume, Edward Gibbon) as an evocative, dignified stylistic choice to describe early peoples erecting rustic huts and monuments; Victorian prescriptive grammarians elevated "to rear children" as the only refined, polite expression, strictly opposing it to "raising" crops.
- 20th Century Regional & Idiomatic Bifurcation - In common transatlantic speech, "raised" largely supplanted "reared" for child-rearing in American English, while British English retained "reared" in formal and dialectal use; the physical sense receded into historical prose, while the dramatic equestrian sense and the idiom "rearing its ugly head" cemented strong, vivid colloquial footholds.
- Contemporary Usage - Today *reared* survives in literary and historiographical prose for constructing ancient dwellings, in equestrian terminology, in rural agriculture (poultry and calf rearing), and in the idiom of emerging troubles, consistently conveying antiquity, vertical elevation, or steadfast cultivation.
