• Twill: Similar because serge is fundamentally a type of twill weave, characterized by its diagonal ribs; differs because twill refers to the weaving pattern itself (a broad category), while serge is a specific fabric made with that weave, often implying a particular fiber (wool) and a distinct finish.
  • Gabardine: Similar because it is also a durable, tightly woven twill fabric, frequently made of wool, and used for outerwear; differs because gabardine typically features a very fine, steep, and distinct diagonal rib with a smooth, hard, and often water-repellent finish, whereas serge can be coarser and is defined more broadly by its twill structure rather than a specific surface characteristic.
  • Worsted: Similar because many high-quality serges are produced from worsted wool, meaning the fibers are combed and spun into smooth, strong, and lustrous yarns; differs because worsted refers to the type of yarn and the process of its creation (combing long fibers), not the specific weave or fabric type like serge, though worsted yarns are commonly used to make serge.
  • Flannel: Similar because it can be made of wool and used for suits or coats, offering warmth; differs because flannel is typically a plain or twill weave fabric that is napped (brushed) on one or both sides to create a soft, fuzzy surface, lacking the distinct, hard diagonal ribs and crisp hand characteristic of serge.
  • Tweed: Similar because it is a durable woolen fabric often used for outerwear, particularly in rustic or casual styles; differs because tweed is typically a rougher, heavier fabric, often woven with multi-colored yarns in complex patterns (e.g., herringbone, houndstooth), and possesses a more textured, less smooth finish than most serges, which tend to be more uniform and refined.
