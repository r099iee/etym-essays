Earliest attested stage: Latin lacere "to entice, lure, decoy, trap" (the verb is attested chiefly in prefixed compounds and has no securely reconstructible Indo-European root; its origin is debated — see note below) → Latin ēlicere "to draw out, lure forth, elicit" (compound of ē-/ex- "out, forth" + lacere "to entice"; attested 1st c. BCE in Classical Latin, used of drawing forth water, spirits, omens, or answers) → Latin ēlicitus (past participle of ēlicere) "drawn out, coaxed forth" (Classical Latin) → Latin ēlicitāre / ēlicitum (frequentative and supine forms circulating in post-classical Latin legal and rhetorical texts, from which the English verb is formed) (Late Latin, c. 3rd–6th c. CE) → English elicit "to draw out, evoke" (borrowed into English in the mid-17th c. CE, c. 1640s, directly from Latin ēlicere / past participle ēlicitus, likely via post-classical Latin ēlicitāre) → English elicited (past tense and past participle / participial adjective) "drawn out, evoked" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The ultimate origin of Latin lacere "to entice, lure" is uncertain. It has been speculatively connected to the Indo-European root *lak- "to entice, allure, snare" (a root some scholars also link to Greek līgó-/lēg- words for "talk, tell," and to the Germanic *lahōjanan "to invite, summon"), but no consensus exists and many etymologists treat lacere as a root of its own restricted to Latin, with no securely attested PIE etymon. The compound ēlicere, its participle ēlicitus, and the English borrowing are all firmly attested. Note that the modern English adjective illicit ("unlawful") is a different word, unrelated to elicit; the resemblance is coincidental.
