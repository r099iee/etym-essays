buzzsaw in contemporary American English most basically denotes a circular power saw — a toothed steel disc spun at high speed by electricity, steam, or petrol — named for the loud sustained buzzing roar it makes while cutting. It is the mill saw of lumberyards, the table saw of workshops, and in older use any large mechanical circular saw. British English more often says buzz saw in two words or simply circular saw, while American English favours the closed compound buzzsaw.

Technically a buzzsaw cuts by continuous rotary motion rather than reciprocating push-pull. Teeth around the rim shear fibres as the disc turns at thousands of revolutions per minute, throwing sawdust and emitting a high harsh drone that carries across a yard. The word therefore encodes sound as much as shape: any saw that buzzes qualifies, but in practice it means the powered circular type, not a hand saw, hacksaw, or chainsaw, though those also buzz. Related workshop terms are table saw, rip saw, crosscut saw, buzz planer.

  • Core tool sense: a powered circular saw for ripping boards, logs, or firewood — He fed the log into the buzzsaw.
  • Simile for snoring / loud drone (your context): snoring like a buzzsaw means snoring extremely loudly, harshly, and continuously, with a grating vibratory roar that fills a room. Labouise's comrade is not merely asleep but sawing the air.
  • Figurative shredder: anything that cuts through opposition relentlessly — a buzzsaw defence, to run into a buzzsaw, a political buzzsaw — evokes being sliced apart.
  • Verb use: to buzzsaw through means to slice or advance ruthlessly — She buzzsawed through the paperwork.

In the Maupassant poaching tale the simile is comic and physical. Two vagabond poachers sleep rough by the Seine; one snores with mill-like violence. The translator's buzzsaw upgrades a generic loud snore into industrial noise, fitting for rough men who live by stealing boats, shooting game, and drinking. The register is colloquial, masculine, working-class American, slightly humorous and admiring of sheer volume.

Secondary nuances include danger and menace. Buzzsaws maim; safety manuals warn of kickback, missing guards, and deafness. Horror and pulp fiction exploit this: the screaming blade that cannot be reasoned with. Sports journalism borrows the menace without gore: to hit a buzzsaw is to meet a superior opponent who dismantles you efficiently. The semantic bridge is always acoustic plus cutting — loud continuous destruction — whether of wood, sleep, or an opposing team. The word is almost never gentle, never quiet, never precise in a delicate sense; it is brute rotary power made audible.

Additional usage notes: in American hardware stores buzzsaw appears on signage for blades, guards, and second-hand mills, while in everyday speech like a buzzsaw intensifies any grating continuity — a neighbour snores like a buzzsaw, a motorbike idles like a buzzsaw, criticism cuts like a buzzsaw. The spelling varies historically (buzz-saw, buzz saw, buzzsaw), with the closed form now dominant in the United States and the open form lingering in British reprints of American fiction. Translators favour it precisely because it compresses tool, noise, and working-class humour into one vivid image that needs no footnote.
