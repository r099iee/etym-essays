Snag is a word of the northern seas, and its biography has to begin with what it is not: it is not an Indo-European word. No Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it, and the family is confined to the North and West Germanic languages. The Proto-Germanic base *snag- meant "projecting point, knob," and its attestations are a small, coherent northern cluster: Old Norse snagi, "a projecting point; a point of land," Norwegian dialectal snag, "a sharp point," and Middle Low German snagge, "a branch; a broken tooth." The formation is probably expressive — the sn- onset and the hard stop mimic the sudden, catching jab of a point — and the OED treats the whole family as of Germanic-only origin with no demonstrated relatives elsewhere in Indo-European. The precise route by which the word entered English is likewise uncertain: both the Scandinavian and the Low German forms are plausible donors, and English took the word up around 1570 as a timber and seafaring term.

That nautical origin shaped everything that followed. To an Elizabethan seaman, a snag was a tree-trunk, stump, or large branch fixed in the bed of a river or lake, its broken top just under the surface — a hidden hazard waiting to hole a passing boat. For two centuries the word lived quietly in this watery, dialectal register, a specialist term of timbermen, rivermen, and northern sailors. It was the American frontier that carried the word to the centre of the national vocabulary. In the early nineteenth century the Mississippi and its tributaries were the highways of the young republic, and the steamboats that thronged them faced a deadly enemy: submerged logs. The great steamboat disasters of the age were snaggings — boats ripped open by sunken timber. "To snag" (1807) meant precisely to run aground on or be holed by such a stump, and the newspapers of the era are full of snagged steamers, snag-boats, and snagging expeditions sent to clear the channels.

From the river the word spread in two directions at once. On land, any sharp, jagged projecting point that caught or tore things became a snag: a stocking is snagged on a nail, a sweater snagged on a fence, a foot trips on a snag in the floor. The abstract sense followed naturally in the nineteenth century: "to hit a snag" — to meet an unexpected difficulty that halts a plan — treats a schedule or a scheme as if it were a boat steaming blindly into a hidden obstruction. A second verb sense, purely American slang of the 1890s, flipped the word around: to snag became "to seize, grab, or obtain quickly and opportunistically" — snag a bargain, snag a seat — so that the hidden catcher became the quick grabber, the noun of being caught converted into the verb of catching. Meanwhile the compound snaggletooth, "an irregular projecting tooth," attested from around 1600, kept the oldest physical sense alive in the mouths of the people.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word has gone thoroughly abstract. A snag in project management is a minor unexpected difficulty, flaw, or bug; a "snag list" itemises the small defects to be corrected before a building or project is handed over; in software, a snag is a glitch or hitch in code. Yet the physical image has never faded — the word still conjures a sharp, concealed point lying in wait. Tolkien's sentence deploys the word in its oldest and most concrete form: a snag in the cave floor, a hidden sharp projection of rock that catches the running hobbit's toes and throws him flat with his little sword under him. The submerged river-hazard of the old frontier has moved underground into the dark, still lying in wait to trip the unwary — and the word's whole northern, watery, frontier history is compressed into that single noun.
