- 14th c. (Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French from Latin *assumere*; primarily theological, referring to the reception of the Virgin Mary or Christ into heaven (The Assumption).
- 15th–16th c. (Late Middle / Early Modern English) - Expanded to taking on physical forms, garments, attitudes, postures, or royal offices ('assume the crown').
- 16th–17th c. (Renaissance Drama & Philosophy) - Developed the sense of putting on an artificial appearance, feigning an emotion, or taking airs ('assume a virtue if you have it not' - *Hamlet*).
- 18th c. (Enlightenment & Logic) - Specialized into epistemology and formal debate: taking a premise for granted or supposing something to be true without immediate empirical proof.
- 19th–21st c. (Modern Global English) - Broadened across corporate governance ('assume liabilities'), computer science ('assuming default values'), international law, and daily speech.
