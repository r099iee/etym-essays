Old Turkic *sağrı "rump of a horse, back of a beast of burden; the skin of that part" (pre-Islamic Central Asian; attested in Old Turkic texts, c. 8th century CE and earlier) → Ottoman Turkish sağrı "horse's rump; rough untanned leather from the rump of a horse or ass" (attested from the 15th century CE) → Italian zigrino "rough granular leather" - borrowed through Mediterranean trade (attested 16th century CE; the exact Romance intermediary is uncertain) → Middle French chagrin "rough granular leather (of horse, ass, shark, or ray skin) used as an abrasive or for binding" (attested 16th century CE) → French chagrin (metaphorical) "melancholy, sorrow, vexation, grief" (attested 16th-17th centuries CE; developed in French by blending the leather word with a native word for grief - see debate note) → English chagrin "distress, vexation, anxiety" (borrowed from French, attested 1656) → English chagrin "distress or embarrassment at having failed or been humiliated" - modern dominant sense → chagrin TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The Turkic origin of the leather sense is well established, but the origin of the French emotion sense is genuinely debated. The most widely accepted account holds that the two French words chagrin (leather) and chagrin (grief) merged: the rough leather's chafing was taken as a metaphor for how grief and vexation "chafe" the heart, an image made vivid by the leather's use as an abrasive. Some scholars, however, argue that the grief-word is a native French or dialectal development of uncertain source, and that the semantic link to the leather is a later folk association. The English doublet shagreen (attested 17th century) preserves the material sense that chagrin itself shed.
