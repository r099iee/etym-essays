PART ONE — HAY: that which is cut The story of the hay in haymaking goes back to the prehistoric Indo-European root *kewh₂-, a verb of cutting and striking whose most familiar English descendant is the verb to hew. The root's meaning was violent and concrete — to strike, to cut down, to fell — and the Proto-Germanic verb *hawwaną "to hew, cut down" carried it into all the northern languages, where it is visible today in English hew, German hauen "to hew, strike," and Dutch houwen. From that verb, Germanic formed a noun *hawją with a simple and revealing logic: hay is literally "that which is cut," the grass that has been felled, dried, and gathered. This is one of those ancient etymologies that shows through the surface of the word: the hay of the hayfield and the hewing of the woodman are, at bottom, the same act of cutting, and the word itself preserves that kinship after three thousand years. In Old English the noun appears as hēg and hīeg (and in the northern dialect form hēh), attested from the eighth century, and it never left the language. The Middle English hey (13th c.) was the plain daily word for the dried grass that fed the ox, the cow, and the horse through the winter; the word sat at the centre of a whole vocabulary of fodder and husbandry — hay-cocks, hay-rakes, hay-stacks, hay-lofts, hay-ricks — and of proverbs. The most famous, "make hay while the sun shines," is recorded by the sixteenth century (John Heywood's proverb collections, 1546) and preserves the crucial fact of the trade: haymaking is a race against the weather, and the opportunity, once the sun is right, must be seized at once. The proverb's structure — an imperative of opportunity — is the bridge by which the word hay left the meadow and entered the language of prudence, opportunity, and profit, so that "making hay" could come to mean exploiting a favourable moment in any field of life. By the nineteenth century hay had become, in the industrialising countryside, the type of honest seasonal labour — the subject of countless poems and paintings of the English summer — while the word itself retained its old Germanic form almost unchanged. It is a striking fact that a noun inherited directly from the Indo-European root for "cutting" still names today, in every dialect of English, exactly what it named in Old English: grass cut, dried, and stored against the winter. This continuity is part of what makes Tolkien's single word so effective: in the depths of the Misty Mountains, Bilbo's haymaking is a thought of a world where cutting, drying, and storing still governed the rhythm of the year — the world, that is, of the homely, sunlit, communal Shire. The word's sound, too, is the old sound: the Great Vowel Shift carried the Middle English long /eː/ of hey into the modern diphthong /eɪ/ of hay, but the form has otherwise travelled eleven centuries unbroken. Key points for HAY
  • hay < PIE *kewh₂- "to cut, strike", via Proto-Germanic *hawwaną "to hew" and *hawją "that which is cut".
  • Unbroken form and sense from Old English hēg (8th c.) to modern hay.
  • The proverb "make hay while the sun shines" (16th c.) anchors the opportunity-metaphor.
  • The word's pastoral associations intensified in the 19th century, making it Tolkien's image of the homely world the quest has left behind.
PART TWO — MAKE: the master verb of creation The second element, making, carries the word make, the most general English verb of creation, and its own history is a model of Germanic word-formation with a genuinely uncertain pre-Germanic past. The verb is attested across the whole Germanic world — Gothic gamaikōn "to do, make," Old Saxon makōn, Old Frisian makia, Old High German mahhōn, Old Norse maka, Old English macian — and the reconstructed Proto-Germanic form *makōną is secure. But its origin before Germanic is not. The most frequently offered theory connects it to a PIE root *mag- meaning "to knead, shape, fashion," on the analogy that early making was the plastic, hand-familiar work of kneading dough or moulding clay: the Greek words for cook, mágeiros, and for kneading-trough, mágis, are cited as cousins. The theory is attractive and widely repeated, but it remains unproven; the OED marks the further etymology as uncertain, and it is an honest guess that the word may simply have been the Germanic word for doing and working, without a deeper Indo-European pedigree. The uncertainty itself is part of the word's character: make is so basic that it seems older than the record. What is certain is the English history. Old English macian (attested from the 9th century) meant "to make, construct, form, cause, do," and it was already the master verb of creation — God macode heofonas, "God made the heavens." From the outset the word carried both the physical sense (to build, to shape) and the causal sense (to cause something to happen), a double life it retains today in pairs like "to make a table" and "to make someone angry." In Middle English, maken (12th c. onward) became simply make; the gerund making and the present participle making were formed within English on the pattern of the native -ung/-ing nouns, and by the 14th century making was a noun of its own, meaning both "the act of creating" and "a composition, a work" (hence a poet's "making"). The suffix -ing is itself a native Germanic inheritance, so the whole compound haymaking is built of purely English materials: a Germanic root of cutting joined to a Germanic verb of doing. The compound haymaking joins the two words in the order that mirrors the phrase to make hay, and it is attested in English from the 16th century, exactly when the proverb was in circulation. Such object + verbal-noun compounds (haymaking, housekeeping, bookkeeping, lacemaking) are a characteristic English formation: the object of the verb is placed first and the action follows, producing a single word for a whole trade or seasonal operation. Haymaking is thus a precisely English word whose internal grammar still tells you, without any Latin or French help, exactly what it names: the making of hay. Its cultural career since the 16th century has run from plain calendar-chore to pastoral emblem, and in The Hobbit the compound's transparency is the source of its power — the reader needs no glossary to see the sunlit fields behind the word. Key points for MAKE
  • make is the Germanic master-verb *makōną, attested from Gothic to Old English.
  • The PIE connection (*mag- "to knead, shape") is debated and uncertain; the OED marks it as unproven.
  • Old English macian (9th c.) passed through Middle English maken to modern make; making is the native gerund.
  • haymaking is a native object + verbal-noun compound (16th c.), built on the phrase "to make hay."
