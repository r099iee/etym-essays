Audacious means daring in a way that is strikingly bold, fearless, and often surprising or even shameless — willing to take risks that others would call rash, presumptuous, or magnificent. The word names a readiness to venture beyond prudence, but it always carries a double emotional valence: the same act can be admired as heroic nerve or condemned as insolent cheek, and frequently it is both at once. Unlike brave or courageous, which are morally approving and focus on facing fear, audacious focuses on the audacity of the venture itself — the sheer nerve of attempting something that ordinary good sense would not attempt. In the toast from The Hobbit, Gandalf calls Bilbo "this most excellent and audacious hobbit." The adjective is ceremonial flattery with a sting of truth: recruiting a comfortable, respectable bachelor as burglar for an expedition against a dragon is itself an audacious scheme, and the dwarves project that quality onto Bilbo before he has shown it. The tone is elevated, slightly Latinate praise, comic when set against Bilbo's later shuddering relapse into respectability. The word marks boldness of venture, not mere loudness of manner; it is exactly the mix of admiration and conspiracy that the speech needs. Core senses and nuances.
  • Daring, bold, fearless: the primary sense — willing to take surprising risks, as in "an audacious plan" or "an audacious climb without safety ropes." Here the word is admiring: enterprise and nerve.
  • Impudent, shameless, presumptuous: the censuring sense — nerve that oversteps manners, as in "an audacious lie" or "an audacious claim." The risk is social and moral rather than physical.
  • Brazenly original, groundbreaking: the modern prestige sense — in business and technology, an "audacious goal" or "audacious moonshot" is one so bold it seems impossible, and is therefore admired as visionary ambition.
  • Boldly (and successfully) unconventional in art or taste: critics praise "audacious" designs, films, or experiments that break the expected frame and get away with it.
Historical and semantic bridges among the senses. The core image is Latin audēre "to dare," built on an older sense of eagerness and desire: to dare is to be keen enough to venture. From that root Latin audāx already swung between praise (heroic daring) and blame (rashness, insolence), and audācia named boldness as either enterprise or impudence depending on the moral weather of the sentence. English borrowed the pair in the sixteenth century and has kept both poles ever since. The modern business sense ("audacious goal") simply re-admires the daring pole, but now in the language of innovation and ambition rather than of battle or exploration. Morphology and usage notes. Audacious is the adjective; the noun is audacity; the adverb is audaciously. The word is more formal and Latinate than native bold, which is why it suits ceremonial speech and elevated narration. It is unrelated to audio, audible, and audience, which descend from Latin audīre "to hear" — the shared aud- opening is a coincidence of spelling, not of ancestry.
