- [14th century: the verb is born] - English turns the old noun sheath into the verb sheathe, 'to put a blade into its case'; sheathed names both the act and the resulting state of being covered and concealed.
- [Medieval to early modern: the martial sense dominates] - 'Sheathe the sword' is the soldier's and knight's gesture of ending combat; the word carries chivalric weight, and a sheathed blade means peace, control, and stored menace.
- [17th-19th century: beyond weapons] - The word generalises to any close-fitting protective covering: cables are sheathed in rubber, hillsides sheathed in ice, a wire sheathed in metal; the technical sense takes hold.
- [Contemporary sense] - Largely technical ('sheathed cable') while the literary and martial flavour survives in the contrast with unsheathed; a sheathed sword stays dormant and harmless until the one who draws it again acts.
