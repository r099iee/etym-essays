The biography of the word gown is a study in how a garment word can rise and fall through the social scale while keeping its identity intact. Unlike most English clothing words, gown has no Indo-European pedigree to trace. It surfaces first, as far as scholarship can tell, in the Mediterranean world as the name of a coarse garment of skin or fur. Byzantine Greek γούνα (goúna) meant “a fur, a skin, a coarse garment,” the protective outer layer of a shepherd, a peasant, or a monk; and the word behind that Greek form is likely a Balkan or Illyrian substrate word — a relic of a language spoken before Greek arrived, or of a tongue whose records have not come down to us. Some scholars have proposed a Celtic source for the family, and others have tried to force the word under the Indo-European root *wes-, “to clothe,” which gave us wear and vestis; but none of these connections is secure, and the standard dictionaries record the word as of unknown origin. What is certain is the medieval trail: from Byzantine Greek the word passed into Late Latin as gunna, “a leather or fur garment,” attested in glossaries from the sixth century, and from Late Latin into the Romance languages of the medieval world. The medieval journey of the word is instructive for what it tells us about both cloth and language. The Late Latin gunna travelled with the garment itself through the early medieval world. In Old French it became goune or gonne, “a loose outer garment, a robe,” attested from the eleventh and twelfth centuries, and from that single French form several languages drew their own clothing words: English took gown; Italian took gonna, which still means “skirt”; Catalan developed gonella and Old Spanish gonela, loose tunics of the same family. In French itself the word was later displaced by robe, borrowed from the Germanic Frankish *rauba, “booty, spoils, robe,” so that modern French says une robe where medieval French would have said une goune. English, however, kept the older word, receiving it through Anglo-Norman in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries: goune appears in English records of the period, and by c. 1300 Middle English had the settled form goune/gowne, “a long loose outer garment.” In English the word’s semantic history is a slow climb up the social ladder. At its first appearance it named a simple loose outer garment worn over other clothes — the practical robe of ordinary life. But because the garment was worn long and full, it lent itself naturally to the dress of dignity. By the later Middle Ages the gown was already the standard outer garment of the respectable: men of substance wore gowns in town, lawyers and clerics wore gowns of office, and the word began to accumulate its professional and ceremonial associations. The academic gown, the judge’s gown, and the clerical gown all descend from this period, and the phrase town and gown — the lay community versus the university — testifies to how deeply the gown became the emblem of learning. The word’s career was also shaped by sumptuary and social codes: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, gowns of different lengths, fabrics, and trimmings were legally regulated by rank in some European states, so that the garment literally encoded status. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries completed the transformation. On the one hand, the gown remained the dress of office and ceremony — the graduation gown, the judge’s gown, the mayor’s gown. On the other hand, it became the supreme formal garment of women’s fashion: the evening gown, the ball gown, and above all the wedding gown, the most ceremonial dress in a woman’s life. This is the register in which Victorian literature and the fashion plates use the word, and it is the register John Ruskin plays upon in the Preface to Modern Painters when he speaks of the “vain acclamation” poured out to exalt the dead. The twentieth century then democratised the word in an unexpected direction: the hospital gown, the surgical gown, and the patient gown — utilitarian, functional, often backless — extended the word to the most impersonal garment in existence. The same word that names the bride’s masterpiece also names the flimsy gown of the hospital ward; both are equally gowns, and the semantic range of the word is the whole social ladder compressed into a single garment. Culturally, gown has served as shorthand for institutions and their authority. “Town and gown” opposes the university to the town; “the gown” stood for the learned or legal professions; a gownsman was a civilian or scholar set against the uniformed soldier. In British academic life the gown remains a working garment of ceremony, worn at matriculation, graduation, and official university occasions; in the law courts barristers and judges still robe in gowns. The word has also generated a rich compound vocabulary — dressing gown, nightgown, morning gown, evening gown, ball gown, wedding gown, hospital gown, academic gown, surgical gown — each compound pinning the word to a particular social or domestic context. What unites them all is the core idea: a long, loose, enveloping outer garment, worn over other clothing, which in different contexts means comfort, ceremony, office, or utility. And in the passage from The Story of the Stone, the word shows its subtlest power: a worn, rose-coloured, flower-sprigged gown, the very picture of relaxed domestic comfort, is still a gown of silk and embroidery — proof that in a house of great wealth even the informal body is dressed in beauty.
