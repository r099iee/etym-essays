NOTE ON ORIGIN: the ultimate ancestry of creek is probable-but-unproven. Its documented home is the coastal vocabulary of the North and Channel seas, and the PIE link below (Watkins) is plausible but not certain — it is explicitly flagged, not asserted.\n\n(SPECULATIVE ULTIMATE BASE — flagged, not asserted) PIE *ger- (Watkins' *ger-, "to turn, bend, wind"; c. 4000+ BCE) — "to turn, bend, wind"; probable ancestor of the Greek "circle" words and, very probably, of the Germanic "hook, bend, nook" words — but the connection to creek is probable-not-proven.\n\nSTAGE 2 (Germanic channel) — Old Norse kriki (Viking Age, c. 8th-11th c. CE) — "a nook, corner, recess"; from the same base as Old Norse krókr, "hook, bend," which gave English crook and crooked.\n\nSTAGE 3 (alternative Romance channel) — Old French crique (12th c.) — "a bay, cove, inlet"; some scholars derive crique from a pre-Latin Mediterranean substrate word; the English word may partly reflect this form through Anglo-French crique.\n\nSTAGE 4 — Middle English creke (c. 13th-14th c.) — "a fissure, crack, narrow opening in the coast"; the "bend/nook in the land" image applied to where the sea reaches inward.\n\nSTAGE 5 — English creek (14th c. onward) — coastal sense "a narrow inlet of the sea," preserved in British English (Lewis's sense here); from the 17th c. in America the word shifts to "a small fresh-water stream or tributary" (dialectal crick), and it also becomes the English ethnonym of the Muscogee people, the Creek, from their riverside settlements.\n\nSTAGE 6 — English CREEK — the word as used on this card, in both its British tidal-inlet sense and its American stream sense.\n\nNOTE ON THE DEBATE: whether the English word descends chiefly from Old Norse kriki, from Old French crique, or from a convergence of both is genuinely disputed, and the OED leaves the balance open; the ultimate link to PIE *ger- is probable-not-proven. Both uncertainties are stated here rather than papered over.
