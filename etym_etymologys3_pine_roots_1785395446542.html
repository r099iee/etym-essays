TWO BIOGRAPHIES JOINED BY A HYPHEN

Pine-roots is a transparent English compound, and its biography is really two biographies joined by a hyphen: one for pine, the tree, and one for roots, the organ. Neither element changed the way it looks in English; the compound simply sets two ancient words side by side and points them at the mountainside.

ESSAY 1: PINE

The first element, pine, descends from Old English pīn, which was itself an early continental borrowing of Latin pīnus "pine-tree". Old English took the word from Latin — Roman traders and missionaries brought the name for a conifer the Germanic peoples already knew — and Middle English pīn was later reinforced by Old French pin, so that two streams, an early Germanic-Latin loan and a later Romance loan, flowed together and settled into the single modern form pine. Behind Latin pīnus lies the Italic noun *pīnos, and behind that the traditional Proto-Indo-European root *peiH- "to be fat, to swell": the pine is conceived as the resinous, sap-swollen tree, its trunk bloated with pitch. The connection is elegant — the swollen, sticky conifer — but it is not certain: de Vaan, in his Etymological Dictionary of Latin, treats the PIE etymon as doubtful and allows that pīnus may be a pre-Indo-European Mediterranean substrate word for the tree.

Whatever its ultimate source, the word's descendants — Italian pino, Spanish pino, Portuguese pinho, French pin — all cluster around the same conifer, and English pine has never meant anything but the tree. A false friend must be set aside: the verb and adjective to pine (for), "to yearn, waste away", comes from a different Latin word, pīna "punishment, torment, pain", and is a pure homonym; it has nothing to do with the tree's name, though the two have been jostling in English since the Middle Ages.

Latin built a small family around pīnus — pīnea "pine-nut, pine-cone" (whence English pineal, the gland named for its pine-cone shape), pīnētum "a pine-grove" (whence the learned borrowing pinetum) — and English topped that with compounds of its own: pine-cone, pine-needle, pine-forest, and the pine-roots of this card. The sound history is uneventful in English: the long vowel of Old English pīn remained through the Great Vowel Shift, giving the modern diphthong /aɪ/, exactly as in line and wine.

ESSAY 2: ROOTS

The second element, roots, has a far older and purely Germanic story. It reaches back to the Indo-European root *wréh₂d- "root, branch, twig", which produced Proto-Germanic *wrōt- "root". The Germanic family long kept the initial w-: Gothic waúrts, Old High German wurz (modern German Wurzel), and Old English wyrt, which survives in English only as the fossilised plant-name suffix -wort (liverwort, St John's-wort, pennywort). But in North Germanic the cluster was simplified: before r the w- was lost, and so Old Norse had rót.

It is precisely this Norse form that entered English in the twelfth century as rote — a Scandinavian loan like sky and window — and gradually displaced the native wyrt, so that modern English root is Norse-descended, not directly native. The same PIE root went south as well: Latin rādīx "root" gives English radish, radical "going to the root" and eradicate "to root out", while Greek rhíza gives rhizome and mycorrhiza; Welsh gwraidd "root" continues it in Celtic.

From its plain literal sense — the underground organ — English root has sprouted a vast metaphoric and technical garden:

  • Figurative: the root of a problem, to get to the root of things, one's roots, root-and-branch reform.
  • Technical: square root, cube root, root canal (dentistry), root beer (brewed from sassafras and sarsaparilla roots).
  • Verbal: to root out, to root around (searching); the unrelated to root for "to cheer" has a separate history.

COMPOUND AND CULTURAL LIFE

In pine-roots the two biographies join. The compound is endocentric — the roots of the pine — and its plural is the telling form: English speakers do not usually picture one root, but the whole exposed mat of cables that laces a thin-soiled conifer slope. This is the sense the word carries in topographical and nature-writing prose and in fiction such as The Hobbit, where the crashing stones of the mountainslide spin "among the bracken and the pine-roots". Practically, pine roots have real life: resinous and slow to rot, they burn well even when green, so pine-root knots were kindling and torchwood in rural craft; the same resin gives the roots their toughness and their distinctive smell. The compound itself has never developed transferred senses — it is a plain, literal word — but its second element brings with it the whole buried wealth of "root", so that a reader sees in pine-roots both the concrete cables and, faintly, the idea of things taking hold.

SUMMARY

Pine descends from Latin pīnus through Old English and Old French — a Mediterranean word for a northern tree, with a disputed PIE past and a thriving family of compounds; roots descends from the Indo-European *wréh₂d- through a lost w- and a Norse loan, a word whose literal organ-sense became one of English's most productive metaphors. The hyphen holds them together as one image of an exposed, root-laced mountainside.
