- 16th c. - recess (from Latin recessus, 'a going back') enters English first meaning a withdrawal or retiring, and already an inner corner or alcove cut back from a surrounding surface.
- 16th–17th c. - the architectural sense solidifies: a recess is a niche or set-back in a wall, and the plural recesses comes to mean the deepest, most hidden parts of a structure - the places a surface 'goes back' into.
- 17th–18th c. - the word turns inward: poets and divines speak of 'the recesses of the heart or mind', the secret interior where thoughts and motives lodge; the legal 'recess' (an adjournment) splits off as a separate homograph.
- 18th–19th c. - in Gothic and Romantic prose, recesses names the shadowy deeps of caves, forests, and ruined buildings; the plural multiplies menace, each recess a void into which eye and light cannot fully reach.
- 20th–21st c. - still the word for the innermost, least accessible parts of a place or a psyche; it keeps a faintly literary, awe-filled register, while in architecture it remains the technical term for a wall set-back where anything might be hidden.
