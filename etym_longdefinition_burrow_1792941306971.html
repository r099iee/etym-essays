Burrow is both the snug hole and the act of making it: as a noun, a tunnel or chamber dug by an animal for shelter and breeding — a rabbit's warren, a badger's sett, a fox's earth — and by beloved extension a hobbit-hole, all round doors and pantries; as a verb, to dig such a refuge, to hide oneself away within it, or to press questingly into anything as a mole presses into soil. In the source sentence it carries the settlement verb sense: the Brandybucks and their dependants began to burrow — to dig hobbit-holes into the banks of Buckland — and later to build, all round about, the digging phase of colonisation preceding the building phase, tunnels first and timber houses after.

Core meaning in detail: The animal noun is the older heart: a burrow is an excavated dwelling with one or more entrances, side chambers for nesting and food, and spoil heaped outside the mouth — the rabbit warren with its runs and stop-holes, the badger sett passed down generations, the puffin burrow sloping into cliff turf. The verb follows the claws: to burrow is to excavate by scraping and shoving, to hollow a home out of resistance, and hence to lodge oneself deep — burrowed under the blankets, burrowed into the archives. Hobbit lore, which Tolkien built as a philologist, restores the word's deepest layer: burrow, borough, and barrow are triplets, all from the hill-word, so to burrow is etymologically to make oneself a hill from inside, throwing up the mound that names the act.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Animal tunnel-dwelling (primary noun sense, from c. 1300): the rabbit burrow, fox burrow, worm burrow. The bridge is the mound: Middle English borow meant both hillock and the hole beneath it, since diggers heap the hill they hollow, and the hill-word came to name the hollow by the most natural of metonymies.
  • To dig or live in such a hole (primary verb sense; the source sense): rabbits burrow, hobbits burrow, children burrow under duvets. The bridge is conversion of dwelling into doing: the noun's shelter becomes the verb's labour, and Tolkien's Brandybucks enact it as history, digging first and building after.
  • To hide, nestle, or ensconce oneself (figurative): she burrowed into his shoulder, burrowed away in a country cottage. The bridge is the animal's motive carried to humans: seeking warmth, safety, and snugness by pressing into something soft and enclosing.
  • To investigate searchingly, to dig into a subject (figurative, 17th c. onward): to burrow through parish registers, burrowing into the evidence. The bridge runs from snout to scholar: patient, head-down, spoil-throwing persistence against resistance, whether the resistance is clay or uncatalogued boxes.
  • Medical and zoological borrowings: the itch-mite's burrow under the skin, the burrowing owl of the prairies, burrowing clams and urchins. The bridge is strict zoology: any organism that excavates living space in a substrate inhabits a burrow, from mite to mollusc to mammal.
  • Military and mining extension: to burrow under defences, burrowing journals of siegecraft, the burrowing of sappers. The bridge is the tunnel weaponised: the rabbit's refuge becomes the soldier's sap and mine, shelter turned to approach.

Register and connotation: burrow is the warmest of all digging-words — never the violence of gouge or the industry of excavate, but cosy, whiskered, purposeful homemaking. It smells of earth, straw, and lamplight round a bend in the tunnel. Even its scholarly sense keeps the affection: the researcher who burrows is diligent and loving, nose down, tail up, coming out with something worth keeping. Tolkien chose it for the Brandybucks precisely because no other verb says industrious comfort quite so completely: they did not merely settle or colonise, they burrowed, and Buckland still feels like the spoil-heap of that happy labour.
