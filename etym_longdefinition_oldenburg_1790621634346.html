Oldenburg in your sentence is first of all a proper name: Claes Oldenburg (1929–2022), the Swedish-American Pop artist famous for colossal soft sculptures of everyday objects and for the ‘soft letters’ and ‘alphabet’ works that treat language as physical information. The sentence pairs him with Jasper Johns and his alphabet paintings as artists trying to depict information itself. As artist: Claes Thure Oldenburg, born Stockholm, raised Chicago, central figure of 1960s New York Pop alongside Warhol, Lichtenstein, Rosenquist, and Johns. Famous for The Store (1961), happenings, and from 1962 for giant vinyl Floor Burger, Floor Cake, Soft Toilet, Soft Pay-Telephone, later with Coosje van Bruggen for monumental public sculptures — Clothespin (Philadelphia), Spoonbridge and Cherry (Minneapolis), Spring, Mistral. The ‘soft letters’ — floppy, stuffed canvas alphabet letters that slump, lean, and collapse — mock monumentality and make language tactile, erotic, and comic. They depict information not as transparent message but as heavy, sagging, bodily thing. That is exactly the book's theme: prose and letters as stuff, not invisible window.

As place: Oldenburg is a historic city in Lower Saxony, north-west Germany (c. 170,000 inhabitants), former capital of the Grand Duchy and Free State of Oldenburg, seat of the House of Oldenburg that gave kings to Denmark, Norway, Greece, Russia, and the United Kingdom (through Prince Philip's line). Name means old fortified town. The city is known for its Renaissance-Baroque Schloss, university (Carl von Ossietzky Universität), and marshland landscape between Weser and Ems.

  • Art-historical sense: Oldenburg = Pop Art's sculptor of the mundane, soft sculpture, happenings, monumental everyday objects, play versus purpose in art. Connotation: witty, democratic, anti-heroic, tactile. To say “When Oldenburg created…” invokes an entire 1960s moment when high art embraced comics, advertising, and letters.
  • Toponymic sense: Oldenburg = city and former state in Oldenburger Land. Connotation: north German, Hanseatic sobriety, Protestant, equestrian (Oldenburger horses), dynastic. Used in addresses, history, travel.
  • Dynastic / surname sense: von Oldenburg / Oldenburg = member or descendant of the comital house first attested 1108 with Count Egilmar. Connotation: aristocratic, pan-European royalty (Oldenburg dynasty ruled Denmark 1448–1863). Many emigrants carried the surname to America and Sweden, including Claes's family.
  • Linguistic sense: Oldenburg illustrates Germanic place-name formation: old + burg (old fortress), cognate with English old borough, Oldbury. Helps learners decode countless German city names: Hamburg, Bamberg, Regensburg, Marburg.
  • Cultural-metaphorical sense in context: Oldenburg and Johns depicting information — letters as image, text as texture — prefigure digital typography, concrete poetry, and information art. Their alphabet works argue that information always has a material style.

Pronunciation: Anglicized /ˈəʊldənbɜːɡ/ with English old + reduced -en- + hard -burg as in burg, not French -bourg. German pronunciation is /ˈɔldənbʊrk/ with short o, clear e, and final -rk. The artist himself, American-naturalized, accepted the American form. Plural and possessive are regular: Oldenburgs, Oldenburg's. Adjective for the city is Oldenburgish or Oldenburger (Oldenburger Pferd).
