The name is a Greek compound of two independent Indo-European roots, so the derivation must be given for each element in isolation and then fused. CHAIN 1 — the "ox" element: PIE *gʷōus "cow, ox" (the ancestral Indo-European word for cattle; attested across the family: Sanskrit gáuḥ, Latin bōs, Old English cū, German Kuh, Old Norse kýr) → Proto-Hellenic *gʷous "cow" → Ancient Greek boûs (βοῦς) "ox, cow" → the first member of the compound Bósporos. CHAIN 2 — the "ford" element: PIE *per- "to cross, pass through" (the root of crossing; also in Latin portus "harbour", porta "gate", English fare, ferry, ford, and Sanskrit pāra- "the far bank") → Proto-Hellenic *poros "crossing" → Ancient Greek póros (πόρος) "ford, passage, crossing, strait, way" → the second member of the compound Bósporos. FUSION: Ancient Greek Bósporos (Βόσπορος) "ox-ford, cow-passage", the name of the strait at Istanbul (and, from the earliest references, also of the Cimmerian Bosporus, the Kerch Strait), so called because the narrows were imagined shallow enough for cattle to cross and because legend said the heifer Io swam them → Latin Bosporus "Bosporus", the classical form used by Cicero, Pliny the Elder, and Ovid → Medieval Latin Bosphorus, a pseudo-learned respelling that introduced -ph- under the mistaken belief that the second element was Greek phérein "to carry" (the true element is póros) → English Bosphorus (traditional spelling; also Bosporus), the strait between Europe and Asia at Istanbul. Note on the debate: the spelling Bosphorus rests on a false medieval etymology and classical philology restores Bosporus; both forms remain standard, Bosphorus in traditional English usage and Bosporus in exact scholarship. The mythological connection to Io is an explanatory legend attached to the transparent compound, not the source of the word itself.
