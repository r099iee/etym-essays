1. Introduction and Morphological Architecture. The word frankincense represents one of the most culturally evocative compound nouns in the English lexicon. Morphologically, it is a compound borrowed into Middle English from Old French franc encens during the Anglo-Norman period (late 12th to early 13th century). Rather than denoting a single native Germanic root, the word is an exquisite synthesis of two distinct linguistic lineages: a Germanic ethnonym-turned-adjective (frank) modifying an Italic verb-derived substantive (incense). To understand the historical trajectory of frankincense, each constitutive element must be examined in complete historical and phonetic isolation before analysing their medieval fusion and subsequent literary evolution.

2. Evolution of Element 1: FRANK (From Germanic Spear to Nobility and Purity).

  • Proto-Germanic Roots (*frankô): The adjective frank originates in Proto-Germanic *frankô, which originally denoted a weapon—specifically a light javelin or throwing spear (cognate with Old English franca "spear, lance, javelin" and Old Norse frakka). The Germanic tribal confederation that formed along the lower and middle Rhine in the 3rd century CE adopted this weapon as their emblem and ethnonym, calling themselves the Franks ("the spear-men" or "the fierce ones").
  • Socio-Political Semantic Shift in Post-Roman Gaul: Following the collapse of the Western Roman Empire and Clovis I’s establishment of the Merovingian Frankish Kingdom (c. 500 CE), the Franks constituted the ruling military aristocracy of Gaul. While the conquered Gallo-Roman populace was subject to heavy agricultural taxation, servitude, and Roman fiscal law, Frankish warriors were sovereign, exempt from tribute, and possessed full civil rights. Consequently, in Late and Medieval Latin, the ethnic adjective francus underwent a profound sociolinguistic shift: it ceased to be purely tribal and came to signify "free, unbound, exempt from tribute or servitude".
  • Old French Semantic Expansion (franc): By the 11th and 12th centuries, Old French franc had evolved far beyond mere legal freedom. Within the feudal code of chivalry, a person who was franc was "noble, generous, open-hearted, sincere, and pure of character." When applied to physical commodities, franc signified "pure, unadulterated, genuine, of the highest quality" (as in or franc, "pure gold"). It is this exact qualitative sense—"pure, high-grade, noble"—that was selected when medieval merchants and clerics described the supreme Arabian resin.

3. Evolution of Element 2: INCENSE (From Indo-European Fire to Sacred Smoke).

  • Proto-Indo-European (*kand-): The noun incense traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *kand- or *(s)kand-, signifying "to shine, glow, be bright or white, burn." This highly productive root generated Sanskrit candana ("sandalwood", literally "the shining aromatic wood"), Greek kandaros ("charcoal"), and a vast Latin family including candere ("to glow white with heat"), candidus ("shining white, candid"), and candela ("candle").
  • Latin Prefixation and Nominalisation (incendere → incensum): In Proto-Italic and early Latin, the prepositional prefix in- ("in, into, upon") was joined to the root verb candere, yielding the compound verb incendere ("to set on fire, kindle, inflame, scorch"). From its perfect passive participle incensus, the neuter substantive incensum was formed, literally meaning "that which is set on fire, that which is kindled". In Late Latin and the Vulgate Bible, incensum became the specialised term for the fragrant gums and spices burned as ritual burnt-offerings upon the altar (calquing Greek thymiama and Hebrew qəṭōreṯ).
  • Gallo-Romance and Anglo-Norman Transmission (encens): In the transition from Latin to Old French, regular phonetic reduction softened the word into encens (12th century), which was subsequently adopted into Middle English as encens and later re-latinised in spelling as incense.

4. Compounding, Trade Routes, and Middle English Adoption.

  • The Medieval Commercial Distinction: Throughout the Middle Ages, local European incenses were frequently compounded from cheaper native conifers (spruce, pine resin, juniper) or adulterated with pitch and sawdust. When genuine gum resin harvested from the arid cliffs of Oman, Yemen, and the Somali coast was imported via Venetian, Genoese, and Levantine trade routes, merchants designated it as franc encens—the "true, pure, noble incense"—to guarantee its authenticity and superior aromatic potency.
  • Attestation in Middle English Literature: The compound entered Middle English in the late 13th and 14th centuries, appearing as frankencens, fraunkencens, and frankincense. Early appearances occur in the Cursor Mundi (c. 1300), Wycliffe’s Bible translations (1382), and Geoffrey Chaucer’s works. In The Knight's Tale and religious lyrics, Chaucer invokes the burning of frankincense in temple rites, illustrating that the term had achieved complete lexical integration as the designated English name for Boswellia resin.

5. Scriptural, Literary, and Modern Trajectory.

  • The Biblical Canon: The King James Bible of 1611 firmly canonised frankincense across both testaments—from the priestly ordinances of Exodus and Leviticus to the Song of Solomon ("Who is this that cometh out of the wilderness... perfumed with myrrh and frankincense?") and Matthew’s Nativity narrative (2:11). The tripartite association of gold, frankincense, and myrrh permanently cemented the word’s sacred resonance in the English-speaking imagination.
  • Aestheticism and Romantic Prose: In the 19th century, writers of the Decadent and Aesthetic movements, notably Walter Pater and Oscar Wilde, appropriated liturgical vocabulary to express intense sensory and romantic devotion. When Wilde writes in The Nightingale and the Rose that the breath of Love is "like frankincense," he relies directly on the centuries-old cultural memory of the word—evoking an aroma that is simultaneously sacrificial, heavenly, and passionately pure.
  • Phonetic Stability and Contemporary Standing: Phonetically, the compound underwent standard English vowel reduction in its unstressed syllables, shifting from Middle English /ˌfraŋk.ɛnˈsɛns/ to modern Received Pronunciation /ˈfræŋ.kɪn.sɛns/. Today, while synthetic fragrances dominate mass markets, frankincense retains an unblemished aura of timeless luxury, spiritual elevation, and natural therapeutic excellence across pharmacology, perfumery, and literature.
