Buoyantly is built in three transparent steps - buoy plus -ant plus -ly - but each step has its own sea history, and the adverb's modern cheerfulness still smells of salt water. Its journey runs from fetters to floats to feelings, and then to financial pages, before returning to Bertie Wooster's drawing-room voice.

Buoy: The noun buoy appears in English in the later Middle Ages for an anchored floating mark guiding ships clear of shoals. Immediate sources are Middle Dutch boeye and Middle French buie, buee, themselves usually referred to Latin boia a leather collar, fetter or yoke for prisoners, and by extension a floating log or cork marking nets. The semantic link is binding and floating: a fetter of wood that both holds and floats. An alternative Germanic theory derives it from a beacon-mark, but Dutch-French transmission into naval English is undisputed. Early spellings boy, buoy show the oi diphthong settling; pronunciation shifted from boy to bwoy to modern buoy. The object was vital in an age of coastal navigation before lighthouses were dense: to miss the buoy was to hit the bank.

Buoyant: The adjective buoyant is first recorded in the late 16th century, probably modelled on French bouyant floating, present participle of bouyer to float. Initially it was purely physical in natural philosophy: a buoyant body is one apt to float, that will not sink, that rises in fluid. Seventeenth-century writers on hydrostatics use it technically alongside specific gravity. By the 18th century the moral transfer is complete: spirits, hopes, minds and tempers are called buoyant when they rise under pressure as wood rises in water. The image proved irresistible to economists: by the early 19th century markets, prices, funds and trade are buoyant when they stay high or rebound after a dip, a usage that still dominates financial journalism.

  • Nautical technique (medieval-early modern): buoy as harbour furniture, charted and legislated; neutral technical term among pilots and admiralties.
  • Natural philosophy (16th-17th c.): buoyant as property of bodies in fluid, discussed by Galileo, Boyle and Newton; precise and admiring of nature's lift.
  • Moral and literary metaphor (18th c.): buoyant spirits, buoyant hope, buoyant youth - Addison, Johnson and Romantic poets use it for resilience; fully positive, associating lightness with virtue.
  • Political economy (19th c.): buoyant revenue, buoyant market - the Exchequer and City adopt it for fiscal health; technical again but with cheerful overtone.
  • Manner adverb buoyantly (19th-20th c.): to speak, move, carry on buoyantly - ordinary formation with -ly, but Wodehouse and contemporaries fix it as the sound of Edwardian bounce meeting modern worry.

Phonetically the family shows the Great Vowel Shift and French influence: buoy with its ui spelling for the wi sound, buoyant with stress on the first syllable, buoyantly with regular -ly reduction to a light syllable. Semantically the drift is from concrete support (that which holds ships up) to abstract support (that which holds hearts up), with economics as the bridge that kept the word daily before the public. Socio-culturally buoyantly encodes a very English ideal: to be unsinkable without being solemn, to treat gravity as a passing wave. In Wodehouse that ideal becomes both lovable and comic - Bertie stays afloat, but Aunt Dahlia needs a cheque, not a life-buoy of words. Modern readers still hear the harbour in the manner: someone speaking buoyantly bobs on the conversation and refuses, for the moment, to go under.
