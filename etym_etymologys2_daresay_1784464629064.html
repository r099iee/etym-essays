DARESAY is a fused English conversational formula of two elements, each with a deep Indo-European history; the linear chains follow in isolation. Chain 1 — the base dare: PIE *dʰers- 'to dare, be bold, venture' (reconstructed; attested through cognates: Sanskrit dhṛṣṇoti 'dares', Greek tharséō 'I am of good courage', Old Church Slavonic drъznǫti 'to dare') → Proto-Germanic *durzaną 'to dare' (preterite-present verb; 'I have dared' → 'I am bold enough to', the class of may, can, shall) → Old English durran 'to dare' (present singular dear, 'ic dear' = 'I dare'; attested) → Middle English durren / daren 'to dare' (13th c.) → English dare 'to be bold enough to do or say' → base element of the fused formula. Chain 2 — the base say: PIE *sekʷ- 'to say, utter, tell' (reconstructed; attested through cognates: Lithuanian sakýti 'to say', Greek ennépein 'to tell') → Proto-Germanic *sagjaną 'to say' (attested through reflexes: Old Saxon seggian, Old Norse segja, Old High German sagēn) → Old English secgan 'to say, tell, speak' (weak verb; attested) → Middle English seien / saye 'to say' (12th–15th c.) → English say → base element of the fused formula. Fusion of the two elements: Middle English phrase (I) dar seyen 'I venture to say' (13th–14th c.; the formula 'I dar say' recorded) → fixed first-person formula I dare say 'I should think, probably' (14th–16th c.) → fused single word daresay (also still written dare say; 16th–17th c.) → English daresay 'I venture to say; I should think; probably' (formula) → DARESAY (card form). Note on the form: the fusion into one word was gradual, and the spelling has never fully settled — daresay and dare say both appear in print to this day. The formula is essentially restricted to the first-person singular; other persons use the two words with full modal force (he dare not say), and the fused adverb-like daresay does not occur in them.
