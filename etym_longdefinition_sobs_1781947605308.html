Sobs — plural noun: convulsive gasps made while crying or weeping, the broken, audible intake of breath that accompanies heavy grief; also the verb form (to sob) and the gerund (sobbing). In Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit, Peter's sobs are the audible, bodily proof that he has "given himself up for lost": "he shed big tears; but his sobs were overheard by some friendly sparrows." The sound, not the tears, summons rescue. CORE SENSES
  • The convulsive gasp of weeping. The noun sob names the sharp, broken, audible inspiration that racks the chest and throat during intense crying — not silent tears, but the noisy catch-breath that can be heard through a door or across a garden.
  • Plural as the act of weeping. "Sobs" as a plural noun covers the whole series of convulsions — "her sobs filled the room" — and is the standard plural for the repeated sound of heavy weeping.
  • Verb and gerund. To sob is to make these convulsive gasps, usually while crying; sobbing is the continuous activity; "to sob out" is to speak while sobbing, in broken, gasping sentences.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • Expanded emotional range. From the nineteenth century the word extends beyond grief to overwhelming emotion of any sign — sobs of relief, sobs of joy, sobs of laughter — keeping the respiratory spasm as the core image.
  • Of things. Transfer uses: an engine or machine may "sob" with a broken, gasping rhythm; the word describes any choked, irregular, breath-like sound outside the human body.
  • Register boundary. Sobs is plain, bodily, Germanic English — shorter and blunter than the Latinate weeping, lamentation, or wailing, and unlike tears it insists on sound.
WHY THIS WORD IN THIS SCENE The sobs are a hinge in the narrative: they mark childish despair and simultaneously summon rescue. The reader is invited to pity Peter without lingering in tragedy, because the very loudness of his grief brings help — the friendly sparrows overhear the sobs and fly to him "in great excitement." The word is concrete and physical, not abstract sadness, and its short blunt shape suits the raw physicality of the sound it names. FURTHER POLYSEMY AND GRAMMATICAL FACETS
  • Single sob vs. the plural series. A single sob is one convulsive gasp; the plural sobs names the whole broken sequence of heavy weeping, and this plurality is idiomatic — we say "her sobs were heard," rarely "her sob was heard," because grief is experienced as a series of convulsions.
  • Sob as transitive verb. "To sob out a confession," "to sob her grief into his shoulder" — the verb takes objects when the broken words or the emotion are themselves named, extending the sound-word into a verb of speech and expression.
  • Sob as gerund and adjective. "Sobbing" as a noun ("the sobbing of a child") and as a present participle ("a sobbing child") completes the verbal paradigm; the adjective emphasises the state, the noun the activity.
  • Sob story and sob sister. From the late nineteenth century the noun compounds into "sob story" (a tale designed to wring sympathy, often sarcastic) and "sob sister" (a sentimental newspaper columnist), turning the pure sound-word into a label for calculated sentiment — a sharp modern shift from genuine grief to performed emotion.
  • Onomatopoeic kinship. The word belongs to a family of English sound-words built on s- + short vowel + stop (hiccup, sniffle, snivel, gasp), and its very form mimes the sound — a phonetic transparency that makes it instantly vivid to children and adults alike, which is why Potter needs no explanation.
  • Restrained vs. unrestrained. Register ranges from the suppressed ("stifled sobs," "choked sobs," "half-smothered sobs") to the unrestrained ("loud sobs," "racking sobs"), the modifiers controlling how much of the sound reaches the world — exactly the axis that matters when, as in Potter, the sobs must be loud enough to be overheard.
REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS Collocations: choked with sobs, stifle a sob, sobs of relief, body wracked by sobs, between sobs, racking/heaving sobs, sob story, sob sister. Register is ordinary narrative and spoken English, fully at home in children's literature and adult fiction alike.
