Versailles is a proper noun naming a town in the Île-de-France, about seventeen kilometres south-west of Paris, and above all the vast palace that stands there: the principal residence of the French kings from 1682 until 1789 and the model of absolutist grandeur in Europe. In the reading Carlyle writes that the dying king has been “put to bed in his own Chateau of Versailles” — the palace to which Louis XV was carried back from the Petit Trianon in May 1774. The word here is the building and the seat of power at once.

What the name denotes

  • The palace: a hunting lodge of Louis XIII, enlarged from 1661 by Louis Le Vau and Jules Hardouin-Mansart for Louis XIV, with gardens by André Le Nôtre, the Galerie des Glaces (Hall of Mirrors) completed in 1684, the Royal Chapel, the Grand Trianon and, later, the Petit Trianon and the Opéra Royal.
  • The town: the planned town that grew around the palace to house courtiers, servants and suppliers; its three avenues converge on the Cour d’Honneur, an urban geometry copied across Europe.
  • The court and the regime: by metonymy, “Versailles” means the court, its etiquette, its patronage and, in revolutionary rhetoric, the whole system of absolute monarchy.
  • The treaty: the Treaty of Versailles, signed in the Hall of Mirrors on 28 June 1919, ended the First World War with Germany; “Versailles” in the twentieth century often means that settlement rather than the building.

History and significance

Louis XIV moved the court to Versailles in 1682 and made it the centre of French political life: nobles were drawn there, kept busy by etiquette, and separated from their provincial power bases. The palace became a theatre of rank, where a glance or a stool could be a political event. Louis XV added the Salon d’Hercule, the Opéra Royal and the Petit Trianon; Louis XVI and Marie-Antoinette lived there until the October Days of 1789, when the royal family was brought back to Paris and the palace was left empty.

In the nineteenth century Versailles was converted by Louis-Philippe into a museum of French history (1837). It then acquired a second political life: the Hall of Mirrors witnessed the proclamation of the German Empire in January 1871 and the signing of the peace treaty in 1919. The palace is now a museum and a UNESCO World Heritage site, and the name has become a byword for opulence — hence the modern French joke that somewhere modest “is not Versailles”.

In this reading

The phrase “his own Chateau of Versailles” sets the king’s public palace against the private “Little Trianon” from which he has been carried. Carlyle’s irony is double: the shepherd of the people returns to the greatest house in Europe to die, and the flock does not care. The name carries the weight of the whole ancien régime — its scale, its ceremonial, its isolation from ordinary life — at the precise moment when that world is beginning to look fragile.

Register and use

As a proper noun, Versailles is always capitalised and takes no article when it names the place (“the court at Versailles”, “the gardens of Versailles”). It is used attributively in fixed phrases — Versailles treaty, Versailles style, Versailles gardens — and in transferred senses for any enormous or ostentatious residence (“a Versailles of the Gulf”). The English pronunciation /veəˈsaɪ/ anglicises the French /vɛʁ.saj/, in which the final s is silent and the -ille is a simple /j/.
