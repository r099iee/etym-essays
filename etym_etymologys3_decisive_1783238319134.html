The biography of decisive begins in an act of violence. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *kh₂eyd- meant 'to cut, hew, strike', a root of severing and slaughter, and its clearest reflex is the Latin verb caedō, caedere, cecīdī, caesum, which uniquely carries both 'to cut' and 'to kill' — a reminder that in ancient thought cutting and killing were one action, the stroke that severs life. The reconstruction with a laryngeal *kh₂- reflects the correspondence between Latin caed- and the probable Indo-Aryan cognate Sanskrit chid- (chinátti, 'he cuts off'), though careful philologists note that the Sanskrit root may instead belong to the neighbouring *skeyd- 'to split', and the Germanic *hawwan- behind English hew is only doubtfully related. Whatever the precise membership of the family, the Italic branch alone produced an astonishing crop of English words: concise and precision, incise and incisive, homicide and suicide, parricide and regicide, caesura, and — through the proper name Caesar, folk-linked to caedere by the story of his 'cutting' birth — the imperial titles Kaiser and tsar. Sound shifts and forms. Within Latin the root shows its apophonic grades — caed- in the present (caedō), cecīd- in the perfect (cecīdī), caes- in the supine (caesum) — a classic ablaut pattern inherited from Indo-European. In the derived family the stem reduced and fronted: dēcīdere (dē- 'off' + caedere) lengthens and simplifies the first vowel, and the supine stem dēcīsus gives the adjective dēcīsīvus and the noun dēcīsiō. When the adjective travelled into English it came through Old French décisif (15th–16th centuries), which is why English decisive preserves the French sibilant before the ending rather than the plain Latin spelling it ultimately reflects. The decisive semantic turn. The crucial shift happened inside Latin, in the compound dēcīdere. Literally 'to cut off' — used of felling timber and cutting things away — it developed a juridical-metaphorical sense of extraordinary fertility: 'to cut the issue', 'to settle a dispute', 'to determine'. The latent image is the cutting of a knot — the same image as Alexander severing the Gordian knot — where a problem that cannot be untied is resolved by a single clean stroke. From the participle dēcīsus came the abstract dēcīsiō, 'a decision', and from dēcīsīvus the Late Latin adjective recorded from the fourth century. By then the semantics had completed their migration from physical severing to intellectual settling: the decision is the cut that ends the argument. The word arrived in English as a verb first — decide, late 14th century, via Old French décider — and the adjective decisive followed about 1610, with the noun decision already present since the 15th century. For two centuries the new adjective lived in the formal registers of law, logic, and theology: a decisive argument, a decisive judgement, a decisive answer. From the bar to the battlefield. The 18th and 19th centuries carried decisive into history and war. Military writers made it a term of art: the decisive battle, the decisive moment, the decisive blow, and in the strategic theory of Clausewitz and Jomini the decisive point — the place where the outcome of a campaign is settled. Macaulay's 'decisive crisis' (History of England, 1848) is exactly this historiographical usage: the Whig historian's teleological confidence that the Glorious Revolution was the settled verdict of history. The word's confident, almost providential ring suited an age that believed in progress and in events that finally settle questions. Note also the linguistic echo: crisis (Greek krísis, 'a judgement, a decision') makes 'decisive crisis' almost a tautology — a moment of judgement that judges — a coincidence Macaulay's classical ear would have relished. The inward turn. In the 20th century decisive moved from events to persons. A decisive leader, a decisive temperament, a decisive manner: the adjective now describes the character that makes up its mind quickly and acts. This is a genuine semantic extension, not merely a metaphor — it transferred the settlement of a dispute to the disposition that produces settlements. Its antonym indecisive, recorded from the 18th century, gained enormous everyday currency in the 20th as a mild personal reproach. Today decisive straddles two lives: the event that settles a matter (a decisive victory, a decisive vote) and the person who settles it (a decisive CEO). Both still carry, faintly, the ancient blade — the cut that severs all other possibilities.
