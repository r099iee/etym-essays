• Frail: Similar because both describe a weakened elderly person; differs because frail stresses delicacy and liability to break or fall ill, while the mind may remain sharp, and it lacks the “broken-down machinery” image and the pitying or contemptuous edge of decrepit.
  • Infirm: Similar because both denote weakness from age or disease; differs because infirm is more formal and medical, a neutral clinical word, whereas decrepit is stronger and pictures the body or structure cracked into near-uselessness.
  • Dilapidated: Similar because both mean fallen into ruin; differs because dilapidated is chiefly for buildings and objects (and derives from Latin lapis “stone”), rarely for people, whereas decrepit applies readily to aged persons.
  • Senile: Similar because both can describe the aged; differs because senile targets mental decline and dementia and is pejorative in that specific way, while decrepit describes physical and structural decay without claiming mental deterioration.
  • Enfeebled: Similar because both denote weakened; differs because enfeebled focuses on the loss of strength (from feeble) and can be temporary or caused by illness, while decrepit implies long-term wearing out by age or use.
  • Worn-out: Similar because both mean exhausted by use; differs because worn-out is informal and also used of non-physical exhaustion (tiredness), while decrepit is more formal and specifically about age and decay.
  • Run-down: Similar because both mean in poor, decayed condition; differs because run-down is informal and describes buildings, health, or energy generally, with a milder, less architectural-ruin force than decrepit.
  • Decrepitude (noun): Similar because it is the abstract noun of the same state; differs grammatically (noun versus adjective) and names the condition itself, “the state of being decrepit.”
  • Bedraggled: Similar because both can describe something worn and dragged down; differs because bedraggled is about being wet, untidy, and limp (of clothes, hair), not about age or structural collapse.
  • Doddering: Similar because both describe the unsteady aged; differs because doddering emphasizes trembling, unsteady movement, and is more narrowly physical and somewhat affectionate or comic, while decrepit is broader and more severe.
