DERIVATION CHAIN — DITHER → DITHERING: PIE ROOT: NONE RECONSTRUCTABLE. Dither is of obscure, probably imitative (echoic) origin. No Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed, and no earlier attested form is known before Middle English. The English family of "trembling" words (didder, dadder, totter, titter, dither) is built on expressive consonant-and-vowel reduplication that imitates shaking or quivering, and such expressive formations typically resist deep reconstruction. → Middle English didderen "to tremble, quake, shake" (attested; spelling variants daderen, didren; cf. also Middle English doder "to quake") [attested 14th–16th c.] → Early Modern English didder "to tremble, shake" and its regional variant dither "to tremble, quiver" (the didder/dither alternation of vowels is characteristic of English expressive/echoic words, as in titter/totter, shiver/quiver) [attested 16th–17th c.] → Modern English dither, verb, sense 1 "to tremble, shake, quiver" (now rare or archaic) [17th c.–] → Modern English dither, verb, sense 2 "to be nervously irresolute, to vacillate, to hesitate in agitation" (semantic shift from physical trembling to mental agitation; the physical tremor becomes the outward sign of indecision) [attested from the 19th c.] → Modern English dithering, noun/participle "indecisive hesitation" (present participle and verbal noun of dither) → DITHERING NOTE ON THE DEBATE / NUANCE: The origin is genuinely uncertain: dither is classed as of obscure, echoic origin, and no attested intermediate connects it to any Indo-European root. The 19th-century shift from "tremble" to "hesitate" is semantically transparent (nervous trembling as the outward sign of indecision), but it is not documented stage-by-stage, and some authorities suggest influence from the Scots and Irish dialectal didder/dodder family. The 20th-century engineering sense — adding random noise to a digital signal to reduce quantization error, called "dithering" — is a separate, consciously coined technical extension named for the word's older sense of trembling or shakiness of values; it is not an organic semantic development but an engineered borrowing.
