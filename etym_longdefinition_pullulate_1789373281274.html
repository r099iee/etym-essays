Pullulate is an intransitive verb denoting the action of sprouting, budding, germinating, breeding, multiplying rapidly, or teeming and swarming in overwhelming abundance. Operating at the dynamic crossroads of botanical morphology, zoological reproduction, social critique, and literary textual criticism, the word characterizes a vital, exuberant, and often chaotic surge of generative energy. Whether describing physical seedlings bursting from rich soil, insects swarming in an enclosed space, or abstract defects multiplying across an uncorrected text, pullulate conveys an unstoppable, self-generating fecundity that bursts beyond conventional boundaries.

In literary textual scholarship and bibliographical history—as vividly illustrated in the source context examining the catastrophic print history of Marcel Proust's À la recherche du temps perdu—pullulate serves as a potent metaphor for textual corruption. When a critic writes that the original printed editions "pullulate with errors, misreadings and omissions," the verb evokes an almost biological infestation of typographical blunders. Because Proust made frantic additions on proofs, suffered from severe illness, and contended with printers who struggled with his handwriting, errors sprouted like invasive weeds across the printed pages, multiplying organically from volume to volume until subsequent generations of editors undertook the massive task of purging them.

The verb displays extensive polysemy, diverse registers, and specialized applications across numerous domains:

  • Textual Criticism, Bibliography, and Errata (Primary Literary Sense): In philology and the history of the book, pullulate describes texts, editions, or manuscripts that are rife with typographical errors, misreadings, homoeoteleuton corruptions, and omitted clauses. The verb emphasizes that the blunders are not isolated or sporadic, but exist in teeming, uncontrollable proliferation, transforming the page into a chaotic landscape of textual noise.
  • Botanical Germination and Vegetative Budding: In plant morphology and classical agricultural science, pullulate denotes the physical sprouting, budding, and shooting forth of young twigs, suckers, or germinating seeds. In spring, a healthy forest floor or vine pullulates with fresh emerald shoots and fungal hyphae bursting forth from dormant roots.
  • Entomology, Microbiology, and Biological Swarming: In biology and ecology, the term describes organisms that multiply, crawl, or swarm in enormous, writhing numbers. One speaks of maggots pullulating within decaying matter, bacterial colonies pullulating within an agar medium, or locusts pullulating across an arid basin prior to a destructive swarm.
  • Urban Sociology and Demographics: In social commentary and historical writing, pullulate vividly depicts overcrowded, bustling, and chaotic human environments. Historians describe nineteenth-century industrial slums, subterranean markets, or immigrant tenements pullulating with desperate human activity, noise, and vitality, emphasizing high population density and organic human ferment.
  • Ideological, Cultural, and Intellectual Proliferation: In intellectual history and political science, the verb characterizes the sudden, rapid outbreak of competing ideas, factions, heresies, or cultural trends. Following the collapse of an authoritarian regime or the advent of a new communications technology (such as the printing press or the internet), alternative political parties, conspiracy theories, and artistic manifestos pullulate across the cultural sphere.

The semantic bridge linking agricultural shoots (*pulli*), swarming insects, and typographical blunders is the concept of unbridled, organic reproduction. From the innocent bursting forth of a young chick or plant bud, the word evolved to encompass any scene where individual entities multiply so fast and so abundantly that they overwhelm their environment with chaotic, teeming vitality.
