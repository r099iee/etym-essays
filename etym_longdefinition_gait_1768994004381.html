gait — a person’s or animal’s particular manner of walking or moving on foot, comprehending rhythm, bearing, stride, length of step, and speed taken together as a single describable performance. In the specific literary moment on this card the word is narrowed by its adjective: “They had been walking at a fair gait for fifteen minutes” — a good, steady, reasonably quick pace sustained through the Martian cave. The nuance is therefore less about individual style than about sustained, purposeful forward motion; “gait” treats walking as something measurable and clockable, and lends the prose a cool, observational, almost technical quality, as if the narrator were timing the party’s progress like an instrument.

Because “gait” names the whole observable manner of locomotion, it is genuinely polysemous across several domains, and an exhaustive definition must separate them.

  • Human gait — the characteristic manner of walking: The core modern sense. Every person has a recognisable gait — a spring in the step, a shuffle, a swagger — and the word carries a cool, diagnostic, sometimes clinical attention: in medicine, “gait abnormalities” are symptoms read by neurologists, as in the festinating gait of Parkinson’s disease or the ataxic gait of cerebellar damage.
  • Equine and zoological gait — the distinct footfalls of animals: In horsemanship the gaits are the named patterns of foot movement — walk, trot, canter, gallop, and the “ambling” gaits such as the pace, rack, and tolt. The term is exact and technical: each gait is a distinct rhythm of hooves. The word extends to other animals (the gaits of dogs, camels, birds) and to robotic locomotion in modern biomechanics (“a dynamic walking gait”).
  • Obsolete and dialectal sense — a way, path, road, or journey: Historically (and still in Scottish and northern English dialect) gate or gait meant “a way or road” and, by extension, “a going, a journey”: “he’s on his gait,” “to go one’s gait.” The modern “manner of walking” sense grew out of this by a natural metonymy — the way became the way of going.
  • Figurative extension — pace, rate of progress: From “manner of going” the word readily extends to any measured rate of advance: the gait of a project, of an argument, of a narrative, of history. “To go at a good gait” means to progress briskly. This sense is frequent in nineteenth-century prose and still natural in modern usage.
  • Rare verbal sense: To gait (a horse) is to train it to perform its gaits; to be “gaiting” is to move. The noun dominates overwhelmingly, but the verb is recorded from the sixteenth century.

In synonym boundaries, “gait” occupies the intersection of movement, speed, and bearing. Pace overlaps heavily but stresses speed or step-frequency rather than the whole bodily manner; stride emphasises the length and confidence of individual steps; walk is the neutral verb for any pedestrian motion and carries no descriptive weight; tread foregrounds the sound and pressure of the feet on the ground; carriage and bearing name posture and demeanour above the legs themselves. “Gait” is the whole performance — the sum of all of these — which is why doctors, horse-trainers, novelists, and biomechanists all use it with the same precision. Collocations complete the range: a slow gait, an easy gait, a rolling gait, to walk with a measured gait, to adjust one’s gait, to recognize someone’s gait from a distance, and the modern scientific set: gait cycle, gait analysis, gait training, gait disorders, gait abnormalities. In the cave passage the word is the narrator’s instrument of cool measurement — the party’s progress is clocked, timed, and read like the dial of an instrument as they push deeper into the dark.
