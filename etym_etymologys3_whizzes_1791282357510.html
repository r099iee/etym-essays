Whizzes is the regular third-person singular present and plural noun form of the imitative verb and noun whizz, also spelled whiz, whose history is one of sound symbolism rather than inheritance from an ancient root. The Oxford English Dictionary and Etymonline agree that whiz appears in the 1540s as an imitative formation, coined to represent the humming or hissing rush of swift movement through air. Unlike inherited words like water or heart, imitative words are repeatedly reinvented because human vocal apparatus naturally renders rushing as a sibilant plus high vowel. There is therefore no PIE etymon to reconstruct with confidence, and dictionaries explicitly label the origin imitative.

Phonetically the development shows spelling vacillation rather than sound law. Early forms whiz, whizz, whuzze and quizze-like variants compete in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The doubling of -zz marks a short vowel before a voiced sibilant, parallel to buzz, fizz and whirr. The vowel is the short high front /ɪ/, stable since Early Modern English. Inflection is fully regular: whizz plus -es after a sibilant, pronounced /ɪz/, giving RP /ˈwɪzɪz/. The noun whizz, a swift movement or expert, as in a maths whizz, derives from the verb by conversion in the nineteenth century. The plural and third-person -es is the normal Germanic agreement suffix from Old English -as, -es.

Semantic development proceeds by metaphor from sound to speed to skill:

  • Sound 1540s-1600s: to whiz is to hum, hiss or whistle in flight, of arrows, bullets, stones. The focus is auditory.
  • Rapid movement 1700s-1800s: to whiz along means to rush past with that implied sound, even when the sound is faint. The brain whizzes along in the source context belongs here: thought figured as a speeding vehicle.
  • Brief visit or transit 19th c.: to whiz off, whiz round, whiz through town. Speed implies casual brevity.
  • Expertise late 19th-20th c.: a whizz, a whiz-kid, to whiz at maths. The bridge is that one who moves swiftly through problems is clever; American English especially favours whiz for a prodigy. Spelling whiz is common in the US for this sense.
  • Urination slang and euphoria: to take a whizz, whizzed up. Minor slang extensions playing on quick release and buzz.

Culturally, whizz belongs to the industrial age of speed: steam, motor cars, aircraft and brains imagined as engines. Wodehouse writes A man's brain whizzes along for years exceeding the speed limit, blending motoring slang with psychology. The motoring metaphor of steering-gear, skids and ditch would have been vivid to 1920s readers living through mass motorisation. Literary attestations run from sixteenth-century hunting and warfare descriptions of whizzing arrows, through Dickensian whizzing carriages, to modern whizzing hard drives and whizz-kids. The form has remained colloquial and energetic, never fully formalised, which is why the double-z spelling persists as a visual cue of fizzing speed.
