This card carries four root words from a single sentence of acoustic horror: "It was a sharp rustling and rattling as of metal dragged over rock; and also it was somehow like the smacking of myriad wet, enormous mouths." Each word is a sound-word or a number-word, and together they build the passage's central dread: an unclassifiable noise that is at once metallic and organic, singular and countless.

(1) rustling — a soft, dry, scratching, sibilant sound, as of many small surfaces moving together. In the sentence the word names the friction of metal on stone, rendered with the sibilant hiss of its own phonetics: not loud, but everywhere, a sound that approaches rather than announces itself. The primary sense is the quiet dry agitation of leaves, silk, paper, and reeds — implying stealth, dryness, and secrecy ("the rustling of leaves", "rustling silk"). A famous separate sense grew on the American frontier: to "rustle" cattle is to steal them (a rustler is the thief), a sense that derives not from the sound but from a dialectal "to hustle, to drive" — a striking case of homonymic drift within one verb. As a noun it names the sound itself; as a present participle (this card) it describes the sound in progress.

(2) rattling — a rapid series of short, sharp, irregular sounds, as of things shaken, knocked, or dragged colliding. In the sentence it adds a percussive, mechanical layer to the rustling: chains, plates, or loose metal colliding, things not fastened down being dragged. The primary sense is the rapid percussion of the rattle; secondary senses radiate widely: to "rattle on" is to talk rapidly and endlessly; to "rattle" someone is to discompose or fluster them (a nervous rattling of the nerves); the "death rattle" is the pre-terminal breathing sound; the baby's rattle is the toy; the rattlesnake is named from its tail; and the colloquial intensifier appears in "a rattling good yarn". As a present participle (this card) it describes a clatter in progress, alarming and lawless.

(3) smacking — the sharp, quick noise of lips striking; also of a sharp blow or a loud kiss. In the sentence it is the most uncanny word: the noise is "somehow like the smacking of myriad wet, enormous mouths" — the ordinary sound of eating, of lip-smacking relish, turned into the voice of a cave. The word's history is double: an old native word smack meant "taste, flavour" (from the perception of the mouth), while a second, imitative smack arose for the sound itself — the sharp strike of lips in tasting or kissing. "To smack one's lips" is the audible sign of appetite and relish (cf. Hindi चटकारा chatkārā, "lip-smacking"). The senses converge: a smack is a sharp blow or a loud kiss, and smacking keeps its wet, percussive, strikingly physical quality. In this passage it turns appetite itself into something monstrous.

(4) myriad — a countless or extremely great number; also, historically, exactly ten thousand. In the sentence it is used as an adjective — "myriad wet, enormous mouths" — asserting a number beyond counting, a swarming multitude. The primary sense in ancient Greek was precise: myrias meant "ten thousand", the full strength of a Greek regiment. The precision dissolved into hyperbole in later usage: a myriad became any number too great to count, and the word works with equal ease as a noun ("a myriad of possibilities") and an adjective ("myriad ways"). Its register is formal and literary, suggesting scale beyond enumeration; it collocates with stars, choices, possibilities, and (in horror) mouths. The word's modern career is that of an ancient numeral that outgrew arithmetic to become a figure of the sublime.

Together the four words build a single descending arc of perception: the ear first registers the dry friction of rustling, then the mechanical percussion of rattling, then the wet, organic smacking of lips, and finally the swarm — a myriad of mouths — whose number destroys the possibility of counting or classifying the thing that makes the noise. The scene moves from the mineral to the organic to the infinite, and each word is chosen with almost scientific precision for the sense it contributes.
