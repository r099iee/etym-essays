• Alongshore: Similar because both mean "along the shore, following the coastline"; differs because alongshore is the plain descriptive term without the technical, legal weight that coastwise carries in maritime law, cabotage, and course-recording.
  • Coastal: Similar because both relate to the coast; differs because coastal is a pure adjective of location — belonging to or standing on the coast — with no sense of motion along the land, whereas coastwise is directional and describes movement tracing the coast's line.
  • Inshore: Similar because both concern the near-coast zone; differs because inshore fixes position relative to the sea (opposed to offshore), while coastwise follows the shore's course as a path of travel.
  • Offshore: Similar because both are maritime directional words; differs because offshore means away from the land, at sea beyond the coast, the exact opposite of a course that hugs the coastline.
  • Littoral: Similar because both refer to the coastal margin; differs because littoral is the scientific, ecological term for the intertidal zone between high and low tide, positional and static, while coastwise is about motion and bearing.
  • Seaboard: Similar because both denote the land–sea margin; differs because seaboard is a noun for the coastal region (chiefly American), while coastwise is an adverb/adjective of direction along that region.
  • Lengthwise: Similar because both are built with the directional suffix -wise; differs because lengthwise means "in the direction of the length" of a thing, without the specifically maritime, coastal reference of coastwise.
  • Wave: Similar to swells because both are moving ridges of water; differs because wave is the general term for any moving ridge, while a swell is specifically the long, regular, unbreaking undulation of open water that has outrun its storm.
  • Billow: Similar to swells in being a great swelling mass; differs because billow is a literary word applicable to water, smoke, or sail, while swells is the oceanographic term for deep-bodied, unbroken sea undulations.
  • Breaker: Similar to swells in being a sea wave; differs because a breaker is a wave that collapses on shore or reef, while a swell does not break until it shallows — the two are phases of the same motion.
  • Surge: Similar to swells in a powerful movement of water; differs because a surge is a sudden, forceful, abrupt rush, while swells are slow, rhythmic, and recurrent.
  • Roller: Similar to swells in a long, heavy sea wave; differs because roller emphasizes mass and weight rolling in to shore, while swell emphasizes the deep, regular undulation of open water before any shore.
  • Groundswell: Similar because the political groundswell is metaphorically built on the sea swell; differs because groundswell is now almost wholly figurative, naming a rising mass of public opinion, while swells remains the literal oceanic term.
