FROM STRETCHING TO HOLDING: THE STORY OF RETAINERS The word retainer is a lesson in how far a single verb can travel. Behind the English plural stands the Latin retinere, 'to hold back, keep,' built from re- 'back' and tenere 'to hold.' And behind Latin tenere lies one of the oldest and most productive roots in the Indo-European family, *ten-, 'to stretch.' The path from stretching a rope to keeping a loyal household servant, then to paying a lawyer and finally to wearing an orthodontic device, is six centuries of continuous metaphor. THE PIE ROOT *TEN-. The Proto-Indo-European root *ten- means 'to stretch,' and its descendants are everywhere in the modern European word-stock. Sanskrit tanóti means 'stretches, extends'; Greek teínō means 'I stretch.' The Latin branch specialized the idea: something pulled taut and held is held firmly, so tenēre comes to mean 'to hold, keep, possess,' and from it English derives tenacious ('holding fast'), tenure (the holding of office or land), and the huge family contain, sustain, obtain, pertain, maintain, retain. The physical image of a stretched, taut line underlies them all: what is stretched is under control, and what is under control is held. LATIN RETINERE. Latin added the prefix re- 'back' to make retinēre, 'to hold back, keep, restrain, preserve.' In classical Latin the verb is rich: it means to keep possession of something, to restrain a person, to remember (hold a thing back in the mind), and to preserve. The Roman poets and lawyers use it freely; the sense 'keep in mind' gives English retention, our word for memory. The core picture never changes: something is pulled back and kept rather than released. THROUGH OLD FRENCH INTO MIDDLE ENGLISH. Old French retenir carries the verb into the medieval world with the added institutional meaning 'to engage in service.' When Middle English borrows retenen/retain in the fourteenth century, it means both 'to keep, continue to hold' and, crucially, 'to engage someone in service, to keep in one's pay.' Out of this grows the agent noun retainer, formed in English or Anglo-Norman from retain plus the agent suffix -er: literally 'one who is retained,' a person kept in a lord's service. THE FEUDAL WORLD OF RETAINERS. In late medieval and early modern England, a retainer was not a hired day-laborer but someone bound to a lord's household, often wearing his livery or badge, following him in peace and war. The great lords kept whole armies of such men, and the Crown repeatedly legislated against 'livery and maintenance' and 'excessive retainers' because private armed followings blurred the line between household and faction, threatening public order. The word in this period is heavy with the social structure of bastard feudalism: loyalty purchased by keeping, identity marked by livery. It is exactly this world that the fiction of aristocratic households, whether set in medieval Europe or echoing such structures in imperial China, needs, and it is the world the card's passage invokes when a few retainers remain after the guilty have fled. THE PARALLEL LEGAL SENSE. From the same 'hold back / keep in reserve' idea comes the legal and professional sense: a retainer is a fee paid in advance to keep a lawyer or other professional available, or the agreement that does so. The service is 'held' for the client; the professional is 'retained' against future need. This sense appears from the seventeenth century and is today the most common everyday use of the word in many regions: a lawyer is 'on retainer,' an ongoing engagement 'retains' the firm. The semantic kinship with the feudal follower is exact, because both rest on keeping a person bound to oneself over time. THE DENTAL AND TECHNICAL SENSES. In the twentieth century the same verb was applied to objects. A dental retainer is a wire-and-plastic device worn after braces to hold the teeth in their corrected alignment; the tooth position is 'retained.' A bearing retainer, retainer ring, or retainer bolt is any mechanical part that holds another part in place. Water-retaining soil holds moisture. All these technical senses are late applications of the ancient 'keep, hold in place' verb to the material world. THE MEANING ON THE CARD. In the literary scene at hand, retainers are exactly those who are still 'kept' when the more culpable companions have fled: residual household dependents without proven share in the crime. The word's slightly antique register suits a great family's structure better than employees or staff. Semantic drift from 'stretch' to 'hold' to 'keep in service' to 'loyal leftover follower' is continuous, and the modern technical uses simply reapply the same 'keep/hold' verb to objects. The plural retainers on the card preserves the social, human sense of long-standing personal followers rather than the fee or device senses. Key points to remember:
  • Retainer rests on Latin retinere, 'hold back, keep,' from re- 'back' and tenere 'to hold.'
  • Tenere is the Latin representative of PIE *ten-, 'to stretch'; 'stretch taut' became 'hold firm.'
  • The follower sense (15th c.) comes from the Old French/Middle English institutional meaning 'engage in service.'
  • Retainers were the liveried, kept core of a lord's following, notorious enough to be regulated by statute.
  • The legal retainer fee (17th c.) and the dental retainer (20th c.) are the same 'hold/keep' verb applied to services and objects.
