MENACE functions as both a noun and a verb. As a noun it means a threat - a statement, gesture, quality, or person that threatens harm, danger, or trouble - and, in extended modern use, a person or thing regarded as a source of danger or nuisance ("a public menace"). As a verb it means to threaten, to appear threateningly before someone, or to endanger. In the supplied context - "a dire menace even in his sleep" - the word is a noun naming the sleeping dragon himself as an embodiment of danger: the threat is not a spoken one but a physical presence whose very existence endangers the company of dwarves.

Primary senses

  • Noun - a threat of harm: a declaration or indication of an intention to hurt, punish, or destroy; the disposition of something dangerous. "The letter was a menace of eviction."
  • Noun - a person or thing that is a source of danger: "The drunk driver was a menace on the road." Extended to anything or anyone endangering public safety or order.
  • Verb - to threaten: to express an intention to inflict injury or damage; to intimidate. "The storm menaced the coast all night."
  • Verb - to be a danger to; to put at risk: "The rising waters menaced the village." Also of looming shapes or atmospheres: "The mountain menaced the travellers."

Secondary, figurative, and register shifts

  • Quality of threat ("menace" as an atmosphere): a pervasive sense of impending danger, often visual - a menacing sky, a menacing silence. The abstract noun can mean the quality itself: "There was menace in his voice."
  • Playful or mild register: "little menace" - a mischievous child, a handful; used affectionately or with mock sternness. Here the word is emptied of real danger and kept for its comic energy.
  • Cultural and political slogans: "public menace", "menace to society" - the criminal or social outcast as a danger to the community; a staple of moral-panic journalism from the 19th century to the present (the 1993 film "Menace II Society").
  • Pest and nuisance register: a "menace" of rabbits, weeds, or pests to crops and gardens - a destructive nuisance.
  • Cinematic and atmospheric register: "menacing" as a stock quality of villains, storms, and landscapes; a staple adjective of film and thriller prose.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridges

The Latin root is wonderfully concrete: Latin minae meant both "threats" and "projecting points" (the jutting points of cliffs, the tips of spears, the corners of the eyes), and the verb minārī meant both "to threaten" and "to jut out, to overhang". The bridge is the threatening posture of something that juts out over you - a crag leaning over a path, a row of spear-points aimed at you. From "overhang" came "threaten". English inherited through Old French menace (12th c.) and Middle English menace (13th c.) the full noun-verb pair. The noun's drift from "a spoken threat" to "a dangerous person or thing" (a "public menace", 19th c.) and finally to "a nuisance" is a classic widening in which the degree of danger diminished as the word grew more common - the same drift that produced "awful" from "awe-ful".

Grammar and usage notes

  • Both noun and verb are well-established in formal and literary English; the verb is slightly formal and rare in casual speech.
  • The adjective "menacing" and adverb "menacingly" are very frequent in narrative prose ("a menacing growl").
  • "Menace" as a verb takes a direct object ("menaced the town") and may be followed by "with" for the threatened harm ("menaced them with death").
  • In British usage the word also appears in playful domestic phrases, but the most durable collocation is "public menace".

In the Tolkien sentence the noun is preceded by the adjective "dire" ("a dire menace even in his sleep") - the phrase intensifies the threat: a menace is bad enough, a dire menace is terrible; and the dragon is menacing even while unconscious, because his mere presence is danger.
