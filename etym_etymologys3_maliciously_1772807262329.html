English maliciously is the adverbial form of malicious, which is built on the noun malice — a word that entered Middle English from Old French in the thirteenth century and that carries two thousand years of Roman moral and legal thinking inside it. The Latin core. The noun malice continues Old French malice, from Latin malitia, a noun of quality derived from the adjective malus "bad, evil." In Classical Latin malus was one of the central evaluative words — covering bad, evil, wicked, ugly, and unfortunate — and malitia meant badness or wickedness, with a distinctly interpersonal colouring: spite, ill will, crafty evil. Roman moralists used malitia to describe deliberate wrongdoing, the corruption of good men by greed or envy, and the cunning that does harm on purpose. This is the crucial semantic deposit: malitia is not merely "being bad" but "badness directed at others, wilful ill intent." From malus Latin also built male "badly" (the adverb) and a broad derivational family — maleficus "evil-doing", malefactor "evildoer", malevolentia "ill will", maledicere "to speak evil of" — a whole vocabulary of harm that English later borrowed piece by piece. The deepest pre-Latin origin of malus itself is uncertain: scholars treat the further etymology as unclear, and any reconstruction (for example a PIE *mel- "bad, wrong") is speculative and should be labelled as such rather than stated as fact. Through French. Old French took malitia as malice and sharpened its interpersonal sense: ill will, wickedness, the crafty malice of a schemer. When English borrowed malice in the thirteenth century, it imported a word already loaded with deliberate, harming intent. Middle English usage, and then Early Modern English, keeps the full charge: malice is the desire to hurt, humiliate, or destroy, often secret and patient rather than angry. This is the sense that animates the word to this day: malice is not anger but a settled intention to harm. The adjective and adverb. English malicious developed from Old French malicieux, itself from Latin malitiosus "full of malice" (malitia + -osus). By the fifteenth century both malicious and maliciously were available, and they quickly acquired a distinctly legal life. In the common law, malice became a term of art for the mental element of certain wrongs: malice aforethought in homicide, malicious prosecution, malicious damage, malicious falsehood. To act maliciously, in law, is to act deliberately with intent to injure — not merely carelessly and not merely badly. The adverb thus straddles moral psychology and legal proof: it names both a feeling (delight in another's harm) and a standard of intent that courts can test. The survival of the legal idiom — "maliciously damaged", "malicious prosecution" — keeps the word precise where ordinary synonyms like nastily or meanly would be too weak. The wider mal- family. The same Latin base radiates through English: malevolent and malevolence, malign and malignant (via Latin malignus "of an evil nature"), malefactor, malediction, malady (through French maladie "sickness"), and the modern productive prefix mal- in malfunction, malpractice, malformed, maladaptive. Romance languages kept everyday words from the same base — French mal "bad, evil, hurt", Spanish mal, Italian male — while English reserved mal- for formal, medical, and legal compounds and kept native bad, evil, and ill for the core lexicon. That distribution gives maliciously a precise, slightly elevated tone in English: it is the formal word for acting with intent to harm, exactly the register that suits both the law reports and the narrator's cold appraisal of the usher's smile. In the novel. The single adverb "smiled maliciously" activates both layers of this history. Psychologically it names Schadenfreude — the usher savours the magistrate's discomfiture, enjoying hidden knowledge of a monstrous case. Institutionally it colours the legal world of the scene: a servant, inside the very machinery of justice, takes deliberate pleasure in his superior's shame. The etymology contributes the weight: maliciously is not "angrily" or "meanly" but "with evil intent", a word the law reserves for deliberate wrongdoing. By choosing it, the narrator convicts the usher's motive in a single word — the smile becomes evidence, and the adverb, like malice itself, does its harm quietly and on purpose.
