The word turbulence begins as a noise in Greek: týrbē (τύρβη), “turmoil, confusion, disorder,” a word whose own ancestry is dark. Greek scholars now regard it as probably Pre-Greek — that is, a relic of the languages spoken before the Hellenic migrations — and attempts to give it an Indo-European pedigree have not won assent. A traditional reconstruction connected it to PIE *(s)twer-, “to stir, turn, whirl,” a root that does exist: Sanskrit tvár-, “to hurry,” and Germanic *sturmaz, “storm,” with *sturjan, “to stir,” descend from it. But the phonetic and semantic bridge from that root to Greek týrbē is weak, and de Vaan and Beekes both decline to cross it; turba is now treated as a straightforward loanword from Greek. The deep history is accordingly one of borrowing, not of native inheritance — the word travelled, like the confusion it names, from one language into another.

Latin borrowed the Greek word as turba, and here the crucial semantic shift occurred: in Latin turba came to mean both “disorder, commotion” and, more concretely, “a crowd, a mob, the rabble” — the noun for people in disorderly motion. The double meaning is the pivot of the whole history: for a Roman, turmoil and a mob were the same image, a churning mass of ungoverned bodies. From turba, Latin produced a rich family: turbāre, “to throw into confusion,” which lies under English disturb (disturbāre, “to scatter apart violently”), perturb (perturbāre, “to throw into utter confusion”), and trouble (through Vulgar Latin *turbulāre and Old French troubler, “to stir up, to muddy,” as of water); and turbō, “a whirlwind” — also the name of the spinning top children twirled — which engineers rescued in the nineteenth century as the name of the turbine. The direct ancestor of our word is the adjective turbulentus, “full of commotion, storm-tossed, restless,” applied in Latin to seas, winds, and rebellious peoples alike; from turbulentia, “a state of commotion,” Middle French took turbulence, and English borrowed it in the fifteenth century, using it for physical and political agitation alike.

The word’s modern destiny was fixed by physics. In the nineteenth century, work on fluid dynamics — William Thomson (Lord Kelvin), and above all Osborne Reynolds’s famous 1883 experiments on eddying flow — made turbulence the precise technical term for the chaotic, swirling state in which a fluid abandons its smooth, laminar calm and breaks into eddies. The transition is governed by the Reynolds number, the dimensionless ratio that still bears his name; turbulent flow is today a central problem of mathematics and physics, the subject of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Navier–Stokes prize problem. In the twentieth century the word spread from the laboratory into daily life: aircraft passengers hit turbulence, weather forecasters track atmospheric turbulence, financial journalists speak of market turbulence, and political writers of political turbulence. Along the way the old human, moral sense drained away, leaving a word that is today the neutral, impersonal name for violent, irregular motion — the rough air of a flight, the swirling storm, the mob made of wind — with almost no trace of the human crowd left in it. Vance’s sentence restores the ancient connection at a stroke: the demonic voice issues from the turbulence as though the churning cloud itself were speaking — the old Latin image of the mob and the modern physics of the storm fused in a single word.
