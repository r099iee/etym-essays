bridle is a noun naming the headgear used to control a horse — the harness of straps, bit, and reins that fits over a horse's head and through its mouth, by which the rider steers and stops the animal; and a verb meaning (1) to put a bridle on a horse, (2) to restrain, curb, or control, and (3) to show resentment or offence — to toss the head as a horse does against the bit.

Primary sense (equestrian): in the passage the word appears as an attributive noun in the compound "bridle paths": a bridle path is a narrow country lane along which a rider can lead a horse but along which a cart cannot pass. The bridle itself — leather headstall, bit, and reins — is the emblem of horsemanship, the rider's quiet command over his mount; and the "bridle path" is the modest, winding lane of the horseman as opposed to the high road of the wagoner. The choice is deliberate in the story: the boy is choosing secrecy and countryside, the world of horse and rider, over the open king's highway, and the small word quietly establishes the whole equestrian register of the journey.

  • The equipment: a bridle consists of the headstall (the leather bands over the head), the bit (the metal mouthpiece), and the reins; it is distinguished from the halter, a simple headcollar without a bit, used for leading and tying rather than steering.
  • Bridle path / bridleway: a right of way for horse riders (in British law, open also to walkers and cyclists) — a lane too narrow for wheeled traffic.
  • To bridle (a horse): to put a bridle on the animal and so gain command of it.

Secondary sense (figurative restraint): the verb to bridle means to restrain, check, curb, or govern — to put a bit on unruly force or passion: "bridle your tongue," "bridle your temper," "bridle your enthusiasm." The metaphor is ancient and moral: the horse's headgear is the image of self-control, and the unbridled is the uncontrolled. The negative form unbridled (unrestrained, raging — "unbridled fury," "unbridled ambition") is one of the word's most common modern uses.

Tertiary sense (resentment): to bridle at, or bridle up, means to show sudden anger, offence, or stiffening pride — to toss one's head like a horse taking the bit between its teeth or resisting the reins. "She bridled at the insult" is the standard literary use, recorded from the 17th century; the motion is physical pride, an arching of the neck in protest against restraint.

Modern technical jargon:

  • Nautical: a bridle is a mooring cable or rope arrangement that moors a boat to two points.
  • Engineering: a bridle is a restraining device — a chain, strap, or wire that holds or guides a moving part (e.g., a derrick bridle, an elevator bridle).
  • Electronics and rigging: a bridle or bridle wire can be a supporting or tying wire or connector arrangement.
  • Anatomy and veterinary: bridle can name a band or restraining fold of tissue that ties one part to another.

Register and nuance: the concrete riding sense remains everyday wherever horses are ridden; the figurative senses are the living core of the word in general English. As a noun bridle is practical and precise; as a verb it is at once moral (restraint) and expressive (anger). The word carries the whole culture of horsemanship and self-government into ordinary speech, and its three senses — control of the horse, control of passion, and offended resistance to control — have coexisted in English for centuries.

Historical semantic bridge: the word descends from Old English brīdel, itself formed from the Germanic root for "to weave, braid, jerk" — the bridle as "a thing of braided cords, a thing that pulls and jerks." All three senses hang on the same physical object: control of the horse (literal), control of passion (metaphor), and the horse's own resentful resistance to control (the angry toss of the head). All are visible in English texts by the 14th–17th centuries and have survived together into the present day.
