- Old English (æspe / æps): Exclusively denoted the Eurasian trembling poplar tree (*Populus tremula*), valued for lightweight timber, shields, and arrow shafts.
- 14th–16th Century (Middle English & Early Modern): The adjective form 'aspen' (originally meaning 'made of asp-wood' or 'pertaining to the asp') gradually supplanted the original noun 'asp/æps', heavily cemented by the pervasive cultural idiom 'to shake/tremble like an aspen leaf' (prominent in Chaucer and Spenser).
- 17th–18th Century: Became firmly established across poetic and dramatic literature as the universal archetype of vulnerability, anxiety, emotional agitation, and rhythmic somatic shivering.
- 19th Century–Present: Expanded in North America to designate the vast clonal colonies of the quaking aspen (*Populus tremuloides*), while remaining a staple literary metaphor for nervous quaking.
