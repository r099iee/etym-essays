- Classical Latin — taming beasts: domitare names the thorough taming of wild animals, the strong frequentative of domare.
- 12th c. — Old French softens it: danter means to tame, subdue, or break the spirit of beasts and rebels alike.
- 13th c. — Middle English borrows courage into it: daunten now subdues hearts as well as horses — to daunt is to quell daring itself.
- 14th–16th c. — knights and steeds: romances tell of daunted chargers and daunted foes, the word equally at home in stable and battlefield.
- 16th c. onward — the negative triumphs: undaunted becomes the set phrase of eulogy and epic, outshining its positive as courage's favourite adjective.
- Today — the inward participle: daunted survives chiefly as a feeling — daunted by tasks, distances, and odds — dignified, literary, and quietly heroic in the negative.
