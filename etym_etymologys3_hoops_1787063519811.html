A word with no known parent. Most of the words in this batch can be traced to a confident Indo-European root. Hoop is the exception: its documented history begins in the West Germanic branch of the family, as Proto-West Germanic *hōp, a noun for a circular band, and beyond that point the trail simply gives out. There are no convincing cognates in Latin, Greek, or Sanskrit, and etymologists from the OED onward describe the ultimate origin as unknown — perhaps an ancient borrowing, perhaps a native Germanic creation for the cooper's craft. The honest answer, therefore, is that the first hoopmaker's word for their invention is lost to us.

Old English: the cooper's band. In Old English hōp (with a long o) was the everyday word for such a band, and it belongs squarely to the world of craft and trade: barrels, tubs, buckets, and drums all needed hoops if they were to hold together, and the language also used the verb in the sense of binding with hoops. The word is recorded from around the eighth and ninth centuries, a workman's term in a vocabulary still close to its Germanic roots.

Middle English and the iron age of the wheel. Middle English kept hoop (spelled variously hoop, hope, houp) as metalworking and transport advanced. The great consumers of hoops were now the cart and wagon wheels, whose wooden rims were shod and strengthened with iron hoops — the blacksmith's familiar trade of heating a hoop, dropping it over the wheel, and quenching it to shrink tight. The same technique armed buckets, tubs, and increasingly large casks for trade, and French cooper vocabulary mingled with English in the workshop.

Renaissance to nineteenth century: the hoop goes ornamental. From the 16th century the hoop began to escape the workshop. Whalebone and later steel hoops built the famous hoop petticoat (the farthingale of the 1500s and the crinoline of the 1800s), turning the word into a fashion term, sometimes admired and often satirised for the huge circles it gave skirts. In the 19th century hoops became children's play: boys rolled wooden hoops with sticks through Victorian streets, and croquet's wire arches were called hoops in Britain. A different sense — the hoop as a ring to throw at in fairground games — also spread.

The basketball revolution. In 1891 James Naismith hung a peach basket on a gymnasium wall, and the "hoop" acquired its most famous modern meaning: the ring and net of the basketball goal. Within decades "shooting hoops" meant playing the game, and by the late 20th century the bare plural "hoops" was a common American synonym for basketball itself — an astonishing career for a word that once named a barrel band. The 1958 Hula Hoop added a global toy craze, and the idiom "go through hoops" gave the old circus image a bureaucratic life.

  • Sound history. The vowel has been essentially stable: PGmc *ō survives into Old English as hōp, and Modern English /huːp/ shows the Great Vowel Shift raising ō → uː; the -s plural hoops is regular.
  • Attestations. Old English glossaries; Middle English craft records; Shakespeare uses "hoop" figuratively in Julius Caesar; Dickens and nineteenth-century fiction widely use the hoop-skirt sense.
  • Cultural note. No other English word has migrated from the cooper's shed to the basketball court in quite so direct a line — proof that a plain, useful circle keeps getting new jobs.
