1. The deep root: breathing and blowing — Blast descends from the Indo-European root *bʰleh₁-, "to blow", one of a family of expressive roots (with variants *bʰel-, *bʰlē-) denoting blowing, swelling, and breathing. The same root gave Latin flāre "to blow" (whence inflate, flatulence), Greek blastós "sprout" (via the sense of swelling), and the Germanic verb blow itself. The image at the base is a puff of breath; everything else — wind, explosion, blight, and curse — grows out of that single gust.

2. The Germanic formation — In Proto-Germanic the noun *blēstaz, "a blowing, a blast, a breath of wind", was formed on the verb *blēsan "to blow" with an -st/-þ abstract suffix. Old English inherited it as blǣst, meaning "a blowing, a blast of wind, a breeze; a breath; an inspiration". Already in Old English the word was used of violent wind and of a divine or destructive "blast", so the association of a blast with harm is as old as the word itself.

3. Middle English expansion — In Middle English blast remained primarily a wind-word: a "blast" is a sudden, violent gust. But the word also began to mean a loud sound (a trumpet-blast) and — crucially — a sudden destructive influence. From this emerges the verb: by the 14th century, to blast means "to blow violently", and by the 16th century it has acquired two explosive meanings: "to blight or wither (as if by a malevolent wind)" and "to blow up with gunpowder". The gunpowder revolution of the early modern period cemented the modern sense of an explosion, in war, in quarrying, and in engineering.

4. The blight sense — The agricultural sense is very old: a "blasting wind" was a dry, searing wind that withered crops, and "to blast" meant to strike with blight. In the 16th-17th centuries this became a dominant poetic sense — Milton's "blast of Heaven", the blasted tree struck by lightning, the "blasted heath" of Macbeth (a landscape withered by evil). This is the register Tolkien taps into when he describes the dragon-blasted halls of the mountain: the word brings with it centuries of scorched heaths and accursed wastelands.

5. The oath sense — By the 17th century, "blast!" developed as a mild imprecation — to "blast" someone was to curse them with blight or destruction. Hence blasted = "damned, confounded": "that blasted fellow", "you blasted idiot". The interjection and adjective became fixed features of colloquial English, and remain slightly old-fashioned but fully alive today. In the 20th century, slang extended "blasted" to mean extremely drunk, and "to have a blast" flipped the word into an idiom of pure enjoyment — a spectacular reversal of a word that once meant destruction.

  • c. 4500-2500 BCE: PIE *bʰleh₁- "to blow".
  • c. 500 BCE - 100 CE: Proto-Germanic *blēstaz "a blowing, a blast".
  • c. 700 CE: Old English blǣst "a blowing, blast of wind, breath".
  • 14th c.: Middle English verb blasten "to blow violently".
  • 16th c.: Senses of blight/withering and of exploding gunpowder appear.
  • 17th c.: Miltonic "blasted heath"; colloquial curse "blast it!" and adjective blasted = "damned".
  • 20th-21st c.: Explosion sense dominates; "have a blast" = enjoy enormously; "blasted" survives as a mild oath.

6. Sound shifts — The chain blǣst → blast shows the Old English long æ (æː) shortening and lowering to /a/ in Middle English before a final consonant cluster, a routine reduction in English phonology. The final -t of the noun (from the Germanic abstract suffix) is what distinguishes blast from blow, its sibling verb from the same root: both ultimately go back to the same PIE base, one with and one without the suffix.

7. Summary — Blasted is a single word carrying 2,500 years of wind, blight, gunpowder, and cursing. In Tolkien's prose it gathers the oldest strata — the searing wind of the blasting heath and the devastation of force — into a description of a dragon's ruin. From a puff of breath it has become the vocabulary of a blasted land.
