PIE *per- “to pass through, lead across, go forth; a crossing” (reconstructed root, conventionally dated c. 4000–3000 BCE) → Latin porta “gate, entrance, city-gate” (attested 1st c. BCE; the passage one goes through) → Latin porticus “colonnade, covered gallery, portico attached to a public building” (attested 1st c. BCE) → Old French porche “porch, covered entrance” (attested 9th–12th c.; Vulgar Latin -tic- develops to Old French -ch-) → Middle English porche / porch “covered entrance structure” (attested c. 1300, after the Norman Conquest) → Modern English PORCH “covered shelter projecting at the entrance of a building” (present day). Debated / nuanced note: The linking of Latin porta (and its family portus “harbor,” portāre “to carry”) to PIE *per- is the standard handbook reconstruction — the root denotes passage across, and Italic nominal forms built on it yield “a way through.” It is uncontroversial as a family tree. What is sometimes debated is whether the root's original sense was “cross over / go through” or “spread / penetrate” (the same root yields English far, fare, ford, and Latin per “through”). Additionally, English later re-borrowed the Latin form directly as portico (17th c.), giving the pair porch (French-medieval, domestic) versus portico (Latin-learned, monumental) — two shapes of one ancestor. The historical, attested portion (Latin through English) is secure; only the PIE stage is reconstructed.
