BOLSTER is both a noun and a verb. As a noun it means a long, usually cylindrical pillow or cushion, or any padded support used to prop something up; as a verb it means to support, strengthen, prop up, or reinforce, whether physically or figuratively. The verb is a direct descendant of the noun: to bolster something is, at root, to put a bolster under it so it does not sag or fall. Because a bolster is a thing placed beneath another thing to hold it up, the word has grown, by the most natural of metaphors, from the bed to the building site, the workshop, and the most abstract reaches of modern language — we now bolster confidence, arguments, economies, and morale.

1. The noun — a long pillow or padded support. In its oldest and plainest sense a bolster is a long, firm, cylindrical pillow placed across the head of a bed, under the head of a sleeper, or along the back of a sofa or bench — the kind of rolled cushion that props a head or a back. The word is first recorded in Old English, and the object is older still: the long padded roll is a piece of furniture that has supported sleepers for millennia. In modern homes the bolster survives as a decorative or supportive roll, and in the language of furnishing it remains the standard term for a long cylindrical cushion as distinct from a flat pillow.

2. The verb — to support, prop up, or reinforce. The verb is first recorded in the sixteenth century and means, concretely, "to prop up or support with a bolster," and then, swiftly, "to support, hold up, or keep from falling by any means." From the beginning the verb had a figurative dimension: a weak argument, a wavering reputation, a failing institution could be "bolstered" — propped up from beneath, kept standing when it would otherwise collapse. This is the sense in the card's example ("They took steps to BOLSTER the local economy") and the reading context ("We will need to BOLSTER both the Lannister").

3. Architecture and engineering — the structural bolster. In masonry, joinery, and engineering a "bolster" is a short horizontal timber, stone, or steel plate set atop a post or wall to spread the load and support a beam, girder, or girder-end. The bolster here does for a building exactly what the pillow does for a sleeper: it sits under the load-bearing member and keeps it from sagging, spreading the pressure over a wider surface. Related technical uses include the bolster plate under a spring, the cross-member of a railway carriage or bogie that carries the body of the vehicle, and the bolster used to support a anvil or a heavy object in workshops.

4. Cutlery and tools — the knife bolster and workshop bolsters. In cutlery, the "bolster" is the thick metal junction between the blade and the handle of a knife — a solid collar that gives the knife balance, keeps the hand from slipping onto the blade, and strengthens the joint. It is a small, precise technical sense, but the logic is the same: the bolster is the strengthening, load-bearing piece at a point of stress. In other trades the word recurs for padded or strengthening supports: the pad worn under armour, the support for a glassblower's pipe, the blunt padded knife used in tanning, and the pad that cushions a typewriter carriage or a printing press.

5. Psychology, rhetoric, and economics — the modern abstract verb. In modern usage bolster is overwhelmingly abstract. We "bolster confidence," "bolster self-esteem," "bolster morale," "bolster an argument," "bolster the case for reform," "bolster the economy," "bolster national defence," "bolster the troops." The word has become the standard verb for strengthening anything — a person, an institution, a position, a case — by adding support from without. In this abstract use the word has largely lost the old suggestion of artificial or precarious padding and simply means "to strengthen, reinforce, or encourage." The pillow has become a verb for every kind of support in public life.

How the senses hang together (the semantic bridge). Every sense of bolster, concrete or abstract, rests on one image: a thing placed under another thing to hold it up. The ancient Germanic word for a stuffed pillow moved from the bed into the workshop, the building site, the railway yard, and the knife-maker's bench, and then — through the natural metaphor of "propping up" — into the language of argument, morale, and economics. A government "bolsters" an ailing bank exactly as a medieval housewife propped a sleeper's head on a rolled cushion: the support is placed beneath, and the sagging thing is held upright.

Summary of senses.

  • Noun: a long, cylindrical pillow or padded support; a cushion that props a head or back.
  • Noun (technical): a horizontal timber or steel plate that spreads load onto a post or wall; a railway carriage cross-member; the thick metal collar between a knife blade and handle.
  • Verb: to support or prop up with a bolster; to hold something up from beneath.
  • Verb (figurative): to strengthen, reinforce, or encourage — arguments, confidence, morale, economies, institutions.
  • Noun/verb continuity: the verb is a functional shift of the noun, and every sense preserves the "support placed beneath" image.
