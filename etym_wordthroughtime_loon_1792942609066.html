- Medieval Scots — the idle youth: loun scolds lazy lads and low wenches hanging about the parish with nothing good in hand.
- 17th c. — the bird lands in English: northern sailors and settlers borrow Norse lómr for the great diver, spelled loom and loon in logs and journals.
- 18th c. — Burns's loons: the rogue-word colours Scots song and verse, sturdy, comic, and slightly disreputable.
- 19th c. — the fool proper: loon south of the border now means lout, simpleton, and idle fellow, the ballad insult of the company killjoy.
- 20th c. — madness joins in: lunatic clips to loony, cartoons marry it to the bird's crazy laughter, and acting like a loon means acting unhinged but harmless.
- Today — the daft mate and the wild lake share one word: affectionate insult for the eccentric, reverent name for the wilderness diver with the loneliest cry on water.
