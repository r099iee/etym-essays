Derivation chain for EMBODIMENT (morphological compound: en- + body + -ment): 1. Old English bodig "trunk, frame, body" (attested in Old English prose and poetry) — no secure PIE ancestry; attested only in the West Germanic dialects (Old English bodig, Old Frisian bodi, Middle Dutch boteghe, Old Saxon bodig) with no accepted cognates in Gothic, Old Norse, Latin, Greek, or Sanskrit. The safest label is Unknown/Isolate; proposals range from a pre-Germanic substrate word to a wandering loan, but none commands general assent. 2. Middle English bodi "body, the physical frame of a living thing" (13th c.) — regular phonological development from bodig, with the final -g voiced/weakened to -i. 3. English body "the physical frame of a person or animal" — the plain Germanic noun, whose extended senses (a body of men, a body of water, a body of work, the body politic, bodyguard, somebody, nobody) were all built in English. 4. English EMBODIMENT "tangible form of an abstraction; the act of giving body" (coined c. 1820) — formed on the English verb embody (c. 1540), which is en- "in, into" (from Latin in- and Greek en-) + body, plus the noun-forming suffix -ment (from Latin -mentum, the old suffix of instrument and result, as in payment, movement, judgement). Note on the morphological components: - The prefix en- in embody goes back ultimately to Latin in- "in, into" and Greek en-, the productive English prefix for "put into, make, provide with" (as in enclose, enable, enact). It is a grammatical element, not an independent root word with its own semantic chain. - The suffix -ment comes from Latin -mentum via Old French -ment, and marks the result or instrument of an action. Again a grammatical element. - There is therefore a single lexical root word — body (from Old English bodig) — and the derivation chain for the whole word runs through it. Note on a parallel, unrelated line (debated points / do not merge): - Latin corpus "body" is a completely different etymon. It yields English corporal, corporeal, corps, corpse, corporation, incorporate, corpulence, and corpus itself, but it has no connection with Old English bodig, whose origin is unknown. The two "body" lines must never be merged. - The exact formation of embody in the 1540s is modelled on earlier uses of "to body" (as in the archaic to body forth), with the prefix en- added; some handbooks cite Tyndale-era religious prose, where the verb's first notable uses concern the Eucharist "embodying" the body of Christ.
