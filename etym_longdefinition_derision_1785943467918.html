DERISION denotes contemptuous, mocking, or scornful laughter and ridicule directed at someone or something perceived as absurd, foolish, or unworthy of respect. It implies an attitude of malicious superiority, in which the speaker or crowd laughs down upon the target in order to humiliate, invalidate, or ostracize them. In rhetorical, literary, and social registers, derision is not lighthearted teasing; it is a sharp social weapon designed to inflict embarrassment and express open disdain. The word is etymologically "laughing down at" someone — from the Latin dē- "down" and rīdēre "to laugh" — and that spatial image of looking down while laughing captures the contemptuous stance perfectly.

In the source context ("He faced the derision of his peers"), the word describes the hostile social reception of a person by his equals: his peers subjected him to contemptuous mockery, so that he had to endure open ridicule from the very people whose approval he might have sought. The collocation "the derision of" signals a collective, organized contempt.

Primary Senses

  • Contemptuous ridicule / mockery: the act of openly laughing at someone or something in a scornful, demeaning way; the standard modern sense.
  • Scornful laughter itself: the laughter or jeering that expresses contempt — the vocal, visible manifestation of disdain (e.g. "the crowd burst into derision").
  • The state of being derided: "to be held in derision" means to be the object of general contemptuous mockery; "to bring into derision" means to expose someone or something to public ridicule.
  • Rhetorical / literary device (as effect): the mocking laughter provoked by a target, used by satirists and polemicists as the intended effect of their attack (though derision itself is direct contempt, whereas satire is an artful vehicle).

Secondary, Figurative and Register Senses

  • Social and psychological dimension: in modern social psychology and discourse, "derision" names a form of social aggression — mobbing, public shaming, cancel-culture jeering — used to marginalize a person or idea; it is the laughing face of ostracism.
  • Journalistic / political register: "facing derision," "met with derision," "greeted with derision" are standard collocations in news writing for a policy, proposal, or public figure treated with contemptuous mockery.
  • Religious / biblical register: "to have (someone) in derision" is a biblical and homiletic phrase (e.g. Psalm 2:4, "the Lord shall have them in derision"), meaning to mock or laugh at from a position of absolute superiority; the phrase preserves the older, more formal sense.
  • Legal / parliamentary nuance (rare, technical): in some procedural writing, "derision" can describe the treatment of an argument as beneath serious consideration — dismissal through mockery rather than reasoned rebuttal.
  • Metonymic extension: "derision" can stand for the whole social atmosphere of contemptuous mockery surrounding a target ("a climate of derision").

Collocations and Phraseology

  • "in derision," "with derision," "a shout/laugh of derision," "a wave of derision," "derision of his peers," "held in derision," "to bring (something) into derision," "to laugh someone to scorn (an allied idiom)."
  • "Howls of derision," "snorts of derision," "hisses of derision" — collocations that make the vocal, auditory nature of the mockery explicit.

Word Family and Related Forms

  • Deride (verb): to mock contemptuously, to laugh at with scorn; the direct English verb from Latin dērīdēre.
  • Derisive (adjective): showing derision; mocking, scornful ("a derisive laugh").
  • Derisory (adjective): deserving or inviting derision because absurdly small or inadequate ("a derisory offer"); note that derisive (active, mocking) and derisory (passive, laughably inadequate) are distinct in meaning.
  • Risible (adjective): laughable, capable of provoking laughter — from the same Latin root rīdēre, but focused on the object's laughability rather than the mocker's contempt.
  • Ridicule (noun/verb) and ridiculous (adjective): the most frequent near-synonyms, likewise from rīdēre.

Semantic Boundaries and Near-Synonym Comparisons

  • Derision vs. Ridicule: ridicule is the act of making fun through mockery or satire; derision carries the harsher, more contemptuous tone of hostile laughter meant to demean.
  • Derision vs. Mockery: mockery often works by imitation, parody, or absurd representation; derision focuses on the malicious emotional expression of laughing down at someone.
  • Derision vs. Scorn: scorn is a general feeling of extreme contempt that may be silent; derision is that contempt made audible and visible as mocking laughter or jeering.
  • Derision vs. Satire: satire is an artful, often constructive literary genre using irony and exaggeration to criticize vices; derision is direct, destructive, personal mockery.
  • Derision vs. Contempt: contempt is the attitude; derision is the active expression of that attitude in mocking laughter.

Historical-Semantic Bridge

The word entered English in the 14th century from Old French, carrying the Latin sense built on dē- "down" + rīdēre "to laugh": literally "to laugh down at," i.e. to laugh at someone from a position of superiority. The spatial and social image has remained remarkably stable for seven centuries: derision is still laughter directed downward from a position of felt superiority, and the word's power lies in that unshakeable image of contemptuous descending laughter.
