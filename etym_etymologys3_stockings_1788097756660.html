The word STOCKINGS is a quiet monument to the history of dress. Its chain runs from an Indo-European root meaning “to push, strike”, through the Germanic world’s words for the trunk of a tree, into the medieval wardrobe, the knitting frames of Elizabethan England, and the nylon-buying crowds of the twentieth century.

The root: PIE *(s)teu-. The base of the family is the Indo-European root *(s)teu-, “to push, strike, stick”, with its k-extension *(s)teuk-, which produced the Proto-Germanic noun *stukkaz, “trunk, stump, stock” — the broad, rooted lower part of a tree, a meaning that remained the core of the word for two thousand years. Old English stocc continues it directly: “stump, log, post, trunk”, the timber spar from which stocks (punishment frames) and stockades were made. The semantic line to clothing is a metaphor: the leg was conceived as the body’s stock, its sturdy trunk-like support, so that the fabric which covered the leg was the cloth “of the stock” — the stocking.

From hose to stockings: the clothing revolution of the 16th century. In medieval England, as across Europe, men and women wore hose — a single close garment covering the feet, legs, and (for men) the trunk, joined at the waist. During the sixteenth century the garment split. The upper part became the padded “trunk hose” or “upper stocks”; the lower, leg-covering part became the “nether stocks” — the stockings. Elizabethan wardrobes record stockings as the most expensive single garment a person could own: tradition relates that in 1561 Mrs. Montague, the Queen’s silk woman, offered Elizabeth I a pair of knitted silk stockings, and that the Queen never again wore cloth hose. Whether or not the anecdote is exact, it is certain that by 1600 silk knit stockings were luxury objects at court and that the 1589 invention of the stocking-frame — the hand-knitting machine credited to William Lee of Calverton — began the mechanisation of hosiery. The “stocking frame” was among the first industrial machines, and its spread through Nottingham, Leicester, and London made stockings the first mass-produced knitted garment in Europe.

  • c. 1500–1600: hose splits; “stocks” and “stockings” name the leg part of the garment; knitted stockings begin to replace woven cloth hose.
  • 1589: William Lee’s stocking-frame — hosiery becomes machine industry; a stocking could be knit in hours instead of days.
  • 1600s–1700s: worsted and silk stockings are standard dress for both sexes; exports of English stockings become a major trade.

The 18th century: blue-stockings and cotton. In eighteenth-century London, the literary salon of Elizabeth Montagu drew guests who, it is recorded, preferred plain comfort to powdered court dress; Benjamin Stillingfleet is said to have appeared in blue worsted stockings rather than the silk of fashion, and the circle was mocked — and proudly named — the “Bluestockings”. Whatever the anecdote’s precise truth, the word entered the language as a label for learned, bookish women. Meanwhile cotton yarn and machine knitting lowered prices until, by 1800, stockings were universal: worn by labourers, servants, soldiers, and children, in cotton, wool, and silk.

The 19th century: universality and concealment. In Victorian dress the stocking was a garment that everybody wore and almost nobody spoke about: concealed under long skirts and trouser legs, it belonged to the folded, intimate world of underwear. Its invisibility in public made it a potent literary synecdoche — “no shoes or stockings” as the mark of poverty, as in Oscar Wilde’s The Happy Prince (1888), where the barefoot, bare-headed little girl is a figure of want. The century also completed the semantic split of the century’s tailoring: short “socks” (a different word, from Latin soccus, the classical actor’s slipper) took over ordinary men’s wear, leaving “stockings” increasingly for women and children.

The 20th century: nylon, glamour, and nostalgia. The twentieth century turned the word into an icon of femininity and modernity. Rayon and then DuPont’s nylon (announced 1938) made sheer stockings cheap; nylon stockings, first sold in 1940, were so desirable that when American nylon production was diverted to the war effort in 1941–42, a black market flourished and women painted “seams” down their legs with make-up in imitation of the vanished garment. After the war stockings became the standard dress of the urban woman, held up by suspenders and garters, until the invention of pantyhose in the early 1960s offered a combined garment and supplanted them for daily wear. Since then the plural stockings has drifted toward the festive and the nostalgic: the Christmas stocking hung by the fire (the St Nicholas tradition of gold dropped into stockings left drying), the “stocking stuffer”, the vintage-shop silk stocking, the lingerie shelf. The word has completed a full arc — from everyday cloth to courtly luxury to hidden underwear to rationed glamour to festive nostalgia — without ever leaving the semantic territory of the knitted leg-tube first staked out by the medieval “stock” of the leg.
