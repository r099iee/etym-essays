First root word: brine - Origin uncertain: no secure PIE ancestry is established. The traditional hypothesis attaches Old English brīne to the PIE root *bhreu- "to boil, to bubble, to seethe" (reconstructed) — the image of salt water "brewing" and effervescing — whose other reflexes are Latin fervere "to boil" (English fervent, fervid, effervesce) and Germanic *brewwaną "to brew" (English brew, broth). But this link is a hypothesis only, and the OED treats the word's deeper origin as uncertain; there are no secure cognates of brīne outside Germanic. (Sanskrit bhurvan "restless, agitated" is a possible but debated reflex of *bhreu-.) - Old English brīne "salt water" (attested in the earliest English texts and glosses) — the first recorded English form. - Old Norse brími "the sea; brine" (attested, poetic) — a Germanic relative pointing to an old Germanic word for salt water. - Middle English brine (attested, 12th–15th c.) — the medieval continuant. - English brine "salt water, the pickling bath" (attested from Middle English onward) — the modern word. - English brine (the form required by the passage: "the brine of desert seas"). Note on debate/nuance: the origin of brine is genuinely uncertain. The *bhreu- "to boil" connection is traditional and plausible but unproven; the word has no established cognates outside Germanic, and some scholars treat it as an isolated Germanic formation of expressive origin.
Second root word: ebbed - PIE root *apo- "away, off, from" (reconstructed; one of the family's most widespread prepositions) — reflexes include Sanskrit ápa "away" (apakrama, "departure"; Hindi aparādh "crime," literally "acting against"), Greek apó "from, away" (English apology, apogee, apostle, apocalypse), and Latin ab "away from" (abduct, abstract, absent, aborigine "from the beginning"). - Proto-Germanic *abjōn- "receding water, ebb" (reconstructed) — the Germanic specialization of "away" applied to the withdrawing tide; alongside it the verb *abjāną "to ebb, to flow back." - Old English ebba "low tide" (attested, with the verb ebbian "to flow back") — the direct ancestor; German Ebbe and Dutch eb descend from related Low German forms. - Middle English ebbe (attested, 12th–15th c.) — the noun and verb. - English ebb "the going out of the tide; to flow back" (attested from Middle English onward) — the modern noun and verb. - English ebbed (past tense / past participle, attested) — the form required by the passage: "desert seas that have ebbed to desert pools." - English ebbed. Note on nuance: the chain is secure; *apo- is one of the best-attested PIE roots. The only point worth noting is the Germanic specialization — the root's abstract "away" was narrowed to the specific motion of the retreating tide, a narrowing that happened in the Germanic branch alone, and that later widened again in the figurative "to ebb away" (of life, strength, hope).
