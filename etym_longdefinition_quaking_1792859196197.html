Quaking, the present participle of the verb quake, means shaking, shivering, or trembling with rapid small involuntary movements, most often from fear, dread, cold, weakness, or overwhelming emotion. Where shaking can be voluntary (shaking a bottle, shaking hands) and shuddering suggests a single convulsive ripple, quaking implies sustained, helpless vibration: knees that knock together, a voice that will not hold steady, ground that will not stay still. It is the body confessing what the will would rather conceal.

In the source sentence the word appears as reported speech breaking under feeling: Frodo, sir! cried Sam quaking. Sam’s voice and frame shake as he cries out, and the participle tells us his terror or grief is stronger than his Hobbit courage. The reader hears the tremor inside the cry: loyalty forcing the words out while fear shakes them on the way. Tolkien, who knew Old English poetry intimately, chose one of the oldest fear-words in the language for this moment, letting Sam’s body speak the dread his devotion refuses to obey.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Trembling from fear or dread (primary human sense): quaking with terror, quaking in one’s boots. The bridge from earthquake to emotion is the shared experience of uncontrollable vibration: the frightened body feels internally what the earthquake does externally, ground giving way beneath the self.
  • Shaking of the earth itself (oldest literal sense): the quaking earth, quaking ground before an earthquake. Old English used the verb for the shaking of anything solid, and this geological use preserves the original scale: whole landscapes trembling as a frightened animal trembles.
  • Shivering from cold, fever, or weakness (bodily sense): quaking limbs after exposure, a quaking voice in illness. The bridge is physiological: cold and fever produce the same rapid muscular oscillation as fear, so one verb serves every involuntary tremor regardless of cause.
  • Vibrating or Resonating (extended physical sense): quaking leaves, quaking strings, the quaking aspen whose foliage shimmers at the slightest breeze. The bridge is visual and acoustic: leaves of the aspen (Populus tremula, literally the trembling poplar) shake continuously, and poets borrowed the fear-word to honour their restlessness.
  • The Quaker connection (historical religious sense): members of the Society of Friends were nicknamed Quakers in the 1650s partly for their trembling under religious emotion. The bridge is mockery turned badge: outsiders heard quaking voices in worship and coined a name the community eventually owned with pride.
  • Moral or institutional trembling (figurative): quaking thrones, quaking markets, a quaking resolve. The bridge personifies abstractions: a regime or confidence imagined as a body that can tremble, so political fear borrows the physiology of knees and voices.

Semantic bridges and register: all senses share the entailment of involuntariness: quaking is what happens to you, never what you perform. That is why the word carries pity where trembling can carry delicacy and shaking can carry energy. A quaking voice asks for protection; a quaking earth warns of judgement. The register is literary and earnest rather than clinical: doctors write tremor, seismologists write quake as a noun, but storytellers write quaking when they want the reader to feel the vibration in their own chest, as the source sentence does with Sam’s cry.
