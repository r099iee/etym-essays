• Disposition: Similar because both denote a person's characteristic nature; differs because "disposition" stresses habitual inclination (a disposition to be cheerful), while "temperament" emphasizes the inborn emotional constitution itself.
  • Nature: Similar because both name inherent character; differs because "nature" is the broadest word for everything innate to a person or thing, while "temperament" specifically concerns emotional and behavioural style.
  • Character: Similar because both describe a person's qualities; differs because "character" emphasizes learned and morally judged traits (good or bad character), while "temperament" is the inborn emotional makeup, not a moral judgment.
  • Personality: Similar because both describe the individual pattern of behaviour; differs because in modern psychology "personality" is the whole outward self, including learned traits, while "temperament" is the biologically given core on which personality builds.
  • Temper: Similar because both come from the same Latin root and both describe emotional nature; differs because "temper" is informal and often names a momentary mood or irritability ("to lose one's temper"), while "temperament" is the stable disposition, not a passing mood.
  • Constitution: Similar because both describe the make-up of a person; differs because "constitution" emphasizes bodily health and robustness (a strong constitution), while "temperament" emphasizes emotional nature.
  • Mettle: Similar because both describe inherent quality of character; differs because "mettle" (historically "metal") concerns courage and spirit under trial — a narrower valour sense — while "temperament" is the whole emotional framework.
  • Humour (archaic "humor"): Similar because in old medicine both describe the temperament type produced by the balance of the humors; differs because "humour" as a personality term is now archaic except in phrases like "good humour", while "temperament" is current.
  • Frame of mind: Similar because both describe a person's mental state; differs because "frame of mind" is temporary and situational, while "temperament" is stable and characteristic.
  • Mood: Similar because both concern emotional state; differs because a mood is transient, lasting hours or days, while temperament is lifelong and typical.
  • Bent: Similar because both describe an inherent inclination; differs because "bent" is a natural leaning toward an activity or talent (a bent for mathematics), while "temperament" is the emotional make-up as a whole.
  • Makeup: Similar because both describe the composite of qualities that form a person; differs because "makeup" is colloquial and general, while "temperament" specifically names the emotional constitution.
  • In music — tuning / intonation: Similar because "temperament" in music is a system of tuning intervals; differs because "temperament" names the specific system of interval adjustment (equal temperament), while "tuning" and "intonation" are the general acts and results of setting pitch.
  • The four types (sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic): Similar because each names a temperament type under humoral theory; differs because each names one specific type, while "temperament" is the general category that contains them.
