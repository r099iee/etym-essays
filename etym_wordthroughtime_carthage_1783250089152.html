- [Ancient emporium] - In antiquity the current English name (from Latin Carthago) denoted the great Phoenician mercantile republic of North Africa, famed for wealth, silver and sea-power - the archetype of the rich trading state.
- [Rome's rival] - Through the Punic Wars the name came to stand for Rome's greatest rival and near-destroyer, its perception in the Roman world shifting to fear and hatred, summed up in the cry 'Carthage must be destroyed'.
- [Emblem of ruin] - After its destruction in 146 BC, 'Carthage' became the classical symbol of total annihilation - a once-glorious city razed so completely that little remained - invoked as a moral fable of hubris punished.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today Carthage is used in English as shorthand for a vanished commercial empire and for the archaeological site near Tunis, its double charge of fabulous wealth and total ruin still intact.
