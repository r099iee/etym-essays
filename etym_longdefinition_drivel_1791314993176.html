The verb and noun drivel denotes foolish, senseless, trivial, or infantile talk and writing, as well as the literal physical act of drooling, slavering, or allowing saliva and mucus to run unchecked from the mouth. In the source passage—where an exasperated interlocutor demands, “‘Must you __________, Bertie?’”—the term functions as an intransitive verb signifying the utterance of silly, inane, or ungrounded nonsense. In the comedic universe of P. G. Wodehouse, this reprimand is a classic conversational weapon used by formidable matriarchs (such as Aunt Agatha or Aunt Dahlia) to puncture Bertie Wooster’s lighthearted, rambling explanations.

Possessing an unbroken lineage rooted in early medieval Germanic terms for thick dregs and bodily slime, drivel is celebrated for its evocative, visceral metaphor: nonsensical speech conceived as oral effluent escaping an undisciplined, slack mouth:

  • Verbal sense in conversational context — to utter foolish nonsense: Used intransitively to describe someone speaking or writing without substance, logic, or maturity: “he continued to drivel on about astrology and fate”; “stop drivelling and get to the point.” It implies mental slackness and garrulous vanity.
  • Literal physiological verb — to drool or slaver: The original historical meaning: to let saliva flow involuntarily from the lips, as observed in teething infants, sleeping animals, intoxicated individuals, or sufferers of extreme senile dotage: “the hound began to drivel at the scent of roast meat.”
  • Nominal sense — inane talk, writing, or media: Functions extensively as an uncountable noun representing utter twaddle, worthless intellectual output, or low-grade entertainment: “the political speech was pure drivel”; “how can you read such sentimental drivel?” It is a staple term of literary and political criticism.
  • Senile decay and cognitive impairment: Historically applied to the physical and mental decline of extreme old age, where involuntary drooling accompanies rambling, childish speech (“drivelling dotage”). This gave the word a particularly cutting edge when hurled as an insult against an adult.
  • Agent noun and participial forms: Produces driveller (a senseless chatterbox or idiot) and the present participle / adjective drivelling (e.g. “a drivelling idiot”), widely used in British satire and comedy to express supreme exasperation.
  • Tone and social register: Direct, punchy, and sharply dismissive. While not vulgar, it possesses far more bite and contempt than mild terms like chatter or prattle, though in polite British drawing-room comedy (as in Wodehouse) it serves as an affectionate, highly stylized reprimand.
  • The visceral semantic bridge: The conceptual bridge is physical and anatomical: just as a slack, uncoordinated mouth fails to contain saliva, so a lazy, undisciplined mind fails to regulate speech. Inane words drip and dribble from the tongue just as slobber does from the lips.

Whether employed in literary reviews to eviscerate bad novels, in political debate to dismiss empty rhetoric, or in comedy to deflate Bertie Wooster, drivel retains its vivid, sensory potency across the modern English-speaking world.
