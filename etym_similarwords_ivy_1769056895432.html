• vine: Similar because it is a climbing plant that covers walls and trellises; differs because vine is a structural term for any plant that climbs by twining, tendrils, or suckers—grapes, gourds, and climbing roses alike—and it says nothing about clinging to walls or staying green through winter; a grape arbour is never "ivy-clad."
  • creeper: Similar because it describes a plant that spreads over a surface and can signal neglect; differs because creeper pictures low, trailing stems rooting as they run along the ground, while ivy grips stone with aerial rootlets and climbs purposefully up a wall; ivy is a creeper, but the word creeper alone loses the vertical tenacity and the evergreenery.
  • climber: Similar because it names a plant that needs support to rise; differs because climber is a neutral gardening word covering anything from twining beans to trained roses—descriptive rather than atmospheric, and it carries none of ivy's connotations of tenacity, evergreenery, or the ruining of masonry.
  • evergreen: Similar because ivy is the paradigmatic evergreen, staying green through winter; differs because evergreen is a broad botanical category naming any plant that keeps its leaves year-round (pines, holly, rhododendron), while ivy is specifically the clinging vine whose winter-green habit makes it a symbol of constancy.
  • moss: Similar because it is a green growth on old stone that also signals neglect and age; differs because moss does not climb or cover windows—it forms soft, damp cushions that speak of moisture and decay rather than the vigorous, spreading possession ivy takes of a ruin; moss clings to dampness, ivy to walls.
  • poison ivy: Similar because the name borrows ivy for a clinging North American climber with a similar leaf-silhouette; differs because poison ivy (Toxicodendron radicans) is a sumac-family plant of a different genus whose resin causes a severe rash and which is deciduous, not evergreen—a name-transfer that shows how loosely "ivy" can be applied.
  • Boston ivy: Similar because it is a wall-clinging climber grown on brick and masonry, often in the same positions as true ivy; differs because Boston ivy (Parthenocissus tricuspidata) belongs to the grape family and is deciduous, turning brilliant red in autumn, while true ivy is evergreen and of the genus Hedera.
  • ground ivy: Similar because it shares the name and the family of creeping green ground-cover; differs because ground ivy (Glechoma hederacea) is a low creeping mint that roots at the nodes and rarely climbs, with no woody stems and no aerial rootlets—its name is a folk transfer from the true climber.
