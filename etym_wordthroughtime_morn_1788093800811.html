- Old English morgen — the everyday word for the day's beginning (and for 'tomorrow'), with no poetic colour in ordinary prose.
- Middle English — the short form morn(e) emerges beside morwen and morwe; verse quickly favours its single syllable.
- Early Modern English — morn is standard poetic diction: 'the morn, in russet mantle clad' (Shakespeare, Hamlet); Milton's 'Sweet is the breath of morn' (Paradise Lost IV); still unmarked in speech, while morning claims the prose.
- 18th–19th century — the Romantics and Victorians fix morn as the poetical word par excellence; fixed phrases ('from morn till noon') keep it in circulation; Scots keeps 'the morn' = tomorrow in living speech.
- 20th–21st century — morn survives in hymns, headlines ('election morn') and fantasy prose; its perception today: wistful, antique, gently lofty — poetry's dawn.
