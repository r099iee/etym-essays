RECREATION — noun. Core meaning: the refreshment of the body or mind through pleasant activity; diversion, amusement, or play that restores one's spent strength and spirits. Etymologically the word is literally "re-creation": the prefix re- ("again") + the verb create ("to make, bring into being"), so that recreation names the making anew of a person, the rebuilding of energy that labour has consumed.

In this context the word carries its full restorative force, sharpened by irony. The sentence records that re-reading the Iliad and the Odyssey in the original Greek before reviewing Grote's Greece "involved a good deal of work" and yet was "recreation to the student." The paradox is deliberate and admiring: what ordinary minds would judge the most gruelling scholarship — two ancient epics, in the original tongue, in full — operated on this student as the very thing that renewed his powers. Recreation here is not the absence of effort but the redirection of it; the mind is rested not by stopping but by changing what it does. The word therefore asserts that for a temperament of exceptional intellectual appetite, intense study is itself restorative: it re-creates mental vigour rather than exhausting it. The sentence preserves the older, medicinal logic of the term, in which recreation is an activity that rebuilds a person from weariness.

Full polysemy and historical senses. The word has accumulated a wide set of meanings, each of which can be understood as a development from the root idea of "re-creating" or "remaking":

  • Restorative refreshment (primary sense, 14th–17th centuries): the renewing of body or spirit after labour or illness; an activity that restores strength and vigour. This is the sense active in the present context, where scholarship itself is the refreshment.
  • Leisure activity / amusement (dominant modern sense): any pleasant pastime, game, sport, hobby, or playful pursuit carried out in free time. Here the stress falls on enjoyment rather than on literal rebuilding.
  • School recess (chiefly British, via French récréation): the short, scheduled break during the school day when children leave the classroom to play — institutionalised recreation, the older restorative logic reduced to a change of occupation.
  • Re-creation, the making again of something (hyphenated): the reconstruction or re-enactment of an event, scene, or object — a historical re-creation of a battle, the re-creation of a lost manuscript. Here the prefix keeps full force: something is made anew. This is the literal etymological sense, nominalising "re-create."
  • Administrative and bureaucratic jargon: recreation as a public provision — recreation centres, recreation grounds, recreation departments, the "recreation industry." The term becomes the official name of organised leisure.
  • Professional and academic jargon: recreation therapy (or therapeutic recreation), recreation leadership, recreation programming, and recreation ecology (the study of leisure's impact on natural environments).
  • Metaphoric and extended uses: "mental recreation" as a change of work that rests the mind; in criticism, the "recreation" of a historical period as its imaginative reconstruction; in law and insurance, "recreational activities" as a liability category.

Semantic bridges. The development runs from the physical to the social and the institutional. In Latin and early French the word named the action of restoring a person (recreatio, "refreshment, recovery"); the notion of rebuilding strength then attached itself to the activity that produces that refreshment — a pleasant pursuit undertaken after work; from there the meaning widened to any leisure occupation, whatever its effect; and finally, with the growth of organised leisure in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the word became the administrative label for play itself. The separate hyphenated sense of "re-creation" (making something again) has run in parallel, anchored in the verb "re-create," and the two now collide in writing: "recreation" (relaxation) and "re-creation" (remaking) are homographs distinguished only by stress and, optionally, the hyphen.

Register and usage notes.

  • Formal/literary: recreation in the restorative sense, as here, is deliberately dignified and slightly archaic in flavour.
  • Colloquial: fun, relaxation, down time — words that replace recreation in casual speech.
  • British administrative: "the Rec" (recreation ground), recreation centre, recreational facilities.
  • Spelling trap: to recreate = to take recreation; to re-create = to make again; a standing hazard in English prose.
