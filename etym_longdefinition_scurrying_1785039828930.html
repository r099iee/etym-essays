**SCURRYING — short, rapid, agitated steps** At its core, *scurrying* (the present participle of *scurry*) means moving hurriedly with short, rapid, agitated steps — the scuttling, scampering motion of startled small creatures, or of a crowd in panicked flight. It is a young word (coined about 1810) with a young word's energy: it pictures feet working fast, close together, and a little frantically, and it almost always carries a hint of the small, the undignified, the furtive, or the easily routed. In *The Hobbit*, chapter 4, the goblins of the Misty Mountains have just learned that prisoners are loose in their tunnels; they come **scurrying** round the corner "in full cry," a wave of short, frantic footfalls and yells, only to be frozen in their tracks by the sight of "Goblin-cleaver" and "Foe-hammer" — Orcrist and Glamdring, the elven blades from Gondolin — "shining cold and bright right in their astonished eyes." The nuance is crucial to the comedy and irony of the moment: scurrying suggests an unheroic, animal-like, almost comical haste, the swarming energy of creatures who are numerous and aggressive but also panicked and easily routed. Tolkien sets the goblins' busy, scrambling rush directly against the still, cold, radiant gleam of the swords, so that their frantic motion collapses in an instant before an older, more terrible power. **The verb field: how each hasty motion is bounded** - To *scamper* adds a playful, light-footed, bounding quality — often of puppies or children at play. - To *scuttle* suggests a quick, sidling, furtive withdrawal, like a crab darting sideways into cover. - To *dart* emphasizes a single sudden, straight, swift burst. - To *skitter* pictures something light skipping erratically across a surface, like an insect on water. - To *hurry* is the neutral term for making haste, without the diminutive, animal-like, scrambling flavor. - *Scurry* alone combines short quick steps with agitation and swarm-like multiplicity — it is the verb of insects, rodents, and harried crowds — and it almost always carries a hint of the small, the undignified, or the fugitive. **Exhaustive polysemy: every sense the word wears today** - **Sense 1 — literal: to move with short, rapid, hurried steps (the governing sense).** The core motion of startled animals and bustling people: "mice scurrying across the floor," "commuters scurrying through the rain," "goblins scurrying round the corner." - **Sense 2 — figurative: to move or act with fussy, agitated haste.** Of people going about small urgent tasks: "servants scurried to obey," "aides scurried in and out of the office." The bridge is the same short, quick, busy motion, read as bustle rather than fear. - **Sense 3 — noun *scurry*: a hurried, bustling movement.** From 1823, "a scurry of activity," "a scurry of feet"; the noun preserves the same diminutive, agitated energy. - **Sense 4 — the reduplicative ancestor *hurry-scurry* (1732).** The phrase "hurry-scurry" (also "hurry-skurry") means confused, flurried bustle; it survives as a mild idiom of disorderly haste and is the historical source from which the verb scurry was back-formed. - **Sense 5 — biological and ecological jargon.** In zoology and ecology, scurrying is the standard verb for the rapid footfall of small mammals, insects, and crustaceans; it denotes a gait — short, quick, irregular — as much as a speed. - **Sense 6 — the negative connotations: furtiveness and panic.** Because scurrying creatures are usually startled or hunted, the word carries a permanent edge of fear, evasion, or disorder — a scurry is never a dignified procession. **How the senses hang together** Scurrying's whole career runs from a single image: many small feet moving fast and close together, in a hurry and out of control. The literal senses (mice, servants, goblins) are the same motion seen at different scales; the figurative and idiomatic uses (hurry-scurry, a scurry of activity) generalize that motion to any fussy, flurried, disorderly haste; and the permanent overtone of the small, the panicked, and the easily routed explains why Tolkien chose the word for goblins — busy, numerous, aggressive, and yet, in the end, no more than creatures scuttling in the dark.
