CHAIN: FROM PIE TO RELISH (8 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *sleg- 'to be slack, to droop, to be languid' (traditional reconstruction, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; DEBATED — see note; the ultimate source of Latin laxus 'loose, slack'; cognates include Proto-Germanic *slakaz > English slack and Greek lēgein 'to leave off, cease').
  2. Latin laxus 'loose, slack, lax, not tense' (attested classical; from PIE *sleg- with regular developments, though the precise detail is debated).
  3. Latin laxāre 'to loosen, to make slack, to release, to relax' (attested classical; verb built on laxus).
  4. Latin relaxāre 'to relax, to loosen, to release' (attested classical; re- 'back' + laxāre).
  5. Old French relaschier / relachier / relasser 'to leave behind, to release, to loosen' (12th century; from Latin relaxāre, with the late Latin and medieval reduction of the x to s or ch).
  6. Old French relais / reles 'remainder, that which is left behind; the scent left by a hunted animal' (12th–13th centuries; noun formed from relasser — 'that which is released or left behind').
  7. Middle English reles 'the scent of game; taste, aftertaste, flavour' (13th century, borrowed from Anglo-French and Old French reles; the taste senses develop in the 14th–15th centuries).
  8. English RELISH — noun 'taste, flavour, keen enjoyment' (16th century onward), verb 'to enjoy eagerly' (16th century), condiment sense (18th century); modern form with the modern spelling relish (the earlier reles shifted to relish by the 16th century).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The PIE stage for laxus is traditional rather than certain: de Vaan records the deeper etymology of Latin laxus as uncertain; the link to *sleg- 'to be slack' (cognate with Germanic slack) is the standard older account but should be treated as probable, not proven. The chain from Latin laxus onward is secure.
  • Old French relais 'remainder' and the modern English relay are the SAME word — the horses left behind at staging posts — so relay is a cousin of relish.
  • The modern condiment sense is an 18th-century development of the taste and flavour sense; the earliest English senses were the hunting one (the scent of game) and the taste one (aftertaste).
