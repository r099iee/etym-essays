Antiquarian enters English in 1610 as the adjectival extension of antiquary (1586), itself from Latin antiquarius, one who deals in or studies old things, from antiquus, ancient or former. Latin antiquarius in Cicero and later writers already carries the double sense the English word never loses: respectful student of the past and slightly dusty dealer in its leftovers. Old French antiquaire passes the dealer-scholar on to Renaissance England, where collectors of coins, manuscripts, and ruins need a name for themselves and their pursuits.

The Latin base antiquus (older form anticus) means what is in front by being former — what came before and therefore commands honour: ancient customs, time-honoured examples. It derives from ante, before, whose deeper PIE source is disputed between *h₂ent- (front, forehead) and wider demonstrative stems. What matters for English is not the ultimate particle but the cultural charge: antiquus praises age as authority, while its-edged noun antiquarius narrows that praise to a specialist temperament — someone who prefers the old to the new as a vocation.

  • 1610–1700: learned novelty. Early uses describe antiquarian studies, medals, and curiosities with unmixed prestige. Camden Britannia, Cotton library, Royal Society virtuosi: to be antiquarian is to rescue the national past from oblivion. The noun (an antiquarian) and adjective (antiquarian interests) emerge together.
  • 1700–1800: the dusty turn. As polite letters define themselves against pedantry, antiquarian acquires its famous condescending edge. Pope, Fielding, and later Scott affectionate-mock the antiquary who prefers a rusty brooch to a living neighbour. Antiquarian as adjective begins to mean narrowly obsessed with trifles, uncritical, nostalgic — the very exercise the source sentence sets against vital activity. The Society of Antiquaries (1707/1717) institutionalizes both the scholarship and the joke.
  • 1800–1900: trade and taste. The antiquarian book trade gives the word a commercial sense still current: antiquarian bookseller, antiquarian bookshop, antiquarian prices. Simultaneously the Gothic Revival and Pre-Raphaelites make antiquarian taste fashionable again — lovingly medieval, not dustily pedantic. The word thus splits:dealer (neutral-positive) versus dilettante (mildly pejorative).
  • 1900–present: professional versus amateur. Academic archaeology, art history, and textual bibliography professionalize what amateurs once did, and antiquarian becomes the label professionals use to mark pre-scientific or extra-academic work: antiquarian history (narrative without analysis), antiquarian bibliography (collation without interpretation). Nietzsche On the Uses and Disadvantages of History (1874) canonizes the critique: antiquarian history preserves life but can also mummify it. The source sentence belongs squarely here — descriptive stylistics must be vital, not an antiquarian exercise in collecting dead terms.

Formally the word is antiquary + -an, the Latinate suffix forming adjectives of belonging (as in grammarian, librarian). The qu spelling preserves Latin, pronounced /kw/ in English: RP /ˌæntɪˈkwɛəriən/, stress on the penultimate region with the characteristic -arian diphthong. The noun-antiquarian (a person) and adjective-antiquarian (a pursuit) remain identical in form, distinguished only by syntax: an antiquarian (person) versus an antiquarian exercise (lifeless). Related forms — antiquarianism (the temperament or movement), antiquarianize (rare, to make antiquarian) — cluster in the nineteenth century.

Literary attestation traces the arc from praise to tease to trade: Camden and Dugdale for earnest county antiquarianism; Scott The Antiquary (1816) for the immortal comic portrait of Jonathan Oldbuck,Matthew Lewis and M. R. James for antiquarian ghosts summoned by meddling scholars; Virginia Woolf and T. S. Eliot for modernist impatience with antiquarian reproduction; and textbook prefaces, like the source passage, where authors anxiously insist their descriptive work is not antiquarian diddling but a living inquiry. The irony the passage navigates is that every vital discipline needs its antiquarians — collectors, listers, preservers — even as it must transcend them.
