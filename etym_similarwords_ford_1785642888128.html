• Bridge: Similar because both are places where a river may be crossed; differs because a bridge is an artificial structure elevated above the water, separating the traveller from the riverbed, whereas a ford is a natural shallow place waded directly through the current.
  • Ferry: Similar because both provide a way across water; differs because a ferry carries people and goods over deep water in a vessel, while a ford needs no craft at all and depends on the river's natural shallowness.
  • Crossing: Similar because both name a place where a river is got over; differs because crossing is the generic term covering bridges, fords, ferries, and causeways, while ford names specifically the natural shallow one.
  • Causeway: Similar because both are routes across wet ground or water; differs because a causeway is a raised, constructed road built to avoid wading entirely, whereas a ford is waded on its natural bed.
  • Shallows: Similar because both involve shallow water; differs because shallows names the physical state of the water, while ford names the usable crossing-route across it.
  • Wade: Similar because both concern walking through water; differs because wade is the verb of the act (one wades at a ford), while ford is the noun of the place — and the verb 'to ford' adds the idea of a planned crossing.
  • Pass: Similar because both denote a negotiable way through a natural barrier; differs because a pass is a route through mountains, while a ford is a route across water — the parallel is figurative.
  • Firth / Fjord: Similar because they are cognate water-words from the same ancient root; differs because firths and fjords are deep, tidal inlets and drowned valleys, while a ford is a shallow freshwater crossing — kin in form, opposed in meaning.
