The biography of bellowed begins in the imitative layer of Proto-Indo-European: the root *bʰel-, "to sound, roar", a sound-symbolic formation in which a voiced breathy bilabial and a lateral liquid capture the deep, resonant blast of a bull's cry — the same sound-fabric that lies behind the ringing of a bell. In Germanic the root formed *bellaną, "to roar, bark, sound", which entered Old English twice over: as bylgan / bylgian, "to bellow, roar", and as bellan, "to roar", the latter surviving into modern bell, the hollow instrument named for the sound it makes. Old English bylgan passed into Middle English as belwen / belgen, "to roar", and by the early fourteenth century the verb bellow stood in the language, at first strictly of animals — especially cows and bulls — and only from about 1600 applied to human beings, so that when a man bellows he is heard to borrow the voice of a beast.

The sister branches extend the root across the whole Germanic world. Old Norse belja, "to roar, bellow", is a direct sibling, used of cattle, and Old Norse bjalla, "bell", repeats the sound-to-instrument transfer; Middle High German bellen, "to bark", narrows the same root to the sharp cry of dogs, surviving in modern German bellen, while Dutch blaffen, "to bark", is a parallel West Germanic formation of identical shape. Alongside this sound-root ran a parallel Germanic formation *belganą, "to swell, be angry", which gave Old English belgan, "to be angry": ancient speakers imagined anger as a swelling of the chest, and it is this channel that colours the word with fury, so that to bellow is to roar from a chest puffed full of rage. The Baltic branch preserves the zero-grade *bʰl̥-s-o- in Lithuanian balsas, "voice", and Latvian balss, "voice", showing the root's oldest meaning — a sonorous sound — carried into a noun for the human voice itself. No Slavic cognate is securely established, and — most importantly for the range of the word — the root did not reach the Indo-Iranian branch, so Sanskrit, Hindi, and Odia have no reflex of it.

Phonetically, the modern form shows the normal Germanic and English development. The Proto-Germanic *bellaną (a strong verb of class 3) displays the characteristic geminate -ll- of the expressive layer, and Old English bylgian added a -j- suffix to form a weak verb. The Middle English belwen / belgen levelled these into a single stem, and the modern bellow /ˈbɛləʊ/ is formed with the final unstressed -ow syllable that English uses for many sound verbs (follow, hollow, swallow). The past tense is a regular weak formation in -ed, bellowed, since the verb was weak in its animal sense from the start; there is no strong past, no ablaut, no irregularity — the word is as regular as the deep, steady sound it names.

In society and literature, the word's career runs from the farmyard to the battlefield. In Old and Middle English it was the natural cry of cattle and stags, a sound of the countryside. With the growth of the military and nautical worlds, the deep-voiced command entered the language of sergeants, captains, and boatswains: to bellow an order is to make it carry over noise and danger, and the verb took on firm overtones of authority — a man who bellows is a man used to being obeyed. In fiction the word became a favourite speech-tag for commanding or furious characters, from the sea-captains of adventure novels to the drill-sergeants of war writing, and the modern idiom "bellow with laughter" carries the same deep, full-chested, unapologetic energy into joy. Tolkien's use is wholly in this tradition: the captain's "Look out for the door" is bellowed because it must slice through the chaos of a collapsing scene, and the verb's animal depth makes the command feel elemental rather than merely loud — a roar from the chest of authority itself.
