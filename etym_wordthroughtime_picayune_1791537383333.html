- Late 18th century (Colonial Louisiana & Gulf Coast) - A tangible Spanish half-real silver coin worth 6¼ cents, circulating widely as everyday pocket change with neutral, utilitarian mercantile connotations.
- Early to mid-19th century (Frontier American Currency & Print Culture) - Expanded into the lowest denomination of casual urban trade and popularized by George Wilkins Kendall's newspaper *The Picayune* in New Orleans (costing one coin), acquiring the connotation of cheapness, affordability, and modest street value.
- Late 19th century (Gilded Age Figurative Shift) - Transitioned decisively from physical currency to an adjective meaning 'paltry,' 'worthless,' or 'contemptibly small,' carrying pejorative social connotations toward miserly, nitpicking behavior.
- 20th century to Present (Modern Idiomatic & Bureaucratic Usage) - Solidified entirely as an adjective describing trivial, pedantic disputes, bureaucratic obsessiveness, and small-minded arguments, with its numismatic origin remembered chiefly in historic New Orleans journalism.
