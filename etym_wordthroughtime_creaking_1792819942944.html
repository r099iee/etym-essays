- Early 14th c. — the raven's cry: Middle English creken first means to croak or caw, the harsh bird-voice, an imitative word for throats.
- 15th–16th c. — wood learns to speak: the verb spreads to hinges, doors, and wheels, anything stiff that complains dryly on turning.
- 17th–18th c. — ships and houses: sailors' creaking timbers and novelists' creaking stairs fix the sound as the voice of old structures under strain.
- 19th c. — bodies join in: creaking joints, creaking backs, and creaking voices carry the carpenter's word into age and illness, tenderly comic.
- 20th c. — the system creaks: journalists discover the creaking bureaucracy, economy, and infrastructure, stiffness as political satire.
- Today — age that still moves: creaking describes anything old but going — knees, cottages, constitutions — affectionate for people, eerie for staircases, mocking for ministries.
