1. GLOOM — from a scowl to a cave. Gloom begins as a verb of the face, not a word of the dark. Middle English gloumen meant "to look sullen, to frown, to stare moodily" (attested from about 1300), and its oldest kin are verbs of expression — the wrinkled brow, the downcast eye, the silent sulk. The handbooks offer only guesses about its source: a Scandinavian dialectal glome "to stare thoughtfully", or a Middle Low German glūmen "to look morose", or some lost Anglo-Scandinavian compound — and the OED leaves the origin open, explicitly. A red herring must be dismissed: because gloom sounds like glow, popular etymologists have wanted to attach it to the PIE shine-root *ghel- that gave us glow, glimmer, gold, and gleam. The authorities reject this — *ghel- is a family of brightness and warmth, and no morphological bridge leads from it to gloumen. The modern noun is a transfer: gloom meaning "darkness" becomes common only in the eighteenth century, a back-formation from gloomy, which had meant "sullen, morose" since the sixteenth. The mood came first, the shadow second; darkness was imagined as the face of the world gone sullen. The gothic fiction of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries made gloom a supreme atmospheric word — the deepening gloom of caves, forests, and ruined abbeys that presses on the spirits and seems to scowl — and the two senses, physical and psychological, fed each other so thoroughly that the modern word runs both lives at once, reinforced by the idiom "gloom and doom". In the cave, "the gloom ahead" is thus the perfectly chosen word: a darkness that seems to scowl, shadow with a temper, an atmosphere with an expression.

2. EQUIVOCAL — a word of two voices. Equivocal is a word built of two parts with unequal genealogies. The first half, Latin aequus "equal, level, fair", is a stubborn puzzle: de Vaan records no secure Indo-European etymology for it, and scholars can only point toward an ancient *aikʷo- whose ultimate source is lost — the stage is honestly stated as unknown. The second half is beautifully documented: vocāre "to call" descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *wekʷ- "to speak", whose descendants include Sanskrit vāk "voice, speech", Greek épos "word", and Latin vox "voice" — the ancestor of voice, vocal, vocabulary, and invoke. The compound aequivocus, "of equal voice", was formed in Latin to translate a Greek idea: homōnymos, "bearing the same name", used of words that sound alike but differ in meaning. Latin sharpened the image: a word that "calls equally" in two directions at once, giving two readings of equal weight. English borrowed aequivocus as equivocal in the early seventeenth century, and the word immediately acquired its double life: in logic and rhetoric it names the deliberate or structural doubleness of meaning (the vice of equivocation, the double-tongued answer designed to keep two possibilities alive); in everyday and scientific use an "equivocal result" is one that cannot be classified — a finding that points two ways. The clarifying negation "unequivocal" was formed to name the ideal of single-voiced clarity. In the cave, the noise is equivocal because it calls equally as metal and as mouth — a sound that is structurally double, and therefore unclassifiable by the ear.

3. BAFFLED — from public disgrace to a baffled ear. Baffled has the most dramatic biography of the four words. Its probable continental source, Old French bafouer, is itself an imitative verb of contempt — a slap of a word (compare baffe, "a blow") meaning "to mock, to abuse" — and the earliest English sense of to baffle, in the 1540s, was a public ritual of disgrace: to subject someone to ridicule, mockery, or infamy, a shaming of the offender before others. The OED leaves the source uncertain between French bafouer and a Scots tradition of baff, but the violent, mocking origin is clear. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the sense turned from shame to frustration: to baffle a plan, a pursuit, or an enemy was to defeat and foil it, to check its progress — the sense that survives in "the storm baffled all attempts at rescue". In the nineteenth century the mind entered the picture: to baffle the understanding is to bewilder and confound it, and baffled became the state of having one's comprehension defeated. The older senses survive as undertones — a baffling thing both frustrates and mocks, it defeats and humiliates the understanding. The word's physical kin, the baffle board (a device that blocks or deflects sound), makes the card's usage almost literal: a baffling noise is one that a board seems to slap away, and "baffled the ear" transfers the blockage from a device to a sense organ — the ear meets something that actively dodges every attempt to name it.

4. INCONGRUOUS — things that refuse to fall together. Incongruous is built from the Latin verb congruere, "to come together, to meet, to agree", itself a compound of con- "together" and ruere "to rush, to fall" — from the Indo-European root *h₃er- "to move, to stir", cognate with English run. The root image is kinetic: things are congruent when they "run together" into agreement, when their parts rush toward one another and fit. The negative prefix in- inverts the whole picture: incongruous things are those that do not run together, that refuse to fall into place with each other or with their surroundings. Latin built congruus "fitting, agreeing" in the first century BCE, and post-classical Latin added incongruus before English borrowed the word in the early seventeenth century. The aesthetic history is rich: in the eighteenth century, theories of the ridiculous made the "incongruous" — the sudden jarring of mismatched things — a principal source of laughter, and incongruity became a technical term of comedy. In the nineteenth century the word took on an uncanny, sinister edge in gothic and horror writing: an incongruous detail unsettles precisely because it should not belong — a telephone ringing in a tomb, a doll's face in a cave wall. In the card's sentence, the "incongruous elements" of the noise are wet mouths and dry metal, organic and mineral, appetite and machine — elements that refuse to fall together, and whose refusal to harmonize is the very source of the dread. The word thus combines logical, aesthetic, comic, and uncanny senses into one precise instrument of unease.

  • gloom: Middle English gloumen "to frown" (14th c.) → gloomy (16th c.) → gloom the darkness-noun (18th c.); PIE *ghel- link rejected; origin honestly left open.
  • equivocal: PIE *wekʷ- "to speak" → Latin vocāre "to call" + aequus "equal" (pre-Italic origin unknown) → aequivocus "of equal voice" → English equivocal (17th c.).
  • baffled: Old French bafouer "to mock" (debated) → to baffle "to disgrace publicly" (16th c.) → "to foil, defeat" (17th–18th c.) → "to bewilder" (19th c.) → baffled, the defeated understanding.
  • incongruous: PIE *h₃er- "to move" → Latin ruere "to rush" + con- → congruere "to come together" → congruus "fitting" → incongruus → English incongruous (17th c.).
