• Swamps: Similar because both are waterlogged wetlands; differs because swamps are wetter and often forested (or scrub-covered) with standing water most of the year, while marshes are open grassland/reeds and may dry seasonally.
  • Bogs: Similar because both are soft, wet ground; differs because bogs are acidic, rain-fed peatlands with a spongy sphagnum surface, while marshes are mineral-rich and refreshed by groundwater, rivers, or tides.
  • Fens: Similar because both are peat-forming wetlands; differs because fens are alkaline or neutral and fed by mineral-rich groundwater with a distinctive sedge and reed flora, whereas "marsh" is the broader, less chemically specific term.
  • Moors: Similar because both are uncultivated open low ground; differs because moors are usually upland, drier, and heather- or grass-covered — they need not be waterlogged at all.
  • Wetlands: Similar because a marsh is a kind of wetland; differs because "wetland" is the generic scientific umbrella term (also covering bogs, fens, swamps, and mudflats), while "marsh" names one specific hydrology and vegetation.
  • Quagmires: Similar because both are soft, yielding, muddy ground; differs because a quagmire is defined by trembling, sinking footing and is usually small, and its figurative force ("a quagmire of debt") is far stronger than any figurative use of marsh.
  • Mires: Similar because both mean wet, muddy ground; differs because mire is archaic/literary, also a verb ("to mire"), and lacks the ecological precision of marsh.
  • Sloughs: Similar because both are waterlogged ground; differs because slough specifically denotes a stagnant side-channel or muddy hole, is regional/dialectal, and has two pronunciations (/sluː/, /slaʊ/) with different senses.
  • Morasses: Similar because both mean soft swampy ground; differs because morass is chiefly literary and its figurative sense (a hopelessly tangled situation) dominates modern use.
  • Saltings: Similar because saltings are tidal marsh flats; differs because they name the upper tidal zone's bare or sparsely vegetated flats specifically, not the general landform.
  • Reedbeds / carrs: Similar because both are marsh vegetation zones; differs because they are habitat sub-types (reed-dominated; wet woodland) rather than the general word for waterlogged land.
  • Polders: Similar by subject matter — drained marshland; differs because a polder is reclaimed, diked farmland: the human-made opposite of a living marsh.
