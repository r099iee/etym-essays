The noun soup itself is ancient and respectable. It entered Middle English as soupe, sowpe from Old French soupe, souppe “slice of bread soaked in broth, pottage,” itself a borrowing of Late Latin suppa from Proto-Germanic *sup- / *suppō- “to sup, sip, drink” — the same Germanic family that gives sup, sip, sop (bread dipped in liquid) and, distantly, PIE *seup- / *seub- “to sip, lap.” Medieval soupe thus meant both the liquid and the sopped bread in it; by Early Modern English the liquid sense won, giving the familiar bowl of soup, while sop and supper (the evening meal at which broth was sopped) branched off. The phonology is regular: French /u/ retained as English /uː/, spelling fixed as ou, plural soups, verb to soup rare and culinary.

The hot-rod sense has nothing to do with broth directly; it grows from American slang that turned soup into fuel, dope, and power around 1900–1920. Horse-racing slang is the earliest bridge: turf writers used soup for any stimulant or narcotic administered to a horse, parallel to dope, and to soup a horse meant to dope it. Aviation and motorcycle racing then used soup for high-energy fuel mixtures — nitromethane blends, “hot” gasoline — poured into tanks the way soup is ladled. From fuel to engine work the metonymy is short: the machine that has drunk strong soup runs strong, so to soup up becomes to treat, tune, or fit for strong running.

  • Medieval culinary stratum (1300s–1700s): soupe as bread-and-broth; honest peasant food, central and nourishing.
  • Turf-slave slang stratum (1900s–1920s): soup as racehorse dope; illicit boost, secret and frowned upon.
  • Aviation / auto-racing stratum (1920s–1930s): soup as doped fuel; souped up first applied to aircraft and racing engines fitted with superchargers (“souper-charged” puns appear in period magazines).
  • Hot-rod stratum (1940s–1960s): teenagers souping up flathead Fords and Chevys in driveways; the term standardizes as soup up / souped-up in Hot Rod magazine, police reports, and Barris custom-shop copy; gerund souping names the nightly activity.
  • Generalized intensifier stratum (1960s–present): from engines to everything — stories, computers, economies — “souped-up” as universal American for pumped beyond stock.

Phonetic and morphological details confirm the slang youth: the p doubles in souped, souping by the regular consonant-doubling rule before vowels; stress stays on soup with up as a separable particle (“soup the engine up / soup up the engine”); the vowel /uː/ stays long and tense, shouted easily over an open manifold. The competing super → soup derivation — supercharger clipped in fast pit speech — cannot be ruled out and may have reinforced the horse-dope source by convergence: two streams, soup-as-dope and super-as-boost, meeting in the same greasy syllable. Dictionaries therefore list the etymology of the phrasal verb as uncertain with both hypotheses noted, a model of honest lexicography.

Literary and cultural attestations track the automobile’s transformation from appliance to identity. Tom Wolfe’s Kandy-Kolored hot-rod essays, S. E. Hinton’s greasers, Springsteen’s souped-up Chevys, and countless detective novels’ “souped-up sedan” all use the word to signal working-class ingenuity and masculine display. The source sentence’s pairing — “souping it up, ‘personalizing’ it” — captures the hinge moment when Detroit’s uniform product meets the owner’s file and spray-gun: practical transportation becoming social competition and pure decorative play.
