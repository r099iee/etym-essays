laden: past participle of the verb "to lade," and now also a fully independent adjective meaning "heavily loaded, weighed down with a burden." In the passage — "their laden beasts of burden" — the word insists that the pack-animals are not merely carrying their loads but visibly bowed and sagging beneath them; the participle states a state, not an action: the weight is already there, pressing into the beasts' flanks as part of the scene's heavy mineral atmosphere of iron-veined rock, ferro-oxide, and basaltic dust.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Primary literal sense — loaded, carrying a heavy load: "a tree laden with fruit," "a ship laden with treasure," "a table laden with food."
  • Figurative / moral sense — weighed down by something immaterial: "a heart laden with grief," "a soul laden with sins," "laden with guilt or care."
  • Combining-form / compound use — "debt-laden," "stress-laden," "gas-laden," "carbon-laden," "traffic-laden," "image-laden" — used productively in journalism and science to mean "full of, saturated with, burdened by."
  • Verb "to lade" (now rare) — to load, to burden, to charge; and, in a second old sense, "to draw up or bail water" (a ship "lades" water); from this sense descends the noun ladle, a spoon for lifting liquid (Old English hlædel).
  • Related noun "load" — the thing carried; historically a separate word (Old English lād "way, course, journey") whose sense converged with "lade" in Middle English, so that "load" and "lade" are felt as one family.
  • Register — the adjective keeps a grave, old, even scriptural tone (a soul laden with sins, a tree laden with fruit); in modern prose it is more literary than "loaded," which is the everyday, more casual word.
  • Context and texture — in the passage the participle works against the surrounding mineral heaviness: the cavern lies in a cliff of iron-veined rock, the entrance crumbles in heaps of ferro-oxide and dark basaltic dust, and the darkness beyond "clogs" the interior "as if with a weaving of black webs." The laden beasts belong to this atmosphere; their bowed flanks are one more load-bearing thing in a landscape that is itself weighed down with metal and dust. The word turns the earthmen's animals into slow cargo, part of an expedition that is itself a kind of heavy freight moving through darkness.
  • Common collocations — "laden with meaning," "laden with symbolism," "snow-laden branches," "a laden table," "fully laden" (of trucks, ships, and aircraft), "heavy-laden."
  • Grammar note — as an adjective "laden" is normally predicated with "with" ("laden with spices"), though older poetic usage allowed a bare object; the word is used both attributively ("laden beasts") and predicatively ("the ship was laden").
  • Semantic distinction from "loaded" — "loaded" can imply deliberate, efficient stowing and has picked up informal senses (loaded = rich, or drunk), while "laden" is purely the passive, physical or moral weight — a ship is laden, a conscience is laden, and the two are felt as one heavy condition of being weighed upon.
