The history of animated reaches back to the deepest Proto-Indo-European linguistic intuitions concerning physical breath as the universal, indispensable vehicle of life, consciousness, and spiritual existence.

The word originates in the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₂enh₁-, meaning "to breathe" or "to blow." This fundamental somatic root generated an extraordinary constellation of ancient cognates across the Indo-European linguistic family tree, illustrating how archaic cultures equated respiration with the animating life-force. Among its prominent cognates are:

  • Sanskrit ániti ("he breathes") and prāṇa ("vital breath, spirit, life-force").
  • Ancient Greek ánemos (ἄνεμος, "wind, breeze, gust") and anemōnē ("windflower").
  • Old Irish anál ("breath") and anim ("soul").
  • Gothic uz-anan ("to breathe out, expire, die") and Old Norse önd ("breath, soul").
  • Old English ōþian ("to breathe") and Old High German anst ("grace, divine favor").

In Proto-Italic (*anamos) and Classical Latin, this root divided into two twin nouns that shaped the entire landscape of Western philosophy, metaphysics, and psychology:

  • animus: The rational mind, intellect, cognitive power, courage, passion, disposition, and conscious human will.
  • anima: The vital breath, life-force, vegetative soul, animal vitality, and living entity.

From the feminine noun anima, the Romans derived the denominative transitive verb animāre, signifying "to fill with breath," "to endow with life," "to quicken into consciousness," or "to encourage and enliven." Its past participle animātus described that which had received the vital spark of breath and was therefore living, dynamic, conscious, and mobile. In Roman philosophical discourse (such as the philosophical treatises of Cicero, Lucretius's De Rerum Natura, and Seneca's moral letters), animatus stood in profound ontological contrast to inanimus (lifeless matter, rocks, clay, unquickened stone).

The word entered English during the Renaissance in the 1530s, initially as a participial adjective in theological, metaphysical, and philosophical translations discussing God animating Adam with the breath of life (Genesis 2:7: "and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul"). By the late 16th and 17th centuries, humanist writers such as Sir Francis Bacon and John Milton expanded the word beyond biology to describe human temperament, expressive facial features, and lively social discourse—signifying a person whose eyes and gestures were visibly "filled with spirit and vitality."

During the 19th-century flowering of European realism and naturalism, novelists (such as Charles Dickens, Thomas Hardy, Guy de Maupassant, and Émile Zola) frequently applied animated to physical settings, enclosures, markets, farmyards, and civic gatherings to describe the bustling, multi-voiced clamor of human and animal life before contrasting it with sudden silence or abandonment. In the late 19th and 20th centuries, the invention of optical toys (the zoetrope, praxinoscope) and the subsequent development of cinematography gave rise to animation as a revolutionary cinematic art form, forever cementing animated as a defining keyword of both human expressive vitality and visual technological artistry.
