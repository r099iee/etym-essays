- 14th–15th c. — Middle English relied on loose phrasal constructions such as so fer as or in as muche as; spatial distance (Old English feorr) was actively extended into intellectual and legal domains to delineate the reach of knowledge.
- 16th c. — Emerged as the distinct three-word analytical prepositional phrase in so far as (often in so far forth as) in Tudor state documents, legal statutes, and philosophical debate (e.g., Sir Thomas More, Thomas Cranmer).
- 17th–18th c. — Became a crucial syntactic operator in Enlightenment philosophy (Hobbes, Locke, Hume) and common-law jurisprudence to establish rigorous conditional boundaries and severability in arguments.
- 19th c. — Ubiquitous in Victorian legal opinions, political economy, and German-to-English philosophical translations (Kantian and Hegelian critique); began solidifying in print as a single word in American typography.
- Early–mid 20th c. — Solid compound spelling insofar gained dominance in American English stylebooks, while British authorities (such as H. W. Fowler) fought a rear-guard action advocating three words (in so far as).
- Late 20th–21st c. — Solid insofar as recognized as the universal academic, philosophical, and legal standard across all major English-speaking varieties, functioning as an essential hedging and qualification device.
