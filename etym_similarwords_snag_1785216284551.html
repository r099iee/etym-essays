• obstacle: Similar because both block or hinder progress; differs because an obstacle need not be sharp or leftover at all — it is any block in the way, while a snag is a sharp, concealed, jagged remnant lying in wait to catch.
  • obstruction: Similar because both obstruct movement or progress; differs because obstruction is the general, often structural term (a blockage in a pipe, a road), while snag stresses the sharp, accidental projection.
  • impediment: Similar because both hinder movement; differs because impediment is a general hindrance (from Latin "to entangle the feet"), lacking snag's sharpness and its sense of an accidental, hidden remnant.
  • stub: Similar because both are remnant points of a larger thing; differs because a stub is blunt and rounded — a cut-off remainder — while a snag is sharp and jagged.
  • stump: Similar because both are tree remnants left behind; differs because a stump is massive and blunt, the heavy base left after cutting, while a snag is the sharp, jagged projection that catches and tears.
  • barb: Similar because both are sharp projecting points; differs because a barb is a deliberate, backward-pointing hook designed to hold fast, while a snag is an accidental, leftover point lying in wait.
  • spike / prong: Similar because both are pointed projections; differ because a spike or prong is deliberately shaped and functional (a fence spike, a fork prong), while a snag is an unworked, leftover point of nature.
  • hitch: Similar because both name an unexpected difficulty; differs because a hitch is purely abstract and temporary (a hitch in the plan), with no physical sharpness, while a snag keeps the image of a hidden point.
  • catch: Similar because both can name a hidden drawback or difficulty; differs because catch is a general word for a concealed problem ("what's the catch?"), while snag emphasises the jagged, obstructing remnant rather than a deception.
  • thorn: Similar because both are sharp, concealed points that catch; differs because a thorn is a natural growth on a plant, while a snag is a broken remnant or protruding obstruction in any material.
