The story of nobility begins with the human eye: with being known. Its ultimate ancestor is the great Indo-European root *ǵneh₃-, to know — the same root that gives English know and knowledge, Greek gnosis, Sanskrit jñāna. In the Italic branch this root produced the verb gnōscere, to come to know, and from that verb the adjective gnōbilis, literally knowable — capable of being known. The semantic step from knowable to well-known, famous is the whole sociology of aristocracy in miniature: those whom everybody knows, the conspicuous families, become by that very conspicuousness the eminent ones. Early Latin still spells the word with its initial g intact — gnōbilis — before the cluster simplifies to classical nōbilis, and already in Plautus the word slides between famous and high-born, because in Rome they were the same thing.

From the adjective Rome derived the abstract noun nōbilitās, and with it a precise constitutional meaning. After the Licinian-Sextian laws opened the consulship to plebeians, a new governing class formed: the nobilēs, descendants of consuls whether patrician or plebeian, distinct both from the old patriciate and from the novi homines, new men like Cicero who had to boast their way in. Nobilitas thus meant simultaneously renown, the quality of the renowned, and the order of the renowned — a triple sense English would inherit entire. The late Republic polemics over who counted as nobilis — Sallust sneers, Cicero preens — fixed the word as the vocabulary of legitimate power, and imperial usage spread it across an empire where every provincial magnate aped the nobilitas of Rome.

Old French received the word as nobleté and later nobilité, and the feudal world gave it feudal content: the noblesse were now those who held fiefs by military service, owed suit to their lord, and paid no taille. The great sound shifts between Latin and French — the weakening of unstressed syllables, the palatal colouring — reshaped the shell while the Norman Conquest carried the word into England, where Middle English nobilite appears by the fourteenth century in both great senses: Chaucer can praise a knight noble of condicioun and also muster the nobilite as a political body. Caxton prints, the Paston letters conspire, and Shakespeare anatomizes the estate — his nobles are brave, factious, magnificent, and doomed, often in a single scene.

  • Known to noble (PIE to Rome): *ǵneh₃-, to know, yields Latin gnōbilis, knowable, then nōbilis, famous and high-born — fame itself becoming rank.
  • Rank to order (Republic to Empire): nōbilitās hardens into the governing class of consular descendants, then into the empire-wide vocabulary of honour and office.
  • Rome to the fief (500–1200): through late Latin and Old French the word acquires feudal content — military tenure, hereditary privilege, exemption — and crosses into Middle English with the Norman aristocracy.
  • The estate at flood tide (1300–1788): the nobility of Europe monopolize land, office, and command; courtesy books preach that blood must be matched by virtue, while satirists from Langland to Pope measure the shortfall.
  • Revolution and reform (1789–1918): France abolishes hereditary nobility in a night, Napoleon manufactures a new one, England radicals denounce the order as in the source sentence, and one assembly after another strips its fiscal and legislative monopoly.
  • Heritage and metaphor (1918 to now): surviving nobilities become cultural heritage — Debrett, Gotha, stately homes — while nobility of mind flourishes as pure moral praise, the coronet awarded by conduct rather than birth.

Literary attestation is a roll-call of English itself. Langland contrasts the true nobility of virtue with titled pride; Shakespeare Henry V and the history plays stage the estate in all its valour and faction; Burke defends the generous loyalty to rank while Paine and Cobbett — the voice behind the source sentence — arraign a nobility that funds repression; Thackeray anatomizes the snobbery of rank in The Book of Snobs; and modern honours lists still manufacture life peers, proving that even democracies cannot quite do without a nobility, provided it be appointed rather than inherited. From a root meaning to know, the word has travelled to the paradox of its present: everybody knows the nobility, and what everybody knows about it is divided between admiration for its manners and doubt about its necessity.
