- Born of rank (c. 1300) - Middle English forms peerless from peer, 'an equal' (originally a noble of equal rank), and the word first appears meaning 'without an equal' - superlative language born in the world of peers and equals
- The chivalric superlative (14th-16th c.) - peerless is the word of courtly praise - a peerless knight, a peerless beauty - attaching itself to the heights of love, valour, and devotion, always above the common ladder
- From people to things (16th-19th c.) - the word broadens beyond persons to art, skill, and achievement; peerless becomes a standard superlative of rhetoric for anything without rival
- The heightened superlative (contemporary) - peerless survives as a formal, literary, and even advertising word for 'matchless,' keeping its aristocratic air - stronger and rarer than mere 'unmatched,' the thing that ends all argument about comparison
