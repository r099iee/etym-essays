- c. 1600 “the biblical book and departure” - restricted entirely to ecclesiastical and scholarly registers as a capitalized proper noun naming the second book of Moses and the departure of the Israelites from Egypt.
- 1620s–1650s “general secular departure” - lowercased and extended by English prose writers to denote any large-scale departure or solemn exit of a company of people.
- 18th–19th c. “demographic and historical phenomenon” - adopted by historians, political philosophers, and journalists to describe major migratory waves, such as the rural exodus fueled by the Industrial Revolution.
- Early 20th c. “literary and mock-heroic flourish” - novelists like P. G. Wodehouse and contemporary essayists regularly invoke the word for humorous hyperbole when describing crowds departing drawing rooms, theaters, or social functions.
- Late 20th c.–present “ubiquitous socioeconomic and political term” - universally used across global media for refugee crises, brain drains, capital flight, and pandemic-era urban shifts, balancing earnest humanitarian gravity with casual journalistic shorthand.
