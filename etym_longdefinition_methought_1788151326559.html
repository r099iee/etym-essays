methought (archaic) is the past tense of the impersonal verb methinks — “it seems to me”. Where a modern speaker says “I thought” or “it seemed to me”, an Early Modern writer said “methought”. The word is a fossilised impersonal construction: the me at its beginning is not the subject but an old dative (indirect-object) pronoun meaning “to me”, and the verb attached to it carries no first-person agreement, so the whole form literally encodes “it appeared to me (that)…”. To the modern ear the fusion is complete and unsegmentable, and the word behaves like a single introductory verb whose clause follows directly: “methought the ship had sailed”.

In its heyday, from the 14th through the 17th centuries, “methought” / “methinks” was entirely normal grammar — not poetic decoration — and appears throughout Chaucer, Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and Milton. Its old-style wording (“me thought”) reflects the impersonal machinery English was discarding in those same centuries. Parallel fossils survive: “meseems” (“it seems to me”), the abandoned “me likes” and “me listeth” (from which modern “I like” and “I list” were re-analysed), and “I ween”. The construction's point is that a perception or vision is received by the speaker rather than produced by an act of thinking — hence its power for dreams, omens, and sudden inward conviction.

Secondary and modern registers. Today “methought” survives only as a conscious archaism, in three distinct uses. (1) Literary-historical: quoted from, or pastiched after, Renaissance and Romantic texts — the source context here is Wordsworth's The Prelude, whose celebrated lines “For I, methought, while the sweet breath of heaven / Was blowing on my body…” use the form for a feeling that arrives unbidden from nature. (2) Comic pseudo-archaism: “methinks the lady doth protest too much”, a free reworking of Shakespeare's line in Hamlet (originally “the lady doth protest too much, methinks”), now a stock internet witticism meaning “that denial sounds suspicious”. (3) Poetic-meditative: 19th-century Romantics revived the whole “methinks / methought / meseems” family for precisely the hushed, inward tone Wordsworth achieves in the source sentence.

  • Primary sense: archaic past of methinks — “it seemed to me, I thought (that)”.
  • Syntax: introduces a subordinate clause; no subject agreement; historically impersonal (dative me + verb of appearing).
  • Register: extinct in speech; literary, biblical, or mock-archaic only.
  • Related fossils: methinks (present), meseems / meseemed (archaic), I ween (archaic).
  • Watch: not an ordinary past of “think” — using it in plain modern prose signals deliberate archaism or comedy.

Pronunciation and form: methought rhymes with “brought” and is normally pronounced /mɪˈθɔːt/; the spelling is fully fused — one word, no space, no apostrophe — and the verb survives in exactly two forms, methinks (present) and methought (past), with no other tenses, persons, participles, or compounds, because it froze in those two forms the moment the impersonal construction died out of ordinary English grammar.
