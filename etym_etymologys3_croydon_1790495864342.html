Croydon is a Saxon valley-name stretched over a modern metropolis. Its second half is certain: Old English denu (valley, dale, from Proto-Germanic *danjō, low ground, cf. German Tenne? Actually Denne, and English den, dean in place-names like Rottingdean, Hazeldean) names the long Wandle trough between the chalk Downs and the London clay. Its first half is famous-uncertain, and candour requires the debate to be stated. Charters write Crogedene (861, 973), Domesday writes Croindene (1086), later rolls Croidon, Craydon (13th-16th c.), modern Croydon with -don as if hill (by analogy with London? No — with Snowdon, Wimbledon).

The favoured reading takes croh- as Old English croh, croa (saffron, crocus), itself an early loan from Latin crocus, from Greek krokos, from a Semitic saffron-word (cf. Hebrew karkom, Sanskrit kunkuma). Croydon would then be saffron valley, the valley where crocuses were grown for dye, medicine and condiment — entirely plausible for a sheltered chalk valley with Roman and Saxon farms, and paralleled by Saffron Walden in Essex. Phonologically croh + denu gives crohdene with loss of h before d to crogedene, then smoothing of -gd- to -yd- /krɔɪdən/, spelling modernised to -don. The rival reading takes crog- as crooked (bent valley, winding Wandle) or as a personal name *Croga (Croga’s valley). Ekwall preferred saffron, Mills marks crooked as possible, Gelling warns that croh as saffron is rare in place-names; responsible gazetteers therefore give saffron valley with a caveat.

  • Manor to market (900-1500): archbishops hold the manor from the Saxon kings; palace, church (St John’s, rebuilt Perpendicular c. 1400, now Minster), corn-market and fairs grow round the valley road. The name means the estate before it means the streets: to Croydon is to the archbishop’s summer lodging.
  • Coaching to railway suburb (1650-1900): the Brighton road makes Market Street inn-keeping (Old King’s Head, Green Dragon, Greyhound) the town’s trade; Ruskin’s grandmother keeps one such house. Surrey Iron Railway (1803, horse-drawn), then steam to London Bridge (1839), then tramways fill the valley with villas. Pronunciation settles to RP /ˈkrɔɪdən/, with /ɔɪ/ as in boy and reduced /dən/.
  • Airport to borough (1920-present): Beddington aerodrome becomes London’s Croydon Airport (1920), Amy Johnson departs from it; closure (1959) frees land for offices. 1965 creates the London Borough of Croydon, 2000 gives Tramlink, 2010s give towers and Westfield plans. The Saxon valley-name now labels 385,000 people, keeping its saffron etymology as civic arms (crocus flowers) even while linguists footnote the doubt. Visitors still enter by winding valley roads that explain the name better than any charter.
