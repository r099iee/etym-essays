- Early 17th Century - Originates in the aristocratic Parisian salons (*la préciosité*) led by Madame de Rambouillet, aiming to civilize French speech, elevate courtly love, and refine manners after decades of brutal religious warfare.
- Mid to Late 17th Century - Undergoes devastating public ridicule following Molière's comedy *Les Précieuses ridicules* (1659); the term is permanently codified across Europe as a synonym for foolish, affected linguistic snobbery and artificial circumlocution.
- Late 17th to 18th Century - Borrowed into English as *preciosity*; utilized in Augustan literary criticism to warn writers against baroque excess, florid conceits, and over-delicate euphemisms that violate neoclassical clarity.
- Late 19th Century (Fin-de-Siècle & Aestheticism) - Re-embraced with proud irony by Decadents and Aesthetes (Huysmans, Pater, Wilde), who celebrated precious, jewel-encrusted diction as the ultimate rebellion against coarse bourgeois industrialism.
- 20th to 21st Century - Cemented in modern literary criticism and translation theory as the definitive diagnosis for strained, mannered, or overly ornate prose that lacks the organic vitality and psychological truth of natural expression.
