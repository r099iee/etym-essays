smote (verb, past tense of smite) — struck, hit, or beat hard and forcefully; in extended senses, afflicted, punished, or deeply affected by a sudden overwhelming force. In the Hobbit passage, 'a blow smote the side of the Mountain': a vast stroke lands against the mountain-side with crushing force. The form is a strong, archaic-sounding past tense, and Tolkien chooses it precisely for its biblical, epic weight.

  • 1. To strike with a heavy blow (core sense): the plain physical sense, 'to hit hard, to deliver a single powerful stroke'. In modern English the base verb smite is literary and archaic, and the past tense smote survives most naturally in fixed, formulaic, or biblical contexts.
  • 2. To afflict, punish, or destroy (biblical and religious sense): e.g. 'God smote the Egyptians' — to visit disaster, plague, or death upon someone as a judgement or vengeance.
  • 3. To be suddenly and completely affected (figurative sense): 'smitten with guilt', 'smitten by love' — the past participle smitten is the most common modern survival of the family, meaning 'suddenly and entirely struck by an emotion, an illness, or a person'.
  • 4. The grammatical paradigm: smite – smote – smitten is a class-I Germanic strong verb; the internal vowel changes (i – o – i) are the direct descendants of the ancient Indo-European ablaut series. Smote is the simple past; smitten is the past participle.
  • 5. Register: poetic, archaic, and solemn. Native speakers know it chiefly from the King James Bible, hymns, and high fantasy; in casual speech the family survives almost only in the fixed participle 'smitten'.

Semantic core: at heart the word is about a single, sudden, overwhelming stroke — a blow that lands once and with full force, whether the force is physical (a battering-ram against a mountain), divine (a plague), or emotional (love, guilt, awe). The modern use of smitten preserves exactly that idea of being struck all at once and without resistance.

Smote vs smitten — a split that matters: the two surviving forms have drifted apart in register and even in meaning. Smote is the archaic, monumental simple past, still felt as belonging to the Bible and epic poetry. Smitten, by contrast, is a living, mildly informal word, used overwhelmingly of romantic attraction ('utterly smitten with her') and occasionally of affliction ('smitten by a virus'). Learners should note that one cannot usually swap them: 'she was smitten' (captivated) is modern and natural, while 'she was smote' is ungrammatical and archaic, and 'love smote him' is consciously grand or playful. The grammatical identity of the family hides a deep register split.

Strong-verb patterns and how they feel: because smite–smote–smitten preserves the old ablaut, the past tense carries a flavour of age even for readers who never studied grammar. English speakers who hear smote in a modern text automatically place it in the world of scripture and saga — this is precisely why Tolkien uses it for the blow on the Mountain and why journalists use it for comically overblown headlines ('passions smote the market'). The word is a small engine of register: one syllable, and the sentence turns solemn, ancient, or mock-heroic.

Common misuses to avoid: do not use smote as a present tense ('he smote the ball' for a present/ongoing action is wrong; it is strictly past), and do not confuse it with the unrelated verb smolder or the noun smote (which does not exist in English). In formal modern prose, prefer struck unless an archaic, biblical, or epic tone is intended; in casual speech, smitten is the only member of the family that is fully at home.
