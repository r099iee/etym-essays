- c. 1300 - bleke pale, wan, of a pallid colour - borrowed from Old Norse bleikr white, pale; describes complexion and colour, not yet landscape
- 14th-15th c. - bleak (adj.) pale, pallid, colourless - applied to faces, bodies, and light; the word is a colour word
- 1550s - bleak bare, windswept, exposed - the meaning transfers from paleness to landscape: an open, cold, treeless expanse that whitens under exposure
- 1719 - bleak cheerless, depressing, offering no hope - the figurative sense extends to prospects, futures, and outlooks (a bleak prospect)
- 18th-19th c. - bleak becomes a standard word of Romantic and Victorian landscape writing: moors, northern coasts, ruined wastes (Tolkien's bleak and barren land of the Desolation)
- 20th-21st c. - bleak dominates in economic and personal registers: bleak prospects, bleak future, bleak assessment; the noun bleak (a small silvery fish) is a separate word
- Modern - bleak keeps both the physical sense (bare, cold, exposed land) and the figurative sense (cheerless, hopeless), with the figurative perhaps now the more frequent in speech
