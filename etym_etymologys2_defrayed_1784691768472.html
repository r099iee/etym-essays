PIE (reconstructed, before c. 3000 BCE): *bhreg- "to break, crack" — the root of Latin frangere "to break"; collateral reflexes include English break, German brechen, and Gothic brikan. Latin (1st c. BCE): frangere "to break"; past participle fractum "broken" — the base of the French cost-word. Old French (9th–13th c.): frait, fraiz "breakage, damage, loss; hence expense, cost" — the past-participle noun; an expense is conceived as a sum "broken off" from a purse, or a loss to be made good. Old French (12th–13th c.): desfrayer / defraier "to pay out, discharge costs, clear expenses" — the prefix des- (from Latin dis- "apart, out of") + frai "cost, expense". Middle English (14th c.): defrayen "to pay the costs of, settle accounts" — borrowed into the formal commercial and administrative vocabulary; first attested in the fourteenth century. Early Modern English (15th–17th c.): defray "to bear or pay the expense of" — used in wills, charters, and official correspondence. Modern English (18th c. to present): defray "to pay or provide for (an expense or cost)"; the past participle defrayed "paid, settled, discharged — of expenses". NOTE ON DEBATE: An older derivation connected Old French frai with the Frankish / Germanic *friþu "peace, protection, security" via Medieval Latin fredum / fredus "fine, penalty — a payment to buy back the peace"; most modern dictionaries now prefer the "breaking" account (frait < fractum) and treat the peace-fine link as doubtful, but the older proposal is still cited and the question is not entirely closed.
