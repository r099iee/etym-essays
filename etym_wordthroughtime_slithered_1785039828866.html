- Middle English 'slideren' - a frequentative of the Old English verb 'slidan' (to slide); early senses mean to slip, slide, or lose one's footing, and the word is thick with friction — the repeated, grating motion of sliding under imperfect control.
- 16th-18th century - 'slither' settles as the dialectal and literary doublet of 'slide': to move with a smooth, twisting, friction-ridden glide; it keeps a rustic, energetic flavour that 'slide' loses.
- 19th century - the word fastens on the snake: to slither becomes the signature verb of a serpent's writhing, noiseless glide, and from this it acquires its sense of ungraceful, repetitive, unsteady motion.
- Contemporary - 'slithered' describes any long, writhing, friction-losing glide — of snakes, or of travellers skidding down loose, steep ground where the feet never quite grip; more prolonged and unsteady than a slide, more repetitive than a slip.
