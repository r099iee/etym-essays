• Moved: Similar because both denote a change of position or state; differs because “moved” is the neutral generic term for any change of place, while “stirred” implies motion that breaks a state of rest, however slight, and also carries the emotional sense.
  • Roused: Similar because both mean brought out of a state of rest; differs because “roused” implies being awakened or provoked from sleep or inactivity by an external cause, while “stirred” can describe a mere spontaneous twitch.
  • Agitated: Similar because both mean set into motion; differs because “agitated” implies repeated, forceful, or nervous motion (and emotional upset), while “stirred” can describe the faintest conceivable movement.
  • Twitched: Similar because both describe small motions; differs because “twitched” names one particular small involuntary muscular motion, while “stirred” is the general word for any motion at any degree above zero.
  • Shaken: Similar because both mean disturbed from a settled state; differs because “shaken” implies a stronger, more violent or sudden disturbance, while “stirred” ranges from the gentlest to the most violent motion.
  • Aroused: Similar because both can mean roused into activity or emotion; differs because “aroused” stresses awakening or stimulation (often sexual), while “stirred” is softer and more general.
  • Excited: Similar because both can describe emotion set in motion; differs because “excited” names the state of heightened emotion directly, while “stirred” names the act of being moved into that state.
  • Whisked: Similar because both describe quick, energetic motion; differs because “whisked” means moved with a rapid sweeping motion (often in cooking), while “stirred” is the slower, round-and-round mixing motion.
  • Churned: Similar because both describe violent agitation of liquid; differs because “churned” implies violent, roiling turbulence, while “stirred” includes the gentlest touch.
  • Bestirred: Similar because both derive from stir and mean roused to activity; differs because “bestirred” (in “bestir oneself”) stresses rousing one’s own efforts, a reflexive and now rather formal use.
