- 14th c. — Sinful depravity and moral corruption: Enters Middle English from Anglo-Norman vicios, designating sinful conduct, heresy, and individuals addicted to moral vices.
- 15th c. — Structural defect and legal invalidity: Applied in legal proceedings and philosophy to flawed arguments, corrupt deeds, or diseased livestock exhibiting physical defects.
- 16th c. — Animal aggression and unruliness: English horsemanship treatises apply it to dangerous horses and dogs that bite, kick, or refuse control, establishing the bridge to physical violence.
- 17th–18th c. — Malicious human cruelty and speech: Expands to human interpersonal malice, characterizing venomous satire, cruel slander, and savage physical assaults.
- 19th c. — Systemic loops and physical severity: Logic formalizes the 'vicious circle' (escalating reciprocal crises), while common speech adopts the adjective for agonizing headaches and brutal winter weather.
- 20th–21st c. — Dominance of savage aggression: In modern vernacular speech, primary meaning shifts to ferocious violence (vicious mugging, vicious dog) and vindictive rhetoric, while moral vice remains an elevated literary nuance.
