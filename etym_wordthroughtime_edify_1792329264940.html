- [14th c. Middle English] — Borrowed from Old French edefiier and Latin aedificāre; possessed dual physical and spiritual meanings: literally "to build or construct a masonry edifice/dwelling", and spiritually "to build up the faith, piety, and moral character of Christian believers" — [perception: reverent architectural and theological term for physical construction and spiritual foundation]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English & Reformation] — The physical construction sense became obsolete as native *build* dominated secular masonry; edify specialized exclusively into Protestant theological and sermonizing discourse, denoting the systematic instruction and spiritual sanctification of the soul — [perception: sacred religious term symbolizing earnest piety and godly instruction]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Eras] — Secularized to encompass general intellectual enlightenment, philosophical instruction, and the moral improvement of society through high literature, fine arts, and didactic education — [perception: esteemed hallmark of cultural refinement, moral earnestness, and Victorian civic virtue]
- [Late 19th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Retained its noble philosophical and educational meaning, but simultaneously developed a wry, mildly ironic or satirical nuance when applied to smug, self-righteous, or unsolicited moralizing from sanctimonious authorities — [perception: respected cultural verb of genuine intellectual enlightenment, but occasionally used with subtle ironic detachment toward preachiness]
