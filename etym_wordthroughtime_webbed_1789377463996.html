- 8th to 11th Century (Old English) - The verbal participle *ġewebbod* / *webbed* meant literally "woven, fabricated on a loom, or interlaced with threads," rooted in domestic cloth-making.
- 14th to 15th Century (Middle English) - Extended metaphorically from woven wool and linen to organic structures resembling net-like fabrics, notably spiders' silk traps and membranous animal tissues.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - Naturalists and ornithologists formally adopted *webbed* to categorize waterfowl (ducks, geese, swans) based on their interdigital swimming membranes, contrasting them with taloned and cloven-footed birds.
- 18th to 19th Century (Industrial & Victorian Eras) - Expanded to heavy woven industrial strapping ("webbing") and structural girder webs; deployed by Oscar Wilde in *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888) with satirical flair to celebrate rustic waterfowl morphology against vain aristocratic standards.
- 20th to 21st Century - Codified in developmental genetics (apoptosis and BMP signaling in syndactyly), sports equipment design (webbed swimming gloves, baseball mitts), and materials engineering (crazed glass patterns).
