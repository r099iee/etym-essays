The word suckling is a quintessentially native Germanic formation whose roots reach back through Old and Middle English to Proto-Indo-European roots representing the primal onomatopoeic act of drawing liquid into the mouth.

1. Proto-Indo-European Onomatopoeic Roots:

  • Comparative Indo-European linguistics reconstructs the root *sug- / *suk- (alongside variants like *seug-) as an imitative or sound-symbolic root mimicking the physiological sound of suction and breast-nursing.
  • This root proliferated across multiple Indo-European branches: in Latin it yielded sūgere ("to suck") and sūcus ("juice, moisture"), which later entered English via Romance borrowings like succulent and suction; in Celtic it gave Old Irish sūgid; in Baltic it gave Lithuanian sùkti.

2. Proto-Germanic Evolution and Old English Antecedents:

  • In the Proto-Germanic branch, the root developed into the strong verb *sūganą and its variant *sūkaną ("to suck"). Cognates evolved across all Germanic languages, including Old High German sūgan (modern German saugen), Old Saxon sūgan, and Old Norse súga.
  • In Anglo-Saxon England, Old English inherited two closely related forms: the strong Class II verb sūcan and the weak verb sūcian. In Old English medical and homiletic manuscripts, these verbs specifically denoted the nursing of an infant (e.g., in Ælfric’s homilies). While Old English possessed words for infants such as cild, bearn, and meolcsūcend (literally "milk-sucker"), the exact compound suckling had not yet consolidated into its modern morphological form.

3. Middle English Morphological Innovation and the Suffix -ling:

  • During the early Middle English period (12th to 13th centuries), English speakers combined the verb souken (the Middle English descendant of sūcan) with the productive Germanic diminutive suffix -ling (from Proto-Germanic *-lingaz, denoting origin, association, or smallness).
  • This new substantive appeared in manuscripts as soukelyng, sokelyng, or suckeling around the early 14th century. It filled an important lexical niche: while infant (borrowed from Anglo-Norman enfant) indicated social age and legal minority, suckelyng provided a vivid, biologically precise description of an unweaned baby or animal.
  • The term was immortalized in John Wycliffe's Bible translation (c. 1382), translating the Latin Vulgate's lactantes in Psalm 8:2 as "Of the mouth of vnspekende children and soukende [soukelyngis]...", permanently intertwining the word with innocence and divine praise.

4. Early Modern English, Agriculture, and Gastronomy:

  • By the 16th century, spelling coalesced into suckling. The King James Version (1611) preserved the iconic phrasing: "Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength", which ensured that every generation of English speakers was intimately familiar with the term.
  • Simultaneously, the growth of commercial agriculture and Renaissance cookery books expanded the word's domain. Treatises on husbandry by writers like Thomas Tusser (Five Hundred Points of Good Husbandry, 1573) regularly categorized livestock as "sucklings" until weaning, while recipe collections popularized suckling pig as a feast-day delicacy.

5. Modern Usage and Persistence:

  • In contemporary English, suckling survives in full health across biology, veterinary science, literature, and gastronomy. It retains a distinct warmth and natural immediacy that purely Latinate clinical terms like lactating neonate completely lack.
