The missile at the root.

The story of bolted begins not with running but with a weapon: the bolt, the short, stout, heavy-headed arrow discharged by a crossbow. In Old English, attested from around the year 1000, bolt already meant exactly that — a blunt missile built to fly straight and fast and to strike with concentrated force, quite unlike the lighter, feathered longbow arrow. The word’s Germanic ancestry is secure at one level: its cognates run right across the family — German Bolzen, Dutch bout, Swedish bult, all meaning a bolt, a pin, a thick shaft — and these all point back to Proto-Germanic *bultaz. Beyond Proto-Germanic the trail grows uncertain: the most widely proposed source is an Indo-European root meaning “to strike, beat, cut,” which would connect bolt with bale (“to beat”) and ball (“the round thing struck”), but the connection is debated, the root form is disputed, and some handbooks admit that the pre-Germanic origin of the word may be lost in a substrate from which nothing more can be recovered. Honest etymology therefore stops at Proto-Germanic and marks the deepest stage as probable rather than certain.

From missile to bar.

What is certain is how the image multiplied. The bolt’s essential character — a thick, straight, driven shaft — spread sideways into everyday technology: the sliding metal bar that fastens a door is a bolt, its movement a direct echo of the missile’s straight flight (and the door is “bolted” shut with exactly the same word, in the same century, that the horse is “bolting” away in our text). By Middle English the noun had also captured the great forked flash of a storm — a bolt of lightning — because the forked lightning was imagined as the crossbow’s missile hurled from the sky, and it captured the rolled length of cloth and paper, wound like the shaft of a spindle. The core idea in every case is a thing driven straight and hard along its length.

The verb is born: to spring and to fly.

From the missile the verb followed naturally. To bolt is, at root, to move like a bolt — suddenly, straight, and unstoppably. The medieval and early-modern record shows the verb taking shape in both directions at once. On one side it meant “to shoot forth,” “to spring away,” “to dart off” — and from that sense came the horse that bolts, the prisoner who bolts, the political delegate who bolts his party and breaks away from his own side (a colourful Americanism of the nineteenth century), and, in the horticultural world, the plant that bolts, shooting up to seed before its time. On the other side the verb kept its mechanical meaning, “to fasten with a bolt,” so that the same word came to govern two opposite actions — to secure firmly and to flee wildly — a split that English simply tolerates, letting context decide whether a “bolted door” is locked shut or a “bolted horse” has vanished over the horizon. Even the sense “to bolt one’s food,” swallowing hurriedly without chewing, belongs to the same family: the food is imagined as shot straight down the throat, as if launched.

Sound and form.

The word’s form has been exceptionally stable. The Old English bolt has kept its spelling almost unchanged for a thousand years; only the vowel has drifted with the ordinary history of English, the short o of Old English lengthening and shifting in the modern period so that the word rhymes with “bolt / colt” (modern /boʊlt/ in RP). The past tense bolted, formed by the regular weak -ed suffix that has made English verbs for the same millennium, has been the standard form since Middle English. There is nothing exotic in the word’s phonology — and that stability is part of its power: bolted feels like a plain, native, workmanlike verb, exactly the sort of word a man would use of a terrified horse in a dark wood.

Bolted in the scene at the storm.

In the Caspian passage the word does its full work. When thunder splits the sky, Destrier “bolted in good earnest”: the horse is not galloping — galloping is a gait the rider commands — he is being launched, converted by terror into a missile, and the crossbow image glows beneath the sentence. The bolt flies straight and cannot be recalled; the rider can only cling on, “his life hung by a thread,” as the trees of the dusk rush past. The word gathers a millennium of history — the Anglo-Saxon crossbow, the bolted door, the lightning bolt, the political bolt, the bolted meal — into a single instant of animal panic, and it is exactly the right word for the moment when control is lost and motion becomes fate.
