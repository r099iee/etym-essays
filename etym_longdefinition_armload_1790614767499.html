An armload is a measure of quantity defined by the human body, meaning as much as a person can hold and carry in the crook of one arm or in both arms pressed against the chest. It is both a concrete noun denoting a physical burden and an informal unit of measure, similar to a handful or a lapful, but larger and more embracing, evoking the image of someone gathering objects that are too numerous or bulky for the hands alone. In everyday use it most often describes light but bulky things such as firewood, laundry, books, magazines, groceries, flowers, or hay, where the limiting factor is not weight but volume and the ability to encircle and contain.

In its primary literal sense, an armload emphasizes gathering and transport in a single trip, often with connotations of abundance, haste, domestic labour, or harvest. To bring in an armload of wood suggests industrious provision, to drop an armload of files on a desk suggests busy work, and to carry an armload of magazines home, as in the source context, suggests enthusiastic collecting. The word implies a somewhat unwieldy, miscellaneous, piled-up quality, things heaped together rather than neatly packed, held together by bodily effort rather than by a container.

  • Literal armful-measure: as much as the arms can encircle and carry, e.g. an armload of logs, an armload of blankets. Polysemy bridge: from body-part noun arm plus load, the natural extension from hand-handful to arm-armful, generalizing a bodily container into a unit.
  • Figurative abundance: a large, generous amount obtained at once, e.g. an armload of trouble, an armload of awards. Polysemy bridge: via metaphor from physical bulk to abstract plenty, where carrying capacity stands for receiving capacity.
  • Commercial and agricultural use: in farming, floristry and wood-selling, an armload as an informal bundle size, not standardized but understood. Polysemy bridge: via trade practice where bodily measures precede standardized weights, retaining rustic authenticity.
  • Extended verbal sense to arm-load as verb phrase: though rare, to arm-load meaning to heap into the arms. Polysemy bridge: via zero-derivation from noun compound back to action, focusing on the act of gathering rather than the quantity itself.

Stylistically, armload belongs to plain Anglo-Saxon domestic vocabulary, concrete, warm, and slightly informal, contrasting with more technical measures like bushel, cubic foot, or kilogram. It is chiefly American and British colloquial, common in fiction, memoir, and journalism to create tactile visual immediacy. Unlike armful, which stresses fullness of the arms to capacity, armload stresses weight and burden of what is loaded onto the arms, subtly foregrounding effort, and unlike bundle or bale, it implies no tying or wrapping, only bodily containment. Literary nuance often uses armload to signal homeliness, harvest, childlike gathering of treasures, or overwhelmed carrying, as when a character staggers under an armload and must stack or shed items.

Domain-specific notes include its use in forestry and gardening catalogues, in library and archival work for uncounted lots, and in child language acquisition as an early compound illustrating productive compounding. Grammatically it is countable with plural armloads or armsload in older dialect, and it functions attributively as in armload lot. Its semantic opposite is not emptiness but precision, since armload proudly remains vague, human-scaled, and situational, its exact volume varying with arm length, strength, and the shape of the objects carried.
