- 14th c. "the pungent medicinal and culinary root" - Middle English gingivere borrowed via Old French and Latin from Sanskrit; valued as an exotic luxury spice of high status in feastcraft.
- Late 18th c. "feaguing / gingering horses" - equine dealers apply ginger to stimulate lethargic horses before sale, associating the spice with forced vitality and erect bearing.
- Mid-19th c. "spirit, spunk, fiery pluck" - British colloquialism adopts ginger as a metaphor for lively vigor, punch, and spirited courage in humans.
- Late 19th c. "to ginger up" (verb) - spreads into political and institutional parlance for energizing sluggish committees or public campaigns.
- Early 20th c. "reddish-brown / red hair" - colloquial nominalization and adjective in Britain and the Commonwealth, eventually acquiring lighthearted or playground teasing overtones ("gingerism").
- Present "polysemous colloquial & culinary mainstay" - firmly established in cuisine worldwide, while remaining standard British idiom for vitality, pep, and activist pressure groups ("ginger groups").
