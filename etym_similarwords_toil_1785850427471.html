• Labour: Similar because both denote hard, productive effort; differs because "labour" is the more formal, neutral word (and names childbirth and a political class), while "toil" emphasizes exhausting continuous effort and carries a more poetic, sympathetic colour.
  • Work: Similar because both name effort directed to a purpose; differs because "work" is the neutral generic word for any activity, pleasant or routine, while "toil" specifically means hard, wearying effort.
  • Drudgery: Similar because both are wearying, repetitive work; differs because "drudgery" is dull, menial, mindless routine, while "toil" can be heroic and meaningful even when exhausting.
  • Grind: Similar because both suggest unremitting hard effort; differs because "the grind" is modern colloquial slang for the daily routine slog, while "toil" is older, elevated, and literary.
  • Slog: Similar because both mean slow, hard, effortful progress; differs because "slog" (British colloquial) emphasizes trudging heavily through difficulty, while "toil" is the dignified older word.
  • Exertion: Similar because both involve physical or mental effort; differs because "exertion" is the single act of applying effort or strength, neutral and often brief, while "toil" is prolonged exhausting labour.
  • Effort: Similar because both name energy applied to a task; differs because "effort" is a measured, often short application of energy, while "toil" is the whole continuing grind.
  • Sweat: Similar because both connote hard physical work ("the sweat of one's brow"); differs because "sweat" names the physical symptom and is metaphorical, while "toil" names the labour itself.
  • Travail: Similar because both mean painful, arduous labour; differs because "travail" (from Old French, ultimately the same "struggle" family) is archaic and literary and also means childbirth pangs, while "toil" is the standard elevated word.
  • Struggle: Similar because both involve great effort against difficulty; differs because "struggle" is a fight or contest against an opposing force, while "toil" is steady exhausting work, not necessarily against an opponent.
  • Moil: Similar because "toil and moil" pairs the two as exhausting work; differs because "moil" (from Old French moillier, "to wet") now chiefly means to work hard in a confused way and is rare and dialectal, while "toil" is standard.
  • Snare / net / trap: Similar because the archaic plural "toils" are hunting nets or snares, and these words share the entrapment sense; differs because "snare", "net", and "trap" are the everyday words for catching devices, while "toils" is archaic and literary and normally plural.
  • Graft: Similar because "graft" (British) names hard, dogged work; differs because "graft" is informal slang, while "toil" is elevated and can also mean movement with painful effort.
  • Elbow grease: Similar because both name hard manual effort; differs because "elbow grease" is a humorous informal phrase, while "toil" is dignified and literary.
