- 14th to 15th Century: Itinerant, wandering, and vagrant (phonetic variant of errant) - Middle English legal borrowing designating roving outlaws, wandering beggars, and judicial circuits (e.g., 'justice arrant', 'thief arrant').
- 16th Century: Semantic pejoration from roving outlaw to confirmed rogue - Through constant collocation with 'thief' and 'knave', the word lost its physical meaning of traveling and became an intensifier for notorious, hardened scoundrelism.
- 17th to 18th Century: Expansion to intellectual folly, cowardice, and nonsense - Elizabethan dramatists (Shakespeare, Jonson) and Enlightenment satirists broadened arrant to characterize cognitive absurdity ('arrant fool', 'arrant nonsense', 'arrant coward').
- 19th to 21st Century: High-register literary intensifier for shameless wrongdoing and blatant falsity - Preserved in educated prose, historical essays, and judicial critiques to emphatically denounce barefaced deceit or brazen historical usurpation.
