- [c. 1300: the animal foot] - Middle English paue is a noun only, naming the foot of a cat, dog, or bear; neutral, descriptive, wholly animal, with no human application.
- [early 17th century: the noun becomes a verb] - to paw first means, of an animal, 'to strike, scrape, or beat at the ground with the forefoot'; a horse paws the stable floor in impatience, and the word is purely kinetic.
- [17th century: the human turn] - within a generation the verb extends to people, meaning 'to handle roughly or clumsily'; the word begins to shed dignity and gains its first edge of reproach.
- [18th century: the pejorative sense] - to paw acquires its current, chiefly pejorative force - 'to fondle or maul with the hands'; animality becomes accusation, and the word carries a faint undertone of the beast even in neutral uses.
- [Contemporary: fixed phrases and register] - paw at, paw over, paw through, and paw about crystallize into set patterns, informal, vivid, and faintly demeaning; formal writing prefers grope or fumble. In the passage pawing is restored to full literalness - a creature reduced to forepaws, touching everything with the flat, pad-like surfaces of the hands.
