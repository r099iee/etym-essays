- 14th century: "scrutiny" enters English via Old French, meaning a close, careful search or examination — in ecclesiastical use, the formal probing of candidates, relics, and the conscience.
- 17th century: the verb "scrutinize" is coined on the Latin stem, and scrutiny widens from religious formality to any sustained, item-by-item sifting in which nothing is taken at face value.
- 19th-20th centuries: scrutiny becomes the watchful, distrustful gaze of law, journalism, and the public eye — a concentrated taking-apart of a person or thing, the untrusting look of one who turns evidence over like a rag-picker turning over refuse.
- 14th century: "conjecture" enters English from Old French — a "casting together," a guess formed by throwing scattered hints into one heap and reading a pattern; an intellectual, provisional judgment.
- 16th-18th centuries: the word hardens into its modern sense — an opinion or conclusion drawn from incomplete or uncertain evidence, the reasoned guess of the scholar or detective.
- Modern: conjecture stands opposed to blind guessing and to provable fact — "beyond conjecture" means past what the mind can throw together and infer at all.
