- 13th–14th c. — the formal legal petition: enters Middle English from Anglo-Norman as a direct borrowing of Latin libellus ('little book'), naming the initial written bill of complaint in ecclesiastical and admiralty courts.
- 15th–16th c. — defamatory bills and placards: shifts to describe clandestine defamatory verses, bills, or ballads posted publicly on church doors or market crosses (short for libel famous / libellus famosus).
- 17th c. — Star Chamber and seditious repression: codified by the Court of Star Chamber as a criminal offense against state authority; truth is denied as a defense ('the greater the truth, the greater the libel').
- 18th c. — Augustan pamphlet warfare: Swift, Pope, and London authors use libels to describe the relentless stream of pirated pamphlets, keys, and personal character assassinations produced by Grub Street hacks.
- 1792 — Fox's Libel Act: a major constitutional milestone in Britain, transferring the power to determine whether a publication is libelous from crown-appointed judges to independent juries.
- 19th c. — common law tort differentiation: civil courts firmly distinguish permanent written defamation (libel) from transient spoken defamation (slander), requiring different evidentiary standards.
- 20th c. — constitutional free speech standards: Landmark US Supreme Court ruling (*New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 1964) establishes the 'actual malice' standard for public officials, transforming libel defense.
- Today — digital defamation in the internet era: applied globally to defamatory social media posts, blogs, online reviews, and viral tweets, grappling with instantaneous digital republishing.
