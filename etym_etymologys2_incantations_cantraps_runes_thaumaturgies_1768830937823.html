Linear derivation chain for INCANTATIONS (root word one of four): PIE *kan- (to sing, resound) — reconstructed root, c. 4500–3500 BCE; well attested in the Celtic and Italic branches (Old Irish canaid "he sings", Welsh canu "to sing"). → Latin canere (to sing) — attested from the 3rd century BCE (Plautus, Ennius). → Latin cantāre (frequentative: to sing repeatedly, to chant) — attested classical Latin, 1st century BCE. → Latin incantāre (in- + cantāre: to chant over or upon someone, to enchant) — attested 1st century BCE (Cicero; the magical "singing over" a person or object). → Latin incantātiō, incantātiōnis (a chanting, an incantation, a spell) — attested Imperial Latin, 1st–4th century CE. → Old French incantation (incantation, spell) — attested 12th century. → English incantation (chanted spell) — from the 14th century → incantations (plural) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain is entirely secure; incantation is a transparent Latin compound (in + cantāre + -tiō). English relatives from the same stem: chant, enchant, canticle, cantor.
Linear derivation chain for CANTRAPS (root word two of four): Origin obscure and DEBATED — no secure PIE or earlier stage can be asserted; the word first appears in early modern Scots. → Scots cantrap / cantraip / cantrip (a charm, a spell, a trick; a piece of witchcraft) — attested early modern Scots, 16th century. → English (literary, from the 18th century; popularized by Sir Walter Scott): cantrap / cantrip → cantraps (plural) ◄ TARGET. Proposed etymologies (all debated, none commanding full scholarly agreement): (1) from Scots cant (a toss, a throw, a trick; also to speak in set phrases) + the -rap/-rip of trap or trip; (2) from Middle English cant (a corner or edge), with the notion of boundary or threshold magic; (3) a blend whose second element carries the "trifling, worthless" sense of a small thing. Note: ZERO GUESSWORK — the word's origin is genuinely uncertain. The earliest recorded forms are the Scots cantrap/cantraip of the 16th century, and no single etymology commands scholarly agreement; it is safest to report the Scots family and flag the obscurity. The modern fantasy sense (a minor spell) is a 20th-century revival, especially in role-playing games.
Linear derivation chain for RUNES (root word three of four): PIE *rew- (to roar, murmur, whisper; some handbooks write the IE base *ru-n- with a nasal) — proposed reconstruction, c. 4500–3500 BCE; this PIE stage is itself debated (see note below). → Proto-Germanic *rūnō (secret, mystery, whispered counsel; also a runic letter) — reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE. → Old Norse rún (secret, mystery; a runic letter) — attested from the earliest runic inscriptions (2nd century CE onward; e.g., the Vimose comb) and in Eddaic verse; Old English rūn (secret, counsel, mystery; a runic letter) — attested from the 8th century CE (e.g., the Franks Casket, the Old English Rune Poem). → Middle English rūn / rune (secret, counsel; runic letter) — 12th–15th century. → Modern English rune — the LETTER sense was re-established in the 17th century by antiquarian scholarship (Old Norse rún had meant both secret and letter) → runes (plural) ◄ TARGET. Wider cognates: Gothic rūna (mystery — Wulfila's Gothic Bible uses rūna to translate Greek mystērion), Old Irish rūn (secret; a Celtic loan from Germanic), Welsh rhin (secret, charm). Note: The PIE stage is speculative — *rew- (to murmur) is the common proposal, but several handbooks reconstruct only a Germanic *rūnō without a secure Indo-European etymon; the connection to a root meaning roar/murmur/whisper is widely accepted but not absolutely certain. The Germanic stage and its meaning (secret, counsel) are secure.
Linear derivation chain for THAUMATURGIES (root word four of four): Compound root: Greek thauma (wonder, marvel; etymology DEBATED — possibly a Pre-Greek substrate word, with no agreed Indo-European derivation) + Greek érgon (work, deed; from PIE *werg-, to do, make, work). → PIE *werg- (to do, make, work) — reconstructed root, c. 4500–3500 BCE; the root of English work, Greek érgon, and the English organ, erg, energy (through Greek). → Greek érgon (work, deed, task) — attested from Homer (8th century BCE); Greek thauma (wonder, marvel) — attested from the 6th century BCE, of debated origin. → Greek thaumatourgós (adjective and noun: working wonders, a wonder-worker; thauma + -ourgós "working") — attested from the 5th century BCE (Herodotus uses thaumata "wonders"). → Greek thaumatourgíā (the working of wonders, miracle-working) — attested Hellenistic Greek, 3rd–1st century BCE. → Late Latin thaumaturgia (wonder-working) — attested Late Antiquity, 4th–6th century CE. → English thaumaturgy (learned borrowing) — from the 16th century; plural thaumaturgies ◄ TARGET. Note: Only the érgon half of the compound has a clear PIE etymology (*werg-, to work). The Greek noun thauma (wonder, marvel) has no agreed Indo-European derivation — Beekes suggests a Pre-Greek substrate origin, and older proposals linking it to PIE roots meaning "to become" or "to shine/strike wonder" are speculative; this is reported as an open question rather than a settled chain.
