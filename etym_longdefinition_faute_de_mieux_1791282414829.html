faute de mieux is a French borrowing used in English to mean "for lack of something better" — an explicit admission that the person is settling for an imperfect option because no preferable alternative is available. It does not praise the chosen thing; it excuses the choice by confessing that circumstances, time, or scarcity forced it. If a committee appoints a reluctant volunteer leader faute de mieux, it means nobody better could be found, not that the volunteer was ideal.

In Wodehouse and in broad British usage it is a dry, self-deprecating, slightly arch aside. The speaker anticipates the objection — "this plan is open to criticism" — and pre-empts it by conceding its imperfection while insisting it was the best available under the circumstances. The tone is rueful, pragmatic, and faintly comic: making do, muddling through, choosing the least-bad course.

Grammatically it functions as an adverbial or parenthetical phrase: placed after a noun, verb, or whole clause, often set off by commas or dashes. It can modify a choice ("a compromise candidate faute de mieux"), a decision ("we stayed faute de mieux"), or an attitude ("he was welcomed faute de mieux"). It is never inflected and is usually printed in italics in formal prose, though roman is now common.

Pragmatically it signals awareness, humility, and realism. It tells the listener: I know this is not perfect, do not mistake my acceptance for enthusiasm. It therefore softens criticism, deflects blame, and preserves face for both chooser and chosen.

  • Core sense — for want of anything better: the only available recourse is taken because nothing superior exists. Bridge: literal French faute "lack" + de mieux "of better" maps directly onto this compensatory choice.
  • Provisional acceptance / stopgap: something used temporarily until a better thing appears — a caretaker manager, a makeshift venue, a draft plan. Bridge: extension from permanent scarcity to temporary scarcity.
  • Resigned acquiescence: putting up with a flawed arrangement, partner, or excuse without protest because protest would be futile. Bridge: emotional colouring of the core sense toward world-weary tolerance.
  • Ironic understatement: used humorously to damn with faint praise, as when a host introduces "our star entertainment — faute de mieux." Bridge: deliberate exploitation of the phrase's built-in apology for comic effect.

Register is educated, literary, and slightly formal; it marks the speaker as literate and cosmopolitan. It is not slang and is rare in casual speech, but appears in journalism, criticism, academic writing, and classic comic prose where a shrug in French is more elegant than a long English excuse.

Typical errors to avoid: it never means "the best" or "the ideal" — quite the opposite. Nor does it mean "by accident." It always carries the shadow of comparison: this, because better was missing. Mastering it lets a learner deliver Wodehouse-style dry apologies, admit constraints gracefully, and recognise when an author is quietly telling you not to admire the solution, only to accept its necessity.
