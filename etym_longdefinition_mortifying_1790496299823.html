mortifying is the present participle and adjective of mortify, and it carries three great, historically linked senses: humiliating, gangrenous, and ascetic. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita reporting that his maternal grandfather, thrown from his horse against a wall at Croydon, died of the hurt's mortifying — it is the medical sense: the crushed leg underwent mortification, the death of tissue, what surgeons now call gangrene and necrosis. A contused, compound injury in the pre-antiseptic age quickly turned black, cold, and putrid; once mortification set in, amputation was the only hope, and often no hope at all. The grandfather's death at two-and-thirty is exactly the statistical fate of such injuries before Lister.

In modern everyday use the dominant sense is humiliating, deeply embarrassing: a mortifying defeat, a mortifying typo, mortifying footage. To be mortified is to feel one's pride killed — to wish the ground would swallow one. A student called on in class with no answer, a speaker whose slides fail, a team beaten 7–0 at home, a lover rejected in public: all suffer mortification in this social sense. The intensity is stronger than embarrassing and weaker than traumatic; it implies exposure before witnesses whose good opinion one craves. The bridge from the medical sense is metaphorical but exact: as gangrene kills flesh, shame kills self-regard.

  • Social-humiliation sense (modern head sense): causing acute shame or loss of face; e.g. a mortifying performance review, it was mortifying to be corrected in front of everyone, mortifying ignorance of the basics. Typical subjects are defeats, exposures, blunders, rejections, and bodily mishaps. The connotation is sympathetic rather than clinical: one feels mortified, one endures something mortifying.
  • Medical-gangrenous sense (source sense, now archaic): undergoing mortification, becoming gangrenous; e.g. a mortifying limb, the hurt's mortifying, mortifying flesh. Here mortifying is almost a technical participle: tissue that is dying from loss of blood supply and bacterial invasion. Nineteenth-century casebooks write freely of mortifying wounds, mortifying frostbite, mortifying hernia where modern surgeons would write necrotic, gangrenous, ischaemic. The possessive in Ruskin — the hurt's mortifying — treats the wound almost as an agent performing its own deadly work.
  • Ascetic-disciplinary sense: subduing bodily appetite by self-denial; e.g. mortifying the flesh, a mortifying fast, mortifying one's passions. Monks, evangelicals, and Stoics mortify pride, lust, and sloth by fasting, vigils, cold, and obedience. The bridge is theological: St Paul's mortify therefore your members (Colossians 3:5) commands believers to put to death sinful impulses as one would deaden a limb. Ruskin's evangelical household, saturated in such language, would hear all three senses resonate at once.
  • Intensive-destructive sense (chemistry, etc.): in older technical writing, mortifying can mean deadening or neutralising activity; e.g. mortifying lime, mortifying the action of yeast. The logic is the same Latin root: to mortify is to make dead, whether pride, flesh, or ferment.
  • Grammar and collocation: as an adjective mortifying precedes nouns (a mortifying experience) or follows be/find (it was mortifying, I found it mortifying); as a participle it heads clauses (mortifying his pride, the wound mortifying rapidly). Adverbs pair by sense: deeply/utterly mortifying (shame), rapidly/fatally mortifying (medicine), deliberately/rigorously mortifying (asceticism).
  • Register trajectory: the medical sense, once neutral and frequent, has retreated before gangrenous and necrotic and now sounds quaint or scriptural; the humiliation sense, once metaphorical, has become the plain meaning a learner will meet ten times for every one encounter with gangrene. To read Ruskin correctly one must reverse the modern priority and hear the surgeon's word first.

All three senses share one Latin corpse: mors, mortis (death) made into a verb. Whether it is pride that dies, flesh that dies, or sin that is made to die, mortifying always describes a dying — deserved or dreaded, willed or suffered — and that is why Ruskin's single participle can carry a grandfather's crushed leg, a family's evangelical severity, and every reader's memory of blushing shame in one black word.
