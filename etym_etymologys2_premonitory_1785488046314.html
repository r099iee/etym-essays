Chain for PREMONITORY (all stages attested or securely reconstructed; no letter labels):
1. PIE *men- 'to think, have in mind' — one of the best-attested Indo-European roots (Sanskrit manas 'mind', Greek menos 'mind, spirit', Latin mens 'mind', Gothic gamunds 'memory'). Note: the exact derivational route to Latin monere is debated (see note below).
2. Latin monere 'to warn, advise, remind' (from the o-grade *mon- of *men- with a causative formation; attested from Plautus onward, 3rd–2nd c. BCE). Source of English monitor, admonish, monition.
3. Latin praemonere 'to warn beforehand, forewarn' (prae- 'before' + monere; attested in Classical Latin, e.g., Cicero, 1st c. BCE).
4. Latin praemonitorius 'pertaining to a forewarning, serving to forewarn' (Late Latin; formed from the participle stem of praemonere with the adjectival suffix -torius, which yields English -ory).
5. Middle French premonitoire 'giving advance warning' (the French adjective, from Latin praemonitorius).
6. English premonitory, first recorded c. 1580 in Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy, borrowed from Middle French premonitoire.
7. PREMONITORY (modern English; the exact form on this card).

Note on the debated stage: the PIE root *men- 'to think' is secure, but the specific morphology that yields Latin monere is debated. The traditional analysis derives monere from the o-grade *mon- plus a causative suffix (*mon-eie-), literally 'to make (someone) think, bring to mind', hence 'remind, warn'; alternative analyses posit a different suffix or a denominative origin. The English and French stages, and the meaning 'forewarning', are fully attested. No letter labels A–F are used; every stage is named by its real attested form.
