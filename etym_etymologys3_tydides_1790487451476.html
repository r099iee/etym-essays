Tydides is not a separate personal name but a patronymic: Greek Tudeides (Τυδεΐδης), meaning son of Tydeus, used in Homer almost exclusively for Diomedes, king of Argos. To understand the English word one must therefore tell two stories: the story of the father-name Tydeus and the story of the son-suffix -ides, and then the story of how Latin and English poets preserved the whole frozen formula.

Tydeus himself belongs to the older Theban generation. Son of Oeneus of Calydon, exiled for homicide, he became one of the Seven Against Thebes and was killed there, winning posthumous fame for ferocity. His name, Greek Tudeus (Τυδεύς), appears already in the Iliad and in Hesiod. Its deeper origin is obscure. Unlike transparent Greek names such as Agamemnon "very resolute" or Menelaos "withstanding the host", Tudeus has no convincing Greek etymology. Robert Beekes in his Etymological Dictionary of Greek lists a large class of heroic names in -eus — Odysseus, Peleus, Theseus, Tydeus — as probably Pre-Greek, that is, inherited from the non-Indo-European language spoken in the Aegean before Greek speakers arrived. On this view there is no PIE root to cite; the name is a substrate survival. Ancient scholiasts tried to connect it to tupto "to strike" or to tude-forms, but modern linguists regard this as folk-etymology. This debate must be stated openly: we know the Homeric form and meaning with certainty, but the pre-Homeric root is unknown.

  • The patronymic machine: -ides: Homeric Greek marks filiation with the suffix -ides (masculine) / -is (feminine), added to the father's stem: Tydeus gives Tudeides, Atreus gives Atreides (Agamemnon or Menelaus), Peleus gives Peleides (Achilles), Laertes gives Laertiades (Odysseus). The suffix is productive, metrically useful, and honorific: to call a hero by his father's name recalls lineage, legitimacy, and inherited valour. In oral performance it also supplies ready-made dactylic cola. Tudeides Diomedes fills the hexameter with high regularity, which explains its frequency — some forty occurrences in the Iliad.
  • Phonetic shape: Homeric Tūdeïdēs scans as long-short-long-long. The stem shows the regular -eu- + -ides contraction: Tudeu- + -ides yields Tudeides with hiatus preserved for metre. Latin poets, beginning with Virgil and Ovid, borrowed the form unchanged as Tydides, adapting u to y to represent Greek upsilon and keeping the patronymic force. Prosody in Latin remains long, hence English traditional pronunciation with stressed second syllable.
  • Latin transmission: In Virgil's Aeneid Diomedes reappears as a settled Italian king who refuses to fight Aeneas again, and is called Tydides at key moments to recall his Iliadic past. Ovid, Statius in the Thebaid, and late-antique commentators keep the form alive as a school tag. Through Servius and medieval mythographers it entered the standard apparatus of classical allusion: any educated reader knew Tydides = Diomedes, son of Tydeus without needing explanation.
  • English: Chapman to Pope to Ruskin: George Chapman's Homer (1616) renders Tydides directly, preserving the Greek patronymic for grandeur. Alexander Pope's Iliad (1715–1720), the version young John Ruskin read as a child alongside Walter Scott, uses Tydides systematically wherever Homer has Tudeides. Pope's note explains: Tydides, i.e. Diomed, son of Tydeus. For Ruskin's generation this was therefore not obscure learning but nursery familiarity: Tydides was simply Diomedes' heroic surname, like Pelides for Achilles. Ruskin's sentence — Tydides or Idomeneus always killed twenty Trojans to other people's one — records a child's quantitative impression of the Iliad: the great chiefs enjoy an impossible kill-ratio, proof to a boy that kings work harder than other men.
  • Modern status: Outside classical poetry and Pope-derived reading, Tydides is now rare and markedly literary. The Oxford English Dictionary marks it as poetic and historical. Modern translations (Lattimore, Fagles, Wilson) usually render son of Tydeus or Diomedes outright, reserving Tydides for glossaries. It survives in commentaries, crossword puzzles, and in Ruskin scholarship where this very passage is cited as evidence for Victorian Homeric reception. No verb, adjective, or common-noun sense has ever developed; it remains a frozen proper-name epithet.

In short, Tydides descends vertically from Homeric Greek patronymic formation through Latin epic borrowing into early-modern English poetic diction, with its first element's ultimate prehistory debated between a Pre-Greek substrate origin (Beekes) and discarded ancient folk-etymologies. Its meaning has never shifted: always son of Tydeus, always Diomedes.
