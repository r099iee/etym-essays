→ PIE *ghen- / *gneh₂- (to scratch, scrape, gnaw, bite), reconstructed; Pokorny groups the Germanic gnag- cluster under this base, though the root is also frequently classed as expressive or imitative because guttural-plus-nasal scraping verbs (gn-, kn-) recur independently across many language families → Proto-Germanic *gnagan- (to gnaw, scrape, or wear away with the teeth; a class VI strong verb), late 2nd–1st millennium BCE; continued by Old Saxon gnagan, Old High German gnagan, Middle Dutch gnaghen, and Old Norse gnaga → Old English gnagan (to gnaw, bite off gradually, fret; class VI strong verb: past singular gnōg, plural gnegon, past participle gegnagen), attested from the 8th century CE → Middle English gnawen (also gnagen, knawen — to nibble, chew persistently, wear away by biting), 12th–15th centuries CE → Early Modern English gnaw (to gnaw, with past gnawed; the older strong forms gnew and gnowe were abandoned during this period) → Modern English GNAWED — the card's target form: the past tense and past participle of gnaw, with the now-silent initial preserved in the spelling (the cluster /ɡn-/ was simplified to /n-/ in Early Modern English). NOTE on the debate: the deeper PIE connection is debated — the root may be genuinely inherited from *ghen- / *gneh₂-, but many specialists treat gnaw as part of an expressive "gn-"/"kn-" scraping-word set that could have been coined independently in Germanic; both positions are stated here rather than asserted as certain.
