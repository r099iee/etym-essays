- 14th century - FORGING is smithcraft pure and simple: hot iron beaten into blades and tools; the word carries the honest dignity of the smith's craft.
- 14th-15th centuries - the metaphor extends: poets and chroniclers speak of forging friendships, pacts, and realms; the same period sees forgen mean 'to invent' a tale or a lie, the germ of the criminal sense.
- 15th-16th centuries - the dark turn: forging a writing, seal, or coin comes to mean counterfeiting; public perception shifts from craft to fraud, and the forger becomes a stock villain of the law courts.
- 17th-19th centuries - industrialisation: forging leaves the village anvil for water- and steam-powered hammers (Nasmyth's steam hammer, 1839) and immense drop-forges; 'a forging' now also names the machine-made product; the word grows harder, bigger, more impersonal.
- 19th-20th centuries - political rhetoric: 'forging a nation', 'forging a new destiny' become heroic idioms of state-building and social reform; orators deliberately keep the metallic image alive.
- 20th-21st centuries - everyday flexibility: people forge careers, identities, and friendships while courts prosecute forged passports and artworks; 'forging ahead' (an uncertain relative of 'force') rounds out the family; perception now swings between primal craftsmanship and calculated deception.
