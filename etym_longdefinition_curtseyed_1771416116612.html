Curtseyed is the simple past and past-participle form of the verb curtsey (also spelled curtsy), meaning “to perform a curtsey”: the traditional gesture of greeting or respect made by a girl or woman by bending the knees, dipping the body, and usually inclining the head, often with the skirt gathered lightly in the hands. As a past tense the word denotes a completed action: she curtseyed, that is, she executed that composed downward dip of the body at a particular moment in the past. Because the gesture is gendered, the verb carries cultural freight that “bow” does not: a man bows from the waist or nods the head, while a woman in the same circumstances of formality drops into a curtsey. In the immediate context of the note — from David Hawkes's translation of the Dream of the Red Chamber — Dai-yu “accordingly smiled and curtseyed, greeting her by her correct name as she did so.” The sequence of smile, curtsey, and correct-name greeting is a triple performance of self-possession by a newly arrived orphan; the curtsey is her first bodily statement inside the Jia household. It is a gesture of simultaneous humility and dignity: the body lowers, but the lowering is composed, controlled, and eloquent, and it acknowledges at once the seniority of her hostesses and her own gratitude for being received.

Because the word has a layered history — a phonetic contraction of courtesy — its senses form a family radiating from one physical core. The full polysemy is best surveyed as follows:

  • The gesture proper (core sense): a dip of the knees and body, performed by a girl or woman, as a mark of respect or greeting to a superior, a monarch, a senior relative, a teacher, or a guest. This is the sense in the source sentence, and it is the sense the noun and verb share.
  • The noun curtsey / curtsy: the same gesture treated as an object — “she dropped a curtsey,” “to make one's curtsey,” “a deep curtsey.” The verb and noun are historically one word split by function, exactly as “bow” can be verb or noun.
  • Double spelling curtsey / curtsy: two sanctioned spellings of the same word. Curtsy reflects the modern contracted pronunciation; curtsey keeps the older courtesy-linked spelling. Each makes its own past form — curtseyed and curtsied — and both are correct.
  • Register and context restriction: by the late twentieth century the gesture had retreated from everyday life; the word now appears in royal-presentation contexts, historical novels, theatre, ballet, and period film, and carries an archaic or deliberately formal, courtly colour. Using it today is itself a statement of register.
  • Figurative / transferred use: “to curtsey to somebody” can mean to defer obsequiously, to yield precedence to someone — the physical lowering rendered as a social or political capitulation, as in “the minister curtseyed to the banking lobby.” The concrete gesture becomes a metaphor for submissive accommodation.
  • Ballet and stage technique: the curtsey (French révérence) is the formal bow dancers give to the audience, to partners, or at the close of a class or rehearsal; in this technical use the word is professional jargon with a fixed, drilled execution and a definite musical count.
  • Historical bridge to courtesy: the word is a contraction of courtesy (16th century). A “curtsey” was originally “a courtesy,” a polite, courteous act; the general notion of courtesy narrowed to this one bodily gesture, so the verb inherited the entire chivalric history of courtliness — the court, the courtier, the courteous man, and the bowing body of the courtly woman.
  • Ceremonial and devotional extensions: in some churches a “reverence” or curtsey toward the altar or the reserved sacrament is a devotional act of worship; in royal protocol the deep curtsey to the sovereign is a codified, near-choreographed gesture taught to debutantes and performed at presentations.
  • Historical gender-marking: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the courtesy was performed by both sexes, with the woman's version already the knee-dip; only later did the gesture become exclusively feminine, leaving men with the bow — a social division still built into the word's definition.

Semantically, curtseyed belongs to the small closed family of bodily deference verbs — bow, bob, genuflect, kneel, prostrate, salaam, kowtow — but differs from each of them: it is the only one restricted to women, the only one executed by flexing both knees while the trunk remains fairly upright, and the only one whose very form is a feminine courtly courtesy. In the sentence it therefore performs three simultaneous jobs: it marks rank (the hostesses are senior), it marks gender (a female greeting rather than a bow), and it marks character (the composure with which the orphan manages her first public gesture in the great house). The word in context is thus not simply “performed a respectful gesture”; it is a compressed social autobiography in a single past-tense syllable.
