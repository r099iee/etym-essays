- Late 16th to Early 17th Century (c. 1600) - Borrowed into English from Venetian Italian via French; initially designated foreign, handwritten political newsletters or scandalous continental news-sheets.
- Mid-17th Century (1665) - The founding of *The Oxford Gazette* (promptly becoming *The London Gazette*) transforms the word into the preeminent official crown organ of legal record, state proclamations, and military appointments.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - Expands widely as a prestigious title for independent commercial newspapers, while the verb *to gazette* becomes firmly established in British military parlance and insolvency proceedings.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Possesses strong societal connotations in literature (Wilde, Thackeray, Dickens); appearing "in the Gazette" represented either aristocratic military distinction or public ruinous bankruptcy.
- 20th Century - Becomes formalized in institutional, academic, and statutory administrative registers across Commonwealth governments and global universities.
- 21st Century - Continues primarily as an authoritative online legal database of government notices and an esteemed historic masthead for venerable press institutions.
