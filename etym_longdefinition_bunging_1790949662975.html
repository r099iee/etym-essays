The present participle verb bunging (from the base verb to bung) is a highly versatile, expressive colloquial term in British, Australian, and Commonwealth English. It encompasses two major semantic branches: an informal, energetic sense of carelessly throwing, tossing, or stuffing something into a space, and an older, technical mechanical sense of plugging, corking, or stopping up an opening (such as the bunghole of a barrel or cask).

To establish a thorough lexicographical account of bunging, its multiple primary, secondary, and regional slang applications must be detailed:

  • Primary Colloquial Sense: Carelessly Throwing, Hurling, or Tossing
    In modern British and Commonwealth vernacular, 'bunging' describes the action of casually, moodily, or forcefully tossing or hurling an object without meticulous aim. It often suggests a distracted, irritable, or nonchalant emotional state (e.g. 'moodily bunging stones at a flowerpot' or 'bunging a cricket ball across the lawn'). In literary dialogues, it conveys an informal, unbothered attitude toward the projectile.
  • Secondary Colloquial Sense: Hasty Stuffing, Cramming, or Placing
    By extension of the throwing motion, 'bunging' frequently means hastily, carelessly, or informally putting, stuffing, or cramming something into an enclosure without neat arrangement (e.g. 'just bunging all the laundry into the washing machine' or 'bunging a frozen pizza into the oven for ten minutes').
  • Historical & Technical Sense: Plugging or Sealing a Cask (Bunghole)
    In brewing, coopering, and winemaking, 'bunging' is the technical operation of driving a tapered wooden, rubber, or silicone stopper (a bung) into the hole (the bunghole) of a cask, barrel, or vat to seal the contents against air or regulate carbonation during fermentation. A 'bunging apparatus' is a specialized pressure-release valve used by modern craft brewers.
  • British Underworld & Political Slang: Bribery and Illicit Payments
    In British sporting, police, and political slang, 'bunging' refers to the act of passing illicit money, a tip, or a bribe (a 'bung') to someone under the table. Tabloid exposés in British football, public contracting, and local governance frequently refer to corrupt managers or officials receiving or 'bunging' secret payments to secure transfers or licenses.
  • Australasian Idiomatic & Medical Slang: 'Going Bung' and Injuries
    In Australian and New Zealand English, the root bung functions as an adjective meaning broken, ruined, incapacitated, or diseased (e.g. 'a bung knee', 'a bung eye'). Consequently, 'bunging on an act' means feigning an injury or putting on pretentious airs, while a machine that fails completely has 'gone bung'.
  • Metaphorical & Semantic Bridges
    The semantic evolution of bung showcases two distinct historical streams that converged in British popular slang: the cooper's physical action of thrusting a heavy plug violently into a cask bunghole, and the Elizabethan cant noun bung (a purse or pocket). Thrusting money into someone's pocket yielded the bribery sense, while the vigorous thrusting motion of corking naturally expanded into throwing, chucking, and stuffing items carelessly into any receptacle.
