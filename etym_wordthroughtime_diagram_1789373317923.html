- 5th to 4th Century BCE (Classical Greece) - Originated as Greek *diágramma*, used by geometricians (Plato, Aristotle, Euclid) for figures marked out by lines for mathematical proofs, and by music theorists for scale charts.
- 1st Century BCE to 5th Century CE (Roman Antiquity) - Borrowed into Latin as *diagramma*, used in technical treatises on geometry, astronomy, and Roman surveying (*agrimensores*).
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Early Modern English) - Entered English (c. 1610–1620) via Renaissance Latin; applied to technical geometric figures, astrological charts, and mechanical illustrations.
- 18th to 19th Century (Industrial & Scientific Revolution) - Massively expanded across natural sciences and engineering; John Venn develops Venn diagrams (1880), and American educators Alonzo Reed and Brainerd Kellogg introduce grammatical sentence diagramming (1877).
- Mid-20th Century - Quantum physicist Richard Feynman invents Feynman diagrams (1948); structuralist and semiotic theorists (Barthes, Lévi-Strauss, Lacan) adopt *diagram* as a critical verb meaning to deconstruct and map texts schematically.
- 21st Century - Pervasive in computer science, software architecture (UML diagrams), user interface design, and data visualization as an indispensable cognitive tool.
