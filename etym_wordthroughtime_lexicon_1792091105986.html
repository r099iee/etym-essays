- Hellenistic & Byzantine Eras (c. 2nd c. BCE – 10th c. CE) — Coined in Greek as lexikon biblion (λεξικὸν βιβλίον), meaning literally "word-book"; formalized in monumental Byzantine compilations like the Suda and Hesychius of Alexandria to preserve classical and poetic vocabulary.
- Renaissance & Humanism (15th–16th c.) — Adopted into Renaissance Latin and borrowed into Early Modern English specifically to designate scholarly reference dictionaries of ancient Greek, Classical Hebrew, and Arabic.
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Philology) — Expanded from classical dictionaries to denote the comprehensive, authoritative wordstock of an entire language, literary corpus (e.g. "the Shakespearean lexicon"), or professional discipline.
- Mid-20th c. (The Chomskyan Cognitive Revolution) — Redefined fundamentally in linguistics and cognitive psychology to denote the "mental lexicon"—the biological, neural repository of lexical knowledge in human speakers.
- Late 20th–21st c. (Computational Linguistics & NLP) — Established in computer science and artificial intelligence as structured lexical databases, tokenizer symbol tables, and sentiment evaluation frameworks powering modern language processing.
