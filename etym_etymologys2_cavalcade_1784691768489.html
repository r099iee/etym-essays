Derivation chain for CAVALCADE: Stage 1 — The ultimate root is uncertain. Latin caballus 'work-horse, nag, gelding' is very likely a loanword into Latin from a western Mediterranean or Gallic (Celtic) substrate — the Gaulish word for 'horse' is recorded as caballos — and its deeper connection to Proto-Indo-European is disputed; a link to the root *kab- 'to seize' has been proposed but is not generally accepted. The sound-shape is therefore best described as substrate in origin, with no secure PIE etymon. Stage 2 — Latin caballus 'work-horse, pack-horse, nag, gelding' — the plain, colloquial word for a horse, opposed to the literary equus 'riding horse'; attested from the 1st century BCE (Varro). It is the ancestor of the ordinary Romance words for 'horse': Italian cavallo, French cheval, Spanish caballo, Portuguese cavalo, Romanian cal. Stage 3 — Late Latin caballicāre 'to ride on horseback', a verb derived from caballus. Stage 4 — Late Latin caballicāta 'a mounted ride; a company of riders', a noun formed on the verb stem; from it descend Italian cavalcata, Old Occitan cavalgada, Spanish cabalgada, Portuguese cavalgada, and (via the French branch of the verb) Old French chevauchie / chevauchée 'a mounted ride, a raid'. Stage 5 — Italian cavalcata 'a ride on horseback; a company of horsemen riding out'. Stage 6 — French cavalcade 'a procession of riders' (16th century, borrowed from Italian cavalcata). Stage 7 — English cavalcade 'a procession or company of people riding on horseback, or by extension in vehicles' (late 16th century). Final node: CAVALCADE. Note on debate: the substrate origin of caballus (Gallic caballos) is the most widely accepted account, but because the word appears in several neighbouring Italic and Celtic languages with the same shape, some scholars argue for a common Italo-Celtic or even pre-Indo-European loan-source; a PIE derivation from *kab- 'to seize' is a minority view. The Romance family is fully secure from caballus onward.
