- Middle English — pure noise: ratelen names the clatter of dice, chains, and teeth; an imitative word understood the first time it is heard.
- 16th c. — the tongue learns it: to rattle on is to talk fast and emptily; preachers scold rattling tongues, and schoolmasters rattle off verses.
- 17th c. — coaches take it up: vehicles go rattling along turnpikes; speed and noise fuse, and Cobbett's age hears ostentation in the clatter.
- 18th–19th c. — nerves and gusto: to be rattled is to be shaken in composure, while a rattling pace or rattling story wins praise for dash.
- Victorian age — the intensifier: rattling good, rattling fine — the sound drops out and only the energy remains, slang of the hearty and the sporting.
- Today — shakes and shows: windows rattle in storms, lorries rattle over bridges, witnesses rattle under questioning; the echoic core untouched after six centuries.
