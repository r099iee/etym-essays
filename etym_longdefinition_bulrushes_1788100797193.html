In Wilde's Nile scene the riverhorse (the hippopotamus) hides among the bulrushes — the tall reed-beds of the river's edge. A bulrush is, loosely, any of various large, coarse rush-like or reed-like plants growing in wet ground, shallow water and marshes; botanically the name covers several different genera, and the plural bulrushes is the ordinary form in prose, since the plants grow in dense stands. The word's identity drifts by region and century: in British usage "bulrush" traditionally means the club-rush (Scirpus / Schoenoplectus — round or triangular stems with brown umbels) or the reedmace (Typha, the tall plant with the cigar-shaped brown head); in American English "bulrush" is the everyday name for Typha — the cattail; and in ancient and biblical contexts the "bulrushes" of the Nile are papyrus (Cyperus papyrus), the giant sedge of the delta and its swamps.


Botanical detail. The cattails (Typha latifolia and its allies) are the archetypal bulrushes of the modern eye: 1.5–3 metres tall, with flat leaves and a dense brown cylindrical flower spike, they colonise ditches, pond margins and marshes by vigorous rhizomes. They are famously useful — nearly every part is edible at the right season (young shoots as "cossack asparagus", the yellow pollen as flour, the starch of the rootstocks ground into meal); the leaves weave into mats, baskets and chair seats; and North American peoples used them for thatch and bedding. One celebrated American forager called the cattail "the supermarket of the swamp". The club-rushes (Schoenoplectus lacustris and relatives) are the great rushes of lakes and rivers, their stems once cut for matting and for the wicks of rushlights. Papyrus (Cyperus papyrus), the flagship bulrush of the ancient world, grew in thickets along the Nile: its stems made the world's first true paper, reed boats, ropes, sandals and baskets — and, in Exodus, the cradle of Moses.


Biblical and literary weight. The most famous bulrushes in the language are those of Exodus 2:3, where Moses' mother "took for him an ark of bulrushes, and daubed it with slime and with pitch" and set it among the reeds — a basket of papyrus, pitching-sealed, floated in the very habitat of the crocodile and the hippopotamus. Isaiah 58:5 asks whether the true fast is "to bow down his head as a bulrush", a phrase sermons rang with. It is this scriptural halo, plus the exotic Nile world of Wilde's swallow, that keeps the plural "bulrushes" alive in literary English long after "cattail" took over the marsh in America.


Semantics and register. "Bulrush" is a folk name, not a scientific taxon: it is applied across at least three plant families (Typhaceae, Cyperaceae, Juncaceae), which is why dictionaries must hedge. The plural is the default in use — bulrushes name a stand, a scene, a habitat: one says "among the bulrushes" but rarely "a bulrush" in one's hand. The register is literary, scriptural or ecological. Modern wetland restoration has given the word a second, environmental life: reedbeds of bulrushes are planted to filter water, stabilise shores and shelter birds, and the ancient marsh plant is once again the emblem of the healthy wetland.


  • British: club-rush (Scirpus, Schoenoplectus) or reedmace (Typha).
  • American: the cattail (Typha latifolia).
  • Biblical / ancient: papyrus (Cyperus papyrus) of the Nile; the ark of Moses.
  • Register: literary, scriptural, ecological; the plural names stands and scenes.
