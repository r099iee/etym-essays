- Old English — the noun means any high tower, including siege towers; the church sense is not yet dominant.
- c. 1200 — the word narrows to the church tower and its spire, the tallest thing in a parish.
- 17th c. — Quakers coin “steeple-house” as a term of contempt for church buildings, making the steeple a badge of formal religion.
- 18th–19th c. — “steeplechase” and “steeplejack” turn the steeple into a landmark to race toward and a structure to climb, while “steepled” settles into picturesque landscape description.
- 20th c. — “steepled fingers” gives the word a psychological, faintly sinister gesture sense.
- 21st c. — chiefly literary and journalistic: a nostalgic marker of old skylines, often set against glass and steel.
