Frugal describes the careful, thrifty use of money, food, and resources: spending little, wasting nothing, and finding sufficiency in simplicity.

In the source sentence the travellers eat a very frugal supper — for hobbits — and go on again: the joke lands because hobbits are literature's great trenchermen, so a meal spare enough to count as frugal by hobbit standards must be meagre indeed. The word measures the supper against appetite and finds it, fondly, wanting.

Core meaning in detail: Frugality is thrift as a virtue, not poverty as a condition: the frugal person could spend more but chooses not to, directing resources toward what matters and denying waste everywhere else. Diet is its oldest theatre — the frugal meal, the frugal table — but the word extends to households, habits, governments, and whole philosophies, wherever restraint is practised on principle rather than imposed by need.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Thrifty and economical (primary modern sense, from 1590s): a frugal housekeeper, a frugal supper. The bridge is the Latin root: frux, fruit and produce — to be frugal is to live off the honest fruits of the earth without squandering them.
  • Meagre or scanty in amount (descriptive sense, Tolkien's use): a frugal meal, frugal rations. The bridge is effect-for-habit: the portion looks like what a thrifty person would serve, whether thrift or necessity put it there.
  • Virtuous simplicity (philosophical sense): the frugal life of Stoics, monks, and minimalists — Cato's cabbage, Thoreau's beans. The bridge is moral elevation: thrift raised from housekeeping to wisdom, where wanting little becomes freedom.
  • Careful with resources generally (extended modern sense): frugal with praise, frugal with words, a frugal use of water in drought. The bridge is generalisation: from money and food to anything spendable — attention, speech, fuel.
  • Austere or plain in style (aesthetic sense): frugal prose, a frugal interior. The bridge is taste: the same restraint that saves coins strips sentences and rooms to their essentials.
  • Ecological and sustainable (contemporary green sense): frugal consumption, the frugal planet. The bridge is planetary housekeeping: the household virtue scaled to a civilisation that must live within its harvest.

Register and connotation: frugal is approving but not cosy — it praises the character while faintly apologising for the table. Unlike miserly, which hoards from fear, or stingy, which withholds from meanness, frugal spends wisely and shares willingly; its opposite is not generosity but waste. Politicians claim it, grandparents embody it, and novelists use it to mark both honourable poverty and comic understatement — nowhere more fondly than here, where hobbits do their heroic best on short commons, proving that good company seasons even the sparest board.
