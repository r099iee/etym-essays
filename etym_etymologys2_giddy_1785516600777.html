CHAIN — single root to English giddy: - PIE *ǵʰeu- "to pour, libate, make a ritual offering by pouring" (verbal root; extended *ǵʰew-d- "to pour out" in some branches). - PIE verbal adjective *ǵʰu-tó- "poured out, offered as a libation" (zero-grade *ǵʰu- + *-tó-). - Proto-Germanic *gudą "god, deity" — on the widely held theory, literally "the one to whom libation is poured, the invoked one" (the "poured/invoked" theory; debated, see note). - Proto-Germanic *gudīgaz "possessed by a god; ecstatic; inspired; frenzied; mad" (adjective formed with *-īgaz, ancestor of English -y). - Old English gydig (West Saxon) / gidig (Anglian) "possessed, insane, mad, frenzied" (attested c. 1000). - Middle English gidi, gedy, gydi "mad, insane, foolish, dizzy" (13th–15th c.). - Modern English giddy "dizzy, lightheaded; flighty, frivolous; (archaic) mad, divinely possessed." NOTE ON DEBATE: The etymology of the Germanic word for "god" (*gudą) is not fully settled. The "libation" theory — god as "the one poured to / invoked" — is widely repeated but debated; competing proposals derive the word from a different root meaning "that which is sacrificed to" or from an inherited noun whose original force is uncertain. The later stages of the chain (Old English gydig, Middle English gidi, Modern English giddy) are directly attested and uncontroversial.
