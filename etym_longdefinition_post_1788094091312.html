Core sense in context. In the reading sentence a post is an upright pillar: the dwarves search the hidden door's face "for any sign of post or lintel or threshold" — the doorposts that frame a doorway. The root meaning of this word is an upright timber set into the ground, and from that humble building sense a great fan of modern meanings — doorpost, goalpost, lamppost, signpost, military post, job post, mail post and online post — has grown across three separate historical words that converged on the same form.

Primary senses — the upright.

  • The pillar: a post is a long piece of timber or metal set upright to support, mark or fence: doorpost, gatepost, bedpost, lamppost, signpost, goalpost, starting post (horse racing), whipping post. "Post and lintel" (as in the context) names the oldest building scheme in the world — two uprights carrying a horizontal beam — the grammar of stone temples and wooden doors alike.
  • The fixed position: by extension from "a place where something stands", a post is a place of duty or station: a military post (an army station), a trading post, an outpost, a sentry's post, "to be at one's post". From the place to the job: a post is a position of employment — "she holds the post of treasurer", "to apply for a post", "to be posted abroad". The bridge is the standing-still image: the post is where one is stationed.
  • The mail system: a second, historically distinct word: the post is the postal system — post office, postman, postage, postmark, "by post", "to post a letter" (verb, seventeenth century). This post descends from the Italian word for the relay stations where couriers and their horses were "placed" along a route (see etymology); the system of stations gave its name to the courier, the letters and the whole institution: post-haste, post chaise, post horse, "the last post" (the bugle call of the day's end at a military post — the station-word again).
  • The online message: since the late twentieth century, a post is a message placed on a website, forum or social network — "to post", "a blog post", "posting updates". The bridge is literal: the message is "put up" on the site, exactly as a notice is posted on a board — and the old postal word donated the digital verb.
  • The verb "to post": to post a letter (send by post), to post a notice (put it up in a public place — to post bail, to post a guard), to post a transaction (enter it into a ledger), to post a player (station them). All are the same placing-act under different roofs.
  • The prefix post-: a third historical word, Latin post "after, behind", appears as the English prefix post- in post-war, post-modern, postscript, postdate, post-mortem — "after" in time or position. Though identical in spelling, it is unrelated to the pillar and the mail; it slipped into the same spelling in learned Latin borrowings from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
  • Computing and other jargon: POST (power-on self-test) is the acronym for the start-up test a computer runs when switched on — a purely modern engineered homonym; goalposts in sports, the "post" of a goal frame, the starting post and winning post of the racecourse, and "from pillar to post" (the old idiom of being driven from one place to another) all complete the field.

The semantic bridge, condensed. Three strangers share the name: (1) the upright pillar (Latin postis "doorpost"), which gave doorposts and goalposts; (2) the relay station and so the mail and the job (Latin posita, "placed" — the station where horses were placed), which gave posts, postmen and online posting; (3) the Latin preposition post "after" in the prefix, which gave post-war and postscript. Their collision in one short word is one of the great coincidences of English spelling: the doorpost of the context sentence, the letterbox and the war memorial all say POST, and mean three different things.
