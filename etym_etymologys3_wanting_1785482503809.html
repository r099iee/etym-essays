A root of emptiness. The chain that ends in wanting begins far to the north and very far back in time. The Proto-Indo-European base *h₁wen(h₁)- (older scholarship writes *we-no-) meant "empty, lacking, deficient", and although its exact laryngeal reconstruction is debated, the family it produced is coherent and widespread. From it descend words of hollowness and lack across the Indo-European world. The Indo-Iranian line. In Indo-Iranian the root appears in Sanskrit उन (una), "deficient, short, wanting", attested from the Rigveda onward; the cognate status of this form is debated by some scholars, who prefer to explain it separately. If accepted, it shows the same root of lack operating on the far side of the family. The Italic line. In Italic the root gave Latin vanus, "empty, void, vain" - ancestor of English vain, vanish and vanity, and (through a related formation) of vacuous and vacuum. These are words of emptiness and hollow pretension: what is vain is literally empty of substance. The Italic branch thus preserved the ancient "lack" idea as the language of vanity and vanishing. The Germanic line. The branch that matters for our word is Germanic, where the adjective *wana- meant "wanting, deficient". Old English kept it as the prefix wan-, seen in wanhope, "despair" (literally "want of hope"), wanland, "poor land", and most famously in wanton, from Middle English wantowen, "ill-bred, unruly" - literally "lacking in training" (wan- + togen, "disciplined"). These Old English formations show that the "lack" idea was native to English centuries before the modern word arrived. The Norse input. The decisive development came from Old Norse. The Norse adjective vanr, "wanting, lacking", generated an impersonal verb vanta, "it lacks (to me), there is a lack of" - a construction that describes deficiency as a state that happens to a person rather than an action they perform. When Norse and English speakers mingled in the Danelaw and after the Norman Conquest, Middle English absorbed vanta as wanten, "to be lacking", around 1200. The Danelaw contact - the centuries of everyday bilingualism between English speakers and Norse settlers in northern England - is exactly the kind of socio-linguistic situation in which one language hands a plain, domestic verb to another, and it explains why wanten is so thoroughly at home in English: it entered through speech, not through books. From lack to desire. The English history of want is the story of a single great reversal. The verb entered meaning "to lack, to be without"; the noun want, "deficiency, need", hardened into "poverty" - a person in want is in want of necessities, and the phrase "want and woe" joined want to the vocabulary of distress. Then, from the phrase "to feel the want of something", the verb pivoted from perceiving a deficiency to expressing the accompanying appetite: "I want" originally meant "I lack", and only later, by about 1706, "I wish for". The centre of gravity of the verb migrated from absence to appetite, so that modern English speakers use want for desire a hundred times for every once they use it for lack. The participle and the idiom. The participle wanting, "lacking, missing, deficient", never made that journey. It settled in as an adjective and preposition - "wanting in judgement", "wanting his consent" - and by the sixteenth century the fixed idiom "found wanting" was in place: a phrase with the ring of a weighing, a trial, a verdict. Its most famous literary setting is the biblical account of Belshazzar's feast, where the hand writes on the wall and Daniel reads it: "Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting" (Daniel 5:27). That image of the balances fixed the word forever in the language of judgement, so that even today a report, a performance or a person can be "found wanting" in a tone that recalls the courtroom and the scales. The present passage. In our passage, the sixteenth-century revaluation of classical literature supplies exactly that weighing: once the verse of Greece and Rome is set "on the highest of pedestals", native English poetry is brought under "fresh scrutiny", and "in the ensuing judgement" the home-grown product is "found wanting" - weighed against the classical standard and found short of it. The idiom could hardly be better chosen: the whole scene is a court of literary judgement, and the participle preserves the ancient sense of want as lack, keeping alive in formal English the deficiency that the modern verb of desire has all but forgotten. Conclusion. The career of wanting is a study in semantic drift: a root of emptiness (*h₁wen(h₁)-) that means "lacking" in Sanskrit, Latin and Germanic; a Norse impersonal verb of deficiency (vanta) that enters English as the verb of lack (wanten); a participle that freezes the lack-sense while the verb itself reverses into desire; and a biblical idiom ("found wanting") that preserves the ancient deficiency in modern formal English. The word thus carries, in a single sentence, the whole history of how "to lack" became "to desire" - and how a participle kept the older meaning alive.
