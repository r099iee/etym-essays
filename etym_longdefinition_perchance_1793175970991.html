Perchance is an adverb of the old literary stock meaning perhaps, maybe, or possibly: it marks what is said as uncertain, contingent, open to fortune. In full it still carries its etymological echo — per chance, by chance, as things may fall out — so that every perchance quietly admits that the world might decide otherwise.

In the source sentence — a line of Russian wonder-tale adventure fearing pursuit — the word does exactly this work: the speakers cannot be sure the pursuer will fail to overtake them, so they soften hope into perchance, a maybe uttered against danger. The capital letter is only the sentence's accident; the meaning is the humble adverb of uncertainty, doing duty where modern prose would put perhaps or maybe.

Core meaning in detail: Perchance belongs to the family of epistemic hedges — words that calibrate how sure a speaker claims to be. Saying perchance he will fail asserts less than he will fail and slightly less archly than peradventure he will fail. Its grammar is simple: it stands at the front of a clause or before the verb, never inflected, never governing anything, a pure colouring of mood. Shakespeare's Hamlet made it immortal — to sleep, perchance to dream — fixing it forever to the music of hesitation before the unknown.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Perhaps, maybe, possibly (primary modern and historical sense, from the 14th c.): the straightforward hedge — perchance he will come means maybe he will come. The bridge from the literal by chance is the ordinary slide from cause to epistemology: what happens by chance is what the speaker cannot vouch for, so the phrase for accident becomes the word for uncertainty.
  • By chance, as it may happen (older, more literal sense): if perchance you find it keeps the original per + chance force — if it should so fall out. The bridge is direct composition, still visible whenever perchance follows if, where accident rather than opinion is meant.
  • Peradventure's twin in speculation (literary-philosophical use): in essays and sermons perchance introduces thought-experiments — perchance there are other worlds. The bridge is register: the word's very archaism signals that a large or solemn possibility is being entertained, lending speculation a period dignity that maybe cannot supply.
  • Emphatic certainty by negation — without perchance (idiom, archaic): older writers say without perchance or withouten perchance to mean certainly, without doubt. The bridge is litotes through the noun use: treat perchance as a maybe, deny it, and certainty remains — the double negative of an age that loved them.
  • Ironic or wistful understatement (modern literary survival): a historian writing perchance the king repented usually means the evidence is thin and the hope is tender. The bridge is tone: because the word is marked as old and gentle, it now hedges with a smile, softening claims the author half doubts.

Register and connotation: Perchance is unmistakably literary and slightly theatrical. In ordinary speech it would sound costumed; on the page — in fantasy translation, historical fiction, poetry, and liturgy — it sounds right, lending uncertainty a cloak. Translators of folktales love it, as the source sentence shows, because wonder tales themselves live in a timeless past where perhaps feels too brisk and maybe too modern. Capitalized or lowercased, it is the same word; the capital here is typography, not pride.
