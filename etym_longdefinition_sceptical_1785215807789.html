sceptical (British spelling; American skeptical) is an adjective meaning disposed to question or doubt accepted opinions, claims, motives, or appearances — refusing to accept something as true without sufficient or rigorous evidence, and insisting that belief keep pace with proof. At its core the word names an intellectual stance: the sceptical person does not necessarily deny a claim; they withhold assent from it until the evidence is adequate, treating certainty as something that must be earned. The root image is visual — Greek skeptesthai, 'to look about for, to examine, to spy out' — so that the sceptic is fundamentally the careful observer who refuses to be rushed into conviction.

In the literary context of this card the word carries a sharply physical and satirical nuance. It is the ordinary worldly cynicism of a jaded reader that 'curl[s] a sceptical lip over sentences like these.' The sceptical lip is not merely doubtful but visibly curled — a sneering, half-derisive contraction of the mouth that registers disbelief, amusement, and condescension at once. The world-weary reader, habituated to rhetorical inflation, refuses to take John Stuart Mill's emotional declarations at face value and answers them with a silent, physical protest. The scepticism in play is therefore not the patient, principled suspension of judgment practised by the ancient Sceptics, but the instinctive, knowing mistrust of an audience that has heard too many fine phrases and has stopped believing them.

The full range of senses may be catalogued as follows:

  • Intellectual doubt (the core sense): disinclined to accept claims, especially extraordinary, unproven, or self-serving ones, without evidence; the habit of the careful investigator ('a sceptical approach to the new data').
  • Philosophical scepticism: the position, descending from Pyrrho of Elis and systematized by Sextus Empiricus, that certain knowledge is unattainable and that the wise response is the suspension of judgment (epochē) leading to tranquillity (ataraxia).
  • Everyday mistrust of motives: in the context, shading into world-weariness and a curled lip; a suspicion that sincerity is a pose and that fine phrases cover self-interest.
  • Scientific scepticism: in modern usage, the principled demand for testable evidence that drives experimental method, critical thinking, and the debunking of pseudoscience.
  • Religious and theological use: doubt or disbelief regarding religious doctrines and dogmas, often (but not always) shading toward agnosticism or atheism.
  • Financial and commercial jargon: 'market scepticism' — investor doubt about a company's prospects or a policy's viability, expressed as discounted valuations or withheld investment.
  • Idiomatic and collocational use: 'a sceptical eye', 'sceptical look', 'sceptical about/of/that', 'sceptically' (adverb); the phrase 'healthy scepticism' marks doubt as a virtue rather than a failing.

As an attitude, sceptical must be separated from a cluster of near-synonyms. Cynical is the closest and most dangerous neighbour: a cynic assumes that people are motivated chiefly by self-interest and regards sincerity itself as a pose — the context explicitly links the curling lip to 'worldly cynicism' — whereas a sceptic merely withholds assent until the evidence is adequate, without necessarily imputing bad faith. Doubtful is weaker and more passive, expressing simple uncertainty about a fact rather than a habit of demanding proof. Incredulous describes the state of being unable or unwilling to believe something, usually because it is astonishing, and carries an element of surprise absent from the more deliberate sceptical attitude. Suspicious fixes on hidden motives and wrongdoing, imputing guilt or concealment, while scepticism is directed at the truth-value of claims. Disbelieving asserts a positive conclusion that something is false, where the sceptic only withholds assent. Dubious is milder and colloquial, hovering between doubt and distrust. Distrustful and mistrustful name a settled lack of confidence in a person or institution, close to but not identical with the intellectual doubt of scepticism.

The spelling deserves a note. The British form sceptical retains the Latinate c taken from Renaissance Latin scepticus; the American skeptical, used from the seventeenth century, respells the word with Greek k (kappa) on the model of the Greek source skeptikos. Both are pronounced identically, /skˈɛptɪkəl/. The word is a mark of register as well as of meaning: in a literary, biographical, or intellectual register it denotes principled doubt; in casual speech it slides toward simple 'don't believe it' disbelief, the sense at play when the ordinary reader curls the lip.
