PIE *wáy / *wai ("alas!, woe!," an imitative interjection of grief; reconstructed on the evidence of Latin vae, Ancient Greek ouai, Proto-Celtic *wai, and Gothic wai; dating to the earliest period of the family, before 3000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *wai (interjection of pain and lamentation, the shout raised at funerals and disasters; 1st millennium BCE) → Old English wā / wēa (wā! the cry, and wēa/wā the noun "grief, misery, calamity"; attested from the earliest Old English poetry, 8th–10th c. CE; the noun was built in West Germanic by reduplication, *waiwa-, from the interjection) → Middle English wo / wa / wea (12th–13th c.; spelling stabilized in the forms wo, woe) → Modern English woe (noun and interjection) ◄ TARGET Cognate note (outside the direct English line): Latin vae ("woe," in the famous vae victis "woe to the conquered" reported by Livy); Ancient Greek ouai (οὐαί, "woe"; New Testament "ouai hymin," "woe to you"); Proto-Celtic *wai → Welsh gwae, Cornish goa, Breton gwa; Gothic wai, Old Norse vei, Old Saxon wē, Old High German wē (modern German Weh "pain, woe"). The Old Norse form also produced the verb væla "to lament," borrowed into Middle English as wail (c. 1300).
