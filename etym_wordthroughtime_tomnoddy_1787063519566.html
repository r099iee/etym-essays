- 16th c. - The base noun noddy appears in Early Modern English, denoting a simpleton or sleepy fool who continuously nods his head without comprehending.
- 17th-18th c. - The compound Tom Noddy forms in popular speech and street ballads, pairing the generic commoner name Tom (as in Tom Fool) with noddy to denote a prototypical blockhead.
- 18th-19th c. - Adopted into coastal English and Scottish dialects as a folk name for the puffin; popularized in high Victorian literature by Richard Barham's satirical poem The Execution of Lord Tomnoddy.
- 20th c. - Featured in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) as Bilbo's rhythmic taunt against the giant spiders of Mirkwood.
- 21st c. - Preserved in literature, folklore, and fantasy fiction as a colorful, non-vulgar archaic insult for a bumbling fool.
