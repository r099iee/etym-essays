The history of ford is the history of a word that has scarcely needed to change because the thing it names never changed. It begins in Proto-Indo-European *per-, 'to lead, to pass over, to go across', one of the great verbal roots of the family: from it derive Greek peri 'around', Latin per 'through', the Germanic faran 'to travel' (whence English fare and ferry), and a whole constellation of 'passage' words. The root generated a noun for 'a crossing', *pértus / *por-tu-, and from that noun two water-words emerged on opposite sides of the Indo-European world: Greek poros, 'a ford, a passage, a strait' (as in the Hellespont, 'the Helles' passage'), and Latin portus, 'a harbour, a port' — both places where one passes between land and water. The Romans applied the same idea to their gateways of trade; the northern peoples applied it to their rivers. In the Germanic branch the word followed the famous sound law named after Jacob Grimm: the Indo-European voiceless stop p became the Germanic fricative f. PIE *pér- thus gave Proto-Germanic *far- and the noun *furdu-, 'a ford', with the characteristic u-grade vowel of the Germanic formation. This *furdu- descended into all the old Germanic languages with remarkable uniformity: Old English ford, Old Frisian forda, Old Saxon forda, Middle Dutch vorde, Old High German furt — cognates like Old Norse fjǫrðr (from the variant *ferþu-) took the form later borrowed into English as fjord and firth. The consistency of the form across a thousand years and a thousand miles testifies to how central the ford was to northern European life: in a land of countless streams, the shallow crossing was a piece of everyday infrastructure written into the very map. Old English ford appears in the earliest records and, more tellingly, in the earliest place-names. The Anglo-Saxons named their settlements after their crossings with a vivid concreteness: Oxford is from Oxenaford, 'the oxen's ford'; Hereford, 'the army-ford'; Stratford, 'the ford on the Roman street'; Stamford, 'the stony ford'; Bedford, 'Beda's ford'; Guildford, 'the golden ford'. Hundreds of English towns and villages preserve ford as a living syllable of their names, so that even a modern English speaker who never says the word aloud encounters it every time they read a road atlas. In these names the word did exactly what the language needed: it located a settlement in the landscape by the single most important practical feature of its local river. Through Middle English and into the present, ford changed scarcely at all — phonologically the vowel rounded and lengthened (Old English /ford/ to modern /fɔːd/), and the spelling was fixed, but the meaning stayed locked to the shallow crossing. This stability is extraordinary in a language that has rebuilt its vocabulary several times over: ford is one of the least altered words in English. Its one true grammatical development was the creation of the verb in the 16th–17th centuries — 'to ford a river' — which simply extracted from the noun the act its existence implied. The verb joined the military vocabulary of campaigning armies, who needed to know where a river could be crossed without bridges, and it survives today in the travel and off-road vocabulary of four-wheel-drive drivers fording streams on dirt roads. The word's cultural life, meanwhile, was quietly transformed by the surname it seeded. Because ford named a place, it became a surname for anyone who lived by a crossing — and in the twentieth century the surname Ford was carried into global prominence first by the automaker Henry Ford (whose family name is exactly this old noun) and later by President Gerald Ford. An accidental but fitting echo runs through this: the industrialist who made the automobile a vehicle for crossing the American continent bore a name that had meant 'a place where you cross the water' for over a thousand years. The word thus binds the earliest Anglo-Saxon landscape to the modern highway, the oxen's ford to the Model T. In literature, the ford keeps its ancient liminal charge: it is the threshold in Arthurian romance where challengers wait, in folklore where the otherworld is met, and in pastoral prose — as in the card's 'stony ford' beside its cave and steps — the quiet emblem of a world still small enough to be walked across. No bridge, no toll, no engineering: just a river shallow enough to stand in, and a word unchanged enough to name it.
