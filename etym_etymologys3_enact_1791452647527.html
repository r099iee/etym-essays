From Latin action to English performance

The remote source of enact is Latin agere, “to drive, lead, set in motion, do, perform.” Its past participle actus produced the noun actus “a doing, an act, a performance,” and the related actum “a thing done.” These words entered Old French as acte and Middle English as acte in the fourteenth century. English then formed enact with the prefix en-, from Latin in- “in, into,” which in French and English often has the sense “put into” or “make.” The literal underlying idea is “to put into act” or “to make act.”

The Indo-European root is *h₂eǵ- “to drive, lead,” which also lies behind agent, agenda, action, exact, navigate (through navis + agere), and many other English words. The semantic range of agere was remarkably broad: it could mean to drive cattle, to set in motion, to do, to plead a case, and to play a part on stage. That breadth made it a natural source for a word that would later cover law, theatre, and administration.

The earliest English uses

Enact is recorded in English from the early fifteenth century. The OED dates the verb to 1430. The earliest sense was “to act the part of, represent in performance,” which is striking because modern speakers often think of legislation as the primary meaning. The legal sense, “to decree, establish, or sanction into law,” appears by the mid-fifteenth century. The two senses coexisted from the beginning: a law is something declared and put into force, while a role is something embodied and performed. Both involve turning a script or formula into an event.

This double ancestry helps explain why enact feels more formal and more complete than act. To act is to do something or to play a part; to enact is to bring a specific thing into realised existence. The prefix adds a sense of installation or completion. One enacts a statute, a role, a scene, a policy, or a ritual; in each case something that existed as text, intention, or plan becomes operative.

Law, parliament, and the language of statute

From the fifteenth century onward the legal sense expanded with the growth of parliamentary government. Statutes are enacted by the sovereign or legislature; an enactment is both the process and the resulting law. The language of enactment is central to constitutional theory because it marks the point at which a bill becomes binding. In the British system, an Act of Parliament is enacted when it receives royal assent; in the United States, a bill is enacted when it is passed by both houses and signed or otherwise approved by the president. The verb also appears in phrases such as “enacted by the authority of Parliament” and “the enactment of the Civil Rights Act.”

The noun enactment is recorded from 1766 in the sense “passing of a bill into law,” and by 1783 it also meant “a law, statute.” The earlier noun enaction dates from the 1620s. Re-enact appears in the 1670s, originally for legislation and later for historical or theatrical repetition. These derivatives show how thoroughly the legal sense colonised the word’s noun forms.

Theatre, ritual, and re-enactment

The theatrical sense never disappeared. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries enact could mean to perform a play, a scene, or a part. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it became common in historical pageants and re-enactments, where communities performed episodes from their past. The modern phrase “historical re-enactment” covers everything from battlefield reconstructions to museum demonstrations. In theatre studies, “enactment” can mean the embodied performance of a text or role, as opposed to a merely literary reading.

Ritual is another important extension. Anthropologists speak of enacting a rite, a myth, or a social drama. The word implies that the meaning of the ritual is not exhausted by its script; it is produced in the doing. This is the same logic that connects the legal and theatrical senses: a text or formula becomes real only when it is performed by an authorised person in the right setting.

Policy, implementation, and social science

In modern administration, enact means to put a policy, reform, or procedure into operation. This usage is close to implement but carries a stronger sense of formal adoption. A government enacts reforms; a company enacts a safety policy; a court enacts a remedy. The word suggests that the decision has moved from paper to practice.

In the late twentieth century, social theorists and cognitive scientists gave the word new technical life. Sociologists speak of enacting identities, norms, and institutions; performance theorists speak of enacting gender or social roles; cognitive scientists speak of enactive cognition and enactivism, the view that minds are constituted through embodied interaction with an environment. These uses are modern, but they are intelligible extensions of the old idea of putting something into action.

Phonology, spelling, and usage

Enact is pronounced /ɪˈnækt/ in Received Pronunciation, with the stress on the second syllable. The spelling has been stable since the fifteenth century. The verb is regular and transitive. Its most common collocations are legal and theatrical: enact a law, enact legislation, enact a statute, enact a policy, enact reforms, enact a role, enact a scene. In formal writing, “enact” is often preferable to “act out” because it is more precise and less colloquial.

  • Legal: to give a bill or measure the force of law.
  • Theatrical: to perform or embody a role, scene, or story.
  • Administrative: to put a policy or plan into practice.
  • Sociological: to perform a role, identity, or ritual.
  • Cognitive-scientific: enactive cognition; enactivism.

The word’s history is a history of making things real. A legislature enacts a law; an actor enacts a role; a community enacts a ritual; a government enacts a reform. In every case the same old Latin verb agere is at work: to drive, to do, to perform, to bring into act.
