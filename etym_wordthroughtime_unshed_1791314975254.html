- c. 1450 Late Middle English theological origin - first attested in devotional manuscripts referring strictly to blood not shed or sacramental liquid unspilled; solemn, religious, and formal.
- 16th–17th c. Early Modern drama & poetry - applied by Elizabethan and Jacobean playwrights to unspilled blood in tragedies of state and occasionally to unwept tears in elegies; high gravitas.
- 18th–19th c. The Victorian triumph of "unshed tears" - the Romantic and Victorian literary movements (Byron, Tennyson, the Brontës, Dickens) elevate unshed tears into an essential, ubiquitous trope of tragic beauty, feminine sensibility, and gentlemanly stoicism.
- Early 20th c. Satirical and comedic inversion - modernist and comedic writers (most notably P. G. Wodehouse) adopt the phrase with affectionate irony, mocking the theatrical poses of overly romantic heroines.
- Late 20th–21st c. Enduring literary currency - continues as a powerful descriptive staple in contemporary literary fiction, biography, and poetry, capturing quiet dignity, restrained sorrow, and emotional vulnerability.
