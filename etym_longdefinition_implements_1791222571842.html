In the money-history passage, implements appears as a plural noun: articles universally in demand such as dates, salt, skins, furs, ornaments, implements, weapons. Here it means manufactured tools and utensils - knives, axes, hoes, needles, traps, and other crafted equipment that any community would accept in exchange because of immediate utility. This noun sense is the primary one for learners: countable, usually plural, denoting handy objects contrived for a purpose. A farmer's implements are ploughs and harrows; a surgeon's implements are scalpels and forceps; a writer's implements (now archaic) were pen, ink, and paper. The noun stresses instrumental means, intermediate between raw materials and finished luxuries, and therefore ideal commodity money.

Grammatically, implements is two words in one form: (1) plural noun implement + -s, pronounced /ˈɪmplɪmənts/, and (2) third-person singular present verb he/she/it implements, pronounced /ˈɪmplɪments/ with same stress but verbal syntax, meaning carries out, puts into effect, fulfils. The verb governs abstract objects: implement a plan, implement reforms, implement software, implement policy. In business, law, and computing, to implement is to move from design to execution, from de jure to de facto. An implemented decision has been enacted; an unimplemented idea remains paper. This duality - concrete noun tool versus abstract verb execution - is the central polysemy to master.

Noun extensions are wide. In law, agricultural implements and household implements have specific exemptions from seizure; in archaeology, stone implements, bone implements, flint implements denote artefacts shaped for use, distinguishing worked tools from debitage. In sport, implements can denote throwing equipment (shot, discus, javelin). In religious ritual, sacred implements are vessels and instruments. The singular implement is rarer but identical in sense: a garden implement. Collocations include farm implements, writing implements, eating implements (more often utensils), and implements of war (weapons and engines).

  • Core noun sense: a tool, utensil, or piece of equipment for a practical purpose; typically manufactured, handheld or machine-assisted, reusable.
  • Archaeological sense: any worked object showing human modification for function, even simple flakes; implements versus ornaments marks functional versus decorative.
  • Legal-administrative sense: implements of husbandry, implements of trade; tools of livelihood protected in bankruptcy.
  • Verb sense - general: to carry out, execute, fulfil (implement a promise, implement regulations); stresses faithful realization, not mere attempt.
  • Verb sense - technical: in software engineering, to implement an interface, a class, an algorithm means to write code that satisfies a specification; an implementation is the concrete code.
  • Verb sense - policy: to put legislation or judgments into effect through administrative action; implementation period, implementing regulation (EU).
  • Register nuance: noun is somewhat formal or technical, preferring tool in everyday speech; verb is bureaucratic-formal, preferring carry out, do, install informally.

Semantic bridges: Latin implementum originally meant a filling up, fulfilment, then by metonymy that which fills a need - equipment. English preserves both branches: noun as that which fills a lack (tool), verb as act of filling (fulfilling a plan). Hence implements as trade goods makes sense: tools fill universal human needs for cutting, tilling, hunting, and cooking, so they are always acceptable. Modern management jargon extends the verb to any execution, even software, retaining the idea of making complete. Learners should distinguish implement (neutral tool) from instrument (more precise, often musical/scientific), utensil (domestic), and execute (which adds killing or computing senses the verb implement lacks).
