The story of oft-times is the story of how a spatial word for "up" became a temporal word for "again and again", and then of how a plain adverb grew a poetic double. At the root of the family lies the Proto-Indo-European particle *upo, "up from under, up, over", one of the most productive little stems in the entire language family. When it carried an *-er suffix it became *uper, "over, above", the ancestor of Sanskrit upari, Greek huper - our hyper- - Latin super, and, through Proto-Germanic *uber, the English word over. When it carried the sense of "beneath", with s-mobile, it became Latin sub and English under. But the branch that matters for our word extended the root in a different direction: Proto-Germanic built *ufta, "frequently, often", from the notion of going up, over, beyond - exceeding the ordinary count.
  • The Germanic spread: *ufta travelled across the whole family - Gothic ufta, Old Norse opt (source of Danish ofte and Swedish ofta), Old Frisian ofte, Old Saxon ofto, Old High German ofto (modern German oft), and Old English oft. The Old English word was simple and short: one said oft to mean "frequently" with no further decoration.
  • Middle English innovation: in the fourteenth century the plain oft acquired an adverbial -en on the analogy of selden, "seldom", producing often - a form that gradually displaced oft in ordinary speech, leaving oft to survive as the clipped, poetic, archaic variant. At the same time English began compounding time-words with the plural -times, as in sometimes and betimes, and from that pattern came ofte tymes, "at many times", written later as oft-times or offtimes.
  • The poetic home: the compound had a natural home in verse, where its two syllables and its antique ring gave poets a ready-made rhythm and a tolling, oracular gravity. It is the adverb of fate and recurring sorrow - of habitual action that has hardened into destiny - and its initial position in the line makes it a refrain.
  • Retreat and preservation: by the eighteenth century oft and oft-times had left ordinary prose; they survived in hymns, in Biblical archaism, in the fixed collocations oft-told and oft-repeated, and in the poetic diction of the Romantics and Victorians. A handful of fossilized forms - oft-quoted, oft-cited, oft-repeated - keep the root half-alive in journalistic and academic prose, while "often times" as two words flourishes in relaxed modern speech as a colloquial variant.
The second element, times, has its own quiet history. Old English tima meant "a limited space of time, a period, an occasion", from a Proto-Germanic *timon whose deeper ancestry is uncertain - perhaps, on a debated hypothesis, connected with an Indo-European root meaning "to divide", so that time is literally "an appointed portion of duration". In Middle English the plural tymes came to be used adverbially to mean "on occasions", and English built a whole family of frequency adverbs on it: sometimes, betimes, oft-times. It is this adverbial plural that gives oft-times its second syllable and its kinship with the other -times words. The semantic journey is complete: a particle meaning "up from under" passed through "over, beyond, exceeding" to "frequently", and then, re-attached to the adverbial plural of time, became the solemn two-syllable refrain that opens the couplet on Bao-yu. One caution keeps the history honest: older authorities attached the oft family to the PIE root *h1epi, "near, at, upon", the ancestor of Greek epi, Sanskrit api, and Latin ob - a genuine and parallel root, but a different one. The frequency sense of oft comes from the "over and above" of *upo, not from the "upon" of *h1epi, and the two must not be merged. The word that opens Bao-yu's lines is thus, etymologically, a small monument to the Indo-European habit of making time out of space - a word for "going over" that settled into meaning "again and again", until it tolled in verse as the recurring refrain of a recurring sorrow.
