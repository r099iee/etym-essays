Cellars descends through French and Latin from one of the oldest roots of the Indo-European family, the root *ḱel- 'to cover, conceal, hide'. That root also produced Latin celāre 'to hide' (source of English conceal and occult), Greek kalyptein 'to cover' (source of apocalypse, 'the hidden/revealed'), and Old Irish celim 'I hide'. From this 'covering/hiding' root Latin formed cella, 'a small room, store-chamber, store-room' — the covered, walled-off space — attested in Classical Latin with both domestic and religious senses (a servant's room, a pantry, a monk's cell, a honey-store). The idea at the root is protection by enclosure: the room is a covered place where things are kept safe and hidden. From cella, Late and Medieval Latin built cellārium (also cellarium) 'storehouse, pantry, larder', and it is this enlarged word, not cella itself, that is the direct ancestor of English cellar.

Old French carried cellārium into the vernacular as celier (also celler), 'a cellar or storeroom', and Anglo-Norman passed it to Middle English as celer/sellar from around 1200. The English spelling was later reshaped as cellar under the influence of the Latin cellarium. The underground sense — the store-room sunk below ground for coolness — became the dominant one as European housekeeping specialised: the cellar was the dark, cool vault where wine, ale, and provisions were laid down, a domestic feature of manor houses and town houses alike. The plural cellars is formed with the ordinary English plural suffix -s, which descends from Old English -as, the plural ending of the strong masculine a-stem nouns (from Proto-Germanic *-ōs, ultimately from the Indo-European nominal plural endings), generalised in Middle English to nearly all nouns.

Sound and form. The initial c of Latin cella was a /k/; in Old French the following vowel e palatalised the /k/ to /ts/, and Middle English borrowed the word already palatalised, so English pronounces cellar with /s/ while keeping the Latin spelling with c — the familiar Norman-French softening also seen in cell, centre, and city. The double l reflects the Latin spelling.

Semantic drift and cultural history. The meaning of the word moves from 'covered storeroom' to 'underground wine vault' and beyond:

  • Latin: cella 'small room, store-room, monk's cell' — the covered place; the monastery cell, and the office of the cellarer (the monk in charge of provisions), carry the same word.
  • Medieval French–English: celier / celer 'storeroom, pantry', already associated with provisions and drink.
  • 14th–17th c.: the underground sense fixes itself: the cellar is the vault below ground for wine, beer, and food, cool and dark — from the great wine cellars of noble houses to the humble ale-cellar of the tavern (a public house could be called 'the cellar').
  • 17th–19th c.: the metonymy a fine cellar = a fine stock of wine establishes itself in genteel and commercial language, and the salt cellar — the table-top salt box, from Old French saliere, reshaped in English by folk etymology to look like 'salt' + 'cellar' — fixes the word in the language of dining and of rank (above the salt).
  • 19th–20th c.: American English adds root cellar and storm cellar, the latter doubling as a tornado shelter; sports slang makes a team in the cellar mean one at the bottom of the standings — the last place as the underground.
  • 20th–21st c.: wine cellar dominates popular use, from restaurants to home wine-storage; the word remains comfortably domestic, the everyday name for the cool underground room where drink and provisions wait.

Tolkien's joke. In The Hobbit, cellars appears in a list that makes the word's whole history of 'below ground' the point of the comedy: Bag End is a hobbit-hole built into a hill, so 'no going upstairs' — even the cellars, which by definition lie below, sit on the same floor and passage as the kitchens and dining-rooms. The plural belongs to the inventory of abundance ('cellars, pantries (lots of these), wardrobes (he had whole rooms devoted to clothes)'), a catalogue of bourgeois comfort that turns the underground storage vault into one more cozy room in the single-storey smial. From a PIE root meaning 'to cover and hide', through Latin cella and cellārium, Old French celier, and Middle English celer, the word has kept its core meaning — the covered, cool place where things are stored — for more than two thousand years, and in a hobbit's hillside home it is simply one more room on the same passage as everything else.
