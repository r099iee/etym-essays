The longest journey in this batch belongs to the shortest house: fashionablest begins in the most industrious of Proto-Indo-European roots, *dheh1-, to do, make, put, place — the root behind Greek tithenai (to place), Sanskrit dadhati (puts), Germanic do, and Latin facere (to do, make). Latin facere with its past participle factus generated a vast family: fact (a thing done), factory (a place of making), faction (a party doing together), feasible, feat, feature. Among them was factio, a making or doing, and in political life a company bound by common action. Late Latin used factio for both the shaping of objects and the combining of persons — how a pot is thrown and how a party holds together.

Old French inherited the word as façon (modern spelling with cedilla), narrowing it to shape, form, make, manner, kind: de quelle façon (in what manner), de façon que (so that). Norman knights brought it to England after 1066, where Middle English fasoun, fassioun (c. 1300) meant shape, appearance, and then habitual manner — Chaucer writes of men of strange fasoun and of fashioning verses. The semantic slide from shape to custom is natural: the approved shape of a sleeve becomes the approved manner of behaving. By the sixteenth century fashion unambiguously means prevailing custom, especially in dress: Stubbes's Anatomie of Abuses (1583) thunders against monstrous fashions in hats and ruffs exactly as modern columnists thunder against fast fashion.

  • Fashionable and unfashionable (c. 1600): Jacobean London, with its new West End squares and shopping streets, needed an adjective for what the town wears: fashionable (fit for the fashion) appears around 1600, quickly followed by its opposite unfashionable. At first it describes clothes and lodgings; within a generation it describes persons, hours, churches, and diseases — anything the town adopts. Phonetic change is simple: French nasalised façon /fa.sɔ̃/ is anglicised to /ˈfæʃən/, with palatal -sh- and reduced second vowel, then suffixed with native -able.
  • The fashionable world (1700s): Bath, Tunbridge Wells, and London seasons institutionalise fashion into a power with gazettes, arbiters (Beau Nash, Beau Brummell), and punishments (to be cut, to be out). Novels from Fanny Burney to Thackeray treat the Fashionable World almost as an estate of the realm. Grammarians meanwhile police the superlative: triple-suffixed fashionablest is heard in servants' and children's speech and in provincial towns, while polite London prefers most fashionable for so long a base. Both forms coexist, class-marked.
  • Ruskin's Market Street (1820s): the aunt's baker's house with two windows over the shop is fashionablest only within Market Street, Croydon — a street of bakers, chandlers, and carriers. Ruskin, writing Praeterita sixty years later, deliberately keeps the nursery superlative to honour the aunt's pride and his own boyish geography: shop, bakehouse, crystal spring, and Towzer the dog are the estate, the two windows are the façade. The archaism is tenderness, not ignorance; elsewhere Ruskin writes most fashionable correctly.
  • Modern industry (1900s-present): fashion becomes a global business — haute couture, ready-to-wear, fast fashion, fashion week, fashion victim, fashionista — while fashionable broadens into intellectual slang (fashionable nonsense, Sokal's famous target). Fashionablest retreats to comic and childlike corners, a shibboleth of period voice. Its survival proves a morphological point: synthetic -est never wholly dies in English, it merely waits for affection or irony to recall it.

From putting pots on wheels to counting windows over a baker's shop, the root never rests: to do, to shape, to wear, to outshine the neighbours. The aunt who kept the fashionablest house in Market Street was, without Latin, the last heir of facere — one who had made her little world well, and was awarded the triple-suffixed crown by a boy who loved her.
