“Snapdragon” entered English as a folk name for the flower Antirrhinum — because when pinched at the sides, its two-lipped blossom opens and shuts like a mouth, as if it could snap.
“Snapdragon” from its first use carried the dragon image: the flower was a dragon’s snout, and older English also knew a Christmas game of the same name where raisins were snatched from burning brandy — the “snap” of the catch.
“Snapdragon” through the 18th–19th centuries was a cottage-garden staple, the many-coloured spikes of snap-mouths planted in formal and kitchen gardens alike.
“Snapdragons” today is still the plant word — and in fantasy it shines again as the shape of firework blooms, great lilies and snapdragons and laburnums of fire painted on the twilight sky.
