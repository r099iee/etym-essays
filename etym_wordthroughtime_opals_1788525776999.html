- [Antiquity–c. 500 CE] - Classical antiquity reveres the stone via Sanskrit *upala*, Greek *opallios*, and Latin *opallus*, viewing it as the most complete gemstone that unites the beauty of all gems - [connotation: supreme good fortune, hope, purity, and imperial adoration]
- [c. 1300–1600] - Medieval and Renaissance Europe values opals in royal regalia and lapidary medicine, associating them with keen eyesight and protective invisibility - [connotation: mystical virtue, sovereign prestige, and artistic admiration]
- [1829–c. 1870] - Sir Walter Scott's novel *Anne of Geierstein* inadvertently sparks a widespread Victorian superstition that opals bring misfortune and death - [connotation: ominous mystery, dangerous allure, and tragic fate]
- [Late 19th c.–Present] - Queen Victoria champions Australian opal discoveries (Lightning Ridge, Coober Pedy); Decadent and Aesthetic writers celebrate opals as emblems of shifting beauty, establishing modern admiration - [connotation: exotic splendor, dazzling natural optics, and refined elegance]
