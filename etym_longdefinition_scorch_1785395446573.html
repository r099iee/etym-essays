To SCORCH is to burn or singe the surface of something — cloth, skin, vegetation, or earth — with dry, intense heat, so that the outer layer is discoloured, blackened, shrivelled, or blistered without the whole object being consumed. The verb is both transitive ("the iron scorched the shirt", "the sun scorched the lawn") and intransitive ("the leaves scorched in the heatwave"), and it names a precise middle stage of burning: hotter and darker than singeing, which only blackens the tips, but shallower than charring or carbonising, which reaches into the substance. Scorch always implies dryness, surface, and dark discolouration: a scorched thing has been seared and browned, its moisture driven off, its outer layer marked, but it has not gone up in flame. The word is also used of the sun and of intense meteorological heat — a scorching day, scorching temperatures — and, figuratively, of fierce criticism or speed: a scorching review, a scorching pace. In the chant "Shrivel and scorch!" — the goblins' curse from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit as the trees burn about the fleeing company — the word is an incantation of annihilation: paired with shrivel, it evokes a withering, searing fire that blights living flesh, curling and blackening everything it touches. The tone is apocalyptic and mocking, the speaker calling down a dry, blistering doom — the sear of dragon-fire or a burning waste — in which enemies are not merely destroyed but degraded: shrunken, charred at the edges, robbed of moisture and life. Where shrivel folds the whole body inward, scorch works upon the surface, and the two verbs together command the complete ruin of a living thing from the outside in. The full polysemy of the word:
  • To burn the surface by dry heat (core sense): "the iron scorched the fabric", "the flame scorched his hand", "scorched grass". The sense of singeing, browning, or blackening the outer layer without consuming the whole.
  • To wither, parch, or dry up by heat (the moisture sense): "the scorching sun", "a scorched land", "scorched lips". The sense of drying and shrivelling where fire itself may be absent — the sun scorches as readily as flame.
  • Scorched earth (military): the deliberate burning of everything an advancing enemy could use — crops, buildings, fuel — as a strategy of denial, from the Russian scorched-earth retreats; now generalised to any policy of total, ruinous denial.
  • Scorching as intensifier of heat or speed (informal): "scorching heat", "a scorching pace", "scorching reviews" — fierce, searing, blistering applied metaphorically to temperature, speed, or criticism.
  • Cooking sense: to brown or sear the surface of food quickly — "scorched rice", "scorched milk" (milk scorched to the pan). The kitchen sense stresses the burnt layer on an otherwise edible whole.
  • Figurative of criticism or condemnation: to scorch someone is to denounce them blisteringly — "his speech scorched the opposition". The verbal heat of anger transferred to language.
  • Scorched in botany and gardening: leaves and lawns "scorched" by sun, frost, or chemicals — browned and dried at the tips, the plant's version of a surface burn.
Beside its near-synonyms, scorch holds a precise middle ground:
  • Burn: similar as the general word for destruction by fire; differs because burn implies nothing superficial and can consume entirely, while scorch is limited to the outer layer of the object.
  • Singe: similar in burning a surface; differs because singe is a brief, light contact with flame that only blackens the tips, often trivial and unintentional, while scorch implies discolouration and shrivelling of the whole outer layer.
  • Sear: similar in drying or withering by sudden intense heat; differs because sear stresses quick browning and colour (as in cooking) rather than blackening, with less deep surface damage.
  • Char: similar in burning and blackening something; differs because char implies deeper carbonisation and a smouldering blackness that reaches into the substance, while scorch stays at the surface.
  • Blaze: similar in being a word of intense heat; differs because blaze describes the fierce burning of the flame itself, not its effect on a surface.
  • Parch: similar in emphasising dryness; differs because parch implies dryness without necessarily any fire at all — the sun or a drought parches, while scorch carries the image of heat applied to a surface.
  • Shrivel: similar as the natural twin in destruction; differs because shrivel folds and deforms the whole body, while scorch sears and blackens the surface without changing the shape.
  • Scald: similar in burning by heat; differs because scald is specifically burning by hot liquid or steam, while scorch is dry heat.
Collocations are strikingly military, meteorological, and domestic: "scorched earth policy", "scorching heat", "a scorching sun", "scorched grass", "scorched rice", "scorched milk", "scorched the lawn", "scorched by the sun", "scorching criticism", "scorching pace". Register: standard to informal English, equally at home in weather reports, kitchen mishaps, military history, and apocalyptic curses. The word fuses withering dryness, surface damage, and dark discolouration — the natural twin of shrivel in a curse promising a slow, cruel drying-out of one's enemies — and its paired form "shrivel and scorch" has the ring of an incantation because each verb supplies what the other lacks: shrivel the substance, scorch the surface, and nothing is left alive.
