how (/haʊ/) is one of the most frequent words in English and one of the hardest to define, because it names nothing. It is a wh-word — historically the instrumental case of the pronoun "who/what", hence "by what" — and its core work is to ask for, or to specify, the way, means, degree or condition of something. In grammar it serves as an interrogative adverb, an exclamatory degree word, a relative and fused-relative adverb, a clause-linking conjunction, and a noun ("the how and the why").

In the source fragment — "but in how altered circumstances now" — how is an exclamatory-degree adverb modifying the participial adjective altered: "in how altered circumstances" means "in what greatly changed circumstances". It is not a genuine question. The writer is inviting the reader to measure the distance between 1744 and the present, and the unfinished sentence leaves that measurement hanging. The construction is literary and slightly archaic: ordinary modern prose would write "in what altered circumstances" or "in how greatly altered circumstances". Here how stands inside a prepositional phrase, modifying an adjective — a position where the word is normally barred in questions (*In how did he arrive?), which is exactly what gives the fragment its formal, rhetorical colour.

1. Interrogative of manner

  • "How did you open it?" — asks for the method or way. The answer is an adverbial: with a knife, by force, carefully.
  • In indirect questions the word order becomes statement order: "She asked how I had done it." No inversion, no auxiliary do unless the clause is direct.
  • With an infinitive it expresses procedure or possibility: "I know how to swim", "how to bake bread", "he showed me how to do it". This how to is the source of the attributive noun how-to (a how-to book, a how-to video).
  • In rhetoric and titles: "How to Win Friends", "How It Works" — the word promises a method rather than a fact.

2. Interrogative of degree and measurement

  • Before an adjective or adverb: how tall, how quickly, how old, how far, how often, how long. Here how is a degree word, not a manner word: it asks for a point on a scale.
  • With quantifiers: how much (mass), how many (count), how much of it. The pair much/many preserves the old mass/count distinction, and how merely supplies the interrogative frame.
  • In indirect and embedded questions: "Tell me how much it cost", "It depends on how far you are willing to go".
  • Idiomatic measurements: "How's the weather?", "How's the leg?" — the scale is implied, not stated.

3. Interrogative of state, health and condition

  • "How are you?" asks about health or circumstances, not about manner; the expected answer is well, fine, tired. This is one of the most formulaic uses of the word in English, and the one from which "How do you do?" derives.
  • "How is the patient?", "How goes it?", "How's life?" extend the pattern to any state that can be good, bad or changing.
  • Note the contrast with what: "How is he?" asks after his condition, "What is he?" asks after his identity or profession.
  • In the source, this sense is present only as an undertone: the Prince "lies sick", and how then shifts from condition to degree — how altered his circumstances are.

4. Exclamatory how

  • "How strange!", "How beautiful the garden looks!", "How he suffered!" — the word marks an exclamation of degree, equivalent to "to what a great extent".
  • The exclamative keeps the wh-word at the front but drops inversion; the fall of the intonation carries the emotion.
  • It can stand alone as a reaction: "How!" in disbelief, or "And how!" as emphatic agreement (mid-nineteenth-century American).
  • This is the sense at work in the source, where how altered is an exclamation in disguise, folded into a subordinate phrase.

5. Relative and fused-relative uses

  • "This is how it happened" — how introduces a fused relative clause meaning "the way in which".
  • "I remember how he looked at me" — the clause is the object of the verb, and how fuses antecedent and relative.
  • Standard English does not allow how after an explicit antecedent: "the way how he did it" is nonstandard or archaic, and "the way that he did it" or "the way he did it" is preferred.
  • Archaic and dialectal "as how" ("he told me as how...") preserves an older conjunctive use.

6. Conjunction and complementizer

  • "He told me how the king was dead" is ambiguous: it may report the manner of the telling or, in older English, simply the fact — how functioning as "that".
  • How that was a common Middle and Early Modern English formula ("he said how that..."), now archaic.
  • "Do it how you like" uses how as a conjunction of manner in informal English, where formal usage prefers as or in whatever way.
  • The compounds however, howsoever and howbeit all began as conjunctive phrases built on this word.

7. Fixed phrases and idioms

  • how come — colloquial "why" (American, by the 1840s), with statement order and no inversion: "How come you're late?"
  • how so? — asks for the reasoning behind an assertion; how about — makes a suggestion; how's about — its jocular variant.
  • how now — archaic expostulation ("How now, sirrah?"), frequent in Shakespeare.
  • how's that? — the fielding side's appeal in cricket, and also a request for repetition.
  • know-how — practical technical knowledge (American, 1838); the how and the why — the method and the reason, often paired.

8. Grammar notes and boundaries

  • How is a wh-adverb, not a pronoun: it cannot be the subject or object of a verb (*"How did it?" meaning "what did it?").
  • It resists prepositional pied-piping: With what did you open it? is formal but grammatical, whereas *With how did you open it? is impossible, because how already contains the instrumental notion "by what". The literary phrase "in how altered circumstances" is a different construction: there how modifies an adjective inside the prepositional phrase, not the preposition's object.
  • How asks about manner; what ... like asks about quality: "How was the film?" invites evaluation, "What was the film like?" invites description.
  • It selects adjectives and adverbs, not nouns: *"how a man" is impossible, though "what a man!" is idiomatic.
  • Its negative-interrogative use ("How could you?") is a reproach rather than a request for information.

9. Register and frequency

  • How belongs to the absolute core of English; it appears in the top hundred words of most frequency lists and is among the first interrogatives children acquire.
  • Its colloquial uses (how come, and how) are marked informal; its exclamatory and literary uses (how altered, how now) are marked formal or archaic.
  • Because it carries no lexical content, it is also the base of a large compound family: however, howsoever, howbeit, know-how, how-to, howdy (from "how do ye").
  • A false friend: the Hollywood greeting "How!" is not this word at all, but a rendering of Dakota hau, a salutation.
