Simone Memmi's is the possessive form of the art-historical alias Simone Memmi, the name under which the great Sienese Gothic painter Simone Martini (c. 1284-1344) was known for centuries after Giorgio Vasari. In the source sentence, John Ruskin in Praeterita recalling that his maternal grandmother had been landlady of the Old King's Head in Market Street, Croydon, and wishing he could paint her Simone Memmi's King's Head for a sign, the phrase means a tavern sign for the King's Head inn but painted with the exquisite golden courtly art of Simone, as a loving memorial portrait of his grandmother as landlady. It is at once a joke about English pub signs, a tribute to early Italian art which Ruskin idolised, and a grandson's fantasy of ennobling a humble Croydon landlady with Sienese gold and grace.

Historically, Simone Martini was with Duccio di Buoninsegna the founder of the Sienese School. His masterpieces include the Maesta in Siena's Palazzo Pubblico (1315), the Annunciation with St Margaret and St Ansanus (1333, with Lippo Memmi, now Uffizi), the St Martin Chapel frescoes in Assisi, and late works at the Papal court in Avignon where he befriended Petrarch and painted Laura. His style is the summit of Gothic elegance: elongated swaying figures, rippling tooled gold grounds, jewel colour, fluttering drapery hems inscribed with Latin, courtly gestures of kneeling, offering, and veiling. Where Florentine Giotto seeks weight and drama, Simone seeks music, manners, and mystic refinement. To say a pub sign was Simone Memmi's is therefore to imagine a King's Head, normally a crude profile of a monarch, rendered with halo-thin line, ivory faces, and burnished gold.

The alias itself needs explanation. Simone was the son of a certain Martino; his brother-in-law and workshop partner was the painter Lippo Memmi, son of Memmo di Filippuccio. Vasari in his Lives (1550) misread the partnership and called Simone himself Simone Memmi, as if Memmi were his surname, and the error stuck in English literature for 300 years. Ruskin, writing in the 1880s, still uses the Vasarian form familiar to British readers from Lord Lindsay, Mrs Jameson, and Murray's handbooks. Modern art history corrects it to Simone Martini, but preserves Lippo Memmi as distinct. The possessive 's is the ordinary English enclitic possessive: Simone Memmi's means of or by Simone Memmi.

  • Core art-historical reference (proper-noun possessive): of, by, or in the manner of Simone Martini alias Memmi; for example Simone Memmi's Annunciation, Simone Memmi's gold-ground technique, a Simone Memmi's King's Head. This is the source sense: authorship or style attribution. It presupposes a canon of Gothic Sienese painting against which English sign-painting is playfully measured.
  • Simone as Italian given name: the Italian form of Simon, from Hebrew Shimon the one who hears, God has heard. Borne by saints, popes, and artists. In isolation Simone in Italy is masculine (pronounced see-MO-neh), in France feminine (see-MON). The bridge from personal name to painter's name is ordinary antonomasia: Simone means the famous bearer.
  • Memmi as Tuscan patronymic surname: literally son or descendants of Memmo, Memmo being a pet form of Guglielmo (William). Compare Medici and Bardi. As a surname it marks Sienese workshop kinship: Lippo Memmi, Federigo Memmi. Its secondary modern life is purely as an art-history tag distinguishing Lippo's circle.
  • 's as English possessive clitic: the reduced descendant of Old English -es genitive, now written with an apostrophe (King's, Memmi's). It can mark ownership (Memmi's shop), authorship (Memmi's Maesta), or style (a Memmi's head equals a head such as Memmi would paint). The semantic bridge from possession to style-imitation is habitual in English: a Rembrandt's gloom, a Turner's sky.
  • Literary-Ruskinian nuance and pub-sign culture: Georgian and Victorian inns advertised by hanging painted boards: a King's Head meant loyalty, usually Charles II or George III in crude oils. Ruskin's fantasy upgrades this folk art to Trecento altar art, collapsing high and low, Siena and Croydon, Catholic gold and Protestant ale. The humour depends on incongruity, the pathos on love: he would gild his plebeian grandmother with sacred art because he loved both her and Simone.
  • Modern usage and disambiguation: today scholars write Simone Martini, guides gloss Simone Martini formerly called Simone Memmi. Simone Memmi's survives in older books, auction catalogues, and Ruskin quotations. It must not be confused with Lippo Memmi's (the brother-in-law's own works) or with Memling (Hans Memling, Netherlandish, 15th century). Understanding the Vasarian error is the bridge from obsolete to current naming.

Grammatically the string is two orthographic words plus a clitic: Simone (forename, capitalised, masculine in Italian) plus Memmi (surname, capitalised, ending in -i patronymic plural) plus 's (apostrophe plus s, possessive). Phonetically in Received Pronunciation anglicised, in Italian as see-MO-neh MEM-mee. It functions as a determiner-like possessive: Simone Memmi's King's Head equals the King's-Head sign belonging to the set painted by Simone.
