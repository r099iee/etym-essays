Amoy (pronounced /ɑːˈmɔɪ/ or /əˈmɔɪ/) is the historical English name for the Chinese port city and island now officially known in Standard Mandarin Chinese as Xiamen (廈門), situated on the southeastern coast of Fujian Province along the Taiwan Strait at the mouth of the Jiulong (Nine Dragons) River.

The name encompasses extensive historical, geopolitical, linguistic, mercantile, and literary significance across centuries of Sino-Western interaction:

  • Geographical and Maritime Significance: Located on Xiamen Island and surrounding mainland bays in southern Fujian, Amoy boasts one of China's finest deep-water, typhoon-sheltered natural harbors. For centuries, it served as the primary maritime gateway connecting southern China with Southeast Asia (the Nanyang), Taiwan, and global trade networks.
  • Victorian Literary Reference in Sherlock Holmes: In Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle" (1892), Sherlock Holmes recounts the exotic provenance of the Countess of Morcar's gem: "This stone is not yet twenty years old. It was found in the banks of the Amoy River in Southern China, and is remarkable in having every characteristic of the carbuncle, save that it is blue in shade, instead of ruby red." Doyle references the Jiulong River drainage near Amoy, invoking the romantic Victorian fascination with exotic Far Eastern treaty ports.
  • Treaty Port Era (1842–1943): Following the First Opium War and the signing of the Treaty of Nanking in 1842, Amoy became one of the first five Chinese "Treaty Ports" opened to British and international trade and foreign consular residency, with the nearby island of Gulangyu (Kulangsu) developing into a celebrated International Settlement renowned for its colonial architecture.
  • Linguistic Significance (Hokkien Dialect): The Amoy dialect (Ē-mn̂g-ōe) is historically regarded as the prestige dialect of the Southern Min (Hokkien) language branch. It was the source of several foundational Chinese loanwords into English, most notably tea (from Hokkien tê, contrasting with Mandarin chá) and ketchup (from Hokkien kê-tsiap "fermented fish brine").
  • Commercial and Tea Trade History: During the 18th and 19th centuries, Amoy was a major global export center for famous Fujian oolong and black teas (such as Bohea tea), loaded onto British and American clipper ships bound for London and Boston.
  • Diaspora Capital: Amoy served as the principal port of departure for millions of Chinese emigrants (the Hokkien diaspora) who settled throughout the Philippines, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Taiwan, establishing vibrant overseas commercial communities.

Semantic Bridges & Polysemic Development: The name Amoy is an English phonetic transcription of the local Southern Min (Hokkien) pronunciation Ē-mn̂g for the Chinese characters 廈門 (meaning "Mansion Gate" or "Gate of the House"). The city was originally fortified in 1387 under the Ming Dynasty to serve as a defensive "gate" against coastal pirates. When Portuguese, Dutch, and British merchants arrived in the 16th and 17th centuries, they transliterated the spoken vernacular Hokkien sound *Ē-mn̂g* as *Emouy* or *Amoy*. With the adoption of Hanyu Pinyin in 1958 and the standardization of international toponyms, the Mandarin pronunciation Xiamen became the official international designation, while Amoy remains a prestigious historical, cultural, and commercial brand name.

Modern Economic Standing: Today, Xiamen (Amoy) is one of China's premier Special Economic Zones (SEZ), consistently ranked among the country's most livable and prosperous coastal cities, preserving its historic heritage on the UNESCO World Heritage island of Kulangsu.
