PIE *weik- “to conquer, overcome, fight” (c. 4000 BCE; attested across Celtic, Germanic, Italic, and possibly Indo-Iranian) → Proto-Italic *weike- “to conquer” (reconstructed) → Latin vincere “to conquer, defeat, vanquish” (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; perfect vīcī, past participle victus) → Latin evincere “to conquer wholly, to win one’s point, to prove” (ex- “out” + vincere; attested from the 1st c. BCE; “to conquer out” — to overcome so completely that the truth is forced out into the open) → English evince “to conquer, vanquish” (first recorded 1609, borrowed directly from Latin evincere) → English evince “to convict, refute, disprove in argument” (17th–18th c.) → English EVINCE “to show clearly, make evident, demonstrate” (18th c. onwards). Debated / nuanced stages: the Celtic and Germanic reflexes (Old Irish fichid “he fights”; Gothic weihan, Old English wīgan “to fight”) are secure, but the Indo-Iranian equation — Sanskrit vijáyati “he conquers”, vijaya “victory” — is debated and accepted by some scholars, rejected by others. The Latin evincere also produced a separate legal branch in English: evictus “conquered out, dispossessed” gave Late Latin ēvictiō “recovery of property by law” and hence English evict/eviction, a cousin that split off from evince early and kept the martial sense of driving someone out by force of law.
