Cannery is a two-part American coinage barely 150 years old, joining a vessel word a thousand years old to a place-suffix older still. Each half needs its own history.

A. CAN — from reed to tin. The noun can begins not in metal but in plants. A wandering Semitic word for reed and cane (qan-) entered Greek as kanna and Latin as canna — reed, pipe, small tube — and from tubes came vessels: things made of reed, then things shaped like reeds. Early Germanic borrowed the vessel sense as *kanno, giving Old English canne, a cup or container for drink and grain. For centuries a can was simply a sturdy cup — a can of ale in tavern English — with no thought of preservation.

  • Middle English stability: canne/can sat beside cup, jug, pot as everyday tableware vocabulary; compounds like milk-can and watering-can show the open, pourable prototype. Sound stayed almost frozen because the monosyllable was too useful to erode.
  • The tin revolution, c.1810: Nicolas Appert's heat-sterilisation and Peter Durand's 1810 British patent for sealing food in tin-plated iron vessels created a new object needing a name; English stretched can (American) and tin (British) over it. The verb to can and gerund canning were promptly coined, and canned goods entered commerce by mid-century. The Civil War and the Gold Rush made canned food strategic: armies and mining camps ran on tins.
  • Industrial canning 1860s–1900s: oyster houses of Baltimore, salmon lines of the Columbia and Alaska, fruit sheds of California, and pineapple fields of Hawaii mechanised filling, exhausting, seaming, and retorting. Each region's pack became an annual statistic, and each season demanded armies of hands — the labour world young Vance entered.

B. -ERY — from Latin craft to American factory row. The suffix -ery descends from Latin -arius (pertaining to a person or craft) plus -ia (place or condition), fused in Late Latin and Old French as -erie: boucherie (butcher's craft and shop), chevalerie (chivalry), raperie. Middle English borrowed the pattern wholesale — bakery, brewery, fishery, cutlery, rookery — and turned it into a productive machine: take a product or agent, add -ery, get the place and the trade together.

  • Productivity in English: by 1700 the suffix generated places (bakery, tannery, hatchery), collectives (machinery, greenery), and behaviours (knavery, mockery). Americans of the 1800s applied it briskly to new industries: creamery, refinery, hatchery, cannery. The formation is transparent and democratic — no classical compound needed.
  • Birth of cannery, 1870s–1880s: on the analogy of fishery and packing-house, West Coast and Eastern packers began calling canning factories canneries; newspapers carry the word routinely by the 1880s. The plural canneries with y → ies follows the regular English rule for consonant-plus-y nouns.
  • Cultural career: Alaska salmon canneries with Chinese, Filipino, Japanese, and Native crews; Monterey sardine canneries with Sicilian fishermen; California fruit canneries with Dust Bowl migrants — the cannery became the emblematic seasonal factory of the American West. Labour organising, strikes, and exposés clung to it, and John Steinbeck's Cannery Row (1945) lifted a Monterey street of sardine works into world literature as a parable of work, drink, and fellowship. Deindustrialisation later emptied many rows into tourist sights, while craft canning (artisanal tuna, conservas) gave the suffix new prestige.

Phonetically cannery is unremarkable and therefore successful: stressed can- keeps the product audible, the reduced -ery (/əri/) files it with its institutional siblings, and the whole proclaims a bakery for cans. In Vance's sentence it does exactly that work — naming one hard, smelling, seasonal room in a young man's education by labour.

  • CAN — deeper attestations and dates: Appert's 1809 preservation method, Durand's 1810 English patent, Donkin and Hall's Bermondsey factory (1813) supplying the Royal Navy, Baltimore oyster canneries by the 1840s, the Columbia River salmon pack of 1866, and the Sacramento delta fruit canneries of the 1870s — the very waters of Vance's Oakley youth — trace the tin from novelty to staple within two generations. The Gold Rush made canned food a California necessity, and the Civil War made it a military one, so that by Vance's Depression the cannery was already a familiar seasonal refuge for the uprooted.
  • -ERY — deeper productivity: the suffix minted creamery (1860s dairy reform), refinery (oil age), hatchery (fish culture), winery (California wine boom), and later playful eatery; each follows the same recipe of product plus place-plus-trade. Labour reporting gave every -ery its human plural — bakery girls, cannery girls, cannery row crews of Filipinos, Japanese, Mexicans, and Okie migrants — and 1930s CIO organising drives made the cannery floor a noted battleground of wage-and-hour law. When Monterey's sardines failed after World War II and Alaska's packs consolidated, the empty canneries became museums and tourist rows, preserving the word in brick even where the steam had died.

So the cannery essay must hold both halves at full weight: a reed-word turned tin technology with dated patents and river histories, and a Latin-French place-suffix turned American factory machine with its own sociology — each a complete three-millennium or two-century story on its own, fused in Vance's single hard season. Modern craft canneries printing small-batch labels deliberately revive the 19th-century word to promise hand-packed care against anonymous processing plants.
