Single root word (brigandage is not a compound), but the ultimate origin of the base briga is debated. The linear chain from the earliest plausible source: - Gaulish briga 'strength, might, strife, fight; also fortified height' (Celtic; attested in continental place-names such as Brigantium, modern Briançon / Bragança, from the 2nd c. BCE) — candidate source of Romance briga, but debated. - Vulgar Latin *briga 'strife, quarrel, contention, trouble' (reconstructed; in use by the early medieval period, 6th–8th c. CE). - Old Italian briga 'trouble, strife, quarrel, bother' (attested 13th c.). - Italian brigare 'to strive, contend, scheme, intrigue' (attested 13th–14th c.; denominal verb from briga). - Italian brigante 'irregular soldier, skirmisher; quarrelsome fellow; later bandit, highwayman' (attested 14th–15th c.; present participle of brigare). - French brigand 'irregular soldier; highwayman, bandit' (borrowed from Italian, attested 14th c.). - Middle French brigandage 'robbery and plunder by armed bands; the trade of brigands' (formed with the abstract suffix -age, attested 15th c.). - English brigandage 'the life and practice of brigands; highway robbery and plunder' (borrowed from French, attested from the early 18th c., c. 1718). Debate note: the origin of Vulgar Latin *briga is not settled. (1) The Celtic hypothesis derives it from Gaulish briga 'strength, fight,' itself possibly connected to the PIE root *bʰerǵʰ- 'high, elevated' via the notion of a fortified height. (2) An alternative connects it to the Germanic root *brekan- 'to break,' picturing a quarrel as a 'falling out' or 'breach.' Neither route is directly attested; the chain above follows the most widely accepted (Celtic > Vulgar Latin) reconstruction.
