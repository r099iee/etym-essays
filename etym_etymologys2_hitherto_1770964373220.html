Compound root word: hitherto = English hither "to here" + to. Because this is a compound of two components, a complete derivation chain is given for each component in isolation, followed by the compounding stage. CHAIN FOR THE COMPONENT "hither": 1. Proto-Indo-European deictic base *k̂e- / *k̂i- "this, here" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE; reflected in Latin cis "on this side" and hic "this", Gothic hita "this", and the English demonstrative he. 2. Proto-Germanic *hidrē "to here, to this place" — built on the demonstrative stem *hi- with the directional suffix *-drē; attested by Gothic hidrē "hither", Old Norse heðra, Old High German hedera. 3. Old English hider "hither, to this place" — attested from the early Old English period (8th–9th century). 4. Middle English hider, later hiþer / hither "to this place" — the -th- form appears from the 13th–14th century by analogy with thither (Old English þider). 5. Modern English adverb hither "to this place, here" — now archaic or literary. (Component node: hither.) CHAIN FOR THE COMPONENT "to": 1. Proto-Indo-European directional particle *do "to, toward" — reconstructed; reflected in Ancient Greek -de (enclitic "to", as in oíka-de "homeward") and Old Church Slavonic do "up to". 2. Proto-Germanic *tō "to, toward" — reflected in Old Saxon tō, Old High German zuo, Old Frisian tō. 3. Old English tō "to, toward, until" — attested from the earliest Old English records. 4. Middle English tō / to "to, toward" — attested from the 12th century. 5. Modern English to (preposition and infinitive particle). (Component node: to.) COMPOUND STAGE: 6. English hitherto — hither + to, literally "up to here"; the spatial sense "to this place" is attested in Middle English (as hider-to), and the transferred temporal sense "up to this time, until now" is established by the 14th–15th centuries. Final node: hitherto. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Both component chains are standard and uncontested. The only point worth flagging is the spelling/pronunciation history of hither, whose -th- arose by analogy with thither rather than by any regular sound change. The shift from spatial "up to here" to temporal "until now" is the classic metaphor of time-as-distance and is not a contested etymology.
