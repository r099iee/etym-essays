- [Latin area, "level open space"] - The word begins on the ground as the smooth threshing floor on which Roman farmers beat the grain — a bare, flattened, cleared platform swept clean of vegetation.

- [Old French aire, a falconer's term] - Among falconers the same word names the nest or lair of a bird of prey: the raptor's bare cliff ledge is seen as the bird's own little threshing floor.

- [English adoption (15th c.)] - Borrowed as aire, eyry, or ayrie to mean "an eagle's nest"; for three centuries the spelling drifts aimlessly — aerie, aery, eyrie, eyry — across different books.

- [19th-century fixing] - The form settles as eyrie, a deliberate, faintly antiquarian respelling that ties the word to air and aerial, and the meaning widens to any lofty, isolated, defensible perch.

- [Modern register] - Archaic, majestic, and faintly formidable: an eyrie is not a snug nest but a fastness, a high citadel of the air that can be approached only on the wing, carrying the quiet medieval glamour of falconry and romance.
