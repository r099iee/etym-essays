Derivation chain for PROPRIETY (single root word; one PIE root):
1. Proto-Indo-European *per- “forward, through, in front of, first” (reconstructed; the same root that gives Greek pró, Sanskrit prá, Latin prō and per).
2. → Latin proprius “one’s own, personal, peculiar” (classical Latin; morphology debated — probably a derivative of the *per- family with a *-prio- suffix, “belonging to oneself first or primarily”; cf. de Vaan).
3. → Latin proprietās “ownership, property; distinctive quality” (abstract noun from proprius; classical Latin).
4. → Old French proprieté / propriété.
5. → English propriety, attested 15th c., “ownership; peculiar quality.”
6. → 17th-century semantic shift: “fitness of conduct to social rule, correctness of behaviour.”
7. → Modern English propriety — OUR CARD: PROPRIETY.

Related branch (same root, same noun): English property (14th c., via Anglo-Norman propertie) is a doublet of propriety — both descend from proprietās; the two words diverged, property keeping the sense of “owned thing” and propriety taking the sense of “correct conduct.”

Debate / nuance: the internal morphology of Latin proprius is not fully agreed upon; de Vaan treats it as a derivative of the *per- family, but the exact suffix and original nuance are debated. The rhetorical strand, proprietās verborum (“the propriety of words,” Cicero and Quintilian), is well attested and is the bridge by which the word moved from “peculiar quality” to “fitness of expression,” and then to “fitness of conduct.”
