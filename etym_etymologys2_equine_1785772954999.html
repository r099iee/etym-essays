Proto-Indo-European *h₁éḱwos "horse" (reconstructed; the classic Indo-European horse word, attested through reflexes in Sanskrit áśva-, Avestan aspa-, Greek ἵππος híppos, Latin equus, Old Irish ech, Old English eoh, Lithuanian ašvà, and Tocharian yakwe; possibly connected to a root meaning "swift," though this is debated) → Latin equus "horse" (the regular Italic reflex, in which the PIE word-initial *h₁e- develops into Latin e- and the palatal *ḱ appears as qu [kʷ]; attested from the earliest Latin literature, 3rd century BCE onward; beside equa "mare" and eques "horseman, knight") → Latin equīnus "of a horse, pertaining to horses" (relational adjective formed with the suffix -īnus; attested in classical and later Latin agricultural, descriptive, and veterinary prose) → Neo-Latin maintenance of equīnus in medieval and early-modern scholarly Latin, with parallel Romance forms (French équin, Italian equino, Spanish equino) → English EQUINE (borrowed as a learned adjective in the 18th century and consolidated through scientific and veterinary vocabulary). Note on the debated stage: The connection of *h₁éḱwos to a root meaning "swift" (often cited via *h₁eḱ-) is plausible but not universally accepted. Note also that English's native Germanic noun horse (from Proto-Germanic *hrussą, of obscure deeper origin) is a different word; equine is not the descendant of Old English eoh but a learned re-borrowing from Latin centuries later.
