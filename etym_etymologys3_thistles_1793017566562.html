The thistle is that rarity, a weed with a knighthood. Its linguistic journey runs from a Germanic field curse to the highest order of Scottish chivalry, and the word never changed its spelling enough to hide the distance travelled: the stable's weed and the palace's badge are one continuous name.

A sharp family, perhaps: If the debated deep etymology holds, thistle belongs to a great Indo-European family of pointed things, cousins to words for stakes, stings, and stitches, all from a root meaning pointed or sharp. Proto-Germanic *þistilaz would then be the sharp thing with the plant suffix, literally the sticker. The hypothesis is phonetically plausible and semantically perfect, which is why handbooks repeat it, but the honest ones add that proof is lacking: plant names wander between languages, and similar forms can mislead. What is certain begins with Germanic itself, where every daughter language has its own thistle word, uniform and ancient.

Old and Middle English: Old English þistel glosses Latin carduus and tribulus in herbals and gospel translations alike, already carrying the double charge of botany and moral: the plant that scratches the reaper and the symbol of the cursed earth. The thorn letter þ gives way to th after the Norman Conquest as scribes abandon the old character, and Middle English thistel stands in field accounts as a routine enemy of the harvest, listed beside docks and darnel in manorial weed grievances.

Scotland takes the weed:

  • The legend: A barefoot Norse invader steps on a thistle in a night attack and cries out, betraying the raid, variously dated to the 1200s and attached to Largs; historians doubt the tale and nobody doubts its power.
  • The badge: By the 1400s the crowned thistle is on coinage and carving, paired with the rose of England after the union of the crowns, the prickly partner in a heraldic marriage.
  • The Order: In 1687 James VII and II founds the Order of the Thistle, with the motto no one wounds me with impunity, promoting a ditch weed to the highest chivalry.
  • Burns: The poet's address to a thistle in a farmer's field fixes the plant's literary character: rough, honest, Scottish, and not to be touched lightly.
  • The export: Emigrants carry the emblem worldwide, and the American tumbleweed, the Russian thistle, borrows the name for a wholly different spiny rolling weed of the plains.

Sound and spelling: The consonant cluster -stl- is the word's signature, a small thicket in the mouth, preserved intact while the initial thorn softened into th. Children learn the word as a tongue exercise, and poets roll it for exactly that resistance: to say thistles is to feel the plant.

Why scorn and honour share one stem: The thistle's career proves that emblems need not be beautiful, only indefeasible: no one plants thistles, no one tends them, no one removes them without cost, and a nation of poor soil recognised its own character in the plant that thrives on neglect and wounds the careless hand. The farmer and the herald never disagreed about what the plant is, only about whether to curse or crown it.
