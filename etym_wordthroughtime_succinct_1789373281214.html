- [Early 15th Century] - Enters Middle English directly from Latin succinctus and Middle French succinct, initially used in both literal sartorial descriptions (girded or tucked up garments) and religious-scholastic texts to denote discourse stripped of rambling extraneous digressions - [connotation: formal theological clarity, monastic discipline, unencumbered speech]
- [16th to 17th Century] - Flourishes during the English Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution; embraced by Francis Bacon and humanist rhetoricians as the stylistic ideal for philosophical axioms, aphorisms, and scientific methodology, explicitly contrasted with Ciceronian bombast - [connotation: intellectual rigor, empirical precision, neoclassical elegance]
- [18th to 19th Century] - Deeply integrated into the literary and legal vernacular of the Enlightenment and Victorian eras; praised as the hallmark of moralists, epigrammatists (such as Alexander Pope and Samuel Johnson), and statutory legal draftsmen - [connotation: aristocratic wit, judicial gravity, masterful verbal control]
- [20th Century to Present] - Expands into computer science and information theory with the development of "succinct data structures" (representing data within an asymptotic space bound near information-theoretic entropy while enabling rapid queries), while remaining the universal gold standard for executive summaries, journalistic reporting, and modern digital communication - [connotation: cognitive efficiency, technical optimization, sharp communicative clarity]
