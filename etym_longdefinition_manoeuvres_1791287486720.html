Manoeuvres is the British spelling of the plural noun maneuvers, meaning planned, skilful movements or schemes designed to gain an advantage, used both literally for troops, ships, vehicles, or bodies moved with dexterity, and figuratively for cunning plans, intrigues, or political stratagems.

In the Wodehouse sentence — his low manoeuvres had been rendered null and void — the figurative, pejorative sense dominates: secretive, somewhat shabby plots or dodges, hatched to outwit someone, here comically described in military-legal language as if they were an enemy offensive that has been cancelled. Low marks them morally: mean, underhand, unsportsmanlike, rather than brilliant. The joke depends on inflated diction: a drawing-room intrigue is reported like a War Office communiqué.

More broadly, manoeuvres covers any artful deployment in space or in argument. Armies conduct manoeuvres on exercise; drivers perform manoeuvres to park; physiotherapists and first-aiders perform named manoeuvres on the body; diplomats, lawyers, and lovers engage in manoeuvres to outmanoeuvre each other. The singular manoeuvre can also be a verb: to manoeuvre for position, to manoeuvre someone into agreeing.

Polysemy and modern senses include:
  • Military exercises and tactical movements: large-scale mock battles and troop movements in peacetime — autumn manoeuvres, joint naval manoeuvres. The historical bridge is direct from French manœuvre as handling of troops in the seventeenth century.
  • Skilful physical handling of a vehicle, vessel, or object: parking manoeuvres, docking manoeuvres, a three-point manoeuvre. The bridge is metaphorical extension from moving regiments to moving cars or stretchers through constrained space.
  • Cunning schemes or stratagems, often pejorative: political manoeuvres, legal manoeuvres, low manoeuvres. The bridge is analogy: debate and intrigue are war by other means, requiring feints, flanking, and cover.
  • Medical / technical procedures involving the hands: the Valsalva manoeuvre, the Heimlich manoeuvre, obstetric manoeuvres. The bridge is etymological return: Latin manu operare, to work by hand, survives literally in medicine.
  • Sporting deceptions: in chess, fencing, football, a manoeuvre is a calculated feint or positional play. The bridge is from battlefield tactics to game tactics.
Spelling distinguishes varieties: British manoeuvre / manoeuvres with oe and our, American maneuver / maneuvers without. Pronunciation is identical in meaning. Register is flexible: admiring when praising skill, suspicious or mocking when implying trickery, with low, shady, cynical, or cunning as common collocates for the pejorative pole.

Grammatically, manoeuvres is always plural in the scheming sense because intrigue is conceived as a sequence of moves rather than one act: one lays plans, makes feints, retreats, and advances, exactly as battalions do. The singular is reserved for a single parking or medical procedure, while the plural signals extended campaigning. Collocations such as elaborate manoeuvres, desperate manoeuvres, and transparent manoeuvres show speakers judging both skill and honesty, and verbs like frustrate, expose, and countermine manoeuvres preserve the military metaphor in parliament, courtroom, and boardroom alike.
