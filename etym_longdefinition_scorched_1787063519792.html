Core meaning. Scorched describes something that has been burned or singed on its surface by dry heat or fire, so that its outer layer is browned, blackened, shrivelled, or marked. Unlike "burnt," scorched suggests surface damage rather than complete destruction: a field of grass is scorched when the flames pass over it and crisp the blades; skin is scorched when it is blistered by intense heat; a pan is scorched when food sticks and blackens; a shirt is scorched when an iron is left too long. In the supplied passage, the scorched grass is the grass of the dwarves' mountain campsite that has been singed and crisped by Smaug's fire-breath before the destruction of the ledge begins — a small, burnt token of the dragon's earlier passing.

Primary sense: surface burning by fire or heat. The central sense of scorched is "damaged on the surface by heat or flame": scorched grass, scorched earth, scorched wood, scorched linen, a scorched cake. The word stresses the surface — the outside of the thing is charred and browned while the inside may remain intact. This distinguishes scorched from reduce-to-ash words such as incinerated or cremated. It also carries a dryness: scorching heat parches, withers, and dries out, so that the damaged object is not only darkened but crisped and brittle.

Sense 2: dried, parched, withered. Because intense heat dries as well as burns, scorched often means "dried up, withered, shrivelled by heat and drought": scorched deserts, scorched crops, scorched leaves, a scorched throat. In this sense no flame is needed — the sun alone can scorch a landscape. The participle leans on the weather sense of scorching ("scorching heat," "a scorching day"), and it is this dryness that makes scorched the natural word for drought-stricken farmland and fire-swept hillsides alike.

Sense 3: the "scorched earth" idiom and military sense. Scorched earth is a military strategy of destroying everything — crops, buildings, roads, supplies — so that an invading army finds nothing to use. The strategy is ancient (attested in warfare from antiquity, through Napoleon's campaigns and the Russian retreats of 1812 and 1941), but the exact phrase "scorched earth" is modern, entering English usage in the early twentieth century and becoming standard during the Second World War. From it come "scorched-earth policy" and, in business, a "scorched-earth defence" — a company strategy of making itself unappealing to a hostile takeover, sometimes by damaging its own most valuable assets.

Sense 4: colloquial extensions. In colloquial English, scorched and its verb have stretched to mean "severely reprimanded" or "utterly defeated": "the manager scorched the whole department," "we got scorched in the final." These uses are essentially hyperbolic extensions of surface-burning: to be "scorched" is to be singed, punished, or beaten so completely it feels like burning. The adjective scorching has likewise become a general intensifier for heat and speed, and "scorcher" is informal for a blazing hot day or a ferocious performer.

Grammar and word-formation. Scorched is the past participle and past tense of the verb to scorch, and it functions both as a verb form ("the flames scorched the grass") and as an adjective ("scorched grass," "scorched earth"). The agent noun is scorcher, the noun scorch means a burn-mark (especially on fabric or metal), and scorching is both the present participle and an intensifying adjective/adverb ("scorching hot"). The word is regular in conjugation: scorch, scorched, scorching.

Register and style. Scorched is a vivid, slightly literary word, common in narrative prose, weather writing, and environmental reporting. It is stronger than singed (a light surface burn) and milder than charred (blackened and crusted). Its combination of burning and drying makes it the standard word for landscapes ravaged by fire or drought, and that is precisely the register of Tolkien's scorched camping ground — a patch of grass toasted by dragon-fire and about to be wiped away with everything else.

Collocations worth learning. "scorched earth," "scorched grass/fields/hillside," "scorched by the sun," "scorched and blackened," "scorching heat," "a scorcher of a day," "scorched earth policy." Notice that the collocates split cleanly between literal burning (grass, fields, wood) and figurative devastation (earth, policy, defence), which is exactly the double life of the word.
