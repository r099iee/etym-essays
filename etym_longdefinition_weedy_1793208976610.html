Weedy is in its first and most literal sense full of weeds: ground, gardens, ponds, or riverbeds thickly overgrown with unwanted plants, where cultivation has lapsed and nature has taken the plot back for herself.

In the source sentence the word describes exactly such a place: the travellers follow a brook that trickles and babbles through a weedy bed — a shallow channel choked with water-plants, grasses, and tangles of growth, the water finding its way between stems and roots. The brook is small and the weeds are many, so the stream must chatter and stumble over its weedy obstacle course, which is why it both trickles and babbles.

Core meaning in detail: A weedy garden is one the gardener has neglected; a weedy bed of a stream is one where current is too slack to scour the channel clean, so reeds, rushes, algae, and trailing weeds colonise the bed and slow the water further. Farmers and gardeners use the word as a mild verdict — a weedy field wants hoeing, a weedy pond wants dredging — while naturalists may use it neutrally for rich marginal habitat where fry shelter and dragonflies hunt. The word always implies abundance beyond what is wanted: one dandelion is a flower, a lawn of them is weedy.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Abounding in weeds (primary sense, from the 15th c.): a weedy garden, a weedy bed, weedy pavements; ground where unwanted plants dominate. This is the source sentence's sense, applied to the brook's choked channel.
  • Like a weed in growth — straggly and rank (17th c.): thin drawn-up stems, rank-smelling foliage, plants that shoot tall and weak for lack of light or thinning. The bridge is resemblance: the growth has the weed's legginess and excess without a weed necessarily being present.
  • Thin, scrawny, and puny — of people and animals (19th c.): a weedy schoolboy, a weedy youth, a weedy horse with narrow chest and weak legs. The bridge is the etiolated plant carried into flesh: just as a weed starved of light grows tall, pale, and feeble, so the weedy person is all height and no strength — the classic victim of the playground and the parade ground.
  • Feeble, ineffectual, lacking force (figurative, modern): a weedy voice, a weedy excuse, a weedy performance. The bridge runs from the body to its products: what a weedy constitution produces — thin sound, thin argument — is weedy too, and the word carries gentle contempt.
  • Full of seaweed (specialised, coastal): a weedy shore, weedy rock pools. The bridge is the broad old sense of weed itself, which once meant any wild grass or herb, including wrack and kelp.

Register and connotation: weedy is faintly disparaging in every sense except the naturalist's: gardeners sigh it, sergeant-majors bark it, and school stories trade on it. Applied to land it is a reproach to the keeper; applied to people it is kindly meant only among friends and wounding elsewhere, picturing someone to be pitied or pushed aside. Yet the source sentence shows its tender side too: a weedy bed is also a sheltered, babbling, living place, and the word's very disapproval makes the brook feel secret and undisturbed.
