Core sense. Fabled means "celebrated in fables and legends; legendary, famous, mythical" — applied to places, treasures, creatures, and persons whose fame comes from stories told about them. A fabled treasure is one that everyone has heard of in tale and song, whether it truly exists or not; the word glows with the light of storytelling. In the source context — "the fabled treasure of the Mountain" — the hoard of the dragon Smaug is precisely this: a treasure known through story, lying now without guard or owner.

1. Primary senses.

  • Famous through legend: the dominant modern sense. "Fabled" says that something is renowned because stories circulate about it: fabled city, fabled kingdom, fabled wealth, fabled beauty. The thing may be real or imaginary; what matters is that its fame is narrative — it lives in the telling.
  • Belonging to fable / myth: a narrower literal sense — belonging to the world of fables: talking animals, magic, gods, and heroes. "Fabled creatures" are creatures that exist only in stories.
  • Fictitious, invented: the oldest sense, shading into "untrue" — a "fabled" account is one made up, told as a tale. This sense survives in formal and literary usage and connects the word to its root in "fable" = "a made-up story".
  • Celebrated, much talked of: by extension, anything so talked about that it has become proverbial — a fabled host, a fabled library, a fabled generosity. Here "fabled" approaches "famous" and "renowned" but keeps the storytelling flavour.
  • As a participle of the verb "to fable": grammatically, fabled is the past participle of the verb to fable ("to tell tales, to invent, to feign"): "he fabled a whole biography for himself". The adjective use ("celebrated in fable") grew out of this participle in the 17th century.

2. Semantic bridge. Latin fabula meant "a tale, a story" — literally "something spoken", from fārī "to speak". A fable is a story, and things "fabled" are things that stories have made famous. The interesting drift is from the negative to the positive: in Latin and early English the family of "fable" leaned toward "made-up, false" (a fable is a fiction), yet "fabled" came to mean "celebrated" — because a thing told about in stories acquires fame regardless of its truth. The modern word therefore hovers between "famous in story" and "probably not real", which is exactly the glow of the context: the treasure is fabled, storied, half-believed.

3. The word in the context. In Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) the phrase "the fabled treasure of the Mountain" does exact double work: the treasure is fabled because the songs and tales of the North have made it famous, and it is about to be sought again — lying without guard or owner — by men who believe in the story. The word marks the point where legend turns into motive: a fabled hoard is a hoard worth risking everything for.

4. Register and collocations. Literary, poetic, deliberately old-fashioned; common collocations: fabled treasure/hoard, fabled city/kingdom, fabled land, fabled riches, fabled past. In plain modern prose "legendary" has largely replaced it, and "fabled" now sounds storybookish — a word for fantasy, fairy-tale, and travel-writing wonder. The word-family: fable (the tale), fabulist (a teller of fables, especially Aesop), fabulous ("mythical; astonishing; wonderful"), fabulosity (rare), and the more distant relatives fame and famous — all from the same Indo-European root meaning "to speak".
