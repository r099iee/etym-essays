- 14th c. — translating the law: not withstanding enters as a word-for-word Englishing of Latin non obstante in statutes and chronicles — nothing standing in the way.
- 1400s — the preposition hardens: what began as a loose absolute phrase (the rain not withstanding = the rain not opposing) fuses into a governing preposition meaning despite.
- 1500s — Tudor prose adopts it: chroniclers and statutes use it to override objections in advance; the postpositive order (the cost notwithstanding) preserves the old absolute word order.
- 1700s — lawyers seize it: notwithstanding anything to the contrary becomes conveyancers' armour, the clause that beats all other clauses.
- 1800s — Cobbett's verdict: radicals and historians wield it as the hammer of polemical concession — ugly, the money notwithstanding.
- 1900s — the plain-English siege: style guides from Fowler onward mark it legalistic and urge despite; business and official prose keeps it anyway.
- Today — formal survivor: statutes, contracts, judgments, and deliberate literary gravity keep notwithstanding alive; everywhere else despite rules the sentence.
