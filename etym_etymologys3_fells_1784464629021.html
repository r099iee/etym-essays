1. The problem of the deeper root

The landscape word fell (plural fells) is a Norse gift to English, but its remoter ancestry is genuinely uncertain, and honesty requires saying so. Many handbooks trace the Germanic rock-vocabulary to a Proto-Indo-European root *pels- or *pel-, "rock, cliff, stone," and group Proto-Germanic *felis-/*falisa- (the ancestor of German Fels, "rock, cliff") with it; the reconstruction appears in Pokorny and in many etymological dictionaries. But the cognate set outside Germanic is thin, the correspondence is not universally accepted, and some scholars treat the Germanic term as of uncertain or possibly non-Indo-European (substrate) origin. The honest position, which this card adopts, is: the Germanic rock-words may connect to a PIE *pels-/*pel-, but the deeper layer is debated; the secure history begins at Proto-Germanic.

2. Proto-Germanic rock, and the two faces of the family

Proto-Germanic had a word for "rock, cliff" reconstructed as *felis- or *falisa- (the shapes vary in handbooks because the surviving forms differ across branches). Its West Germanic reflexes are well attested: Old High German felis, modern German Fels, "rock, cliff, crag," and Old English fell (rare, "rock, cliff, mountain"), which survives in place-names. The word's core sense is hard rock — a cliff, a crag, a mountain mass.

The North Germanic branch developed the specifically "mountain" sense that English borrowed. Old Norse had fjall (also fell, fjell), "mountain, fell, high rocky ground," attested across Eddaic verse, the sagas, and runic material from the tenth century onward; modern Scandinavian still has fjäll/fjell, "mountain," the source of the international loanword fjeld/fell used in arctic geography.

3. The Viking bridge: Norse settlement plants fell in English

The decisive event for English was the Viking settlement of northern and eastern England in the ninth and tenth centuries. Norse-speaking settlers — largely Danes and Norwegians — brought their everyday vocabulary into the Danelaw, and among the loans was fjall/fell for the high, barren country of the north. Middle English records the word as fel/fell, "hill, mountain," from the fourteenth century, with a firmly northern and dialectal character.

  • Old Norse fjall (also fell) "mountain, fell" → Middle English fel/fell "hill, mountain" → modern fell (landscape sense).
  • Norse settlement gave English hundreds of everyday loans (sky, knife, window, egg); fell is one of the topographic ones.
  • The word thrived in the dialects and place-names of northern England and Scotland — Scafell, Seatallan, and the "Lake District fells" all carry it.
  • It stayed rare or literary in southern everyday speech, giving it a regional colour it has never lost.

4. Semantic life in English: from Viking hill to Lakeland scenery

In the north the word was always a working topographic term: the fells were the high, treeless pastures where sheep were kept, the rough upland country beyond the valleys. In the eighteenth century, the Lake District poets and the new fashion for "picturesque" scenery turned the same uplands into objects of sublime beauty — Wordsworth's poetry and the guidebooks to the Lakes made "the fells" a beloved, romantic name for northern landscape. In the twentieth century, fell-walking and fell-running made the word an outdoor, recreational term: the fells are the hillwalker's terrain, walked, climbed, and raced. Through all of this the semantic core held steady — high, barren, treeless, northern — and the word never became a mere generic synonym for "hill."

5. Homonym discipline: separate trees, one spelling

The spelling fell carries several unrelated words in English, and careful cards keep the trees apart:

  • fell, noun (this card): high barren hill — from Old Norse fjall;
  • fell, adjective: fierce, cruel, deadly — from Old English felle, a different history;
  • fell, verb: past tense of fall;
  • fell, verb: to cut down (a tree) — a causative built on fall;
  • fell, noun (dialect): a pelt, an animal's skin — Old English fell "skin," unrelated to the landscape word.

Only the landscape sense is this card, and the Norse provenance distinguishes it cleanly from its homonyms.

6. In the dwarves' song

In the song-line — "In hollow halls beneath the fells" — Tolkien places dwarf-architecture under exactly this northern high country. The plural sketches a whole skyline of wild, treeless heights above the deep halls, and the word's Norse-tinted, rugged register — a Viking word for mountain under a Germanic legend of delving — makes the geography itself part of the mythic north. The etymology and the poetry agree: fells are high, old, barren ground, and beneath them lie the hollow halls where dark things sleep.
