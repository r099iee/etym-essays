- 6th–5th c. BCE — Greek genesis: Attested first in Ancient Greek as Κελτοί (Keltoi) by Hecataeus of Miletus and Herodotus, designating the barbarian peoples inhabiting western Europe near the sources of the Danube.
- 1st c. BCE — Latin canonization: Caesar immortalized the autonym in De Bello Gallico, noting that the central peoples of Gaul called themselves Celtae in their own tongue.
- 16th–17th c. — Antiquarian rediscovery: European humanists reintroduced Latin Celtae into academic discourse, leading Scottish scholar George Buchanan to recognize common Celtic linguistic ancestry across Britain and Gaul.
- 18th c. — Historiographical zenith: Adopted in formal British historiography (Hume, Gibbon) with the classical ligature æ as an elevated, unvarnished citation of classical Roman authorities.
- 19th c. — Phonetic debates and Anglicization: The ligature faded as the word anglicized into Celts, accompanied by fierce intellectual debates over whether to pronounce the initial consonant as /s/ or /k/.
- 20th–21st c. — Archaic retention: Preserved in historical and scholarly contexts as an authentic classical form, while standard anthropology and linguistics use Celts.
