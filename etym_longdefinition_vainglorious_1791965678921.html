vainglorious is an adjective formed from the noun vainglory plus the suffix -ous. It means "given to vainglory": inordinately proud, boastful, and — crucially — proud of something hollow. The word is not a mere synonym for proud or conceited. Its distinguishing ingredient is the vain in the middle, which descends from Latin vānus, "empty": a vainglorious person claims glory that is not there. In the source passage the word modifies braggart — "a vainglorious braggart" — so that the noun supplies the behaviour (loud boasting) and the adjective supplies the diagnosis (the boasting is groundless and self-worshipping).

Core sense. "Filled with an exaggerated sense of one's own worth and eager to have it acknowledged; boastful in a way that betrays emptiness." The adjective is used of persons ("a vainglorious general"), of their utterances ("vainglorious rhetoric"), and of works that embody collective self-congratulation ("a vainglorious monument", "vainglorious imperial pomp").

Senses and applications

  • Personal boastfulness: the primary modern sense. The vainglorious man does not merely think well of himself; he needs an audience, and he inflates his record to get one.
  • Rhetoric and public speech: "vainglorious boasts", "vainglorious promises", "vainglorious declarations". The word is common in political commentary, where it describes claims made for effect and unlikely to survive contact with events.
  • National and imperial self-image: "vainglorious empire", "vainglorious nationalism", "a vainglorious chapter of national mythology". Here the emptiness is collective: the glory is manufactured by propaganda.
  • Religious and moral-theological: in Christian moral writing, vainglory (vanagloria) is the vice of seeking human praise for good works, and vainglorious can mean "infected with that vice" — a sin against humility rather than a social failing.
  • Of things and styles: "vainglorious architecture", "vainglorious titles", "vainglorious display" — ostentation that advertises an importance the object cannot sustain.

How the sense developed. The noun vainglory came into English around 1300 from Old French vaine gloire, itself a rendering of Latin vāna glōria, literally "empty glory". The phrase was already a moral technicality in Christian Latin: glory sought from men rather than from God was gloria vana, glory with nothing behind it. Middle English took the compound whole, spelling it vainglorie, and the adjective vainglorious followed in the early fifteenth century. Because the first element was no longer a live English word in the compound, the spelling vain- was retained as a fossil, which is why the word looks and sounds archaic beside its modern paraphrase "vainly glorious".

Register and connotation. Vainglorious is formal, literary and contemptuous, with a faintly archaic or pulpit flavour. It is stronger than boastful (which merely describes the talk) and nastier than proud (which can be neutral or approving): it alleges both exaggeration and emptiness, and it implies that the speaker has seen through the performance. Its natural collocates are boast, rhetoric, claim, display, pomp, monument, empire, title. It is rarely used of children and almost never of legitimate pride in real achievement.

Distinguishing it from vanity. Vanity is the broader vice: concern with one's appearance, reputation or attractiveness, and the pleasure taken in being admired. Vainglory is narrower and more public: it is the appetite for acclaim on account of supposed merits, especially merits one does not possess. A vain man may be vain about his hair; a vainglorious man wants a triumph voted to him. That is why the word attaches so readily to generals, statesmen and empires, and why the source passage uses it of Cicero, whose self-praise — including the notorious verse line on his own consulship — was mocked even in antiquity.

In this reading. The clause "a vainglorious braggart" is the second item in a four-part character assassination. The ordering matters: vacillating attacks the will, vainglorious attacks the self-image, pusillanimous attacks the courage, and pseudo-intellectual attacks the mind. Together they amount to a claim that Cicero's eloquence was vanity, his courage theatre and his philosophy decoration. The charge of vainglory is the one with the deepest classical roots: Cicero's habit of celebrating his own consulship in verse and prose gave his enemies material for centuries, and the Roman biographers transmit the ridicule.

Modern currency. The word survives chiefly in edited prose — leader columns, history books, essays on empire — and in translations of classical and theological texts. It is markedly rarer than boastful or conceited and is felt as a period word, which is part of its point: to call a modern politician vainglorious is to invoke the moral vocabulary of sermons and Roman historiography.
