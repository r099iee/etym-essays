- c. 10th-11th c. - Attested in Late Old English as stūpian, meaning "to bow, bend the body forward, incline, or lean downward."
- 13th-14th c. - In Middle English (stoupen), the word expanded from physical posture to describe social condescension and moral degradation (lowering oneself to commit a shameful deed).
- 15th-16th c. - Specialized within aristocratic falconry literature (Tudor treatises on hawking) to describe the dramatic vertical dive of a bird of prey upon quarry.
- 17th-18th c. - Flourished in English drama and poetry (Shakespeare, Milton, Oliver Goldsmith's She Stoops to Conquer), exploring the tension between nobility and voluntary humility.
- 19th c. - Deployed by Romantic and Victorian poets (Wordsworth, Tennyson, Tolkien's early inspirations) to depict dramatic landscapes, overhangs, and descending forest canopies.
- 20th-21st c. - Standard across contemporary English for physical bending, moral critique ("stoops to low levels"), raptor hunting dynamics, and urban architecture.
