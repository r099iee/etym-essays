Primary sense: directly and without evasion. Squarely is the adverb of square, and its most characteristic modern use is to mean "directly, straight, with no hedging". To face a problem squarely is to look at it full in the face; to place the blame squarely on someone is to assign it without qualification; to be squarely responsible is to be responsible completely. The word conveys decision, honesty and a refusal to fudge, and it is a favourite of journalists, lawyers and moralists for exactly that reason.

Positional sense: exactly, precisely. Squarely also means "exactly in a specified place", usually the centre. The lamp stood squarely in the middle of the table; the blame falls squarely on the shoulders of the minister; the ball landed squarely on the line. Here the underlying image is the square as a shape with a definite, unambiguous centre and edges: something placed squarely is placed fair and true, neither askew nor off-centre.

Manner sense: fairly and honestly. The older moral sense of square as "honest, fair, straightforward" survives in squarely. To deal squarely with someone is to deal honestly with them; a squarely fought contest is a fair one. The phrase "fair and square" fixes the association, and squarely carries a slightly old-fashioned, decent, man-of-his-word flavour, the morality of the counting-house and the playing field rather than the courtroom.

  • Firmly: squarely in the sense of planting one's feet or holding one's ground.
  • Bluntly: squarely in the sense of speaking without softening the truth.
  • Unequivocally: squarely in the sense of assigning responsibility with no ambiguity.
  • Four-square: an archaic and emphatic variant meaning solid, steady and uncompromising.

Geometrical and technical sense. In its literal sense squarely means "in a square form, at right angles, with sides parallel or perpendicular". A picture hangs squarely when its edges are true to the wall; a box sits squarely on the floor when its base is flat and its sides vertical; a mason cuts a block squarely when the corners are right angles. In carpentry, masonry, typography and engineering, squarely is a term of accuracy: it means aligned to the square, and therefore true, stable and correctly fitted.

The polysemy of square behind the adverb. The adverb inherits the extraordinary range of its base word, which is one of the most semantically productive in English:

  • A plane figure with four equal sides and right angles; hence a square in geometry and a square number in arithmetic.
  • An open area in a town, originally a rectangular space, hence Trafalgar Square, the town square.
  • A tool for drawing or testing right angles, the carpenter's square and the set square.
  • A military formation, the infantry square that resisted cavalry.
  • Honest and fair, as in square dealing, a square deal, fair and square.
  • A conventional or old-fashioned person, twentieth-century slang, hence "be there or be square".
  • To settle or balance, as in square accounts, square up, square away.
  • A substantial meal, as in a square meal, originally nautical or American.
  • To multiply a number by itself, and to fit something exactly, as in square the circle.

Idioms that feed the adverb. Square off means to take up a fighting stance or to prepare for a contest; square up means to settle a debt or to face an opponent; square away is a nautical term for setting things in order; square the circle names an impossible task; a square peg in a round hole describes someone misfitted to their situation. In all of them the square stands for order, rectitude and exactness, and the adverb squarely draws on the same cluster of values.

Register and modern usage. Squarely is unmarked in register but slightly formal in flavour, and it is especially common in three contexts: political journalism ("the decision lies squarely with the cabinet"), legal and moral argument ("squarely within the law"), and physical description ("planted squarely in the doorway"). It is rare in casual conversation, where right, straight or dead would do the work.

The semantic bridge. The chain from geometry to ethics is one of the most instructive in English. A square is a shape with true right angles and equal sides; something square is therefore well made, balanced and without distortion; a person who is square is straight, fair and reliable; and an action done squarely is done directly, honestly and completely. The physical image of the set square, the tool that tests whether a corner is true, lies behind the whole moral vocabulary of square dealing.
