- Old English Period (c. 9th–11th Century) - The base verb *crēopan* flourished, denoting the crawling of reptiles, insects, and stealthy human movement; the agent noun *crēopere* existed for crawling things or crippled individuals.
- 14th to 15th Century (Middle English) - *Crepere* or *creper* broadened to designate devices used for dragging riverbeds (iron grapnels/creepers) and crawling animals.
- 16th Century (Tudor Botanical Era) - Applied systematically in English gardening treatises and herbals (William Turner, John Gerard) to trailing, climbing plants that cling to soil or masonry without self-supporting woody trunks.
- 18th to 19th Century (Victorian Golden Age) - Ubiquitous in Victorian domestic fiction and horticultural literature (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*), where cottage "creepers" (clematis, honeysuckle, Virginia creeper) symbolized picturesque rural charm.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Acquired novel mechanical and commercial senses: mechanic's wheeled floor trolleys, infants' one-piece "creeper" garments, and 1950s British "brothel creeper" shoes.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Continues as an essential landscaping term, while colloquially expanding into modern slang for an unsettling, boundary-violating individual ("creeper").
