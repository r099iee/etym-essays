- [Ancient Greek Era (c. 5th–4th c. BCE)] — austēros originated as a physical taste descriptor for dry, astringent, puckering wine or unripe fruit, gradually expanding metaphorically into a description of stern, unyielding, and humorless moral character
- [Classical Roman Era (c. 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE)] — Latin austēritās denoted sensory bitterness, medical astringency, and unbending philosophical gravitas in Stoic self-discipline
- [Patristic & Medieval Monastic Era (c. 4th–14th c. CE)] — absorbed into Christian Latin, Old French, and Middle English (austerite) to signify the physical bodily mortifications, fasting, vigils, and hairshirts of monastic orders
- [Early Modern & Puritan Era (c. 16th–18th c.)] — used in English literature to describe strict puritanical moral codes, plain unadorned dress, and the solemn rejection of theatrical luxury
- [Colonial Indological Era (19th c.)] — established in translations of the Sanskrit epics as the definitive English rendering for Vedic tapas (the severe ascetic penances performed by sages and demons to compel divine boons)
- [Modern Political & Economic Era (20th–21st c.)] — adapted in macroeconomics as a technical term for fiscal spending cuts, balanced budget mandates, and sovereign retrenchment (austerity measures)
