In Wilde's sentence the hippopotamus ("the riverhorse") couches among the bulrushes: it lies down low in the reeds, resting half-hidden. This is the archaic-to-literary verbal sense of couch — "to lie down, to crouch, to settle at rest", used especially of animals, and in older English also of men lying in ambush or at ease. The form couches is the plain third-person singular present of the verb: the hippo couches as the lion crouches; the same spelling is the plural of the noun couch ("two couches stood by the wall").


Primary verbal sense — to lie or crouch down. To couch is to lower the body and rest, or to sink low and unseen. The verb is now rare in everyday speech and lives in literary and technical registers: beasts "couching" in cover; the heraldic beast "couchant" — lying with head raised; older hunting language of animals settling in their lair. From the same root-idea comes the historical military phrase "to couch a lance": to lower the spear into the horizontal charging position, laying it along the horse's neck for the attack.


Secondary verbal sense — to express in words. To "couch" a statement is to set it down, phrase it, or clothe it in language of a specified cast: "the letter was couched in the most courteous terms". This developed from the sense "to set in order, arrange", and is today the verb's most common living use — formal, almost always passive, and standard in diplomatic and editorial English.


Tertiary sense — historical surgery. In old ophthalmology "to couch a cataract" meant to dislodge the opaque lens from the line of sight by pressure or a needle — the ancient operation of couching described in Greco-Roman medicine (Celsus) and the Sushruta tradition; the phrase survives in history-of-medicine writing and links this verb to the neighbouring word "cataract" in the same reading passage.


The noun senses. The noun couch, "a bed", appears in Middle English almost as early as the verb, and widened over time to any lay-by for resting — a lair, a poet's couch — until, in the 19th century, it settled on the upholstered sofa of the parlour; in American English "couch" is the everyday word for the living-room sofa, while Britain prefers "sofa" or "settee". The psychoanalyst's couch gave English the idiom "on the couch" (in therapy), and 20th-century irony produced "couch potato". The two faces of the word — verb of repose and noun of furniture — are one family: a couch is what has been "laid down" and on which one lays oneself.


Homograph caution. "Couch grass", the creeping weed, is NOT from this family: it is "quitch" (Old English cwicu, "living") reshaped by folk etymology — the plant being famously hard to kill.


  • Sense 1 (verb): to lie down, crouch, or rest, especially of animals — the hippo couches in the reeds.
  • Sense 2 (verb): to express in a given style — "couched in diplomatic terms".
  • Sense 3 (verb, historical): to couch a lance (lower it for the charge) or to couch a cataract (push the lens aside).
  • Sense 4 (noun): a bed or sofa; the analyst's couch; "couch potato".
  • Not related: couch grass (quitch grass).
