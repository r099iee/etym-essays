The word courtier is a product of medieval Europe's most important social invention — the court itself — but its deep history reaches back more than six thousand years to a Proto-Indo-European root about physical enclosure. The reconstructed root *gʰer- meant "to grasp, to hold, to enclose," and from it early speakers formed the noun *gʰor-tó-, "the enclosed space, the fenced yard." In every branch of the Indo-European family this idea of boundedness was applied to home and community in parallel but independent ways. The Indo-Iranian branch turned it into the house itself (Vedic gṛhá-, modern Hindi ghar, Bengali ghôr); the Hellenic branch used it for the farmyard and the fodder-pen (Greek khórtos) and, by a striking transfer of "coiled enclosure," for the tightly wound gut (khordḗ, ancestor of our chord and cord); the Germanic branch built *gardaz, the enclosure that became Old English geard (yard), Gothic gards (household), Old Norse garðr (yard and stronghold), and — through the vocabulary of Norse settlement and Romance borrowing — the modern English garden, orchard, and yard.

The Italic branch took the root in a direction that would reshape European politics. Proto-Italic inherited *gʰortos as Latin hortus, "garden, enclosed plot of ground" — the root of our horticulture. But Latin then compounded hortus with the preverb co- ("together"), producing cohors (genitive cohortis): literally "the thing enclosed together," that is, an enclosed yard, and by extension the company of people gathered within that yard. In the Roman republic cohors came to mean a "retinue, throng, train of followers," and it acquired its most famous technical sense as a military unit — the cohort, one-tenth of a legion, a body of men shut up, as it were, in the same formation. In Late Latin and the vulgar speech of the emerging Romance world the word's first syllable eroded: cohors, cohortem, was reduced to cortem, curtem, giving *curtis — the enclosed court and estate of a lord or king, together with the household and dependants enclosed within it.

It is inside this *curtis that the medieval courtier was born. Old French cort (later cour) named both the physical residence of a ruler and the circle of people around him, and from this word medieval French and Anglo-French built a complete vocabulary of privilege:

  • courtier / cortier — "one who attends court," the direct ancestor of our word;
  • cortois, cortoise — "of the court," whence our courtly;
  • corteisie — "courtly behaviour," whence our courtesy and the verb to curtsy;
  • the verb courtoyer — to court, to seek the favour of the great.

Middle English borrowed these forms from the thirteenth century onward: cour, court, and, by about 1300, courtier for "one in attendance at a royal court." The institution these words served was the single most powerful engine of European civilization between the twelfth and eighteenth centuries. The court was where lords competed for office, where kings made war and peace, where marriages and alliances were negotiated, where poetry, music, and the visual arts found patrons, and where a new code of conduct — courtesy — was invented as the behavioural signature of the noble class. To be a courtier was to master that code: wit, eloquence, dress, dancing, self-possession, and above all the delicate art of pleasing the ruler without provoking rivals.

By the Renaissance the courtier had become both an ideal and a warning. Baldassare Castiglione's Il Cortegiano (The Book of the Courtier, 1528) codified the perfect gentleman-courtier as the model of sprezzatura — effortless grace, learning, and moral refinement. But the same period produced the satirical counter-image: the courtier as flatterer, parasite, and intriguer, a figure Shakespeare plants onstage in almost every play about kings. The word's double life — glamour on one side, calculation on the other — has lasted to the present day.

In modern English the plural courtiers (the form on this card) is almost always figurative or historical. When C. S. Lewis writes that "the courtiers whispered" around the sick Queen, he uses the word to conjure a world in which power is exercised through proximity and gossip rather than through law — precisely the semantic core the word has carried for eight centuries, and the descendant of a six-thousand-year-old idea of the enclosed yard where the powerful hold court.
