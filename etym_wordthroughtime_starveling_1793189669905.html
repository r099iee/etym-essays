- Old English — the root exists but not the word: steorfan means to die (of anything), and famine deaths are its commonest harvest; no -ling form yet.
- Middle Ages — starve narrows toward hunger: in the cold north, dying and dying-of-hunger blur, and by Chaucer's time sterven already covers death by cold and famine.
- 16th c. — the insult is born: starveling appears as noun and adjective for famished wretches and stunted things; Shakespeare christens a tailor Starveling and fixes the trade joke forever.
- 17th–18th c. — pulpit and pamphlet word: sermons on charity and satires on misers wield starveling to shame the hard-hearted — the starveling accuses the full-fed.
- 19th c. — the novelist's property: Dickens, Gaskell, and famine reportage fill the word with workhouse children, mill girls, and hollow-eyed migrants; pity overtakes contempt.
- Today — archaic but undead: charities say malnourished, but fables, fantasy, and period drama keep starveling for its instant bony picture.
