taxed is the past tense and past participle of the verb to tax, and a participial adjective in its own right. It means: (1) subjected to a compulsory financial levy imposed by a state or other authority; (2) burdened or oppressed by a heavy demand, whether of money, labour, time, or emotion; (3) strained to the limit of one's resources, patience, or credulity. In Wordsworth's Prelude (the source context of this card), the word is used in the second, figurative sense: "Days of sweet leisure, taxed with patient thought / Abstruse" — days of ease are put under the demand of patient, recondite thought; their leisure is charged, almost conscripted, for intellectual labour.

The financial sense is the dominant one in modern English and comprises a rich field of reference:

  • Fiscal imposition: income, property, goods, and transactions "taxed" by central or local government to raise public revenue — "taxed earnings", "taxed imports", "pre-taxed profits".
  • Legal and constitutional: "taxation without representation", the power of the purse, the rules under which a levy is lawful; "the people may not be taxed except by their consent" (parliamentary and revolutionary slogans).
  • Economics: direct vs indirect taxes; incidence theory (who really bears a taxed burden); distortionary effects of taxed markets; "taxed goods" vs subsidies in welfare analysis.
  • Institutional idiom: "death and taxes" as the two certainties (Benjamin Franklin's phrase, 1789); "he was taxed to the hilt" (charged at the maximum rate).

The figurative, non-financial senses — "to tax one's patience", "to tax the memory", "to tax the imagination", "to tax one's strength" — mean to make heavy demands upon, to impose a strain that approaches, or exceeds, capacity. Here the historical bridge is clear: a government taxes a citizen by charging a sum against him; by extension, anything that charges a claim against one's resources taxes them. The word thus travels the path: money → effort → patience → credibility ("his story taxes belief").

A third, now archaic or literary sense survives from the verb's earliest English history: to accuse, censure, or charge with a fault — "he taxed me with ingratitude" (i.e. charged me with it). This is the oldest English sense of taxen (14th c.) and preserves the Latin taxāre = "to censure, to charge (with a crime)".

Modern extensions continue to multiply: in law, "taxed costs" are legal costs assessed by a court officer; in colloquial American English (21st c.), "he got taxed" can mean cheated, overcharged, or robbed; in daily life, "a taxing job", "a taxing journey" has become the standard adjective for anything demanding or draining.

Collocations map the word's range in a moment: "taxed income" (fiscal), "taxed with responsibility" (burden), "tax one's memory" (effort), "taxed beyond endurance" (breaking point), "taxed with ingratitude" (archaic accusation). In every case the underlying image is the same — an external power asserting a claim against what is yours — and the enduring psychological force of the word comes from that fiscal metaphor made general. This is why the past participle works so well as an adjective: it names a completed state (something has been levied, demanded, claimed), and that finished, passive quality is exactly what fits a person describing their own exhaustion: "I'm taxed", "the system is taxed", "resources are heavily taxed".
