morsel is a noun meaning a small piece or amount of food — a mouthful, a bite, a fragment small enough to be taken at once. In the passage the weary rider "ate a morsel and continued his journey": the word pictures a single small, concentrated portion taken from his store of cold chicken, just enough to stay hunger before he rides on southward. The nuance is one of frugality and economy: a morsel is enough to stay hunger but deliberately modest, the smallest portion that can be spared from a traveller's pack.

Primary sense (food): a morsel is the smallest practical unit of eating — a bite-sized piece. The word is delicate, slightly old-fashioned, and warm: it suggests a piece of food chosen for savor rather than bulk, a little something to sustain a man between meals. Set against the grandeur of the mountains that "grow bigger and blacker ahead," the morsel marks the humble human scale of the journey — the rider is a boy with a mouthful of chicken facing a vast landscape.

  • A bite / mouthful: the literal core — "she ate a morsel of bread," "a morsel of cake."
  • A small portion: a scrap or fragment of food as opposed to a full meal.
  • A choice piece: historically a morsel can be a delicacy, a titbit worth savoring — the "choicest morsel" at a feast.

Secondary sense — any small quantity: by extension, morsel is used of any small, valued fragment of a non-material thing: "a morsel of truth," "a morsel of comfort," "a morsel of news." The bridge is the same as with crumbs and shreds: the word for a tiny portion of food becomes the word for a tiny portion of anything worth having. In this extension smallness is joined to value — a morsel of truth is a rare, valuable scrap, never refuse.

Tertiary usages and idioms:

  • Not a morsel: an emphatic idiom meaning "nothing at all" — "there wasn't a morsel of truth in it," "I haven't eaten a morsel."
  • A morsel of truth: a tiny but real element of fact or honesty inside a larger claim — the standard figurative collocation.
  • A morsel of news / gossip: a small, choice item of information.
  • Diminutive charm: the very shape of the word — a "little bite" — lends it a fond, intimate register; it is the word of fairy tales, old novels, and nursery stories.

Register and nuance: morsel is slightly archaic and refined. Everyday modern speech prefers "a bite," "a snack," or "a piece." Morsel survives as the warm, elegant word for a small satisfying portion, and its figurative uses keep it current in writing and idiom. It always implies smallness plus desirability — a morsel is never waste or refuse; it is a little thing worth having. In cooking and recipe prose it still names a tasty small piece; in narrative it is the word of the thrifty, the hungry, and the careful.

Historical semantic bridge: the word descends from Latin morsus, "a bite" (from the verb mordere, "to bite"), through Old French morsel, "a small bite." The sense history is one of progressive narrowing and then widening: "a bite" (the act and its result) → "a small piece taken by biting" → "any small portion of food" → (figurative) "any small quantity of something desirable." The diminutive suffix (Latin -ellum, French -el) carried the tenderness of "a little bite" from the very beginning, and that fondness has never left the word — it is a bite made precious by being tiny.
