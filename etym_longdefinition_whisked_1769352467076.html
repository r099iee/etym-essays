whisked (past tense and past participle of the verb "to whisk") — in its primary sense it means taken, moved, carried, or swept in a particular direction suddenly and quickly, with a light, smooth, almost effortless motion. The word implies speed without violence: a person, object, or substance is "whisked away," "whisked off," or "whisked up" in an instant, the way a brush of twigs flicks dust from a table. In the example context of this card, "The children were whisked away to the safety of the attic," the word conveys swift, protective removal — someone is snatched out of danger with a single quick, decisive motion, the past participle capturing both the suddenness of the act and its completion.

Primary senses
  • To move or carry off quickly — "she whisked the child out of harm's way", "he was whisked off to a secret location": sudden, smooth, frictionless relocation, often by another agent.
  • To sweep or brush lightly — "she whisked the crumbs off the table": a quick, light brushing motion, the verb's most concrete and original sense.
  • Culinary: to beat rapidly with a whisk — "whisk the eggs until frothy", "whisked cream": to agitate eggs, cream, or sauces rapidly with a wire whisk to incorporate air, the central kitchen sense since the 18th century.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings
  • The noun "whisk" — a small brush or broom of twigs or bristles (the original object), and, from the 18th century, the wire kitchen utensil used to beat and aerate food.
  • Whiskers — the long hairs growing on a cat's, dog's, or rodent's muzzle (and, humorously, a man's facial hair), so called from the 17th century because they "whisk" or brush against things.
  • Figurative removal — "whisked away / off / up": snatched, carried, or removed suddenly and effortlessly, with a hint of drama, urgency, or magic ("whisked away in the night").
  • Sporting strokes — in cricket and other bat-and-ball games, a "whisk" is a quick, light stroke or flick of the bat; in billiards a quick stroke is also called a whisk.
  • Engineering and materials — a "whisker" is a fine, hair-like filament or crystal (as in the growth of tiny metal crystals); "whisker growth" is a phenomenon in electronics where tiny metal threads grow from a surface.
  • Botany — "whisker" is used of bristle-like appendages on some plants; a whisker is also a piece of straw or twig used as a brush.
  • Idioms and compounds — "to whisk away tears" (to wipe quickly), "whisk broom" (a small hand broom), "whisker-thin" (a margin as fine as a whisker).

Historical semantic bridges
  • The bridge from brush to speed is physical: a bundle of twigs moves in a quick, flicking sweep, so the verb that named the motion of the brush named quick motion of any kind.
  • The bridge from sweeping to removal is metonymy: to whisk is to brush something away, hence to carry something away quickly and lightly — "whisked off" to safety.
  • The bridge from brush to kitchen tool is functional: the wire whisk of the 18th century is a metal version of the twig bundle, used with the same rapid beating motion.
  • The bridge from sweeping to whiskers is observational: cat and dog muzzle hairs brush against everything they pass, so they were named "whiskers" — "the things that whisk."
  • The bridge to the participle "whisked" is grammatical: as a past participle it became an adjective of state and a component of narrative phrases ("whisked away"), where its swift, smooth character suits scenes of sudden removal.

Modern technical and professional usage
  • Gastronomy — "whisking" is a standard technique in baking and sauce-making (whisked egg whites, meringue, whipped cream); a balloon whisk and a flat whisk are distinct professional tools.
  • Sports commentary — "a whisk to the boundary" (cricket) and similar quick-stroke uses are common in sporting prose.
  • Electronics — "tin whiskers" and "whisker growth" are a known reliability concern in component manufacturing.
  • Narrative fiction — "whisked away/off" is a stock phrase for sudden, smooth, often magical or clandestine relocation.

Boundaries against near-synonyms
  • Unlike sweep, which is a broad, continuous, deliberate clearing motion, whisk is quick, light, and flick-like.
  • Unlike snatch, which implies grasping by hand, whisked implies smooth carrying or moving without violence.
  • Unlike stir (slow, circular mixing), whisking is fast, light, and designed to aerate.
  • Unlike rushed, which emphasises urgency and effort, whisked emphasises smoothness, lightness, and effortlessness.
