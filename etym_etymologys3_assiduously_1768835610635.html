The biography of assiduously begins with a posture. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *sed-, "to sit, to settle down," one of the most securely reconstructed roots in the entire family and one of the most conservative: its reflexes still mean "sit" in languages separated by thousands of years. In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit sīdati, "he sits down," and sadas, "seat, assembly"; the philosophical texts called upaniṣad are literally the "sitting down near" — the disciple's posture at the teacher's feet; and nīḍa, "nest," the "sitting-down place" built from *ni- "down" + *sed-, shows the root constructing places of rest, matched in Avestan by hadiš, "seat, dwelling." Greek hézomai, "I sit," and hédra, "seat," carried the root into kathedra, the bishop's chair, and hence cathedral — the very word for a church built from a seat. The Germanic branch produced Old English sittan (our sit), setl (our settle and settlement), the saddle (literally "a seat for riding"), and, via Old Norse sæti, the very word seat; even nest is the "down-sitting" place, with its Latin twin nīdus. Balto-Slavic keeps the root in Czech sedět and Russian sidetʹ. From India to Ireland, the family never forgot what the root meant. The decisive event for our word occurred in Latin. From *sed- the Romans inherited sedēre, "to sit," and to it they attached the prefix ad-, "at, toward," producing assidēre, "to sit down at something" — to seat oneself at a task, a bench, a place of work. From assidēre came the adjective assiduus, "sitting constantly at, persistent, unwearied." Rome turned posture into character: the man assiduus remained seated at his post when others rose, and the noun assiduitās, "constancy, perseverance," became a civic virtue, praised in officials, in jurists, and in the assiduous farmer who stayed by his field through drought and rain. The same Latin root threw off other shoots that map its whole range: insidēre, "to sit in wait," gave insidiae, "ambush," and hence English insidious — the sinister cousin of the family; sedīmentum, "that which settles," gave sediment; sessiō, "a sitting," gave session and sedentary; assess, "to sit beside a magistrate and fix a tax," built assessment; and subsidium, "troops seated in reserve," gave subsidy. English entered this world late but completely. The adjective assiduous was borrowed in the early seventeenth century (c. 1620) directly from Latin, at first a formal, learned word for "constant in application"; the adverb assiduously, formed with the native suffix -ly, followed within a few decades and carried the same weight. From the start the adverb meant more than "diligently": it meant diligently in the manner of one who sits by the work — the constancy itself doing the describing. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word settled into earnest, respectable use, the vocabulary of faithful attendance on duty, service, and study; the dictionaries of the period praise it for steady habits rather than brilliance. Through the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the physical image of the seat faded from ordinary awareness — most speakers today could not say why assiduously means what it does — but the meaning held firm: any diligent, unrelenting effort, especially one that implies watchful, anxious faithfulness. It is a small irony, and a felicitous one, that a word born of a sitting posture has become the natural adverb for a care so constant it harries and hounds the thing it tends — the dust given no alternative resting place, the keeper's seat, after three thousand years, still occupied. The cultural trajectory is complete: an ancient verb of the body, a Roman moral virtue, a seventeenth-century English borrowing, a modern adverb of obsessive diligence — and in Peake's sentence, the seated constancy of a curator who has made the carvings his lifelong post.
