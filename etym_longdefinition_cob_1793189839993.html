Cob is one of English's great lump-words: compact, blunt, and round in sound as in sense. Its dominant modern meanings cluster around two poles — the horse and the lump — and both poles were already visible in Middle English. In the stable-yard a cob is a short-legged, thick-set, sturdy horse, built for comfort and work rather than speed; in the field and kitchen a cob is any rounded mass — a corncob, a cob of coal, a cob loaf, a cobnut, a cobble-stone's big brother.

In the source sentence the word names the horse: ‘How now, thou beggarly cob!’ Paired with beggarly, it completes the fable's double insult — a low, scrubby, thick-legged nag in poor condition, the equine equivalent of calling a man a scrub. The speaker affects the disgust of a blood-horse for a cart-horse: short in the leg, long in the back, good for nothing but jeers. The comedy, as throughout the fable, is that the judges are no beauties themselves.

Core meaning in detail: The horseman's cob stands roughly 14 to 15 hands high — between pony and full horse — deep-chested, strong-boned, with a steady temperament: the traditional mount of the country parson, the elderly squire, and the hunting-field follower who values safety over style. Show cobs are still exhibited with hogged manes and powerful quarters. The lump-sense is equally alive: a corncob is the cylindrical core left when the kernels are stripped; a cob loaf is a round crusty cottage loaf; cobnuts are round hazel-nuts; cobbles are rounded paving lumps. Both poles share one image — the compact rounded mass — and Middle English cobbe (‘lump, rounded head, chief’) is the mother of them all.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A short stout horse for riding (primary animate sense, 16th c. onward): ‘a sturdy cob’, ‘a show cob’. The bridge runs from lump to horse: a cob is a lump of a horse — short, thick, round-barrelled — and the stable-yard named the shape, not the pedigree. This is the fable's sense.
  • A rounded lump or mass of anything (primary inanimate sense, Middle English onward): ‘a cob of coal’. The bridge is the original cobbe-sense itself: head-shaped lumps of earth, bread, stone, or coal — anything fist- or head-sized and round.
  • A corncob — the stripped ear of maize (American, 18th c.): ‘corn on the cob’. The bridge is shape pure and simple: the bare cylindrical core is a textbook cob-lump, and the phrase keeps the word on dinner tables worldwide.
  • A cob loaf, cobnut, cobble (compounds, long-standing): round bread, round nuts, round stones. The bridge in each is roundness: bakers, orchardmen, and pavers all reached for the lump-word when the product came out round.
  • A male swan — a cob-swan (ornithological, 17th c.): the pen is the female, the cob the male, named for the knob (the cob-lump) on his beak. The bridge is delightfully literal: the lump on the bill names the bird, as a crown names a king.
  • A chief, leader, head-man (archaic, Middle English): ‘the cob of the county’. The bridge runs from cobbe as ‘head, top’ — the lump at the top of the body politic. The cob as spider (in cobweb) is a different Old English word (coppe/attercoppe) and no kin at all — a famous false friend the dictionary must fence off.

Register and connotation: the horse-cob is affectionate and workaday — the honest tradesman of the stable, praised for sense and stamina, never for glamour. To call a horse a cob is usually to praise its reliability; only with beggarly in front does it become abuse. The lump-cob is dialect-warm: cob loaves and cobnuts belong to farmhouse kitchens and country inns. Both senses share bluntness — cob sounds like what it means, a monosyllable you could throw.
