Woolly means covered with, consisting of, or resembling wool — soft-fibered, fleecy, or densely hairy; by extension, figuratively fuzzy, vague, or unclear in thought. In The Hobbit, on the quiet morning at Bag End, Bilbo Baggins stands "smoking an enormous long wooden pipe that reached nearly down to his woolly toes (neatly brushed)." The adjective is affectionate physical description of hobbit feet — naturally curly-haired, kept neat, comic and endearing rather than merely zoological. The tone is cozy, tactile, slightly mock-heroic inventory of the body; "neatly brushed" undercuts any roughness and confirms hobbit respectability. The context selects the literal fleece-like sense applied to toes, not the figurative "woolly thinking," and the gloss must keep the two senses in view while making clear that this passage uses the literal one. The word is a transparent native formation: the ancient noun wool plus the ordinary English adjective suffix -y, meaning "full of, like, characterized by." It is one of the oldest kinds of English word-building, joining two inherited Germanic pieces, and its literal sense has been completely stable: made of wool, covered with wool, or soft and curly like wool. The figurative sense is a transfer of texture to thought: mental content described as "woolly" is vague, confused, unclear, as if wrapped in obscuring fluff — the same metaphor that runs through fuzzy, fluffy, and woolly itself.
  • Literal sense: made of wool, consisting of wool, covered with wool ("a woolly coat," "woolly socks").
  • Resemblance sense: soft, curly, fleece-like in texture, whether or not actually wool ("woolly hair," "woolly toes").
  • Figurative sense (thought): vague, confused, unclear, ill-defined ("woolly thinking," "a woolly argument," "woolly ideas").
  • Figurative sense (style): fuzzy or imprecise in statement, avoiding sharp definition ("woolly language," "woolly promises").
  • Biological / technical sense: hairy, fleece-like plant and animal forms — woolly bear caterpillar, woolly mammoth, woolly aphid, woolly apple aphid, woolly willow — where the word is literal again, describing surfaces of fine fibers.
  • Clothing / textile uses: woolly (noun, chiefly British) a knitted garment, usually in the plural "woollies" (warm woolen underwear or sweaters).
Polysemy and the historical bridge: the word entered English in its literal sense in the early modern period, and every later sense is a transparent extension. The resemblance sense is built into the suffix itself — woolly means "like wool" as much as "made of wool" — so the leap from sheep's fleece to any soft curly surface (a caterpillar, a mammoth, a hobbit's foot) is immediate. The figurative sense, recorded from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, transfers the soft, loosely structured, obscuring texture of wool to the contents of the mind: what is woolly in thought is soft-edged, cloudy, hard to grasp. The biological and technical uses keep the word literal but widen its subject from the sheep to the whole natural world, and the nominal use in clothing ("woollies") completes the circle by returning the adjective to the garment. All the senses share the same image: fine, soft, curly fibers that cover a surface — whether the surface is an animal, a plant, or a proposition. Register: woolly spans the whole scale. It is the humble word for a lamb's coat, the affectionate word for a hobbit's foot-hair, the contemptuous word for a politician's vague promises, and the precise descriptive word in a naturalist's field guide. Differentiate: hairy is general and less soft; furry suggests animal pelt; fleecy is close but more sheep-specific or cloud-like; downy is finer and lighter; fuzzy can be either tactile or mental but centres on blur rather than fleece. Woolly keeps the wool-resemblance — soft curls of fiber — and in the passage from The Hobbit that is exactly the sense required for the neat, curly-haired toes of a respectable Baggins.
