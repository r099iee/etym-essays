The chain begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *dyeu-, “to shine”, the luminous, lit-up dome of the day-sky. From this root the early language-family forged *deywós, “a shining one, a celestial being, a god”, and every major branch kept a reflex: Sanskrit devá- (देव), Greek Zeus (from *dyēus), Latin deus and dīvus, Proto-Germanic *Tīwaz. In Vedic India devá is the standard word for god—the “shining ones”—and Dyāus pitā́ (Sky-Father) mirrors Latin Iūp(p)iter and Greek Zeus patēr: the same PIE divine title, whose weekday reflexes survive in English Tuesday (Tiw’s day) and German Dienstag (from Old High German Ziestag). The root’s core sense, “to shine”, is preserved almost transparently—the gods are literally the bright sky, and the divine is, at root, the luminous. The Romance path to our word is the decisive one. Latin dīvus, “divine, deified”, yields the adjective dīvīnus, “pertaining to the gods”, and from the adjective grows the verb dīvīnāre: to declare by divine power, to prophesy, to foreshadow. This is the crux of the biography—the semantic shift from “of a god” to “to act like a god” to “to foresee”. The Roman state practised divination officially through the augurs, whose reading of omens determined the timing of war, elections, and public business, so the verb carried real civic weight. Dīvīnāre then enters Gallo-Romance as Old French deviner, “to guess, foretell”, which English adopts in the fourteenth century as the verb divinen; the participial form divined is attested from Middle English onward. On the Romance periphery the same verb wore down differently: Late Latin indīvīnāre and addīvīnāre give Italian indovinare and Spanish adivinar, both meaning simply “to guess”—a perfect illustration of how far the sacred could erode into the everyday. English then runs the same downhill course. In its early life to divine meant to foretell or reveal by supernatural means, the act of a seer or a god; by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it had widened to “to discover by a penetrating, divinely aided insight”, and finally to “to guess correctly by intuition”—the sense at work when Dai-yu “at once divined” what Bao-yu was asking. The professional vocabulary survives around it: divination (the art), diviner (the practitioner), divining-rod (the dowser’s instrument), and the verb’s many Romance cousins meaning plain “guess”. The word thus carries, even in its most secular modern use, a ghost of the sacred: to divine is to know as a god knows, suddenly and without evidence—which is exactly the quasi-oracular quality Hawkes gives to Dai-yu’s instant reading of Bao-yu’s heart. Sound, meaning, and culture:
  • Sound change: PIE *dyeu- → *deywós → Latin dīvus (with the archaic dīv-/deu- alternation; *-u- vocalism, loss of the semivowel in dīv-) → dīvīnus (adjectival -īnus) → dīvīnāre (first-conjugation denominative) → Old French deviner (vowel lowering, loss of Latin length) → Middle English divinen → modern divined.
  • Semantic drift: “bright sky” → “shining one, god” → “divine, of the gods” → “to act as a god, to prophesy” → “to foresee, foretell” → “to discover by intuition” → “to guess correctly”. The curve is a steady descent from sacred to secular, most complete in the Romance verbs (Italian indovinare, Spanish adivinar, “to guess”).
  • Socio-cultural factors: Roman augury and state divination gave the verb civic authority; Christianity kept the adjective divine noble in the theological register (divine providence, the Divine Comedy) while the verb eroded in the vernacular; the post-Enlightenment “disenchantment” of the world secularised divination into intuition, so that by the modern period the verb means a psychological flash rather than a supernatural revelation.
  • Literary attestations: Plautus and Cicero for dīvīnāre; Old French deviner in the medieval romances; Middle English divinen in religious prose (Wyclif); Shakespeare’s divine wordplay; and Hawkes’s The Story of the Stone, where Dai-yu “at once divined” Bao-yu’s meaning—the modern, secularised sense still tinged with its old oracular magic.
