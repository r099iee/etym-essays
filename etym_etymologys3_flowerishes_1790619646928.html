The spelling Flowerishes is not a typo but a revealing archaism that lays bare the whole history of flourish: in Middle English flour, flor, flower and flourish were not yet cleanly separated, because both descend from the same Latin flower-field, and Burke's respelling reactivates that unity for a book about print as image.

The deep root is reconstructed PIE *bʰleh₃- meaning to blossom or thrive, which yields throughout Italic and Celtic a family of bloom words. In Latin this becomes flos, floris — flower — and the verb florere, florui — to bloom, to be in prime, to prosper. Already in Cicero and Ovid the botanical sense extends metaphorically: florere describes eloquence flourishing, youth flourishing, states flourishing. The noun flores already means anthology pieces, choice ornaments of speech, the origin of our flowers of rhetoric.

  • Latin to Romance transmission: Vulgar and Late Latin remodels florere on inchoative -ire as *florire, whence Old French florir / flourir / fleurir — to flower, to prosper, to whiten with flour — a triple homophony English inherits as flower, flour, flourish all cousins. The ou spelling is Anglo-Norman.
  • Middle English borrowing (c. 1300): flourishen enters via Anglo-French meaning first to bloom and thrive, then by c. 1400 to adorn with flowers of rhetoric and penmanship. Trevisa, Chaucer and Lydgate use it for both gardens and style.
  • Early modern specialization: by the 16th century print culture fixes distinct spellings: flower for blossom, flour for meal, flourish for ornament and brandishing. Handwriting manuals teach flourishes as pen-trials; music books teach flourishes as graces; fencing books teach flourishes as salutes. Shakespeare uses all senses: “they flourish” (prosper), “a flourish of trumpets” (fanfare), flourished letters.
  • Phonetic development: Old French o before r diphthongizes in Anglo-Norman to ou / ow /uː/, then shortens in Modern English to /ʌ/ in flourish /ˈflʌrɪʃ/ by analogy with courage, nourish, while flower retains /aʊ/. Burke's flowerishes therefore reads as etymological, restoring /aʊ/ visually.
  • Semantic drift: from neutral thriving to skilled embellishment to suspicious excess. Eighteenth-century taste condemns “mere flourishes” as Gothic or Baroque superfluity; nineteenth-century typographers purge swashes for clarity; modernists — Mallarmé, Marinetti, Tzara, Burke himself — deliberately revive them as anti-phonetic meaning.

Burke's choice belongs to that revival. By writing flowerishes with w, he insists that typographic experiment is not decadent addition but organic blooming of the letter: print flowering into picture, sound into shape. The variant thus condenses etymology into polemic — to understand media history as horticulture, where alphabetic reason periodically bursts into visual flower.

Attestations trace this arc: Latin flores rhetorici in Quintilian; Old French flourir in Chanson de Roland (c. 1100) for prospering knights; Middle English florysche in scribal colophons; Caxton's 1470s flourished initials; 18th-century writing-masters' copybooks titled Flourishes; Burke's Counter-Statement (1931) and later typographic illustrations. Each stage replays the same metaphor: thriving made visible as curling line.
