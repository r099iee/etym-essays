speculate entered English in the 1590s, borrowed from Latin speculātus, the past participle of speculārī, "to watch, observe, spy out, keep a lookout". The Latin verb is built on specula, "a watchtower or look-out post", itself derived from the ancient verb specere, "to look at, observe", which goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *spek-, "to observe, watch". It is one of the most productive roots in the whole Indo-European family: the same *spek- gave Greek skopós "watcher, target" and skopeō "to examine" (whence -scope in telescope and microscope, and sceptic through skeptomai "to look carefully"), Sanskrit páśyati "he sees", and, through Germanic, the English verb to spy.

The root metaphor: the lookout. The entire career of the word is the career of a single image. A specula was a watchtower on a height, and speculārī was the sentry's work — watching the horizon, scanning the road, spying out what approaches. From this physical watching the Romans extended the verb to intellectual watching: to observe, to keep one's eye on, to examine. The pivotal development happened in Medieval Latin, where speculātiō became the technical term for abstract intellectual contemplation — the mind's eye gazing upon ideas and first principles, the highest activity of the medieval philosopher. When English adopted the verb in the 1590s, it was in this lofty sense: to speculate was to contemplate, to theorise, to range with the mind beyond immediate evidence in the service of knowledge.

Sound and form. Latin speculārī (speh-koo-LAH-ree) lost its final syllables and grammatical inflection in the transfer: English took the participle speculātus as the base and shaped the verb speculate, with the nominal forms speculation, speculative, and speculator following within the same generation. The RP pronunciation /spˈɛkjʊlˌeɪt/ preserves the Latin -u- of specula in the second syllable. The spelling with -ul- is a small monument to the watchtower from which the word came.

Semantic drift and the two great senses.

  • 16th–17th centuries. On entry, speculate means to contemplate and theorise; it is a dignified, philosophical verb, and speculation is high intellectual activity. "Speculative" philosophy is contrasted with practical knowledge but honoured as the pursuit of first causes.
  • 18th century — the financial birth. A second life is born in the money markets of London and Amsterdam. To "speculate" in stocks, goods, or property is to act on conjectures about future prices — to buy in the expectation of a rise, to sell in the expectation of a fall. The connection to the lookout is exact: the speculator watches the market the way the sentry watches the horizon, committing his capital to what he believes he can see coming. The philosophical and the commercial senses now run in parallel, and the word takes on the gambling tint it has never lost.
  • 19th century. In the empiricist climate of the age, the philosophical sense is itself slightly depreciated. Mill uses the verb in the Autobiography for fruitless counterfactual musing — "It is idle to speculate on the possible results of a different treatment" — precisely because such speculation can never be tested against fact. The everyday intellectual sense drifts toward mere guesswork, mildly depreciated beside the grand philosophical meaning, which survives in formal and technical use.
  • 20th–21st centuries. The word is split between intellectual speculate on/about (conjecture, often now mildly dismissive) and financial speculate in (risky trading), with speculator used overwhelmingly of the market operator. Both halves still rest on the ancient image of watching for a sign.

Socio-cultural and literary attestations. The word's history is a small mirror of the modern world: it moves from the watchtower of the Roman sentry, through the contemplative heights of medieval and early-modern philosophy, into the counting-houses and exchanges of the 18th century, and out into the global finance of the present. Its philosophical dignity was celebrated in the English Renaissance — Francis Bacon and his contemporaries use "speculation" for the noblest reach of the mind — and its commercial career was consolidated in the age of the South Sea Bubble, when speculation first became a public vice and a public excitement. In Mill's Autobiography, the word is made to carry the empiricist's verdict on all such ranging beyond the evidence: idle. The whole history is contained in that one judgement.
