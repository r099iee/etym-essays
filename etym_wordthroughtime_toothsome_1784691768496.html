- 16th-century coinage - toothsome is formed on the -some pattern (wholesome, handsome, wearisome) with the plain literal force of 'pleasant to the tooth'; it describes food that invites the bite and the chew
- 17th-18th century - it settles in cookery writing as a hearty, sensual word for rich, substantial fare; a toothsome dish is satisfying rather than merely elegant, the cooking that makes the mouth water
- 19th century - the word carries a slightly old-fashioned, genial warmth and is extended to the odour of food and even jocularly to a person, keeping its appetising, almost indulgent tone
- Contemporary sense - it survives as a faintly literary, nostalgic adjective for delicious food; it has retreated from plain speech but keeps a rich, mouth-watering flavour in any tale of feasting
