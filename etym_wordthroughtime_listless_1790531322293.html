- 14th to 15th Century (Middle English): Coined as *listles* or *lystles* (compounding the noun *list*, meaning pleasure, joy, desire, or appetite, with *-les*, "-less"), originally meaning literally "devoid of joy, lacking desire or appetite for food, pleasure, or activity."
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English): Extended across Elizabethan and Jacobean drama to denote physical melancholy, mental weakness, and languid dejection caused by illness, unrequited love, or sorrow.
- 18th to 19th Century: Elevated into a defining aesthetic and psychological descriptor in romantic and realist literature (e.g., Lord Byron, Jane Austen, the Brontë sisters, and Leo Tolstoy)—characterizing aristocratic ennui, world-weary courtly indifference, and the languor of delicate salon society.
- 20th to 21st Century: Widely adopted into medical and psychiatric diagnostic terminology (describing symptoms of clinical depression, chronic fatigue syndrome, and pediatric viral infections), as well as contemporary prose characterizing stagnant economies, uninspired creative works, and disaffected social attitudes.
