“To creep,” with its strong past “crept,” has meant to move slowly and close to the ground since earliest English — the stealthy, low motion of animals and the cautious advance of people.
“Creep” from the physical gait spread to the figurative: things that “creep” up slowly on you — time, age, prices, a creeping dread — a slow, inexorable approach.
“Creep” in the 19th–20th centuries gave “creepy” and “creep” the sense of something that makes you shiver, and “to creep someone out” became a modern idiom of uneasy discomfort.
“Crept” today still names that slow, low, nervous advance — a hobbit edging toward the parlour door like a trespasser in his own hole.
