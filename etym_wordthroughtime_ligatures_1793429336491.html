- Late 14th c. — Medieval surgery & medical bandaging: borrowed from Old French and Latin into Middle English by medical translators like John Trevisa, describing bandages, surgical splint bindings, and magical charms tied around limbs.
- 15th–16th c. — Operative breakthrough & movable type: Ambroise Paré revolutionizes battlefield surgery by using silk ligatures to tie off bleeding arteries; simultaneously, Gutenberg and early printers coin ligature for fused metal type characters like fi and fl.
- 17th c. — Occult demonology & musical notation: applied in church courts to the malevolent knotting of cords to induce impotence (ligature), while music theorists use it for mensural note brackets.
- 18th c. — Swiftian satire & physical immobility: Jonathan Swift immortalizes ligatures in Gulliver's Travels (1726) as the microscopic cords binding the giant to the earth.
- 19th–20th c. — Forensic pathology & social philosophy: criminal pathologists formalize "ligature strangulation", while sociologists adopt the word for moral ties holding society together.
- Today — Digital font design & precision surgery: omnipresent in modern typography (OpenType ligature code) and advanced surgical vascular clips.
