blazoned — Blazoned means decorated with conspicuous heraldic display: to blazon something is to emblazon it, to paint or adorn it with bold, vivid, formally patterned colours and devices, so that it is proclaimed to the eye like a coat of arms. The verb descends from heraldic usage — to “blazon” a shield is to describe or paint its armorial bearings in the technical language of heraldry — and it retains that formal, ceremonial, emblematic charge: a blazoned object is not merely painted but declared, made to announce identity and status through colour and symbol. In the passage being corrected, the carvings are “blazoned in strange colour”: the humble wooden figures of the outer-quarter carvers, ordinarily plain, are on this ceremonial occasion painted in bold, unusual hues in a highly stylised manner peculiar to themselves. The word performs a double elevation: it lifts the peasant carvings into the vocabulary of heraldry and ritual display, while the “strange colour” keeps them estranged — the blazonry is not courtly and proper but alien, tribal, idiosyncratic. The carvings do not simply carry paint; they proclaim, in a language of colour that no one else speaks.
  • Heraldic primary sense: to blazon a coat of arms is to describe it in the formal, coded language of heraldry (tinctures, ordinaries, charges), or to paint it upon a shield or banner; “blazoned” = emblazoned, set out as an armorial device.
  • Extended public sense: from painting on shields the word broadened (16th–17th c.) to mean publishing or proclaiming anything abroad — to “blazon one’s fame,” to trumpet a name or deed far and wide.
  • Vivid adornment sense: to decorate any object with bold, conspicuous, formally patterned colour so that it announces itself like a banner or a coat of arms — a sky “blazoned with stars,” a banner “blazoned with gold.”
  • Modern register: in everyday speech the verb has receded; today blazoned survives as a ceremonial, archaic-flavoured synonym of emblazoned, used when an object is made to proclaim identity and status through colour and symbol.
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: decorated is neutral and general and would flatten the sentence completely; emblazoned is the closest synonym and stresses the bright, displayed character of the adornment; embellished suggests added beauty rather than declaration; adorned suggests ornament worn or worn upon a surface.
In the passage, the choice of blazoned matters because it imports the whole institution of heraldry — of emblems, identity, and proclamation — into a context where the makers are peasants and the language of colour is their own. The carvings are raised to the dignity of banners and standards, and at the same time isolated: their blazonry is “strange,” a private heraldry that no outside eye can read. The word dignifies the objects at the very moment it estranges them, and that double movement — elevation and isolation — is the heart of the sentence.
