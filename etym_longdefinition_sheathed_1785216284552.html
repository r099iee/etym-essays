SHEATHED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to sheathe: to put (a blade) into its sheath or scabbard; to enclose, encase, or cover something in a protective casing; to hide something away inside a covering. The word names both the completed act — sliding the sword home into its scabbard — and the resulting state: a sheathed sword is covered, dormant, enclosed, harmless to all but the one who draws it again. From its martial core the verb has generalised to any close-fitting protective covering, so that cables are sheathed in rubber, hillsides in ice, and a tendon in its sheath.

In The Hobbit, the word carries a double weight of stealth. Bilbo, risen painfully from his fall, "sheathed his sword, which was now glowing faintly again." The little Elvish blade Sting has just saved him — it is glowing, bright, a betrayer in the darkness of the goblin tunnels. Sheathing it is therefore not merely putting a weapon away; it is an act of deliberate concealment, choosing darkness over light in order to follow Gollum unseen. The participle also names the resulting state: the sword is covered, dormant, harmless — its faint light hidden until the moment it is drawn again. The word thus holds together the soldier's careful precision and the spy's quiet caution: sheathing the sword is peace, control, and stored menace at once.

The word's senses and family:

  • Martial (primary): to put a sword, dagger, or blade into its sheath or scabbard — the soldier's and knight's gesture of ending combat. "Sheathe the sword" means to cease fighting, to lay aside hostility, to make peace.
  • Of the resulting state: a "sheathed" blade is covered, dormant, concealed — not in use, and not a threat, though the threat is stored inside.
  • Protective casing (modern extension): to enclose or cover something in a close-fitting protective material — a cable sheathed in rubber or metal, a wire sheathed in plastic, a branch sheathed in ice, a hillside sheathed in snow.
  • Anatomy and biology: a sheath is the protective tissue layer enclosing a structure — the myelin sheath wrapping nerve axons, the sheath of a tendon or muscle; "sheathed" describes the enclosed structure.
  • Figurative: "sheathed" can describe anything hidden, dormant, or covered — anger sheathed in calm, a cat's claws sheathed in velvet (retracted, hidden, non-threatening).
  • Related noun — sheath: any close-fitting cover; a blade's case; a protective casing; a plant's leaf-base. The general word from which the verb is built.
  • Opposites: to draw, to unsheathe — taking the blade out, the moment of menace restored.

The word is native English. It descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *skei-, "to cut, split, separate," through Proto-Germanic *skaid-, "to separate, divide": the blade-cover was conceived as "that which keeps apart" — the piece that separates the sharp steel from the flesh of the wearer. Old English scēaþ meant "scabbard, sheath," Middle English shethe carried it down, and around the fourteenth century English converted the noun into the verb sheathe. Its past participle, sheathed, has named ever since both the act of covering the blade and the state of being safely enclosed — from the scabbard of the knight to the myelin sheath of the nerve.
