- [Antiquity (c. 8th c. BCE–4th c. CE)] - Ancient Greek korállion and Latin corallium denote precious red marine growth harvested from the Mediterranean, explained through the myth of Medusa's petrified blood - [perception: sacred talisman, medicinal amulet, exotic mineralized wonder of the deep]
- [Middle Ages (12th–14th c.)] - enters Old French as coral and Middle English as coralle; fashioned into rosaries, beads, and amulets for infants to ward off demonic infection and the evil eye - [perception: holy, protective, aristocratic luxury material]
- [16th–17th c.] - Renaissance Petrarchan poetry and blazons establish coral as the supreme poetic standard for red lips (Shakespeare's Sonnet 130: "Coral is far more red than her lips' red"); marine naturalists debate whether it is a plant, rock, or animal - [perception: romantic archetype of beauty and philosophical natural mystery]
- [18th–19th c.] - naturalists (Peyssonnel, Linnaeus) prove coral is an animal colony; Victorian Aestheticism (Wilde) and maritime exploration celebrate coral caverns, sea fans, and South Sea atolls - [perception: opulent, exotic, romantic marine architecture]
- [20th–21st c.] - shifts from ornamental luxury to an urgent ecological symbol of climate change, marine biodiversity, and reef bleaching; standard cosmetic and design color term - [perception: fragile ecological treasure, vibrant design aesthetic]
