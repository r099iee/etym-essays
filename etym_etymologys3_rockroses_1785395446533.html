=== ESSAY 1 — THE FIRST ELEMENT: ROCK === Rockrose is a purely English coinage of the late sixteenth century, and neither element carries a secure Indo-European pedigree: the word is a compound of two loanwords, joined in English and applied to the shrubs of the genus Cistus that grow among rocks and bear rose-like blossoms. The compound rock + rose is first recorded in 1578, and it has stayed a plain descriptive name ever since — a rose of the rocks, named for what it is and where it grows. The first element, rock, is not the old Germanic word that one might expect. The native Germanic *rukkaz meant "distaff" — the stick on which wool is spun — and is unrelated to the stone-word; the two are a classic case of accidental homophony in English. The rock that names our stones, cliffs, and this plant is instead a loan from Old North French roque, which passed into Old French as roche, "rock, cliff, crag", and into Anglo-Norman English as rokke, recorded from about 1300. Behind these French forms lies Medieval Latin rocca, first attested in the eighth century, "rock, mass of stone", a word whose own origin is unknown: it is generally taken to be a pre-Roman substrate term, possibly Gaulish or Celtic, perhaps Italic, and no accepted Proto-Indo-European reconstruction exists for it. The Romans had their own words — petra, from Greek, and rūpes, the native "cliff" — but the Romance languages overwhelmingly kept the substrate word for the common sense of "rock": Italian rocca (which also developed the sense "fortress on a rock", giving roccaforte "stronghold"), Spanish roca, Portuguese roca, Occitan roca, and the French roche/roche that English borrowed. English took its rock from the northern French arm of this family, and only in 1578 did the word pair with rose to name the Cistus. The cultural history of the rock-word in English is itself worth a paragraph. When rokke entered Middle English around 1300, it arrived in a language that already had the native words stone and cliff, and for two centuries it competed with them, gradually taking over the general sense of a large mass of stone while stone kept its smaller, more material uses. By the early modern period rock was fully naturalised — in the Bible translations, in the mariners' language of coasts and reefs, in the proverb "the rock of ages", and in the great geological sense that the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries built on it. The word's substrate origin is the reason English shares it so easily across the Romance and Germanic frontiers: it is part of the oldest European stratum of the vocabulary, older than Latin, Greek, or Germanic in the West, a relic of the languages spoken in Gaul and Iberia before the Indo-Europeans arrived. When Shakespeare wrote "the multitudinous seas incarnadine" he could rely on rock being already as English as stone; and when the Elizabethan botanists coined rock-rose, they could rely on it being understood by every gardener in the land. It is this very transparency that makes the plant-name so successful. A rose that grows among rocks — the compound says everything in two syllables: habitat (rock) and appearance (rose). The Elizabethan botanists who first recorded the word (1578) were doing folk-botany in English rather than Latin taxonomy, and the name stuck because it was true to the plant: Cistus and Helianthemum genuinely root in rock crevices and poor stony soils, and their flowers genuinely resemble single wild roses. The plural form rockroses, the shape of this note, is the ordinary English inflection of the compound, used whenever the word denotes more than one shrub or the group as a whole. In the passage from The Hobbit, the plural gathers the whole family of yellow-flowered rock-dwellers into the catalogue of the vanishing hillside, and the word's own etymology — a rose of the rocks — does the same work as Tolkien's landscape: it binds flower and stone together, so that when the landslide turns the slope to bare rubble, the destruction is felt in the very naming of the plant. === ESSAY 2 — THE SECOND ELEMENT: ROSE === The second element of rockrose is older, more famous, and equally foreign to Indo-European. Greek ῥόδον (rhódon), "rose", is attested already in Sappho, writing about 600 BCE, and its etymology has never been securely found within the inherited vocabulary of Greek. The ancient grammarians, confronted with a word that resisted their own derivations, called it Phrygian — the label of a borrowing from a neighbouring non-Greek people, a confession of uncertainty as much as an attribution. Modern scholarship has generally agreed that the word is indeed foreign to Greek, but the source is still disputed between two camps: an Old Iranian origin, argued on the strength of the Avestan varǝδa- "rose" and the Armenian vard "rose" (an Iranian loan), and a Semitic origin, argued on the Aramaic wrd' and the Akkadian wurtinnu. Either way the rose itself, and with it the word, travelled westward into Greek and then Latin — Latin borrowed the flower along with its name as rosa, probably through a South Italian or Greek dialect — and from Latin the word spread into every Romance language: Italian rosa, Spanish rosa, French rose, Portuguese rosa. The rose is thus one of the few great cultural words that enter the European vocabulary already marked as foreign at the very beginning of its attested history, a botanical and linguistic gift of the Near East to the Aegean world. Old English took rōse directly from Latin rosa in the ninth century, and the word has never left the language since. In the Anglo-Saxon period the rose was already the flower of the Virgin and of royalty, the garden rose of Mediterranean cultivation naturalised in the north; Old English poetry uses rōse in the liturgical and courtly senses carried over from Latin Christianity. Middle English rose broadened the word into the full medieval symbol-complex — the rose of courtly love, the white and red roses of the civil wars, the rose-window, the rosary, the many rosy metaphors of beauty — and by the early modern period the word was as central to English as any flower-word in the language. When the Elizabethan botanists formed rock-rose in 1578, they were adding one more transparent compound to an already enormous family: rose-bay, rosebud, rose-water, rose-garden, all built on the same two-hundred-year-old native word. The decisive point for the plant-name is that the rose element in rockrose is a simile made into a noun. The rockrose is not a rose: Rosa belongs to the family Rosaceae, while Cistus and Helianthemum are in the Cistaceae, and the two are related only distantly within the broad flowering-plant tree. The likeness that the name records is purely visual — the single, five-petalled, papery flower with its central boss of stamens, opening flat like a wild rose — and the compound enshrines exactly the comparison a sixteenth-century gardener would have made. That folk-botany has proved so durable that modern scientific names echo it in reverse: the Cistaceae themselves are still called "the rock-rose family", and the genus Helianthemum is known in horticulture as both sunrose and rockrose. The word rockrose therefore sits at the meeting point of two ancient etymological currents: a Mediterranean substrate word for the stony ground (rock) and a Near Eastern loan that named the rose and its whole symbolic world (rose), joined in English in 1578 and now frozen in the plural rockroses — a rose that grows among rocks, in a language where neither half of the compound is native.
