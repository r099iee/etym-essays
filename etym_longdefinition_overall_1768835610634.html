overall (noun): a loose protective garment worn over ordinary clothing — in British usage characteristically a one-piece working suit covering the trunk and limbs, in American usage more often heavy bib-and-brace trousers (overalls). In this second appearance in Titus Groan, the overall is not on Rottcodd's body but in his inventory: it is kept “ready for use” in the small dark ante-room beyond the door of the Hall, in the company of a stock of white candles, a huge visitors' book white with dust, and a stepladder. Peake's catalogue is a still-life of office: the candle-stock, the book, the ladder, and the overall are the entire equipment of a kingdom that consists of one man and seven candelabra. The placement is telling — the overall hangs beside the tools of ascent and of record, a piece of professional furniture rather than an item of dress; it belongs to the same world of ritual readiness as the dust-covered visitors' book that presumably no visitor has ever signed. The word thus carries its full sartorial meaning — a protective shell over the private self — and gains a second nuance: it is the instrument of transformation. Rottcodd sleeps in a hammock at the window end, a man almost fused with the loft; when he envelopes himself in the overall he becomes the custodian, the functionary, the duster of carvings. The overall is the hinge between the sleeping man and the ritual officer, kept ready so that the metamorphosis may be instantaneous. Its tone is utilitarian dignity: humble clothing ennobled by its role in a liturgy of dust.

Beyond the garment, overall is one of the most polysemous words in modern English, and the noun is only the youngest of its senses. The full range:
  • Adjective (the dominant modern use): “total, including everything; general, not particularised” — overall length, overall impression, overall winner, overall responsibility; the word of wholesale assessment in management, statistics, and sport.
  • Adverb (the earliest sense, 14th century): “over the whole extent; everywhere; altogether” — overall, the work is sound; the original spatial force of the Middle English fused phrase.
  • Noun (British): the one-piece protective working suit, the livery of the labourer and the servant; also any loose protective outer garment worn over one's clothes.
  • Noun (American): heavy bib-and-brace trousers, overalls, worn by farmers, railwaymen, and builders; the plural overalls can also mean the full one-piece suit (American coveralls).
  • Technical extensions: “overall dimension” in engineering (the extreme enclosing measurement), “overall rating” in finance and product reviews, “overall winner” in multi-stage sport — the adjective does the work of the comprehensive verdict wherever a whole must be held in a single glance.

The historical bridge is elegant: the Old English phrase ofer eall, “over all, across the whole extent”, fused in the 14th century into the adverb meaning “everywhere, altogether”; by the 16th century it was the adjective of total summary; and in 1759 American usage coined the noun over-alls for bib-and-brace protective trousers, re-materialising the abstract word of totality as workingman's cloth. The garment is literally the one worn “over all” the other clothes — the last layer enclosing the whole person, exactly as the adjective encloses a whole subject. In Peake's inventory the word performs both halves at once: humble cloth kept ready to transform the sleeping man in the hammock into the ritual custodian of the carvings — clothing, and completeness, in a single ready-to-wear word.
