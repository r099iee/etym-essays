The word middling is an authentic Germanic formation rooted in the deepest lexical stratum of the Indo-European language family, constructed through sequential nominal and adjectival suffixation to designate spatial centrality, social stratification, and qualitative moderation over more than a thousand years.

1. Proto-Indo-European and Germanic Foundations:

  • The ultimate root is the Proto-Indo-European adjective *médʰyos, meaning "middle, central, intermediate, in-between." This root is exceptionally fertile across Indo-European daughter branches, yielding Sanskrit madhya ("middle"), Ancient Greek mésos ("middle," as in Mesopotamia and Mesozoic), and Latin medius ("middle," the source of English medium, median, mediate, and medieval).
  • In Proto-Germanic, *médʰyos developed into the adjective *midjaz ("mid, middle"). In Old English (c. 8th c. CE), this produced the primary adjective midd ("middle, midway") and the suffixed diminutive/instrumental noun middel, designating the physical center, waist, or middle section of a body or object.

2. Middle English Suffixation and Semantic Expansion:

  • During the 14th century, Middle English speakers attached the diminutive/adjectival suffix -lyng / -ling (derived from Proto-Germanic *-lingaz, denoting association, condition, or direction) to the stem middel, creating midlyng / middelyng.
  • Initially, the word served as a concrete physical and quantitative descriptor for items of intermediate size, weight, or length (such as timber, livestock, cattle, or cloth) that were neither very large nor very small.

3. Early Modern Social and Economic Stratification:

  • In the 16th and 17th centuries (Tudor and Stuart England), middling underwent a major sociological expansion. Social commentators began categorizing English society into three distinct groups: "the better sort" (nobility and gentry), "the middling sort," and "the poorer sort." The middling sort encompassed freeholders, prosperous master craftsmen, burgesses, and overseas traders whose industriousness drove early capitalist expansion.
  • Simultaneously, grain millers and textile merchants adopted the plural substantive middlings to denote medium-grade flour and fabrics, cementing its role in trade and commerce.

4. Aesthetic Critique and Colloquial Idiom:

  • By the 18th century, Enlightenment critics and philosophers (such as Denis Diderot in French aesthetics and Samuel Johnson in English lexicography) applied middling evaluatively to human talent and art. It acquired its characteristic critical sting: describing the vast cohort of unexceptional artists who possess moderate craft but lack divine inspiration.
  • In 19th-century rural England and North America, the phrase fair to middling crystallized as a standard colloquial response regarding one's health or agricultural crop yield. Today, middling remains a vibrant, multi-layered term across economic history, literary criticism, and everyday speech.
