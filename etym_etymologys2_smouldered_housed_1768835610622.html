smouldered — chain with an explicit statement of unknown ultimate origin: - Origin of the ultimate root: UNKNOWN. The OED records no secure Indo-European etymon; the often-cited reconstruction *smel- 'to burn slowly' (Pokorny) is possible but not universally accepted, and the word may be a native Germanic formation on the idea of smoke/smother. No stage before Middle English can be securely reconstructed — this is stated explicitly rather than invented. - Middle English smolder (noun, 14th c., attested) — 'smoky vapour, stifling smoke' (also smother) - Middle English smolderen (verb, c. 1300, attested) — 'to smother, suffocate' (transitive: to kill or choke with smoke) - English smoulder / smolder (intransitive sense 'to burn and smoke without flame', attested from c. 1520) — 'to burn slowly and smokily' - English smoulder (figurative sense 'to exist in a suppressed state; to burn inwardly', attested from 1810) — 'to harbour concealed or latent feeling' - Modern English smouldered (past tense and past participle). Related forms within the family: Old English smorian, 'to suffocate', the ancestor of smother; German schmoren, 'to stew'. The noun-verb relationship (smolder the smoke > smolderen to choke with smoke > smoulder to burn smokily) is the attested core; the ultimate root being unknown, no PIE or even Proto-Germanic stage is asserted here.
housed — single linear chain: - Proto-Germanic *hūsą (c. 500 BCE–1st c. CE, reconstructed) — 'dwelling, shelter' (attested in all Germanic dialects; the ultimate pre-Germanic origin is UNKNOWN — no certain non-Germanic cognate exists, though a connection to PIE *(s)keu- 'to cover, hide' has been suggested and remains uncertain) - Old Norse hús (attested) — 'house'; Old Frisian hūs; Old Saxon hūs; Dutch huis; German Haus - Old English hūs (c. 7th–8th c., attested) — 'dwelling, house, shelter' - Old English hūsian (c. 9th–10th c., attested) — 'to take into a house, to shelter, to lodge, to provide with a dwelling' - Middle English housen (c. 13th c., attested) — 'to shelter, to give a home to, to store' - Modern English house (verb) (16th c.–) — 'to shelter, to contain, to store' - Modern English housed (past tense and past participle). Note: the suggested link to PIE *(s)keu-, 'to cover', which would also connect English hide and hut, is debated; the attested Germanic record alone is secure, and the pre-Germanic stage is unknown and therefore left open.
