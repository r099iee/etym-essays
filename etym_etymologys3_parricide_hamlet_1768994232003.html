parricide — a biography of the violated equal. The word is a Latin compound of two roots. The first is the Indo-European *per-, "to bring forth, allot, produce," which in Latin yields par "equal, matching" — a counterpart, a match — and pariō "to bring forth, give birth," whose present participle parēns gives English parent. The same par underlies peer, the equal, via Old French pair. The second root is the killing verb caedere "to cut, strike, kill," probably from a PIE *kh₂eyd- "to cut," though its deeper cognates are disputed; its Latin combining forms -cīda ("killer") and -cīdium ("the killing") built the whole English -cide family: homicide, suicide, regicide, and parricide itself.

Folk etymology in the spelling. The Romans believed the word was pater + caedere, "father-killing," and the doubled -rr- of parricida seems to be exactly that folk-etymology hardened into spelling. Modern scholarship reads it differently: paricīda, "one who kills an equal," from pār + -cīda — the kin-slayer destroys the person who is his equal in blood, his counterpart in the family. Either way the semantic register is the same: parricide in Roman law was the most heinous of crimes, punished with dreadful severity — the poena cullei, the "punishment of the sack," in which the condemned was sewn into a sack with a dog, a cock, a viper, and an ape and thrown into water — and extended, in the Republic, to traitors against the state as the "fatherland." English takes the word from Old French parricide in the sixteenth century, and it has carried that juridical horror ever since — the crime of destroying one's own origin. The passage's hanged man, "a parricide" displayed at a crossroads, is a deliberate translation of that ancient terror into the frontier world: the gravest taboo, the harshest penalty, the most public death.



hamlet — a biography of the settled home. The root is the Indo-European *tkei-, "to settle, dwell, be at home," a root that Greek kept in ktízō "to found, build, settle," and that Indic may preserve (disputedly) in Sanskrit kṣeti "to dwell." In Germanic it produced *haimaz, "home, dwelling, village," the ancestor of Gothic haims — which still meant "village," not "house" — of Old Norse heimr "home, world," of Old English hām, our home and the -ham of place-names like Nottingham, and of Old High German heim (German Heim).

Through French back into English. The word reached English twice. The native line gave us home and the -ham place-names. The borrowed line went through Frankish and Gallo-Romance: the Germanic *haim was borrowed into French, where it became ham, hameau "small village," and the diminutive hamelet — and it is this French word, in the fourteenth century, that English took as hamlet, "a small village, a cluster of houses without a church." English thus received its own ancestral root back, diminutived and re-borrowed across the Channel. In 1600 the word gained its second, unshakeable life when Shakespeare named his Danish prince Hamlet, from the earlier legend of Amleth — a name traditionally connected with the same "home" root. Since then the common noun has kept its stable meaning — the smallest settlement — while the proper noun has made it one of the most famous names in the language. The passage's "crossroads hamlet" shows the word at its most ordinary: the tiny cluster of dwellings where a criminal is publicly hanged, the smallest stage for the gravest crime.
