chivalry (noun) — the institution, the class and the code of the medieval knight. Historically it denotes the whole complex of knighthood: the body of knights (the chivalry of France), the status and profession of the mounted warrior of the feudal age, and above all the moral and social ideal that grew up around him — courage, honour, loyalty to lord and faith, protection of the weak, and that ritualized courtesy toward women which the age called “service”. The word still lives in two registers: the historian’s term for the medieval institution, and the living idiom of gallantry — doors held, coats offered, quarrels declined — where “chivalry” means courteous, protective attention, and where, in modern idiom, it is often declared “dead”.

Primary sense — the medieval institution. As Pater uses it — the “notions of chivalry and romantic love” that reached Francis of Assisi “from a French source” — the word denotes the elaborated ideal of the warrior aristocracy: the mounted knight (chevalier) bound by oath to his lord, to God and to his lady, schooled in the martial arts and the courtesies, celebrated in the romances of Chrétien de Troyes and the troubadours. Chivalry in this sense is inseparable from feudalism: it presupposes the horse warrior, the fief, the castle, the tournament and the cult of courtly love whose lyric home was precisely France and Provence.

  • Secondary sense — the moral code. By extension, chivalry is the code of knightly virtue: prowess, honour, loyalty, largesse (generosity), franchise (free-heartedness), courtesy and piety. Medieval writers list these as the knight’s virtutes, and the romances dramatize them as tests; the code sets the gentleman’s standard that later centuries secularized into “honour” and “good manners”.
  • Teritary sense — the class. “The chivalry” in historical prose may mean simply the body of knights and men-at-arms — the mounted force of an army or kingdom — and this collective use is the oldest English sense, inherited from Old French chevalerie “body of knights, knighthood”.
  • Quaternary sense — modern courtesy. In present-day speech, chivalry is usually the behaviour of the courteous and protective man; the phrase “chivalry is dead” laments its supposed extinction, while gender commentary inverts or rejects the ideal outright, seeing it as a mask of paternalism. The word thus carries an emotional temperature that the historian’s use does not.
  • Comic and ironic register. “Chivalrous” (16th c., from the same stock) can be used with a smile: a chivalrous offer, a chivalrous delusion. The semantic bridge from the medieval to the modern is the passage from mounted warfare to manners — what the horse and the sword once secured, the open door and the raised hat now signify.

Morphological bridge. The word is a Norman French import: Old French chevalerie from chevalier “knight, horseman” from cheval “horse” from Latin caballus “work-horse, nag” — so that chivalry is, at root, nothing but “horsemanship”, the art of the man who fights from the saddle. The step from caballus to the Round Table is the semantic history of the mounted warrior’s rise from groom and esquire to the flower of the nobility.
