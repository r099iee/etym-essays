The story of brood — and so of its past-tense form brooded — begins in warmth. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *bhre- (sometimes given as *bhreu- or *bher-) carries the meaning “to burn, to heat, to warm”. From this root Germanic built a noun meaning both the heat of incubation and, by extension, the offspring that heat brings into life: Proto-Germanic *brōd-. The core idea at every stage of the word’s early life is not “thought” at all but warmth applied to new life — a hen warming eggs into chicks.

Old English

  • Old English had both the noun brōd (“brood, offspring, the young of animals”) and the verb brēdan (“to hatch; to warm and sit upon eggs”).
  • The two were closely entwined: to breed and to brood were, at the outset, almost the same act — applying protective warmth to the young.
  • This is why “breed” and “brood” are near twins in English to this day: both spring from the same Germanic well of “warmth that produces life”.

Middle English

  • In Middle English the verb settled as broden, meaning “to incubate eggs” and, more loosely, “to keep something warm and close”.
  • The physical image dominates: the word names a posture — the hen sitting, unmoving, patient, over her nest.
  • It is precisely this posture of long stillness that will later carry the word into psychology.

The great metaphorical shift (1500s–1700s)

  • In the 16th and 17th centuries English writers begin to transfer the hen’s stillness to human behaviour: to “brood” came to mean to sit long and quietly, especially sunk in one’s own thoughts.
  • By the 18th century the modern sense hardens: to brood over something is to dwell on it anxiously, gloomily, or resentfully, nursing a grievance or a fear the way a hen nurses her eggs.
  • The genius of the shift is exact: the hen does not chase her worry; she sits on it, keeps it warm, gives it time — and so does the brooding mind.

Brooding weather and brooding danger (1800s–present)

  • From persons the word spread to scenes: clouds “brood” over a ridge, silence “broods” in a wood, and in literature danger or fate may “brood” over a whole landscape.
  • Tolkien’s line “danger brooded in every rock” is a perfect specimen of this sense: danger is not loud or active; it sits hidden and patient in the stones, watching — a hen of menace.
  • “Brooding” as an adjective (of a look, a temperament, a sky) became standard in the 19th century and is a staple of atmospheric fiction.

Sound and form

  • Old English brēdan (with a long front vowel) developed through Middle English into Modern English brood with the vowel /uː/ — the RP pronunciation /bruːd/, and the regular weak preterite brooded /ˈbruː.dɪd/.
  • The past form is entirely regular and carries exactly the same senses as the base verb.

Semantic weight today

  • Modern “brooded” is almost always dark: silent worry, smouldering resentment, an oppressive sky. The cheerful farmyard hen is long gone from the word’s surface, though she survives at its root.
  • The word’s peculiar strength is that it names both an emotion and a posture — you cannot brood while fidgeting or bustling; brooding requires the stillness of the sitting bird.
  • This is why “danger brooded in every rock” works so well: the danger is given the patience, the hiddenness, and the immobility of the brooding hen.
