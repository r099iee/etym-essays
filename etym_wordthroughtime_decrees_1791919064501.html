- c. 1300 — decreet, borrowed through Anglo-Norman, names an ecclesiastical or royal ruling; the word belongs to charters, councils, and courts rather than to everyday speech.
- 14th–15th c. — English writers use decree for any solemn ordinance of king or Church, and the idea of an unalterable “decree of God” enters theological prose.
- 16th c. — The Reformation and the royal supremacy make decrees instruments of state policy; the word is applied both to Tudor proclamations and to the canons of Trent.
- 17th c. — Puritan and Calvinist writing fixes the phrase “the decrees of Providence,” giving the word a fatalistic, theological colour it never entirely loses.
- 18th–19th c. — European absolutism and then the Napoleonic codes make “government by decree” a political slogan; English equity courts still hand down “decrees,” and the Matrimonial Causes Act of 1857 gives the language decree nisi and decree absolute.
- 20th c. — Dictatorships and military regimes make the decree the emblem of rule without parliament, and American antitrust law popularises the negotiated consent decree.
- 21st c. — “Governing by decree” is a standing accusation in democratic politics, while the word survives technically in canon law, Scots law, and the renamed matrimonial orders.
