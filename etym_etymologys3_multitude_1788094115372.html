The story of MULTITUDE begins with the Latin adjective multus 'much, many', whose own prehistory is debated but which is traditionally grouped with a PIE root *mel- 'strong, great' — the root also claimed for Greek μάλα (mála) 'very', μαλερός (malerós) 'violent, vehement', and possibly Latin melior 'better'. The alternative theory derives multus from a participle *ml̥tós 'ground, crushed' of the root *melh₁- 'to grind', on the idea that 'many' arises from 'broken into many pieces'; this is semantically attractive but unproven, and the major dictionaries (notably de Vaan 2008 and Etymonline) treat multus as of unclear origin while noting the *mel- comparison as the standard one.

From multus, Latin forms the abstract noun multitūdō with the productive suffix -tūdō, which makes nouns of state or condition (cf. altitūdō 'height', solitūdō 'loneliness'). Multitūdō means 'a great number, a crowd, a throng', and already in classical Latin it develops its social dimension: Ciceronian and post-Ciceronian prose uses multitudo for 'the general public, the masses' as distinct from the elite — the seed of all later social senses. It is attested from the 1st century BCE onward (Cicero, Caesar, Livy).

  • Latin → French: Old French takes multitude in the 12th century (first attested c. 1155, in Wace's Roman de Brut), meaning 'great number, crowd'; it remains a straightforward quantitative word in French, with the social sense 'the common people' developing alongside it in the 13th–14th centuries.
  • Entry into English: English borrows the word in the late 14th century — Chaucer uses multitude in The Canterbury Tales ('multitude of peple', c. 1387–1400); Gower and Langland use it too. It arrives already carrying both senses: sheer number, and 'the populace'.
  • 16th–17th century social hardening: 'the multitude' becomes a standing phrase for the common people, frequently with aristocratic or intellectual disdain: Shakespeare has 'the rude multitude' (2 Henry VI, c. 1591), and the political theory of the era wrestles with it — Hobbes (Leviathan, 1651) treats 'the multitude' as the unruly aggregate that must be bound into a people by the sovereign, while Spinoza (Tractatus Politicus, 1677) revalues multitudo as the very basis of political power. This double life — contempt in common usage, technical centrality in political philosophy — defines the word for the next three centuries.
  • 17th–18th century biblical register: the Authorised Version (1611) uses multitudes for the crowds around Christ (the feeding of the five thousand, the healing of 'great multitudes'), giving the word a solemn, vast, scriptural tone; 'charity shall cover the multitude of sins' (James 5:20, after Proverbs 10:12) enters the proverbial stock.
  • 19th century: the word becomes a favourite of oratory and the press — 'the great multitude', 'the labouring multitude' — and in America it is frequent in sermons and Fourth-of-July rhetoric. It also turns mildly ironical: 'a multitude of sins' is used humorously of anything that covers flaws.
  • 20th–21st century: everyday speech shifts to crowd, throng, host, mass, and the word grows bookish. It survives strongly in (a) quantity expressions ('a multitude of options', 'a multitude of problems'), (b) historical and biblical prose, and (c) philosophy: Negri and Hardt's Empire (2000) and Multitude (2004) deliberately revive Spinoza's multitudo as the name of the global, networked, productive many — a technical resurrection that gives the old social sense a 21st-century career.

Phonetic development in English is minimal: Middle English /ˌmʊltɪˈtjuːd(ə)/ → modern RP /ˈmʌltɪtjuːd/, with the stress settling on the first syllable and the second-syllable vowel reducing to /ə/. The suffix -tūdō appears in English in dozens of doublets (altitude, solitude, exactitude), so the word's Latinate anatomy — multus 'many' + -tude 'state of being' — remains transparent to anyone with a little schooling. Key literary anchors: Cicero's multitudo; Wace (c. 1155); Chaucer, Canterbury Tales (c. 1387); Shakespeare, 2 Henry VI (c. 1591); Authorised Version (1611); Hobbes, Leviathan (1651); Spinoza, Tractatus Politicus (1677); Negri & Hardt, Multitude (2004). The word is thus an unusually complete illustration of how a Latin counting word grew a social, sacred, and finally political life.
