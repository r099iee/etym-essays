PEEPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb PEEP, and in the passage from The Hobbit it carries the word's central, best-loved sense: to look quickly, cautiously, and usually secretly, often through a small opening, from behind cover, or in a series of tiny partial glimpses. In the scene, Bilbo is hidden in the boughs of a pine, and his presence is betrayed only by "his eyes gleaming in the moon as he peeped out" — a peep is a look that reveals almost nothing except the looker's presence, so the verb perfectly splits the difference between concealment and exposure. The action is by nature brief, partial, and cautious: the shortest act of vision that still counts as a look. It is the verb of keyholes, cracks, curtains, and leaf-screens; the person who peeps does not commit to the full act of looking but steals a tiny sample of sight, ready to withdraw the instant the risk rises.

Because PEEPED is a verb form, its full meaning is best understood through the polysemy of its base word PEEP, which in modern English is really two homographs that happen to be pronounced alike, plus a rich set of derived nouns and idioms:
  • Sense 1 — the furtive look (the sense of PEEPED in this card): to look quickly and furtively through a small opening, from behind cover, or in partial glimpses; to glance out or over at something one is not supposed to see. The Hobbit context is the canonical literary example — "as he peeped out", a small, bird-like act of vision in the midst of mortal danger.
  • Sense 2 — the chirp (peep, of birds): an entirely separate homograph meaning to make a small, high, shrill cry, or the cry itself — the peep of a chick or nestling. This is the older of the two words in English and the source of the modern verb of sound.
  • Sense 3 — "the peep of day": a transferred figurative extension (18th c.) in which the first light of dawn "chirps" or peeps over the horizon — the smallest first glimpse of morning.
  • Sense 4 — "a peep", noun of quantity: a single brief look or a single small sound; in negation, "not a peep" means utter silence or the complete absence of any protest or complaint.
  • Sense 5 — peep-hole, peep-show, Peeping Tom: the aperture and the voyeur; Peeping Tom (1796) descends from the Lady Godiva legend, and the Victorian peep-show turned the word into a commercial spectacle of small illicit glimpses.
  • Sense 6 — of light and things barely visible: to peep out is used of the moon, of flowers, of buds, and of shy smiles — anything that shows itself only partially, in a small first gleam or edge.
  • Sense 7 — dialectal and regional kin: Scots and Ulster-Scots keek "to glance, peep" survives beside peep as the northern member of the same look-word family; English peek is the near-sibling of peep, and peekaboo the baby-game built on it.
  • Sense 8 — transferred to writing and play: a "peep" into something is a brief inside glimpse (a peep behind the scenes), and in modern gaming the word surfaces in peek-a-boo tactics and "peeping" — short, opportunistic views of an opponent.

The historical bridge between the two homographs is metonymy of scale. A peep is to a stare what a squeak is to a shout: the smallest possible unit of a larger act, sharp, brief, and repeatable. The sound-sense (a small creature's thin cry) is recorded in Middle English pepen around 1400, while the look-sense appears only about 1470 as an alteration of peek, from the North Sea look-word family that gave Dutch kijken and Scots keek. The two words rhymed, both were tiny, and they reinforced each other: the chirp-homograph lent the look-verb its small, sharp, bird-like quality, and the look-homograph gave the chirp a hint of furtiveness. Today PEEPED remains informal and confidential — the word of children's books, of narrative voice, and of affectionate spy-play, the briefest and most comic act of vision, still tinged with the secrecy of a small animal at the edge of a leaf.

In register, peeped sits firmly in the informal-to-neutral band. It is not the word of formal report (“surveyed”, “observed”) nor of scientific description, but the word of anecdote, of suspense and comedy, of the moment when someone shows only their eyes. Its emotional colouring is usually playful, wary, or both — Bilbo's peep in the moonlit pine is simultaneously a child's game and a life-threatening risk, and the word holds both tones at once. In grammar it is a regular weak verb (peep, peeped, peeped), taking prepositions of passage — peep out, peep through, peep over, peep round — that consistently picture an aperture and a hidden observer on the far side.
