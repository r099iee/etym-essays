In this sentence from David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone (Cao Xueqin's Dream of the Red Chamber), repaired does not mean "fixed": it means "went" — specifically, to betake oneself to a place with formality and purpose: "Thither, having chosen a suitable day on which to commence his journey, and having first taken his leave of Jia Zheng, he now repaired to take up his duties." The sense descends from Late Latin repatriare, "to return to one's fatherland," and the ghost of that origin still governs the word's tone. To "repair" somewhere is not to stroll or to hurry; it is to proceed with dignity to a place where one has business, obligation, or a post to assume. The movement is official, orderly, and once-for-all — an act of duty and social protocol rather than a casual visit, exactly suited to a functionary setting out to take up his new responsibilities.

The great trap for the modern reader is homography: English has two verbs spelled repair, and their senses could hardly be more different. A full definition must separate them exhaustively:

  • Repair ("to go, betake oneself"): from Latin repatriare ("to return to one's native land"), via Old French repairer; an intransitive verb used with "to," meaning to go to a place, especially with purpose, formality, or in accordance with a duty — "to repair to one's chambers," "to repair to the dining room," "to repair to church." Register: formal; in modern English archaic and literary. This is the sense on this card.
  • Repair ("to mend, restore, make good"): from Latin reparare ("to make ready again," from re- "again" + parare "to make ready"), via Old French reparer; a transitive verb meaning to fix something broken, to restore it to good condition — "to repair a car," "repairs to the roof." Register: perfectly ordinary; this is now the dominant sense and the one every speaker knows. The two verbs are an etymological doublet that happens to share a spelling.

Within the "going" sense, the lexical detail worth recording is as follows:

  • Construction: intransitive, followed by "to" (or, rarely, "into," "unto") + a place; the subject is usually a person or a company of persons, and the destination is a room, building, town, or station of duty.
  • Register and collocation: characteristically appears in formal narrative of the 16th–18th centuries — "repaired to his private chamber," "repaired to the council," "repaired to dinner" — and in modern prose only as a deliberate archaism or a joke about formality.
  • The past tense repaired (the card's form): the sentence narrates a completed movement — "he now repaired to take up his duties" — and the perfective sense is essential: the movement is presented as a settled, accomplished act of protocol.
  • Related words: the same Late Latin stem survives directly in repatriate / repatriation (to send or bring someone back to their country), which is the modern, concrete continuation of the ancestral meaning that repair has long since blurred.

Boundary comparisons clarify the special flavour. Went is the neutral, colourless default; repaired is formal and loaded with purpose. Betook oneself is the closest equivalent — reflexive, archaic, and literary — but it is more personal and bookish, where repaired is impersonal and administrative. Resorted to implies frequent or habitual going (or recourse to a means), whereas repaired describes a single, ceremonial movement. Hied conveys old-fashioned haste and urgency, the opposite of repaired's unhurried gravity. Proceeded stresses continuing along a course and is procedural; repaired stresses arrival at and betaking oneself to a specific station. Above all, the reader must not confuse the two homographs: repaired ("went") from Latin repatriare, and repaired ("fixed") from Latin reparare — a genuine etymological doublet that only the context can separate.
