- - 1729: coined in English naval usage — "water-logged" describes a ship so full of water that it lies helpless and immovable in the sea, like a floating log.
- - 1700s–1800s: extends from ships to timber, wrecks, and floating objects — anything so soaked that it is heavy, swollen, and useless.
- - 1800s–1900s: transfers to land and soil — "waterlogged ground" enters the vocabulary of agriculture, drainage, and civil engineering; the image is heavy, boggy, unworkable earth.
- - 1900s: extends to people and objects — survivors, clothing, books, machinery; the connotation is sodden helplessness and disablement by water.
- - 1900s–2000s: enters sport (a "waterlogged pitch" ends play) and horticulture (waterlogged roots rot); a standard, vivid term for saturation-damage.
- - Today: occasionally figurative — a mind, schedule, or organisation "waterlogged" by too much input — always retaining the core image of being weighed down and made inert.
