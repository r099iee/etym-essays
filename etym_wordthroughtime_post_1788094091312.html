- Old English–14th century: "post" = doorpost or pillar, borrowed from Latin postis; a plain builder's and carpenter's word, recorded in Old English glosses and continuing through Middle English for gateposts and bedposts.
- 13th–15th centuries: the pillar-sense extends — "post and lintel" framing, goalposts in sports, posts of the pillory; "from pillar to post" (the idiom of being driven about) begins its long life.
- 16th century: a new "post" arrives from Italian and French — the relay station where horses and couriers were placed — and with it post-haste, post horse and the courier himself ("riding post"); the postal machinery begins.
- 17th century: "the post" shifts from the riders to the letters they carry ("to post a letter"); "a post" becomes a place of duty (military post, trading post) and then a job; the learned prefix post- (postscript, postdate) quietly joins the same spelling.
- 18th–19th centuries: the postal institution takes over the word — post office, postman, postage stamp (1840), "by return of post"; the pillar-sense keeps goalposts and lampposts; the bugle's "Last Post" fixes the military station in ceremony.
- 20th–21st centuries: to "post" goes digital — Usenet and forums (1990s), blogs and social media; "post" is now as likely to be a status update as a letter; computing's POST (power-on self-test) and the prefix post- (post-war, post-truth) complete the word's crowded modern life.
