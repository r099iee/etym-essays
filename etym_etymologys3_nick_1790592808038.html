The verb nick in the sense of cadging money presents one of English slang's tangled webs, where a short form accumulates senses by metaphor rather than by clean borrowing, and scholars explicitly debate which notch led to which theft. The most widely accepted starting point is Germanic nikken, to notch, nod, or make a small mark, found in Middle Dutch and Middle Low German for nodding the head and in Northern English dialect for small cuts. Middle English nicken, nykken from the 1400s means to make a slight cut or indentation, as a tally-keeper nicks a stick to record debt. Pronunciation /nɪk/ with short /ɪ/ and final /k/ is stable for six centuries, resisting the Great Vowel Shift because of its checked vowel. From notching develops the crucial idea of precision: the nick that exactly marks time gives us in the nick of time, the exact notch, and sporting to nick as to hit the ball finely or to win narrowly.

From precision to predation is a short slang step but debated in detail. By the late sixteenth century gamblers speak of nicking a throw, hitting lucky, and cheats speak of nicking dice, cutting them to deceive. By the eighteenth century thieves' cant uses nick for to steal, perhaps via the image of snatching quickly as a small cut, or via to nick as to catch unawares, to nab. The OED records nick meaning to steal from the 1860s in mainstream sources though cant evidence is earlier, and to be nicked meaning to be arrested or caught by police from the early twentieth century, now dominant in British procedurals. An alternative theory deriving steal-nick from proper name Nick as in Old Nick, the devil, who steals souls, is considered folk etymology by most lexicographers, though it shows how name and verb interact in popular imagination. This debate must be noted explicitly rather than smoothed over.

  • Craft notch (1400s-1600s): Nicks on tally sticks, arrows, and skin; concrete, artisanal, neutral; survives in shaving nicks and carpentry.
  • Gambling precision (1500s-1700s): Nick as winning throw at dice (nick Laws), to hit the mark; competitive, lucky, slightly roguish.
  • Theft and capture (1800s-1900s): To nick a watch, to nick apples, to get nicked by a bobby; criminal, working-class, London-centered; phonetically clipped to suit quick action.
  • Financial cadge (1900s-present): To nick someone for a quid, for the paltriest sum; comic-upper-class appropriation of underworld word, softening theft into touching; Wodehouse, Sapper, and Dornford Yates popularize among clubmen.

Socio-cultural context explains Wodehouse's choice. Interwar Mayfair youths, perpetually short of allowance yet socially required to tip, drink, and bet, needed a jaunty verb for extracting money without the stigma of begging or stealing. Nick supplied criminal frisson without criminal consequence: to nick Tom for subsidies sounds daring but remains within affectionate family finance, especially when Tom is oofy and indulgent. The construction nick him for X mirrors fine him for, soak him for, sting him for, treating friendship as mock-legal levy. The squawk audible at Land's End—Britain's southwestern extremity, proverbial for great distance—completes the joke: even minimal levy provokes maximal miserly protest, revealing the peculiarity of very oofy men.

Literary attestations track class migration. Dickensian Artful Dodgers nick handkerchiefs; Kipling's soldiers nick rations; Wodehouse's Berties nick uncles for fivers; post-war police reports have suspects nicked for nicking cars, a piquant polysemy where the same verb means both crime and punishment. The capitalized sentence-initial Nick in the card is thus orthographically ambiguous but functionally clear: not the hypocorism of Nicholas from Greek Nikolaos, victory-people, via Old French Nicolas and medieval Nick, Nicky, but the Germanic notch verb repurposed for comic finance. Retaining capital N respects source casing while analysis restores lower-case verbal identity, a reminder that English capitalization often masks grammar and that context—paltriest sums and squawks—disambiguates devil from dinner-money.
