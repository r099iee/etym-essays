“Widow” entered English from its ancient Germanic ancestor meaning bereft or empty — a woman left alone by her husband’s death, the word itself carrying the idea of a loss that empties.
“Widow” through the centuries was the specific legal and social status of a woman whose husband has died and who has not remarried — a category of loss, vulnerability, and protected standing.
“Widow” built a rich idiom: a widow’s cruse, a widow’s mite, and the stock figure of the “widow’s son” — the orphaned underdog hero of fairy tales who wins fortune by luck and courage.
“Widows” today still names women who have lost their husbands, but in the stock phrase “widows’ sons” it carries the storybook magic of the fatherless boy who becomes a hero — the classic luck of the orphan.
