whack most centrally means a sharp, hard, resounding blow or the act of striking such a blow, and by extension an attempt or turn at doing something, as in to have or take a whack at it. It functions as both noun and verb, and in modern slang also as a verb meaning to murder and as an adjective phrase in out of whack meaning misaligned or malfunctioning. The core schema in all senses is a short, forceful application of energy.

As a noun, a whack is a smack delivered with the hand, a stick, a bat, or any implement: a whack on the head, a whack with a ruler. It emphasizes sound and sharpness rather than weight — a crack-like impact. As a verb, to whack is to hit hard and suddenly: he whacked the ball over the fence, she whacked the table in anger, whack the weeds down. The verb can be transitive with or without an instrument, and can take particles: whack off, whack up, whack out.

  • A blow / strike (primary): A single sharp hit. He felt a whack on the back of his head. Near-synonymous with smack, thwack, slap, but whack suggests greater force and noise than slap, less heaviness than thump.
  • An attempt / try: In have / take / give it a whack, meaning an attempt. I had another whack at it. This is informal, cheerful, especially British and American. The bridge is sporting: a turn to hit, as in cricket or baseball, generalized to any endeavour.
  • A share / portion (dated): Historical sense: one's whack or full whack meaning one's full share, as in he paid full whack for the ticket (British: full price). From the idea of a portion dealt out.
  • To kill (US slang): To whack someone means to murder, especially in organized-crime context: the boss ordered them to whack the informer. A euphemism via hit. Past participle whacked also appears in whacked out meaning exhausted, drugged, or crazy.
  • Out of whack (predicative adjective phrase): Out of proper alignment, order or working condition. My back is out of whack, the system is out of whack. Also wack / whack as adjective in US slang meaning bad, lame, crazy: that idea is whack.
  • Intensifier / adverbial uses: Colloquial whack it on, whack it up, whack-a-mole. In children's game whack-a-mole, to whack is to hit the emerging mole. In cooking, to whack cream or to whack up prices means to whip or raise quickly.

Domain-specific nuances matter. In sport, to whack implies enthusiastic power rather than technique: a slogger whacks, a stylist strokes. In school discipline of the past, to get whacked meant corporal punishment. In mechanics and IT, out of whack is standard informal diagnosis for misalignment, timing error, or imbalance. In crime journalism, whack is cold and businesslike, avoiding the moral weight of murder. In youth slang, whack / wack as bad carries contempt: boring, inauthentic, uncool.

Grammatically whack is versatile: countable noun (a whack, two whacks), mass in phrases (full whack), regular verb (whack, whacked, whacking), phrasal combinations, and fixed idioms. Register is informal-colloquial throughout except the murder sense, which is criminal argot, and the attempt sense, which Wodehouse and similar writers made acceptable in light upper-middle-class narration. It is never formal or technical, always vivid, onomatopoeic, and slightly comic even when describing violence, which is why crime writers like it.
