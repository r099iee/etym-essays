SWINBURNE is a proper noun: the surname of Algernon Charles Swinburne (1837–1909), the Victorian poet whose signature technical gifts — incantatory anapaestic rhythms, explosive alliteration, a hypertrophied sound-consciousness, and daring pagan and sensuous themes — make him a fixture in discussions of literary ambiguity. In the passage from William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity, Empson recalls that his search for verbal puzzles "made me discover something about SWINBURNE," so that he could no longer treat Keats's Ode to Melancholy "as a dated object." The nuance is retrospective and methodological: Swinburne functions not as a mere poet's name but as a touchstone for the practice of close reading — a body of verse whose apparent verbosity hides deliberately engineered patterns of meaning. Empson's tone is quietly confessional and revisionist, rehabilitating a poet whom modernist criticism had dismissed as decadent word-music. The word has several distinct senses and uses:
  • Historical person. Algernon Charles Swinburne (1837–1909), born in London into an aristocratic family; educated at Eton and Balliol College, Oxford. His verse drama Atalanta in Calydon (1865) and Poems and Ballads (1866) made him notorious for paganism and sensuousness; later works include Songs before Sunrise and Tristram of Lyonesse. He spent his last three decades in the care of Theodore Watts-Dunton at The Pines, Putney.
  • The poet of rhythm. In literary criticism, "Swinburne" names the supreme Victorian master of verbal music: anapaests, choriambics, logaoedic effects, and stanzaic forms borrowed from Greek lyric — a poet of whom it was said that sound itself carried the meaning.
  • The emblem of Decadence. In modernist polemic (Eliot, Leavis), "Swinburne" became the cautionary emblem of poetry of pure sound over meaning — in Eliot's famous phrase, a poet who "uses words for their sound."
  • The close-reader's touchstone. In Empson's tradition, "Swinburne" stands for deliberately engineered ambiguity: verbal patterns whose surface fluency conceals multiple, structured meanings — the sense in which the name appears in the card's context.
  • The surname and toponym. As a family name it descends from an English place-name, Swinburne (West and East Swinburne) in Northumberland, meaning "swine brook" — the stream where pigs were pastured or watered.
  • Derived adjectives. Swinburnian, Swinburnesque, Swinburnianism — forms used in criticism to describe his manner.
Register and usage: as a proper noun, the word is used with the definite article in critical phrases ("the Swinburne problem," "the Swinburne case"), in apposition with his initials ("A. C. Swinburne"), and in the derived adjectives. Register is formal, scholarly, and critical-historical; in everyday speech it functions as a literary reference whose recognition depends on cultural knowledge. The name also carries a quiet irony: the man who seemed to dissolve meaning into sound is precisely the poet in whom Empson found hidden structure — the "puzzle" that close reading uncovers.
