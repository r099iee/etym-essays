The historical journey of the word dungaree presents a vivid narrative of colonial commerce, maritime labor, and sartorial democratization, linking an ancient Marathi hill promontory on the Arabian Sea to the shipyards of the British Empire and the factory floors of the industrial world.

1. Indo-Aryan Etymology & The Hill of Dongri (1st–16th Century CE):

The etymology begins in western India with the Sanskrit noun ḍoṅgara- (डोंगर), meaning a hill, rocky mound, or rugged elevated terrain. In the Indo-Aryan vernaculars of the Deccan, particularly Old and Middle Marathi, this term developed into ḍoṅgar ("hill") and its adjectival form ḍoṅgrī (डोंगरी, "belonging to a hill, hilly"). Historically, Dongri was a rocky coastal hill and promontory located on the eastern side of what was then Bombay Island (now the bustling Dongri neighborhood in South Mumbai, Maharashtra). Weavers residing in the artisan quarters of Dongri utilized indigenous Indian short-staple cotton to spin an exceptionally thick, coarse, and resilient textile known regionally in Marathi and Hindi as ḍuṅgrī-kapṛā (डुंगरी कपड़ा, "cloth of Dongri").

2. The East India Company & Maritime Mercantilism (Early 17th Century):

When the English East India Company established its commercial headquarters on the west coast of India (first at Surat in 1612 and later acquiring Bombay from the Portuguese Crown in 1668), Company merchants discovered the thriving local textile trade. While fine Indian muslins and luxury chintzes were shipped to London for wealthy European elites, Company agents recognized the immense commercial utility of ḍuṅgrī. English shipping logs, ledgers, and court minutes from the 1610s and 1620s record purchases of bales of dungaree, dungarree, or dangarie cloth. The British bought this cheap, indestructible fabric for rugged utilitarian tasks: wrapping export cargo bales, sewing sailcloth patches, making awnings, and outfitting merchant crews.

3. Royal Navy "Slops" & The Evolution to Garments (18th–19th Century):

Sailors operating across the Indian Ocean and the Atlantic endured brutal shipboard conditions where European wool and delicate linens quickly rotted under constant exposure to salt spray, sweat, and manual tarring. Seamen began purchasing lengths of Indian dungaree cloth from the ship's purser (the "slop chest") to cut and stitch their own working breeches and loose trousers. By the late 18th century, the word had undergone an important grammatical and semantic shift: the singular textile name dungaree was pluralized into dungarees to designate the sturdy trousers worn by sailors, dockworkers, and coal-heavers. In 1826, British naval logs and popular maritime songs routinely celebrated the sailor in his blue dungarees.

4. Industrial Workwear & The British vs. American Divergence (Late 19th–Early 20th Century):

As the Industrial Revolution transformed labor in the late 19th century, manufacturers in Britain and America adapted heavy cotton textiles for factory workers. At this point, the linguistic paths of British and American English diverged:

  • In the United Kingdom: The term dungarees became specifically identified with the "bib-and-brace" overall—a pair of heavy work trousers featuring an integrated chest bib, shoulder straps, and hammer loops, widely worn by railroad engineers, painters, and mechanics.
  • In the United States: Heavy indigo-dyed cotton work pants were known interchangeably as dungarees or waist overalls. Although Levi Strauss popularized the term "denim" (from French serge de Nîmes) and "jeans" (from Italian Genoa fustian), American catalogs (such as Sears, Roebuck & Co.) continued marketing working-class blue jeans as "dungarees" well into the 1950s. Furthermore, the United States Navy officially codified "dungarees" as the regulation daily working uniform for all enlisted sailors from 1913 until the early 2000s.

5. World War II to Counterculture & Fashion (Mid-20th Century–Present):

During World War II, the mass mobilization of female labor into heavy industry transformed dungarees into a powerful symbol of female patriotism, embodied by "Rosie the Riveter" and Britain's Women's Land Army. In the 1960s, student civil rights activists and feminist advocates reclaimed dungarees as a visual rejection of bourgeois propriety. By the late 20th and early 21st centuries, high-fashion designers and streetwear brands regularly reinterpreted dungarees, cementing the garment's evolution from a 17th-century Indian harbor textile into a timeless global wardrobe staple.
