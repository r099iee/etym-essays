Bebother (verb) means to bring bother, trouble, or pestering annoyance upon someone or something; it is an intensive, often mildly imprecatory form of bother, used to wish vexation on a person or thing without real malice. In Bilbo’s paired curse — “Confusticate and bebother these dwarves!” — the word doubles the comic malediction: not only confuse them, but thoroughly beset them with the same sort of nuisance they have inflicted on him. The prefix be- intensifies and transitivizes, exactly as in beset, bedevil, bewilder, and belittle, so the irritation feels total — a full investment of the speaker in the annoyance wished. The word’s own shape is murmurous — bɪbˈɒðə, opening and closing on the same soft b — so that the curse sounds less like a shout than a mumble of grievance, which is precisely the register of a hobbit who wants the dwarves plagued, not damned.

Polysemy — all senses and uses.

  • Transitive verb, core sense: to trouble or annoy thoroughly, to vex completely; the be- prefix makes the action directional and total (“to bother someone thoroughly”).
  • Imprecatory / maledictive use: “Bebother it!” or “Bebother these dwarves!” — a mild wish of nuisance upon a person or thing, the grammar of a gentle curse.
  • Rare participial uses: bebothering (as adjective, “vexatious, troublesome”) and bebothered (as adjective, “annoyed, put out”), both attested but uncommon.
  • Exclamatory / interjectional use: “Bebother!” alone as a soft ejaculation, comparable to “Botheration!” and “Confound it!”.

Historical and semantic bridges. The word is a transparent English compound of the native intensive prefix be- (from Old English be-, Proto-Germanic *bi-) and the verb bother (a colloquial word of uncertain, probably Anglo-Irish origin that rises in English around 1718). The prefix, long used to build transitive or intensive verbs — beset, bewilder, bedevil, befuddle, becalm — attaches to bother to yield “to bother thoroughly, to bring bother upon.” The compound is later and rarer than its parts: late nineteenth-century jocular or dialectal English, confined almost entirely to mild oaths and comic complaint, and popularized for modern readers by Tolkien’s use in The Hobbit (1937).

Register, collocations, and modern life.

  • Register: colloquial, humorous, dated; the register of polite frustration rather than genuine cursing.
  • Collocations: the imperative oath “bebother it!”; the paired formula “confusticate and bebother”; occasional adjectival uses.
  • Modern survival: period-flavoured comic diction, children’s stories, light domestic comedy, and fantasy prose; rarely literal or technical.
  • Jargon status: none; the word has never been a term of any profession or trade.

Synonym boundaries. Bother, the base verb, is the ordinary, mildly vexed word for troubling someone and carries no curse-force at all; bebother adds the be- prefix that makes the annoyance directional and complete. Bedevil suggests diabolical harassment, deliberate malice, something demonic; bebother is far lighter, mere pestering raised to a wish. Beset (also be- + set) means to surround and attack from all sides, military and serious; bebother carries none of that menace. Plague and harass imply a sustained campaign and are stronger by several degrees; bebother is a single rhetorical volley, comic rather than cruel. Annoy is polite and understated; pester is colloquial but not imprecatory; vex is old-fashioned but lacks the intensive prefix’s theatricality. The distinctive value of bebother is precisely its mildness: an oath that vexes without wounding — the linguistic equivalent of a hobbit’s stamp of the foot.
