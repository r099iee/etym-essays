Onomatopoeic Germanic stem *hratt- "rapid, rattling motion" (imitative; no recoverable PIE etymon — the word mimics the sound it names and is classed as imitative/expressive rather than inherited; a PIE ancestor is unknown) → Proto-Germanic *hrattōną (reconstructed, "to rattle, to move quickly with a rattling sound"; reflected indirectly in Old Norse hrata "to reel, totter, stumble") and its frequentative *hrattalōną (reconstructed, "to rattle repeatedly"), attested indirectly in continental Germanic: Middle Dutch ratelen "to rattle" (14th c.), Middle Low German retelen, Old High German razalōn "to rattle" (attested 11th–12th c.) → Middle English ratelen "to make or emit a rapid succession of short, sharp, clattering sounds," first attested c. 1300 → Modern English rattle (verb), continuous in the noise sense; from c. 1500 applied to wheels and machinery; transitive "to disconcert, unnerve" from 1869; noun senses from the 16th–18th c. → RATTLED, the weak past tense and past participle, formed with the past suffix -ed (Middle English -ede/–de, here -ed after final -t), attested from the 14th century. NOTE ON THE DEBATE: some scholars have sought an Indo-European ancestor for "rattle", but the consensus treats the word as onomatopoeic with no securely recoverable PIE root; the reconstructed Germanic stem *hratt- and its link to Old Norse hrata are reconstructions whose details remain debated.
