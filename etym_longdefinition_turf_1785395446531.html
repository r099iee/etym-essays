Turf is the surface layer of the earth bound together by the dense, matted roots of grass and low vegetation — in a word, sod, the living skin of the ground. In its primary and oldest sense it is the standing grassy floor of fields, downs, and hillsides, the close root-knitted mat on which one walks; by immediate extension it also names a piece of this surface cut free as a block or slab, long used in Britain for building walls, roofing cottages, and — when dug from bog-land and dried — as the fuel called peat. In the passage the dying vision of the high country is catalogued item by item — bushes, long grasses between the boulders, "patches of rabbit-cropped turf", thyme and sage and marjoram, yellow rockroses — and turf here names the close-grazed grassy floor of the upland, nibbled flat by rabbits into a tight, springy carpet stretched over boulders and thin soil. The nuance is pastoral and precise: turf is not bare earth but the living skin of the hillside, grass and roots knitted into one resilient mat; the compound "rabbit-cropped" explains how this particular turf came to be so short and smooth, a detail of grazing ecology that gives the whole landscape the quality of being tended, lived-in, and real. Because the vision is a remembered landscape about to dissolve, the word carries an elegiac gravity: it is the very substance of the hillside that vanishes, and the reader feels the loss in a single tactile noun. Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • The standing grassy surface (primary sense): The living, root-bound grass mat of fields, lawns, downs, and pasture — the ground itself as a resilient, green surface, as in "the turf of the meadow".
  • A cut block or slab (sod, earth-rod): A piece of turf cut free with a spade, used for building, thatching, walls, and the medieval "turf-and-dab" construction of cottages; the same blocks, dried, become the peat burned as fuel in treeless regions — "to cut turf" in Ireland and the Highlands means to cut peat.
  • "The turf" — horse-racing (18th c.): A metonymy from the grassy racecourse to the whole sport: the turf, the turf-writer, the turf calendar; "turf accountant" (1928) is the formal name for a bookmaker, and "the Turf" is institutionalised in English sporting idiom.
  • Territory and domain (20th c.): From American slang, "one's own turf" means one's territory, neighbourhood, or sphere of control; "turf war" (c. 1950) is a dispute over a claimed domain, originally of street gangs and then of institutions and bureaucracies.
  • Idiomatic verbs and phrases: "To be on one's own turf", "to turf someone out" (to eject or evict, from flinging someone out onto the grass — a British verb of the 19th c.), "to turf over" (to cover with grass sods).
  • Sports surfaces: In modern sport "turf" names artificial grass — AstroTurf and synthetic pitches — giving "turf" a technical modern sense distinct from the natural article; "field turf" is the industry term.
  • Golf and cricket: "The turf" in golf is the divot cut by the club and the playing surface itself; in cricket and racing journalism "the turf" is the playing field or track.
  • Geological / horticultural jargon: "Turf" in ecology names the dense root-mat of a grassland community (the turf layer); in gardening, laying turf means installing pre-grown sod; "turf building" describes the accumulation of organic root material over time.
  • Figurative and political: "Home turf", "sacred turf", "turf war", "turf rights" — the word is now a standard metaphor for possession and jurisdiction, an abstract sense built directly on the concrete ownership of a piece of ground.
Historical bridge: every secondary sense flows from the one concrete fact that turf is a piece of ground claimed and cut. From "the root-knitted surface" came the cut block, the fuel, the racecourse, the domain, and finally the abstract territory of turf wars and home turf — a single unbroken chain of metonymy in which the ground under one's feet becomes the ground one owns, competes for, and defends.
