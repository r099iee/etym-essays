Kinsfolk is a collective noun for one's relatives considered all together — the people tied to a person by blood, marriage or adoption. It is a double compound: kin (Old English cynn, "family, lineage, kind") + folk (Old English folc, "people"), historically joined by a genitive -s- ("the folk of one's kin": Middle English kynnesfolk). Unlike "family" it has no necessary core of household or parents; unlike "relatives" it carries warmth, solidarity and an old-fashioned, almost heroic dignity — the exact note of Tolkien's battle-cry "To me! O my kinsfolk!", where the heirs of a legendary king summon their folk to him by blood and allegiance together.

  • Primary sense — the collective kindred. The whole body of a person's blood and marital relations, of both sexes and all ages. Formally singular, it is normally treated as plural in agreement ("my kinsfolk are all coming"), and it can denote the relatives of a particular person or family ("the king's kinsfolk"). It is a count-collective, unlike "kin", which is mass-like: one has kinsfolk (a countable body) but can also speak loosely of "kin" in general.
  • Wider circle — clan and tribe. The word readily extends beyond the immediate family to the larger descent group — the clan, sept or tribe, all those who share descent, name or homeland — which is why it is at home in saga, tribal and biblical contexts: the Icelandic sagas' frændr, the Scottish clan's kin, and the Bible's "kinsfolk" in Wyclif, Tyndale and the King James tradition all name the same circle of blood.
  • Register and distribution. Archaic-to-literary in modern English. It survives in King-James-style prose, historical fiction, ballads and dialect; in ordinary conversation "family", "relatives" and "folks" have displaced it. It is also the dignified word of solemn appeal: a king, chief or leader rallying "Elves and Men" addresses the listening host as "my kinsfolk", making the word a register marker of high, old-world speech — which is precisely what Tolkien exploits.
  • Variant forms. "Kinfolk" (without the -s-) is a familiar American form, especially in the South, where it remains a living word for the extended family; "kinsman" and "kinswoman" are the gendered singletons; "next of kin" survives as the fixed legal phrase for a person's nearest relative; the alliterative doublet "kith and kin" (kith = acquaintances and neighbours, from Old English cȳþþu "knownness, home circle") still pairs friends with blood in modern idiom.
  • Figurative reach. Like "kindred spirits", the word can be metaphorized: fellow-believers, compatriots, or members of one's own species may be called kinsfolk. The bridge runs through the old sense of kin as "kind, sort, nature" — things of the same kind are, as it were, born of the same line — the very bridge that produced the adjective "kind" (benevolent, behaving as kin behave) from the noun "kin".

Grammar and legal life. The earlier genitive compound kynnesfolk displays the possessive -es of the singular noun, which survives in the -s- of the modern spelling (contrast kinfolk, an American respelling without it, recorded from 1817). In genealogy, probate and inheritance the family of words carries precise force: "kin", "next of kin", and in 16th–18th-century wills "kinsfolk" designated the whole inheriting class of relatives — a usage still felt in Scottish and Manx legal language, and in the kinship tables of anthropologists, who use the word to translate indigenous terms for the corporate body of relatives.

Semantic bridge. Each component contributes its share: kin supplies the blood-bond, folk the group. Because kin could always mean "kind, sort" as well as "family", the whole kinship vocabulary drifts naturally toward likeness — kind, kindred, akin — and "kinsfolk" can therefore shade from "blood relatives" into "people of the same kind or nation". Tolkien's cry pulls both registers together: Elves, Men and Dwarves are one "kinsfolk" of the Free Peoples ranged against the goblins — a family bond at once genealogical and moral, and the word's whole history in a single word.
