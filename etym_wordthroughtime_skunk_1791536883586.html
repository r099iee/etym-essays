- Early 17th Century - Adopted by English settlers in New England from Algonquian languages to identify the unfamiliar, violently smelly American mammal.
- 18th Century - Transitioned into natural history texts as an emblem of defensive chemical weaponry unmatched in European zoology.
- Early 19th Century - Extended metaphorically into American colloquial speech as a biting insult for a dishonorable, untrustworthy, or thoroughly despised person.
- Mid-19th Century - Verbified in American gaming and sports ('to skunk') to mean completely whitewashing or shutting out an opponent without a single score.
- Mid-20th Century - Cultural reappropriation via Al Capp's cartoons and Lockheed's secretive 'Skunk Works' engineering team, creating an elite corporate innovation idiom.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Technical domain usage describing photochemically spoiled 'skunked' beer and pungent high-THC cannabis cultivars.
