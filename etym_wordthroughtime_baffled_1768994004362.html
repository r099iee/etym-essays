- c. 1540 - to baffle first means "to subject to public disgrace or infamy": the ritual degradation of a perjured knight, his spurs hacked from his heels and his arms hung up ignominiously before the court.
- 17th c. (by 1649) - the decisive interiorisation: the verb shifts to "to bewilder, confound, frustrate"; the outward public shame becomes an inward mental defeat - the mind is now the disgraced knight.
- 17th c. (nautical) - to baffle the wind describes sails so trimmed that the wind's force is checked and wasted; from this the mechanical baffle is born - a plate that turns a current aside and deadens it.
- 19th-20th c. - baffled becomes the stock word of the detective story, the scientific mystery, and the police report; informal-to-neutral prose, journalism, narrative helplessness.
- Contemporary - the passive participle fuses both halves of the word's history: bewilderment and defeat at once; to be baffled is to be emptied and undone, and a faint residue of the old "to disgrace" still vibrates beneath it.
