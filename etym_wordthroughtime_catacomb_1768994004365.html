- 17th c. - English borrows catacombs (via French and Italian from Late Latin catacumbas, a burial district outside Rome) for the famous subterranean Christian cemeteries; the word arrives plural and proper, inseparable from early Christian Rome.
- 17th–18th c. - it generalises from that one site to any underground cemetery of galleries and recessed burial niches; antiquaries, travellers, and church historians spread it, and it acquires archaeological and devotional gravity.
- 19th c. - public imagination seizes on the Roman catacombs and the emptied Paris ossuaries; in travel literature and Gothic prose the word becomes a byword for labyrinthine, bone-choked, mass-death undergrounds.
- 19th–20th c. - figurative life begins: "the catacombs" of a city, a bureaucracy, or a memory — any vast, buried, honeycombed network; the singular catacomb appears for a single gallery and in extended uses.
- 20th–21st c. - in archaeology the word stays precise, while in weird and gothic fiction it still detonates sepulchral awe — depth, engineered darkness, antiquity, and abandonment, the underground map of the dead.
