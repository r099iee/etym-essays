ass is two old words wearing one modern spelling, and its comedy and its offensiveness both come from that collision. In the sense Wodehouse intends — "Don't be an ass, Jeeves" — it means a fool, a dolt, a person behaving with asinine stupidity or obstinacy, and it descends from the animal word: the donkey, Equus asinus, the long-eared beast of burden. In its second, distinct life — chiefly American, vulgar, and unrelated in origin — it means the buttocks, the backside, and by extension the self or person ("get your ass in here"), sexual activity, or an intensifier ("dumb-ass," "crazy-ass"). That second ass is not the donkey at all but the dialectal pronunciation of arse, reshaped by the routine loss of r before s (as in burst/bust, curse/cuss, horse/hoss). Because the two converged in spelling and sound, centuries of puns, euphemisms, and misunderstandings — from Nick Bottom's transformation in A Midsummer Night's Dream to modern playground humour — have clung to the word.

The donkey branch is the older and, in Britain, the more respectable one. Old English assa named the he-ass; medieval proverb, fable, and Scripture made the beast the standing emblem of clumsiness, ignorance, and stubborn patience — "like an ass that listeneth on a harp" for the unappreciative hearer, "to make an ass of oneself" (from the 1580s, via Shakespeare) for public folly, "ass-head" (late 15th century) for the fool himself. Polite speakers eventually retreated to donkey (from 1785, perhaps from dun-colour plus -key as in monkey), leaving ass to Scripture, natural history, proverb, and — in Ireland, where the retreat never fully happened — ordinary speech. The fool sense follows directly: since antiquity the donkey typified stupidity in fables from Aesop to La Fontaine, so calling a man an ass accuses him of braying ignorance and mule-headed obstinacy at once. Wodehouse's use is exactly this: affectionate, upper-class, barely insulting — Bertie calling Jeeves (or being called) an ass means "you are being a lovable idiot," not anything anatomical.

  • Sense 1 — The animal (countable noun, now literary/dated): the donkey, domesticated or wild ("the African wild ass," "the onager or Asiatic ass"). Bridge: direct zoological reference, surviving in Bibles, fables, and natural histories after donkey captured everyday speech.
  • Sense 2 — The fool (countable noun, informal): "a pompous ass," "don't be such an ass," "to make an ass of oneself." Bridge: metaphor from the animal's proverbial stupidity and stubbornness, active since ancient Greek fable and enormously productive (asshead, jackass as insult, pons asinorum or "asses' bridge" for the Euclid proposition that sorts the clever from the plodding).
  • Sense 3 — The backside (vulgar, chiefly US): "cover your ass," "kick ass," "have one's head up one's ass" (not know what one is doing, from 1969). Bridge: none from the donkey — this is phonological convergence with arse (Old English ears), first recorded in nautical slang around 1860 and in popular use from the 1930s. The r-loss that produced it is entirely regular dialect phonology.
  • Sense 4 — The self / the person (vulgar-colloquial, US): "one's ass" meaning one's person ("drag your ass home," 1958), the woman regarded as sexual object (1942), sexual intercourse as mass noun, and the combining intensifier -ass ("dumb-ass game," "crazy-ass idea"). Bridge: metonymy from Sense 3 — the lowest, most exposed part stands for the whole vulnerable self, then bleaches into pure emphasis.

Register is everything with this word. Sense 2 is mild, comic, and safe in nearly all company ("you silly ass" barely registers as swearing). Senses 3–4 are profane in American English and demand care: they are standard in barracks, hip-hop, and Tarantino dialogue but unwelcome in formal writing. British English largely preserves the distinction in spelling and sound — arse for the body, ass for the donkey and the fool — while American English merged them, which is why the same string can be a Wodehouse endearment and a bleeped expletive. The learner's rule: if the sentence is about stupidity, think donkey; if it is about sitting, kicking, or covering, think arse.
