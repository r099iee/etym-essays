**HOPS: a scrambling plant, an obscure name, and a brewing revolution** **An etymological trickster at the root** The word **hops** is, fittingly for a plant that scrambles over hedges, one of the most intractable words in the brewing lexicon. Its West Germanic ancestry is clear: the stem *hopp-ōn- is attested across Old High German (hopfo), Middle Low German (hoppe), Middle Dutch (hoppe), and sparse Old English (hop, plural hoppe). But beyond that West Germanic layer the word resists reconstruction entirely. No secure Proto-Indo-European root can be pinned to it, and reputable dictionaries — the OED and Etymonline included — leave the deeper origin open. The most commonly offered explanation connects the plant-word with the Germanic verbal base *hopp-, "to hop, to leap, to spring," from the vine's exuberant, twining habit: the hop bine climbs by twining clockwise, and it genuinely does "hop" from pole to pole, from string to string, and over the hedgerows of the hop-garden, growing several meters in a single season. This is plausible and evocative, but it is not provable: there is no direct morphological evidence forcing the two words together, and a credible alternative holds that the plant-name is a pre-Germanic substrate word, a remnant of an earlier, non-Indo-European population of central Europe, which would explain why it resists Indo-European reconstruction. Scholarly opinion remains divided, and the honest answer is that the word's deepest origin is unknown. **The cultivation and the continental early Middle Ages** What is certain is that hops as a brewing ingredient are a continental Germanic and Slavic innovation of the early Middle Ages. The plant is first mentioned in central European sources of the 8th and 9th centuries — notably in monastery documents, including the privileges granted to the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés (822 CE), which list humlonem, a Latinized Germanic word for hops — showing that hop cultivation was already established in the gardens of Frankish and Bavarian monasteries. In the Slavic world the plant bore an independent name: Old Church Slavonic chmelĭ, the ancestor of Russian хмель, Polish chmiel, Czech chmel, and Serbian хмељ. This Slavic family is not cognate with the Germanic stem; it is itself almost certainly a Wanderwort, a traveling loanword of unknown ultimate origin, which is exactly the kind of name an old cultigen tends to attract. It is from this Slavic family — or from a related Germanic mediation — that the botanical Latin humulus was eventually coined, and Linnaeus completed it in 1753 as Humulus lupulus, the specific epithet lupulus being Latin for "little wolf," so named because the fast-growing vine strangles whatever it climbs, like a wolf at the throat of its prey. **The English arrival and the ale-versus-beer controversy** Hops entered the English vocabulary with the plant itself, in the great wave of Flemish and Dutch immigration and trade of the 15th century. A very early English attestation, in a mid-15th-century poem, speaks of "hoppes and turkeys, carpes and beer" arriving from the continent — a line that captures the mixed feelings of the age. The new hopped "beer" immediately collided with the native, unhopped "ale," and the battle was fought in Parliament as well as in the tavern: in 1483 and again in 1512 the English Parliament passed statutes against "hoppes" as a "wicked and pernicious weed," and traditionalists insisted that true English ale was made from malt and water alone, flavored and preserved with gruit — the local herbal mixture of bog myrtle, yarrow, rosemary, and other botanicals. This is precisely the semantic world of the context sentence: ale, "traditionally brewed without hops," is defined by its rejection of the continental flower. The word ale and the word beer thus carried opposed histories for two centuries, beer being the hopped foreign product and ale the unhopped native one. **From controversy to national standard** By the 17th and 18th centuries the controversy had resolved decisively in the hop's favor, for two hard-headed reasons. First, the keeping power: hops inhibit bacterial spoilage, so hopped beer survived long sea voyages and colonial trade, while unhopped ale soured quickly — an economic argument no tavern-keeper could ignore. Second, taste: the bitter balance against malt sweetness proved permanently winning. By 1700 hopped beer was the English national drink, and the styles of the age — porter, pale ale, India pale ale (whose very name advertises that it was hopped to survive the voyage to India) — were all built on hops. The plural noun hops, which had settled as the brewing standard in Middle English, carried the whole industry: hop-gardens, hop-pickers, oast-houses, and the word's use in law and commerce. **The modern craft-brewing explosion** In the 20th and 21st centuries the word has undergone its most dramatic expansion. The global craft-beer movement made hops objects of near-obsession: new varieties (Cascade, Citra, Mosaic, Amarillo) are bred, patented, and marketed like fine wines; dry hopping — adding hops after fermentation to maximize aroma — created the "hazy" and "juicy" IPA styles; and the International Bitterness Units (IBU) scale measures their bittering power scientifically. The tasting vocabulary around the word has flourished in parallel: "hoppy" (bitter and aromatic), "hop-forward" (where the flower leads over malt), "wet hop" (fresh, un-dried cones), and "lupulin threshold" in sensory science. Across all this, the old ale-beer divide has survived only as craft nostalgia, and hops — a word of genuinely obscure, possibly substrate origin, adopted with suspicion by English law in the 15th century — has become one of the most celebrated, best-known words in the modern brewing lexicon, the living bridge between a mysterious prehistoric plant-name and the most fashionable beers in the world.
