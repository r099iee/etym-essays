English Borneo does not continue a Proto-Indo-European root at all in any direct sense; it is a place-name borrowed through Portuguese from Malay Brunei (older Barunai, Barune, Waruni), the Muslim entrepôt on the northwest coast encountered by Magellan’s survivors in 1521 and by Portuguese traders shortly before. António Pigafetta’s account of “Burne” and Portuguese charts writing Bornéu, Borneo show the crucial metonymy: the name of one harbour-kingdom was extended by outsiders to the entire, then poorly known, island — a common early-modern pattern also seen in “Sakhalin” or “Taiwan.” Dutch, Spanish, and English maps copied the Portuguese form, regularizing spelling to Borneo and pronunciation to /bɔːniəʊ/.

What Brunei itself meant is the debated part. The Brunei court tradition derives it from the exclamation baru nah! “there it is!” uttered by the founder Awang Alak Betatar upon discovering the river site — charming but folk-etymological. Modern scholars have proposed Malay dialect forms, a tree or settlement name, Sanskrit bhūmi “earth, land” via Indianized toponymy (as in many Southeast Asian -bhumis), or varuṇa (god of the sea), and even Tamil influence. None commands consensus, and the Brunei History Centre treats the origin as uncertain. What is secure is that by the late 16th century Europeans used Borneo for the whole island while Malays and locals used Brunei only for the sultanate and Kalimantan, Banjar, Sambas, Sulu for other parts — an insider/outsider split that persists, since Indonesians today say Kalimantan for their provinces and reserve Borneo largely for English or international use.

  • 1521–1600: port to island: Pigafetta, Barros, and Drake-era charts turn Burne/Bornéu into a large island east of Sumatra; Ortelius (1570) and Mercator engrave Borneo.
  • 1600–1840: vague Dutch Borneo: VOC claims but little control; English interlopers write Borneo for pepper, gold, and diamonds of Banjarmasin and Sambas.
  • 1841–1941: partitioned Borneo: Brooke Sarawak, British North Borneo Company, Brunei protectorate, and Dutch residencies give “British Borneo” vs. “Dutch Borneo” political reality.
  • 1942–1965: wartime Borneo: Japanese occupation, Sandakan death marches, and Indonesia-Malaysia Confrontation fix Borneo in Anglophone memory as jungle war.
  • 1965–present: ecological Borneo: logging, transmigration, palm oil, Nusantara capital, and Heart of Borneo conservation make the name an environmental keyword.

Phonologically Malay [brunai] → Portuguese [borˈnɛw] involved dissimilation of the r…n cluster, vowel lowering, and adaptation to Portuguese nasal diphthongs, later flattened in English. Orthographic variants — Borney, Burneo, Borneu — persisted into the 18th century before Johnson-era dictionaries settled Borneo. Semantically the word narrowed and widened at once: narrowed from any rumoured “Burne” to the mapped third-largest island, widened from port to coasts to interior as blank spaces filled. Its aura moved from “spice island” to “headhunter island” to “orangutan island,” tracking European fantasies more than local self-names — a reminder that exonyms often tell us more about the namers than the named.
