The story of skewer is a story of a small cooking implement with an uncertain birth certificate but a vivid and productive life. The word enters recorded English in the fifteenth century as skevere, a butcher's and cook's term for the wooden pin used to hold meat together during roasting, and it has been in continuous use ever since. Its origin, however, is genuinely obscure, and the honest account must begin by saying so.

The most widely accepted hypothesis reaches back to one of the great Indo-European roots of cutting, *skei-, "to cut, split, or separate," a root whose offspring span the ancient languages: Greek skhízō, "to split," the source of English schism and of the geology term schist; Latin scindere, "to split, cut," the source of rescind and scission; and Sanskrit chinátti (root chid-), "to cut off," which lives on in modern Indic speech. In the Germanic branch the cutting root produced nominal forms naming the results of cutting: *skīd- "a split billet" and *skībō "a slice, a splinter." The Scandinavian reflex of *skībō is Old Norse skífa, "a slice"; the Middle Dutch reflex is schive / schiver, "slice, splinter." It is precisely such a word for "splinter, sliver of wood" that most etymologists see behind the English cooking pin, on the simple logic that a skewer is, in origin, a sharp sliver of wood cut at need. English already had the same family natively in shive ("a slice, a splinter"), shide ("a piece of split wood"), and the later tanning term skiver ("a split leather"), so the semantic field was well prepared.

The difficulty is that no direct donor form of skewer itself is attested: there is no recorded Middle Dutch or Old Norse word exactly corresponding to the English skevere of c. 1400, and the OED accordingly marks the word's origin as obscure or uncertain. It is possible that the word was borrowed from a Scandinavian or Low German dialect form that has simply not survived in writing; it is equally possible that it was formed natively on the same "slice/split" material. What is certain is the meaning and the shape of the development: by the fifteenth century the skewer was the plain kitchen pin that pinned a stuffed bird's neck-flap, closed slits in a joint, held stuffing in place, or laced slices together so that they cooked as one piece, and as metalworking improved, iron and steel skewers joined the wooden ones, widening the word slightly to include the thin rods on which small pieces of meat are threaded for grilling — the ancestor of the modern kebab stick.

The verb followed directly from the noun: to skewer something is to pierce and thread it, recorded from the seventeenth century. The modern figurative sense then grew out of that concrete action: a critic now "skewers" a folly as neatly as a cook skewers a cutlet, impaling a bad argument or a pretentious performance with a single sharp thrust. This metaphorical leap — from kitchen pin to surgical verbal attack — is a striking example of how a tool's name can migrate into the vocabulary of abuse and judgement. The spelling of the word, meanwhile, was remodelled by association with the unrelated verb skew, "to turn aside" (from Old North French eschiuer, "to shun, avoid"), a purely orthographic accident that has nothing to do with the word's meaning — and it is worth insisting on this, because a popular mnemonic would have it that the skewer "skews" the meat, which is etymologically false.

In Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the word appears at a moment of domestic farce among the trolls: when Bert answers "Yer can try" and picks up a skewer, the long roasting-pin becomes an improvised weapon in a quarrel among giants. The detail is masterly comedy, anchoring the trolls' camp in the homely material world of an open fire and a supper interrupted by argument, and it shows the skewer's double life in miniature — a thing of the kitchen that is nonetheless sharp enough to threaten. From a probable "splinter of wood" in a lost Germanic dialect to the satirist's favourite verb, skewer has kept both halves of its personality: the homely tool and the stabbing word.

  • Sound changes: the conjectural Germanic donor would show the normal development of the *skībō/*skīd- family into Middle English and Middle Dutch; the modern spelling skew- reflects later association with skew, and the final -er preserves the original -er(e) of the Middle English noun.
  • Morphology: the verb is zero-derived from the noun (no suffix change), and the figurative sense is a semantic extension of the verb "to pierce."
  • Semantic drift: "splinter, slice" (conjectural) > "wooden pin for roasting meat" (c. 1400) > "any sharp pointed pin" > "to pierce with such a pin" (17th c.) > "to attack with pointed criticism" (19th–20th c.).
  • Socio-cultural context: the rise of commercial grilling and of the kebab/satay trade kept the concrete sense alive, while the growth of journalism and reviewing in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries gave the figurative sense its modern vigour.
  • Literary attestations: first recorded c. 1400 in culinary contexts; the verb from 1670; the figurative "skewering" criticism becomes standard in twentieth-century journalism, and Tolkien's troll scene (1937) remains its most famous literary use in fiction.
