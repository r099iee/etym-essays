In the source passage, “his arm in a sling” — Gandalf's arm is held in the medical sense of the word: a sling is a bandage, usually a triangular or folded cloth, whose ends are tied or pinned behind the neck so that a bent forearm rests in the hanging loop, immobilising and supporting a fractured, dislocated, sprained or post-surgical arm while it heals. This is the everyday sense today, but the word carries a long chain of connected meanings, and a full definition must map them all.

1. The arm support (medical / first aid). A triangular bandage folded into a cradle, or a purpose-made padded carrier; also specialised surgical “slings” — e.g., fascia or synthetic-mesh slings used in pelvic-floor surgery to support weakened pelvic organs (the modern “sling procedure” for stress incontinence), and slings in orthopaedics and trauma (“sling and swathe” immobilisation of the shoulder). The carrying idea is central: the arm is slung from the neck.

2. The weapon. The sling is one of the oldest missile weapons: a strap or thong of leather, cord or woven fibre with a pouch in the middle, whirled overhead and released to hurl a stone with great force and range. It is the sling of David and Goliath, of the Balearic slingers in the armies of Rome and Carthage, and of medieval and early-modern skirmishers. Related forms: the slingshot (US) — a forked stick with an elastic band, a small modern catapult; and Shakespeare's famous “the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” (Hamlet, c. 1600), where “slings” stands for hidden, long-range, indiscriminate attack — hence “slings and arrows” as a fixed phrase for the misfortunes that strike one from without.

3. Verb senses. (a) To throw with force — “to sling a stone”, “slinging mud” (making slanderous accusations), “slinging insults”: a vigorous, swinging throw using the weight of the arm; (b) to suspend or arrange so as to hang loosely — “a bag slung over the shoulder”, “slung round the hips”: the past participle “slung” is the core of this sense; (c) to hoist or carry in a sling — cargo slings, boat slings, and military “slung” rifles on their straps; “to sling a hammock” (nautical: to rig it up); “he slung the child onto his back”.

4. Carrying and hoisting equipment (the noun). A rifle sling (the strap by which a long gun is carried), a camera strap sold as a “sling”, a baby sling (a soft cloth carrier worn across the body, mainstream since the late 20th century), and heavy-duty rope, chain or wire slings used in lifting and rigging — crane slings, pipe slings, and the helicopter underslung “sling load”. Nautical and industrial usage is centuries old.

5. The drink (a different word). The cocktail “sling” — a spirit with water or soda, sugar and flavouring (gin sling; the Singapore Sling, invented at Raffles Hotel, Singapore, in 1915) — is first attested in American English around 1807 and is of obscure origin, possibly from German schlingen “to swallow, gulp”. It is a separate word that happens to be spelled and pronounced identically, and it has nothing to do with straps.

6. Metaphorical and idiomatic extensions. “Sling one's hook” (UK slang: to leave, decamp, 19th century); “slinging accusations/mud” (indiscriminate attack, from the hurling sense); “in a sling” (of an arm); “slingshot” politics (indirect, sniping attack). There is also the imagery of suspension: anything “slung” hangs — swords, bags, cameras, babies, hammocks.

The semantic bridge. One image radiates through almost the entire family: a flexible strap, whirled or hung. The weapon (a strap whirled to throw) gives the verb “to throw with force”; the carrying strap (a strap hung from the shoulder) gives the gun sling, the baby sling and, by extension into medicine, the arm bandage — a strap hung from the neck that cradles the limb; hoisting slings are straps that cradle cargo. The “slung” participle (hanging loosely) covers bags, hammocks and bodies. Only the drink sense stands apart, a homophone of unknown origin.

Register. As a noun, “sling” in the medical sense is plain, everyday, almost tender (a child's arm in a sling); as a weapon it is archaic or historical-literary (the sling of David); as a verb it is casual and energetic (“sling it over here”), often with a hint of roughness; and in the bar it is a drinks-word. The word is unusual in spanning the clinic, the battlefield, the nursery and the bar with perfect ease.
