• Churning: Similar because both denote violent, circular, agitated movement within a liquid or mass; differs because churning emphasizes mechanical, rotational agitation or stomach distress, whereas roiling specifically carries a visual nuance of disturbed sediment, muddy turbulence, or emotional boiling from deep beneath.
  • Seething: Similar because both describe intense, suppressed internal agitation; differs because seething emphasizes intense thermal boiling, foaming, or quiet, dangerous anger simmering right under the surface, whereas roiling emphasizes dynamic physical or psychological swirling and overturning.
  • Boiling: Similar because both involve bubbling and thermal agitation; differs because boiling is primarily a thermodynamic change of state with extreme heat, whereas roiling describes the fluid dynamics of turbulent swirling and cloudiness, whether hot, cold, or metaphorical.
  • Turbulent: Similar because both describe violent, disorderly motion or instability; differs because turbulent is an adjective denoting chaotic flow or air disturbance, whereas roiling is a dynamic verbal participle conveying active churning, clouding, and stirring.
  • Agitating: Similar because both involve disturbing a steady state; differs because agitating implies an external force shaking or stirring things up deliberately, whereas roiling often suggests an internal, autonomous, or natural upwelling of chaotic energy.
  • Swirling: Similar because both describe rotational movement in fluids; differs because swirling can be gentle, graceful, and clear (like autumn leaves in wind), whereas roiling is always thick, heavy, muddy, or deeply tumultuous.
  • Riling: Similar because rile is an etymological variant of roil; differs because riling is almost exclusively used in informal speech to mean irritating, annoying, or angering a person, whereas roiling encompasses physical fluid dynamics, financial market turmoil, and deep psychological undercurrents.
  • Fermenting: Similar because both involve internal biochemical or social bubbling; differs because fermenting implies an organic biochemical breakdown producing gas or slow revolutionary incubation, whereas roiling implies immediate, visible fluid agitation.
