DISUSE (noun, pronounced /dɪsˈjuːs/) is the state of not being used any longer — the condition of something that was once employed, practised, or maintained but has since been abandoned, neglected, or allowed to lapse. It combines the negative prefix dis- with the noun use. In the source sentence, roads "fell into disuse", i.e. ceased to be travelled and so quietly decayed.

  • Primary sense: the condition of no longer being used; abandonment through inactivity. Example: "the railway line fell into disuse".
  • Extended sense: loss of currency, popularity, or validity — a law, custom, word, or practice "falls into disuse" when people stop applying or observing it.
  • Legal and technical sense: in law, disuse relates to the loss of a right or privilege through failure to exercise it, close to desuetude — the lapse of a statute through long non-use.
  • Medical and physiological sense: disuse (attributive, e.g. "disuse atrophy", "disuse osteoporosis") names the wasting or degeneration of body tissue caused by inactivity, a scientific sense that developed in the 19th-20th centuries.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the word is a formation on use with the reversive prefix dis-, which marks separation or reversal. The verb disuse (to cease to use) is attested from the 14th century; the noun developed in the 16th century as its nominal counterpart. Because "use" implies active employment, disuse is never merely "non-use by chance" but carries a sense of deliberate or gradual abandonment: something that was once in service and is no longer. That origin explains the idiom fall/come into disuse, which pictures the object descending into a state of neglect.

Collocations:

  • "fall into disuse" — the standard idiom; also "sink into disuse", "be consigned to disuse".
  • Attributive: "disuse atrophy", "disuse osteoporosis", "disuse decay".
  • Genitive and abstract: "the disuse of Latin", "the disuse of a custom", "disuse of the limb".

Secondary meanings today:

  • Linguistic: the disuse of a word or construction — i.e. obsolescence — is how dictionaries mark entries "archaic" or "obsolete".
  • Physiological: disuse atrophy — muscle or bone loss from inactivity — has been a fixed medical term since the late 19th century.
  • Legal: disuse overlaps with desuetude; a right lost by disuse is said to be "lapsed".
  • Metaphoric: in essays and rhetoric, the disuse of faculties, languages, or skills implies mental or cultural decline through neglect.

Register and nuance: disuse is formal but not rare. It is typically used in writing and semi-formal speech, and it almost always occurs with a verb of motion or change (fall, sink, pass into). It is distinct from misuse (wrong use) and abuse (harmful use), which are active conditions, whereas disuse is the passive condition of neglect. The noun ends in /s/, while the rare verb disuse ends in /z/ — a classic noun/verb pronunciation-shift pair.

Grammar: uncountable abstract noun; it normally takes no plural (disuses is extremely rare and non-standard). It is used with possessive determiners ("its disuse") and in the fixed idiom "fall into disuse".

Relation to synonyms: desuetude is the formal and lawyerly synonym; obsolescence implies being outdated rather than merely unused; neglect focuses on lack of care; abandonment implies a deliberate decision; oblivion and limbo are figurative states of being forgotten.
