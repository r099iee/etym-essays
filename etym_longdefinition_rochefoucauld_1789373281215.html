Rochefoucauld (most commonly encountered in the full aristocratic designation La Rochefoucauld) designates François VI, Duc de La Rochefoucauld, Prince de Marcillac (1613–1680), one of the most brilliant moralists, prose stylists, and psychological anatomists in the history of French literature. Beyond serving as the illustrious historical surname of one of France's most ancient noble lineages, the name operates globally as an intellectual eponym for an uncompromising, razor-sharp brand of psychological cynicism, aphoristic brevity, and the relentless unmasking of human pretension.

La Rochefoucauld is celebrated almost exclusively as the author of the Réflexions ou sentences et maximes morales (commonly known simply as the Maximes), first published in 1665 and revised across five editions during his lifetime. Having fought and been gravely wounded during the Fronde—the aristocratic civil rebellion against the centralization of royal power under Cardinal Mazarin and the young Louis XIV—La Rochefoucauld retired from military and political intrigue to the sophisticated literary salons of Paris (most notably that of Madame de La Fayette). There, amidst courtly conversation, he distilled his bitter observations of human duplicity into five hundred devastating, polished epigrams.

The name and its cultural derivatives encompass rich polysemic applications across literature, psychology, philosophy, and history:

  • The Psychology of Amour-Propre (Self-Love): In philosophical and psychological discourse, La Rochefoucauld is synonymous with the theory of amour-propre—an insidious, all-pervasive self-love and vanity that operates as the hidden engine behind every conceivable human action. According to his analysis, human virtues are rarely pure; rather, virtues are almost always vices in disguise, orchestrated by vanity to preserve social esteem. He famously declared: "Our virtues are most often only vices in disguise" (Nos vertus ne sont, le plus souvent, que des vices déguisés), and "Self-love is the greatest of all flatterers." His work represents a foundational milestone in psychological egoism, directly anticipating the secular disillusionment of Arthur Schopenhauer, Friedrich Nietzsche, and Sigmund Freud.
  • The Aphoristic Paradigm and Rhetorical Concision: In literary theory and stylistic criticism, "a Rochefoucauld" refers to an aphorism crafted with peerless, lapidary perfection. His maxims are characterized by extreme structural symmetry, paradoxical reversals, parallelisms, and the absolute elimination of descriptive fluff. When critics characterize a writer's prose as having a "Rochefoucauldian" quality, they denote a capacity to deliver a devastating psychological truth in a single, perfectly balanced sentence that lingers indefinitely in the reader's memory.
  • The French Classical Moralist Canon (Les Moralistes): In intellectual history, the name represents the zenith of the seventeenth-century French moraliste tradition. Unlike English moralizers who sought to preach sermonizing virtue and correct behavior, French moralists (including Montaigne, Pascal, La Bruyère, and Chamfort) were detached, clinical observers of human behavior. They treated human vanity and social deceit not as sins to be punished by the church, but as fascinating specimens to be dissected with scientific precision in the drawing-room.
  • Aristocratic Feudal Lineage and Architecture: In French history, the House of La Rochefoucauld represents one of the oldest extant aristocratic dynasties of France, tracing its documented descent to Lord Foucauld I in the early eleventh century (circa 1019). The Château de La Rochefoucauld, situated on a limestone cliff above the Tardoire river in the Charente department, stands as a breathtaking architectural monument spanning Romanesque keeps, Flamboyant Gothic chapels, and Renaissance galleried arcades, symbolizing the enduring power of the French feudal nobility before the absolutist rise of Versailles.
  • Figurative Modern Archetype of the Cynical Observer: In educated contemporary English parlance, invoking La Rochefoucauld serves as a shorthand for the worldly, world-weary cynic who refuses to be seduced by sentimental idealism, romantic rhetoric, or corporate philanthropism. To say that an author is "faster than La Rochefoucauld himself" (as Marcel Proust is complimented in the source context) is to confer the highest conceivable accolade for rapid, diamond-hard epigrammatic insight into human nature.

Through centuries of literary imitation and philosophical debate, the name of La Rochefoucauld has endured as the ultimate standard against which all epigrammatic wit and psychological skepticism are measured.
