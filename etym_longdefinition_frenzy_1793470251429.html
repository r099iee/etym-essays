Frenzy denotes a state of violent mental agitation, wild emotional turmoil, frantic excitement, or temporary delirium wherein rational control is thoroughly overwhelmed by passion. Historically rooted in medical pathology as an acute fever of the brain accompanied by violent raving, the word has evolved across literature, politics, psychology, and popular culture to signify any intense paroxysm of irrational fury, mass hysteria, manic creative possession, or feverish commercial activity.

In the source text from Sir Joshua Reynolds's Discourses on Art—"The whole system and discipline of art, the collected results of the experience of ages, might, but for the fixed authority of antiquity, be swept away by the rage of fashion, or lost in the glare of novelty; and the knowledge which it had taken centuries to accumulate, the principles which mighty minds had arrived at only in dying, might be overthrown by the frenzy of a faction, and abandoned in the insolence of an hour"—the word serves as a severe political and intellectual indictment. Reynolds evokes frenzy to expose the blind, reckless passions of ideological factions. In his view, a political or artistic clique, swept away by the fever of partisanship and vanity, can dismantle in sixty minutes the profound wisdom that took generations of genius to construct. Frenzy here is not merely individual madness, but collective intellectual vandalism fueled by irrational zeal.

Core Meaning and Dynamics of Agitation:

The defining psychological dynamic of frenzy is the complete eclipse of equilibrium. Unlike quiet madness or melancholic depression, frenzy is kinetic, loud, explosive, and disorienting. It represents an acute spike of energy—whether driven by anger, terror, panic, religious ecstasy, or speculative greed—that temporarily obliterates foresight, moderation, and moral restraint. Grammatically, it functions both as a mass noun describing an internal emotional state ("in a state of frenzy") and as a countable noun referring to specific episodes or waves of collective madness ("a frenzy of buying").

Polysemy and Senses Across Domains:

  • Acute Mental Derangement and Delirium (Historical Medical Sense): Violent raving, hallucinations, and temporary madness induced by severe physical illness, high fever, or cerebral inflammation. The bridge stems from ancient Greco-Roman medicine (phrenitis), which posited that inflammation of the diaphragm or brain caused acute, raging fever-madness.
  • Collective Political and Social Hysteria (Reynolds's Sense): Uncontrollable passion, demagoguery, and ideological mania overtaking a mob, faction, or populace, leading to destructive riots or the overthrow of established institutions. The bridge scales the pathological loss of individual reason up to mass psychology, where collective fever blinds a multitude to long-term consequences.
  • Ecstatic Inspiration and Poetic Possession: A transcendent state of creative or spiritual rapture wherein an artist or prophet is seized by an overwhelming visionary impulse. Immortalized by Shakespeare in A Midsummer Night's Dream—"The poet's eye, in a fine frenzy rolling"—the bridge connects medical madness with the classical Platonic doctrine of theia mania (divine frenzy), where rational constraint yields to sublime creative genius.
  • Zoological and Behavioral Paroxysm (Feeding Frenzy): Wild, competitive, predatory thrashing and gorging, originally observed in sharks stimulated by blood in the water. The bridge transfers the concept of uncontrollable human raving to primal animal predation.
  • Commercial, Media, and Market Mania: A feverish burst of intense public interest, speculative trading, or aggressive journalistic hounding (e.g., "a speculative market frenzy", "a sensational media frenzy"). The bridge translates predatory feeding and emotional hysteria into the fast-paced, cutthroat dynamics of modern finance and communications.
  • Erotic and Sensual Delirium: An overwhelming flood of passionate desire or sensual ecstasy that sweeps away ordinary inhibitions and moral prudence.

Register, Connotation, and Tone:

Frenzy operates primarily in an elevated literary, analytical, and dramatic register, though its idioms (such as feeding frenzy) are ubiquitous in modern journalism. Connotatively, it carries an alarming, turbulent temperature: to describe a crowd or faction as being in a frenzy is to warn that disaster, violence, or irreversible folly is imminent. When applied to artistic creation, however, it retains a romantic, heroic glow, capturing the electrifying tempest of pure inspiration.
