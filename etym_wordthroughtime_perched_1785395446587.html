- [Middle English (13th c.) - perche, “a pole; a bird's roost”] - A plain, functional word of the farm and the falconer's mews, inherited from Old French perche; names the beam or branch where birds sat at night, with no elevation of tone.
- [The verb perchen (c. 1300) - “to set or rest on a perch”] - Used first of fowls and falcons, carrying the bird's own point of view: settling on a narrow, elevated support and gripping it.
- [Generalization (14th–17th c.) - “to be set high on anything”] - The word leaves the bird-house and extends to houses on cliffs, men on walls, castles on crags; it gains a permanent sense of elevation, poise and faint precariousness, while the noun also names the surveyor's rod or land-measure.
- [Modern register] - An extremely productive, picturesque verb of naturalist, travel and landscape writing (“perched on a branch,” “atop the hill,” “on the edge of”); the noun extends into the world of status (“to knock someone off their perch”), and the tone stays calm, exact and slightly picturesque.
