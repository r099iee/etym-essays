Necromancer is a word with two parents: the Greek element nekro-, "corpse, dead body," and the Greek element -mancer, "diviner, seer," joined in ancient Greek as nekromanteia, "divination by the dead," and transmitted to English through Latin, medieval French, and Middle English — with a famous medieval misreading that darkened the word forever. Because the word is a compound, its full biography is really two biographies, one for each element, meeting at the moment of the compound and then drifting together toward the modern "evil wizard." PART I — NECRO-, the corpse element. 1. The PIE death-root: *nek-. The element nekro- descends from the Indo-European root *nek-, "death, to perish," one of the most widely attested roots of the family: Latin nex "violent death," Sanskrit naśyati "perishes," Avestan nasi- "corpse, body of the dead," Greek nékys "corpse, dead body." In Greek the root appears with different suffixes: nékys (from *nék-u-), already in Homer the body of the dead, and the derived adjective and noun nekrós, "corpse, dead person," built with the -ro- suffix and attested from the earliest Greek literature. The Greek word is the direct source of the combining form. 2. The Greek combining form and its compounds. From nekrós, Greek forms the combining base nekro-, used to build a family of death-compounds: nekromanteía "divination by the dead," nekropolis "city of the dead" (a cemetery), and many words for corpse-procedure and death. Modern learned and scientific English continues the same pattern: necropolis (a large cemetery), necrosis (tissue death), necropsy (an autopsy on an animal), necrophilia (attraction to corpses), necromancy itself. The element thus carries a permanent double valence in English: clinical (pathology, medicine) and occult (magic, horror). 3. Literary attestations of the element.
  • Homer, Odyssey 11 (the nekyia): Odysseus summons the dead, the scene that haunts every later image of consulting corpses.
  • Greek drama and historiography use nekros for the corpse on the battlefield, the unburied dead.
  • Modern compounds keep the element alive in medicine (necrosis) and in horror and fantasy (necromancer).
PART II — -MANCER, the diviner element. 1. The PIE thinking-root: *men-. The second element descends from the Indo-European root *men-, "to think, to be mentally active," the root also of English mind, Greek ménos "mind, force, passion," and Latin mēns "mind." From this root Greek develops a family of words for mental and inspired activity, including the verb maínesthai "to be mad, to be inspired, to rage" — the ecstatic, god-driven state of the prophet. From that verb comes the noun mántis, "seer, diviner, prophet," attested from Homer onward: the mantis is one whose mind is filled by the god, who speaks what is hidden. 2. Manteia "divination" and the -manteia compounds. From mántis Greek forms the abstract noun mantéia / manteía, "divination, prophetic power, oracle," attested from the fifth century BCE, and builds a productive family of compounds naming the many branches of Greek divination: oneiromanteia (divination by dreams), hydromanteia (by water), chiromanteia (by palm-reading), and nekromanteia (by the dead). Each -manteia names a technique, and each has its practitioner; Greek is systematic about classifying the ways of knowing the hidden. 3. The compound: nekromanteia. Joined, nekro- + -manteia gives nekromanteia, "divination by consulting the dead," attested from the fifth century BCE. The nekyia rite — summoning the shade to answer questions about the future — is the ancient reality behind the word: necromancy began as a technology of divination, horrifying to the Greeks themselves (the dead are summoned against their will) but still a recognized art with its own techniques and practitioners. PART III — the transmission to English. 1. Latin and the darkening: from necromantia to nigromancie. Latin takes the word as necromantia, and Medieval Latin transmits it through Europe. But in Old French a famous transformation occurs: the spelling nigromancie assimilates the word to Latin niger "black," so that the learned "divination by the dead" is reinterpreted as "black magic." The folk etymology is real enough to change the spelling of the transmitted word and, permanently, its popular connotation: the art becomes the blackest of arts, condemned by the Church as commerce with demons. Middle English inherits both forms, nigromancie and necromancie, and both senses, technical and popular. 2. English: from art to agent. Modern English necromancy names the art; the agent noun necromancer, formed on the ordinary English -er pattern at the -mancer element, names the practitioner, attested from the sixteenth century. The -mancer shape is productive in modern playful and fantasy coinages (chronomancer, technomancer), a living suffix with the old "diviner" meaning. 3. Literary attestations and cultural weight.
  • The classical nekyia of Homer's Odyssey 11 and the necromantic rites of Roman and medieval literature.
  • Renaissance magic: the necromancer as a Faustian figure, condemned by Church and state alike.
  • Modern fantasy: the necromancer as the dark-wizard archetype who raises the undead — from gothic fiction to role-playing games and film.
  • Tolkien's The Hobbit: the Necromancer of Dol Guldur, later revealed as Sauron, the hidden dread-name whose dungeons hold Thrain prisoner.
4. On this card. Gandalf's "dungeons of the Necromancer" places Thrain in the grip of a death-tainted power. The word itself does the work: Greek corpse plus Greek prophecy, filtered through medieval "black art," fused into a single English dread-title. The company's shudder is philologically apt. The card keeps the singular agent noun of the dialogue — the enemy's name as function.
