DWELLINGS — The word dwelling begins in mist and smoke. PIE *dheu- described clouds and haze — the world becoming dim, obscured, unclear — and its extended form *dhwel- produced the idea of being confused, misled, or benighted: across Indo-European, the words for error and delusion are descendants of the word for clouding. In Sanskrit the root became dhūma, 'smoke', and dhūli, 'dust', surviving in Hindi as dhuan and dhool; in Greek it became thýō, 'to offer sacrifice', because the smoke of the burning incense was the act of worship. In the Germanic north the root took the form *dwaljan, 'to cause to wander, to lead astray, to delay, to deceive'. Old English dwellan meant 'to hinder, to mislead, to cause to linger'; its noun dwola meant 'error, heresy' — the heretic is the one who has been led astray. The Old Norse cognate dvelja meant 'to delay, tarry', and Dutch dwalen means 'to wander, to err' to this day. The phonetic development is regular: the PIE aspirate *dh- became the voiced dental d- in Old English, the medial *-w- survived, and the vowel settled as -e- in the weak verb dwellan; the Middle English form dwellen shows the levelling of the infinitive ending.

Then the crucial reversal occurred. In Middle English, dwellen — which had meant 'to be delayed, to tarry, to linger' — shifted its sense from the temporary to the permanent: if you linger long enough in one place, you are no longer wandering but staying. By the thirteenth century 'to dwell' meant 'to remain, to abide', and by the fourteenth it meant 'to reside, to make one's home'. The shift is a beautiful instance of semantic inversion: the word that meant to be held back, impeded, and deceived came to mean the settled, voluntary act of making a home. The noun dwelling is simply the gerund substantivised — first the act of dwelling, then, by metonymy, the place of it — and from the fifteenth century onwards it stabilised as the sober, administrative word for a place of residence: the dwelling-house of the statute books, the dwelling of the survey and the chronicle. Its register never recovered from those administrative origins, and that flatness is exactly its power.

In Peake's sentence the dwellings of Gormenghast's shadow 'by ancient law, were granted this chill intimacy with the stronghold': the word's buried history of errantry works quietly beneath the legal surface. These are places where generations have lingered so long that lingering became a fixed, granted, and servile estate, pressed against the walls of the castle that loomed above them. The word's modern life is a study in neutrality — a dwelling is not a home (which carries emotion), not a cottage (which carries charm), not a hovel (which carries contempt), but the colourless unit of residence, chosen precisely because it lets the coldness of the sentence be carried by the noun itself. From the smoke of sacrifice to the mean dwelling under the castle wall, the word has travelled from the fog to the hearth, and kept the coldness of both. The gerund sense — 'dwelling on the past' — preserves the older meaning of lingering, so that the modern word still carries, hidden inside it, the ancient image of the wandering soul that stopped at last, and the heretic who was led astray, and the smoke that rose from the altar.

LOOMED — The verb to loom is one of the mysteries of English, a word with no secure ancestry. It appears suddenly in the record in the 1540s, meaning 'to appear indistinctly, to take shape darkly and largely', and its origin lies almost certainly in the slow, heavy motion of the North Sea coastal dialects: East Frisian lōmen or lomen, 'to move slowly, be awkward, be weary', Swedish loma, 'to move slowly', Danish lome, 'to move slowly'. The semantic bridge from motion to vision is one of the most interesting in the language: a thing that moves slowly and heavily, and is perhaps tired and awkward in its movements, comes to fill the eye as a huge, vague, dark presence. The slow heavy motion becomes the slow heavy appearance — the shape that is too large and too indistinct to resolve into clear form, magnified precisely because it is not yet fully seen. There is no verified PIE ancestor for the verb: it is, in the strictest sense, a word of unknown etymology, and the older suggestion that it is connected to the weaver's loom (Old English gelōma, 'tool, implement') has been rejected, since that noun has a perfectly respectable Germanic history of its own and no convincing semantic link to dark shapes.

Once in the language, the verb grew quickly. In the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries it was above all a word of the fog and the sea: ships and land looming out of mist, the mariner's cry when a sail 'loomed' through the weather. Whatever loomed was huge, vague, and menacing — its size magnified by its indistinctness, so that the word developed a built-in charge of threat: a thing that looms is a thing that presses on the vision and the mind. In the nineteenth century the sense extended from the visible shape to the oppressive height: a building looms over a street, mountains loom over a valley, and the physical shape in the fog becomes the image of pressure and intimidation. Then, in the same century, the word turned to time: a crisis looms, a threat looms, a deadline looms — the event that hangs in the future like the ship hanging in the mist, about to arrive and impossible to ignore. The abstract sense is a perfect transfer of the visual one: what looms in time is what approaches menacingly, its shape still indistinct, its size already felt.

In Peake's sentence the stronghold 'loomed above' the dwellings: the castle rises over the houses of the poor as a great, vague, overwhelming presence, and the word does the work of the whole social hierarchy in a single verb — the ancient law grants the chill intimacy, and the stronghold looms, huge and dark and close, over the lives beneath it. The phonetic history of loom is slight (the dialectal form was simply taken into standard English with no significant sound change), but the semantic history is a rich one: from slow heavy motion, to the dark shape in the mist, to the oppressive tower, to the approaching disaster — a word that began as a walk and ended as a fate.

Attestations. The verb's menacing height has made it a favourite of adventure, gothic, and modern fantasy prose: ships loom out of fog, mountains loom over valleys, debts and disasters loom over the future, and the phrase 'looming large' has become a fixed metaphor for anything that fills the attention. In the dying-earth fiction from which this card is drawn, the stronghold looms over the dwellings as the classic emblem of overwhelming power — huge, dark, and close — and the word's obscurity of origin only adds to its power: no one knows where it came from, and it appears in the record already full-grown, like the dark shapes it describes.
