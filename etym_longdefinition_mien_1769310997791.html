mien (noun) — a person's look, manner, or bearing, especially as indicating their character or mood. The word denotes the whole outward self — features, posture, carriage, and expression fused into a single readable impression — rather than any one feature. Where countenance names the expressive surface of the face, mien encompasses the entire figure and the attitude it conveys: the “noble mien” of Darcy advertises his rank and pride before he speaks, and the “forbidding, disagreeable mien” of the same man broadcasts his coldness to the whole room. In Austen's world the two words work as a pair of instruments — mien the shell, countenance the surface.

Grammatical profile and usage
  • Function: noun, always singular, typically with a possessive or qualitative adjective (“his noble mien”, “a pleasant mien”, “a grave mien”).
  • Register: literary and historical prose; it recedes from conversation, where bearing, demeanor, and expression now cover the ground.
  • Collocations: noble mien, grave mien, haughty mien, stately mien, imposing mien, pleasant mien, forbidding mien.
  • Spelling: standard modern spelling is mien; earlier and dialectal forms include mein, mene, and myen.
Polysemy — senses and extensions
  • Primary sense (16th c.–present): a person's look, manner, or bearing as an index of character or mood. This is the dominant meaning in literary prose: “He had a pleasant mien, and easy, unaffected manners.”
  • Characterological sense (17th–18th c.): the whole outward figure read physiognomically as testimony of inner temper, rank, and worth — the “noble mien” that announces a person's consequence before a word is spoken.
  • Extension to non-human things (rare, literary): the dignified outward appearance of places or objects — “the majestic mien of the mountains”, “an air of imposing mien” — transferring the human character-reading sense to grand scenery or architecture.
  • Adjectival–participial uses: chiefly in fixed collocations (grave, haughty, noble); the word rarely stands alone without an epithet, since it is almost always evaluative rather than neutral.
Connotation
Mien is an aristocratic, observant word. It belongs to the language of physiognomy and character-reading, which flourished in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: the assumption behind it is that the soul writes itself on the body. It therefore carries dignity and gravity; one does not speak of a “scruffy mien”. Even a “forbidding, disagreeable mien” is a testimony to strong character felt as pride — the body confessing what the man will not say.

Semantic boundaries against near-synonyms
  • countenance: similar because both relate to the face; differs because countenance is the face itself and its current expression of moral state, while mien is the whole figure's bearing.
  • demeanor: similar because both describe outward bearing; differs because demeanor covers conduct and behaviour, while mien focuses on the look and carriage.
  • bearing: similar because both describe how a person carries themselves; differs because bearing emphasises physical posture and dignity, while mien includes the face and mood.
  • air: similar because both describe a general aura; differs because air is vague and diffuse, while mien is more specific and literary.
  • presence: similar because both describe the impression a person makes; differs because presence suggests commanding charisma, while mien is the quieter visible bearing.
  • appearance: similar because both describe how a person looks; differs because appearance is neutral and factual, while mien implies character-revealing bearing.
  • aspect: similar because both can denote look or appearance; differs because aspect is more formal and may apply to things, while mien applies to a person.
  • look: similar because both denote appearance or expression; differs because look is informal and common, while mien is elevated and literary.
Historical bridge
Mien entered English around 1515 from Middle French mine “appearance, countenance”, whose own origin is debated — possibly a Celtic base meaning “muzzle, snout, brow”, possibly a clipping of demean (Old French demener “to conduct oneself”), possibly connected with Latin minārī “to threaten”. English embraced it precisely when Renaissance physiognomy made the face and body legible documents of the soul, and it survived in elevated prose long after that science faded. Modern usage preserves the word mainly in dignified collocations and in historical fiction.

Literary attestations
Austen's Pride and Prejudice (1813) supplies the canonical uses: Darcy has “a fine, tall person, handsome features, noble mien”, and later “a most forbidding, disagreeable mien”; Bingley has “a pleasant mien”. Milton, the eighteenth-century novelists, and the Romantics all use mien for dignified character-reading.
