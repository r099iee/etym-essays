Straw (chiefly uncountable) is the dried stalks of cereal grain plants — wheat, barley, oats, rye — left after the ears have been harvested and the grain threshed out: the stiff, hollow, pale stalks gathered as the by-product of the harvest. It is among the most ancient of materials: bedding for animals and people, roof thatch, fodder in hard winters, packing and cushioning, basket- and hat-plaiting, erosion-control mulch, and a strengthening fibre in clay brick and daub. In the source context the word is used quite literally — "Wet straw was in his draggled beard" — the harvest remnant plastered on a traveller after his soak in the river; the required meaning is the material itself, not any figure of speech.

Primary senses

  • The material: dried cereal stalks after threshing, distinguished at every turn of farming life from hay (dried grass cut and cured as fodder) and from chaff (the husks winnowed off the grain).
  • Its standard uses: animal litter and bedding; human bedding (a straw pallet); roof thatch; cattle fodder of last resort; packing of fragile goods; plaiting into baskets, mats and hats; mulch to keep fruit and soil clean; and, mixed with clay, a binding fibre for traditional brick and wall-daub.
  • Straw-coloured: the pale dry yellow of the stalks is a standard colour name — "straw-coloured hair", "straw yellow" — so "straw" doubles as an adjective of hue.

The drinking-straw sense

Because cereal stalks are hollow, "a straw" has meant a tube for sucking liquid since early modern English, and in the modern world the object has displaced the substance in the word's everyday centre of gravity: a drinking straw, the thin tube of paper or plastic through which one sips. Manufactured straws date from the 1880s (the waxed paper tube patented by the American Marvin Stone in 1888), plastic versions from the mid-twentieth century, and the café straw is the sense most speakers meet first today. The connection to "a straw of wheat" is direct: a hollow stalk was literally the original drinking tube.

Figurative senses and idioms

  • Worthlessness: "not worth a straw" — straw as the standard of triviality, proverbial from the 15th century and still alive ("it's not worth a straw").
  • The last straw: in full, "the last straw that breaks the camel's back" — the negligible final addition that overturns an already heavy burden; the proverb crystallized in the late 18th–19th centuries and remains among English's most-used idioms.
  • Straw man: a sham opponent or argument, set up only to be knocked down — 19th-century American English, from the straw-stuffed dummies used in target practice; today a standard term of rhetoric and debate ("to attack a straw man").
  • Man of straw: a 16th-century British idiom for a person of no substance, a sham claimant, or a scarecrow-like dummy.
  • Straw in the wind: a slight sign that shows the direction of events ("these rumours are straws in the wind").
  • Straw poll / straw vote: an unofficial test of opinion, from US political usage of the 19th century.
  • To draw straws: to decide by lots drawn from a handful of straws — the short straw loses.
  • To clutch (catch) at straws: to try any desperate remedy when hope is nearly gone; the 16th-century image is of a drowning man catching at floating straws.

The semantic bridges

  • Triviality: straw is cheap, dry, light, and abundant — the cheapest product of the harvest — so it became the standard measure of worthlessness and then of slightness; hence "last straw" and "straw in the wind", both built on how little a straw weighs.
  • The straw man: a dummy of straw is a man-shaped nothing, an opponent with no substance; rhetoric borrowed the image for an argument with no real force.
  • The drinking straw: no metaphor at all — a hollow stalk was the first tube, so the manufactured object simply inherited the name of its material.

Register and usage

Straw is an everyday, concrete word of neutral register, equally at home in barns and in idioms. It is normally uncountable (the material), countable in fixed phrases ("a straw", "straws", "the last straw"). Its relatives include "straw" as adjective (straw-coloured; made of straw), and the compound "strawberry" (Old English strēawberige) — bound to the same ancient root by either of two paths: the plant's runners that "strew" or spread themselves, or the straw traditionally laid under the fruit to keep it clean. No serious synonym exists for the material itself; its idioms, however, form one of the richest proverb-families in the language.
