Mislike is a native English word — one of the oldest compounds in the language — and its biography is the biography of an ordinary medieval verb that was pushed to the edge of the language by a Norman-French rival and survived there, as such words do, with its archaic dignity intact. To tell its story is to follow the Germanic prefix mis- and the Germanic verb like from Anglo-Saxon England to Shakespeare's stage and into the fantasy fiction of today. The two ingredients: mis- and like Both halves of mislike are ancient Germanic stock. The prefix mis- (Proto-Germanic *missa-, "badly, wrongly, amiss, apart") is one of English's most productive negative prefixes; its deeper Indo-European ancestry is uncertain — it is often compared with Latin minus "less" and Sanskrit mithu "falsely, wrongly", but the connections are debated, and dictionaries typically mark the prefix's origin as obscure. It appears in countless Old English compounds: misbeodan "to behave ill", misgangan "to go astray", misniman "to take wrongly". The verb like is far more transparent: Old English līcian, "to please, to be agreeable to", descends from Proto-Germanic *līkōjaną, a denominative verb built on *līką, "body, form, shape" — the underlying idea is "to be like", and "to be like" in the sense "to be suited, to be pleasing" is a classic Germanic semantic move. The root reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European *leyg-, "body, form". Old English: to displease The compound is attested from the earliest records. Old English mislīcian means "to displease, to offend, to be disagreeable": where līcian meant "to please", the prefix reversed it. The word's syntax deserves note: in Old English the verb is typically impersonal — me mislīcað þæt, literally "it displeases me that...", "I dislike the fact that...". The subject of the sentence is the thing that causes displeasure; the person experiencing it is the dative object. This impersonal construction is the sign of an ancient verb of feeling, of the same family as me līcað "it pleases me" and the modern vestiges in such phrases as "methinks". Middle English: standard usage and the polarity shift In Middle English misliken is the ordinary word. Two things happen in this period. First, the verb acquires a transitive use: "I mislike him" appears beside the impersonal "it misliketh me", so that the modern direction of the word — the person disliking as subject — gradually establishes itself. Second, the word's range settles: it means both "to displease" and "to dislike", "to be displeased with". Chaucer uses it; the mystery plays use it; it is simply the standard English way of saying what dislike says today. Had history taken another turn, mislike would be the everyday word and this essay would be about dislike. The sixteenth century: the Norman rival arrives The turning point is the Norman inheritance. Middle English had long had dislike from Old French — des- (Latin dis-, "apart, away") plus the same Germanic like — but it was in the sixteenth century that the French compound decisively won the contest. By the 1500s dislike had become the popular, everyday term, while mislike was increasingly confined to the literary and the archaic. Shakespeare is the great witness: he uses both, and his mislike carries a distinctly formal, even archaic colour — "I mislike this new-fangled world". The two words meant the same thing; they differed only in their date, and the language, in a celebrated case of synonymic crowding, kept the younger one for the street and the older one for the stage. By the eighteenth century mislike is marked in dictionaries as archaic or provincial. The afterlife: poetry, history, fantasy Mislike never quite died. It was kept alive by poets and by writers of historical fiction who valued its ancient, native pedigree; it survives in dialect; and in the twentieth century it found a new home in fantasy literature, where archaic diction is a feature rather than a fault. In the works of George R. R. Martin and his imitators, characters "bear a mislike for" one another, and the word does what dislike cannot: it signals a medieval, formal, slightly menacing world. The word has thus completed a double life — for a thousand years the ordinary English verb of aversion, and for four hundred years the poetic and archaic one. Linguistic notes
  • The impersonal construction ("it misliketh me") survives in early modern English alongside the transitive one; modern use is almost always transitive.
  • The noun mislike ("a feeling of aversion") is now rare but was normal in the 16th–17th centuries.
  • The word has no connection with mistake (which is from Old Norse mistaka, "to take wrongly"), despite the shared prefix.
  • In modern dictionaries the word is labelled archaic or literary; its survival is a small monument to the fact that English keeps its losers as well as its winners, and that a word pushed out of the street may live forever in the library.
