- [14th–15th c.] - Middle English linet enters the language from Old French, denoting the common flax-seed-eating songbird - [connotation: familiar country bird, appreciated by rural folk for its gentle song and association with harvested fields]
- [16th–17th c.] - Early Modern English settles on the spelling linnet; poets contrast its modest song with the louder thrush and noble nightingale - [connotation: emblem of rustic simplicity, unpretentious music, and domestic peace]
- [18th c.] - Enters colloquial speech in idioms like "linnet-headed" (flighty, silly), while naturalists begin formal classification under Linnaean taxonomy - [connotation: split between tender pastoral poetry and lighthearted colloquial mockery]
- [19th c. – Victorian Era] - Becomes an omnipresent working-class pet in urban cage-bird culture and a romanticized herald of spring in Victorian fairy tales and music-hall song - [connotation: warm urban domesticity, cockney working-class nostalgia, and moral innocence]
- [20th–21st c.] - Retains classic literary charm while entering contemporary environmental discourse as a red-listed indicator of farmland biodiversity loss - [connotation: cherished pastoral heritage, musical delicacy, and ecological vulnerability]
