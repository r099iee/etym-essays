Lavished: how washing-water became extravagance

I. The Indo-European base: *leuə- "to wash"

The whole family stands on the PIE root *leuə- (also reconstructed as *leu(h)-) , "to wash, to purify by washing," which produced a wide European progeny: Latin lavāre "to wash" (our lavatory, lave and even lava, molten stone flowing like the wash of water), Greek louein "to wash, to bathe," Old Irish loathar "bathing-tub." The English branch largely lost the inherited root — English wash itself comes from a different Germanic base — but the Romance branch carried the water imagery forward, and it is through French that the root at last reached English in the form of this very word.

II. Latin lavāre → Old French laver → lavasse

  • Latin lavāre, "to wash, to bathe," was a common verb of classical Latin (lavare manus, lavabrum "bath"). Vulgar Latin kept it vigorous, and Old French inherited laver, "to wash" — still alive in modern French (laver les mains), and the parent, through the wash-house laverie, of English laundry.
  • From laver, Old French formed the noun lavasse: "a great rain, a downpour, a torrent." The suffix -asse intensifies and often cheapens (compare pluviasse); lavasse is the weather’s profligacy — water poured out in a flood, more than enough and beyond measure. It is this word that gives the English family its shape, for what is lavished is, etymologically, poured out like a drenching rain.

III. Middle English: lavas, the prodigal adjective

  • Middle English borrowed the Old French noun and adjective as lavas (also lavasse, lavish), attested from the 15th century in the sense "extravagant, prodigal, profuse." The early citations are financial and moral: a lavas spender, a lavas tongue (a tongue that pours out words without measure). The transfer of the downpour to human conduct is immediate and image-rich: extravagance is language behaving like weather.
  • Orthographically the word settled as lavish by the 16th century, probably under the influence of the familiar -ish adjectival suffix (compare foppish, fiendish), which gave it a native English look even though the -ish here is not that suffix etymologically — a spelling assimilation noted by every dictionary of the family.

IV. The verb and its career (16th century onward)

  • The verb to lavish, "to expend or bestow profusely," is attested from the 1540s. From the beginning it carried both directions: lavish money upon oneself or one’s pleasures (waste) and lavish honour, affection or bounty upon others (munificence). Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century usage is strongly negative — the Elizabethans moralised lavishment as prodigality, the vice opposite to thrift.
  • The 18th century rebalances: writers lavish praise, love and care as well as money, and the moral colour shifts toward generosity; Johnson’s Dictionary (1755) glosses lavish primarily as "scattering with profusion; wasteful; prodigal" but records the admiring uses side by side. The double register has never left the word.
  • The 19th century sees the adjective conquer everyday use (lavish entertainment, lavish expense accounts in the age of railways and exhibitions), and the 20th completes the transformation into prestige and spectacle: lavish weddings, lavish budgets, lavish productions. The word has climbed from blame for wastrels to applause for spenders within a century, while the verb kept its quieter place in elegant prose.

V. The modern picture

  • Today the verb lavished is mostly literary and formal; its favourite objects are immaterial (praise, attention, affection, care), and the passive construction — attention was lavished on the project — is a hallmark of serious journalism.
  • Collocations still betray the watery origin: we speak of pouring money into something, of rivers of gold, of showers of praise; lavished is the polite member of that flood family.
  • Register contrasts: casual speech prefers splashed out, shelled out, gone to town; lavished adds dignity and a whiff of old money — which is exactly why Durant chooses it for a sentence about the tragic misallocation of a whole people’s intelligence.

VI. Conclusion

From the wash-tubs of the Indo-European homelands to the river-meadows of this sentence, lavished has travelled no distance at all in literal terms — it has always meant "poured out in excess" — but it has migrated morally from sin to splendour and back, always carrying the image of blessed, wasteful water.
