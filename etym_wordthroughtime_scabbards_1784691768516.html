- Middle English sense - the scabbard is the firm case in which the sword blade is carried: two wooden slats or a rigid core covered in leather, cloth or metal, fitted with a locket and a chape; part of the knight's equipment, distinct from the softer sheath of a dagger
- Medieval & early modern display sense - the scabbard becomes an object of display as much as of protection, ornamented with precious metals, gems, enamel and tooled leather as a mark of the owner's rank and the weapon's worth; the sword's case declares the sword's nobility
- 18th-19th century - as swords leave everyday civilian life, the word retreats to the martial, the ceremonial and the museum; a scabbard is now heard of soldiers, officers and collectors
- Contemporary sense - chiefly literary, military or fantasy register, naming the protective case of a sword, dagger or bayonet; the word keeps a formal, antique dignity and signals craftsmanship and lineage
