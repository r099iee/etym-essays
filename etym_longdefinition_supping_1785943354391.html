SUPPING is the present participle of the verb sup, which in modern English carries two related but historically distinct senses. The primary sense, still familiar from literary, archaic and dialectal usage, is the taking of an evening meal: to eat supper, or to dine at the end of the day in a deliberately unhurried, companionable way. The second, more literal sense is the taking of liquid, soup, broth, or any semi-liquid food into the mouth in small, repeated, rhythmic mouthfuls or spoonfuls, so that the eater sips or ladles rather than gulps. Both senses are typically felt as old-fashioned, warm, intimate, nostalgic, or consciously literary in register, evoking slow evening dining, quiet fellowship, candlelight, hearthside comfort, or the melancholy of a solitary night-time meal.

In the source context ("One you may regret as you're supping alone tonight"), the word stages a deliberate contrast between the cozy communal warmth traditionally associated with supper (a meal shared, a household gathering at the end of the working day) and the cold reality of solitude. The act of supping alone becomes a poignant emblem of isolation, regret, lost companionship, or the uneasy awareness of having made a choice one now rues. The grammatical form supp-ing is a progressive participle, and the context works precisely because the participle places the reader inside the ongoing, lingering act of the lonely evening meal.

Primary Senses

  • To take supper / eat the evening meal: "to sup" in this sense means to have the last full meal of the day, historically a light or simple evening repast; "supp-ing" is the act itself. This sense is now mainly literary, dialectal, or consciously archaic, but it survives in set phrases and in the noun supper.
  • To drink, sip, or take liquid in small quantities: "to sup" (and thus "supping") describes the measured, repeated taking of liquid, soup, or broth by spoonfuls or small draughts: to sip or lap up. This is the etymologically older sense, rooted in the physical act of sipping from a bowl or spoon.
  • Extended / causative use (rare, archaic): in older English, "to sup" could also mean to give someone something to sup, i.e. to feed with a spoon or to administer liquid food — a causative extension now effectively obsolete.

Secondary, Figurative and Register Senses

  • Metonymic / merged sense — the meal and the sipping united: the noun supper and the verb sup are connected through back-formation and historical merger: the verb "sup" meaning "to eat the evening meal" is derived from the noun supper (from Old French souper), while the verb "sup" meaning "to sip" comes directly from Old English sūpan. The two verbs converged in form, so that in many contexts supping may evoke both sipping and dining at once — a productive ambiguity for literary writing.
  • "To sup sorrow" (archaic, figurative): to taste a bitter or unpleasant experience, to drink in misfortune — an extension from the idea of consuming an emotion or fate as if it were food or drink; the collocation "supping with sorrow" carries proverbial weight.
  • "To sup with the devil" (proverbial, figurative): said of accepting an alliance or bargain with an evil or dangerous partner, with the warning that one must have a long spoon; it builds on the shared-meal sense of sup.
  • Culinary and hospitality registers: "supping" survives in cookery writing and historical-fiction dialogue to describe the slow, deliberate evening consumption of soup, porridge, or broth, usually with a spoon, preserving the sensory detail of the older "to sip" sense.
  • Dialectal survivals: in some regional British dialects (notably Northern and Scottish usage), "sup" remains an ordinary word for taking soup or porridge, and "supping" for eating it with a spoon, keeping the Old English sense alive at the colloquial level.
  • Religious / fixed phrase "the Last Supper": although this uses the noun, it keeps the whole word family culturally fixed as the archetypal evening meal, and its visual associations (a table, bread and wine, shared at dusk) reinforce the warm-and-sacred tone that clinging to supping today.

Grammatical and Morphological Notes

  • Sup is now a regular weak verb; the present participle is sup + -ing with the final consonant doubled before the vowel-initial suffix: sup → supping (parallel to cup → cupping, hip → hipping).
  • The related nouns are supper "the evening meal" and the agent noun supper "one who sups", both formed on the same stem.
  • It must be distinguished from sip (strictly about liquid taken in tiny quantities) and soup (the noun for the liquid food itself), although all three words belong to the same ancient family of "taking liquid food" vocabulary and share the PIE root *seup-.

Semantic Boundaries and Near-Synonym Comparisons

  • Supping vs. Dining: dining is formal, elegant, and multi-course; supping is simple, homely, and evening-specific.
  • Supping vs. Sipping: sipping applies strictly to liquid taken in small tastes; supping may include sipping or spoon-taking, but also embraces the whole act of taking the evening meal.
  • Supping vs. Feasting: feasting implies abundance, revelry, and company; supping is subdued, intimate, household fare.
  • Supping vs. Guzzling / Swilling: guzzling is greedy, rapid, and noisy; supping is measured, gentle, and reflective.

Historical-Semantic Bridge

The path from "to sip liquid" to "to eat the evening meal" runs through medieval dining culture, in which the last meal of the day was commonly a light soup or broth — the soper/souper — eaten by dipping bread (a sop) and sipping the liquid food. Old French souper "evening meal" is itself related to soupe "soup, sop," and both look back to the same Germanic root for sipping that gave English sūpan. Supping therefore united, within one word family, the two activities — sipping broth and taking the evening meal — which is exactly why the word can mean either or both today.
