A nook in the coast. The word creek is at heart an image of land, not of water. It entered Middle English around the thirteenth century as creke, meaning a fissure, a crack, or — crucially — a narrow opening in the coastline, and its likeliest sources are Old Norse kriki, "a nook, corner, or recess," and the parallel Old French crique, "a bay or cove." Both words carry the same mental picture: a bend or hook in the shore that lets the sea reach inward. Scholars trace this picture, cautiously, to the PIE root *ger-, "to turn, bend, wind," the ancestor of Greek γῦρος (gŷros), "circle," and hence of English gyre, gyrate, and gyroscope, and very probably of the Germanic "hook" words — Old Norse krókr gave English crook and crooked, and a cramp or crick in the neck is a "bend" of the muscle. But the dictionaries are candid: the ultimate link of creek to *ger- is probable, not proven, and the word's documented home is the coastal vocabulary of the North and Channel seas.

The great Atlantic split. For centuries the English word meant, as it still does in Britain, a narrow inlet of the sea — the snug place of refuge Lewis still uses in the Narnia passage, where the crew "ran the boat up into a little creek and moored her to a tree." Then came the colonisation of North America, and the word performed one of the most famous sense-shifts in the language. American settlers applied creek to the inland streams that ran down to those inlets — and then, as the frontier pressed inland, to any small river or tributary at all. Today an American creek (often crick in dialect) is a fresh-water stream, while a British creek remains a tidal inlet; the same word, carrying the same "bend" image, now names two different kinds of water on the two sides of the Atlantic. The shift is a textbook case of semantic transfer through migration: the first settlers met the sea first, then followed the creeks inland, and the word followed them.

A people's name. The word even hardened into an ethnonym. The Creek, or Muscogee, people of what is now the American Southeast took their English name from the streams and creeks along which they lived — the same word, now a proper noun, carrying the same image of flowing water. Meanwhile the old coastal sense never died in Britain, and the idiom "up the creek (without a paddle)" (c. 1930s) fixed the stranded, in-trouble image in everyday speech, from the picture of a boat stuck on a small stream with nothing to move it.

In Narnia. When Lewis writes "they ran the boat up into a little creek and moored her to a tree," he is using the word in its oldest and most English sense: a narrow, sheltered inlet, a natural harbour hidden from the open sea, the antithesis of the storm-driven deep. The adjective "little" and the image of mooring to a tree give the creek a homely, almost domestic feel — the sea's edge becomes a garden nook — and the word's ancient "bend" image, of the land turning to let the sea in, is exactly what makes it right for this moment of refuge and rest.

  • Semantic drift summary: "to turn, bend, wind" (PIE *ger-, probable) → "a nook, corner, recess" (Old Norse kriki) / "a bay, cove" (Old French crique) → "a fissure, narrow opening in the coast" (Middle English creke) → "a narrow tidal inlet" (British creek) → "a small fresh-water stream" (American creek, 17th c.) and "the Creek people" (ethnonym).
  • Sound changes: the initial k- of the Norse and French sources passed into Middle English as k- and remains /k/ today; the dialectal pronunciation /krɪk/ (crick) preserves an older, shortened vowel that standard English levelled to /kriːk/.
  • Cultural context: the word's two modern lives are the direct record of British and American maritime and frontier history — one sense stayed on the coast, the other followed the settlers inland.
