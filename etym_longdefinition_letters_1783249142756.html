letters. In this context — Macaulay's sentence that Britain "received only a faint tincture of Roman arts and letters" — the plural noun denotes the whole written intellectual culture of Rome: literature, learning, scholarship, eloquence in the bookish arts. The key is that 'letters' here is a fixed plural with a specialised sense; it is not 'letters of the alphabet' and not 'epistles', though both meanings feed into it. In classical Latin, litterae meant both 'writing' and 'literature, learning', and the phrase litterae humaniores ('the more humane letters') came to denote the study of Greek and Latin authors. English inherited this plural institutional meaning: 'arts and letters' is a frozen collocation meaning creative and scholarly culture, and 'a man of letters' (French homme de lettres) is a scholar-writer. The tone of the passage is elegant, classical, and faintly elegiac — Britain, the writer implies, was never steeped in this culture, only brushed by its faint tincture, so that the great edifice of Roman letters barely touched the island before it was withdrawn. Core senses
  • Learning, literature, scholarship. The institutional plural: the whole written intellectual tradition — 'letters' as the collective culture of the written word. Frozen collocations: arts and letters, man of letters, woman of letters, Doctor of Letters (D.Litt.), the republic of letters.
  • Written communications (epistles). The everyday plural: letters as correspondence — to write letters, a letter from home. This is the concrete sense from which the institutional meaning historically grew.
  • The alphabetic characters. Letters of the alphabet — the basic graphic signs of writing. This is the root sense of the singular letter (Latin littera) and feeds the cultural sense: letters are the elements of literacy.
  • Academic degrees and honours. In 'Doctor of Letters' and similar titles, letters designates scholarship in the humane disciplines, parallel to science in Doctor of Science.
  • Metonymy for literature and its community. In 'a lettered culture', 'the republic of letters', the plural stands for the community of writers and scholars and the body of writing they produce.
Historical bridge Letter comes from Latin littera (also litera), 'a letter of the alphabet; a writing, a document', a culture-word of uncertain, probably non-PIE origin: rival theories derive it from Greek diphthera 'skin, hide, writing material' via Etruscan, or from Latin linere 'to smear' (of wax tablets). The plural litterae was already institutional in Latin — 'writing, records, literature, learning' — and French lettres and English letters inherited that plural sense directly. English took the singular via Old French letre/lettre in the 13th century, displacing the native Germanic compound bōcstæf ('book-staff'); the institutional plural sense hardened in the Renaissance and Augustan age. Synonym boundaries
  • Literature in its modern sense — a body of imaginative written works, a usage that hardened only in the nineteenth century — is narrower and more aesthetic than letters, which includes all serious writing (history, oratory, philosophy, correspondence) and the cultivation of learning around them.
  • Learning is broader still and non-verbal: one may have learning in mathematics or music, whereas letters is specifically the bookish, literate tradition.
  • Humanities is the modern academic grouping that opposes letters to the sciences, but it is a curricular label, cooler and more administrative than the dignified, almost reverent word letters.
  • Writing refers to the physical act or system of inscription, the mere technology, whereas letters is the cultural edifice built upon it.
  • Scholarship names the disciplined production of knowledge — the activity — while letters names the culture and tradition that results.
