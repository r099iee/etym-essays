From ante to antiquity: the story begins with the Latin preposition ante, meaning before, in front of, which continues a Proto-Indo-European adverb *h₂enti, from the root *h₂ent-, front, forehead. Latin built on ante an adjective antiquus, literally situated in front, hence former, old, ancient — what stands before us in time. From antiquus came the abstract noun antiquitas, oldness, ancient times, the people of old, already carrying both the temporal and collective senses seen today.

Late Latin and Old French mediation: antiquitas, antiquitatem passed into Old French as antiquité by the twelfth century, meaning oldness, great age, and ancient times. Middle English borrowed it as antiquite(e) in the fourteenth century, first in the sense of old age and former times, as in Chaucer-era chronicles. The spelling with -qu- preserves the Latin stem; the ending -ity is the regular English reflex of Latin -itas via French -ité, as in city, vanity, majesty.

  • 14th–15th c. — old age and former times: early attestations mean great age or the distant past generally, without the modern classical restriction.
  • Renaissance — Classical Antiquity: humanists from Petrarch to Erasmus elevated antiquitas into a cultural ideal. The revival of classical letters, sculpture, and architecture fixed antiquity as shorthand for Greece and Rome. Pater's 1873 Studies in the History of the Renaissance, the source of the card sentence, is a late flowering of this usage.
  • 17th–18th c. — the object sense: with the Grand Tour, excavations at Herculaneum and Pompeii, and the rise of museums, the plural antiquities came to mean physical remains — statues, coins, inscriptions. Antiquaries such as Camden, Aubrey, and later Winckelmann made collecting and classifying antiquities a science.
  • 19th c. — institutionalisation: national museums, antiquities legislation, and university chairs of classical archaeology cemented both senses. The Antiquities of Athens, Egyptian antiquities, and laws against illicit traffic in antiquities belong to this era.
  • Modern — contested heritage: today antiquities carries legal and ethical weight: provenance, repatriation, looting. The Elgin Marbles debate and UNESCO conventions keep the word in the news, far beyond philology.

Phonetic development: Latin antiquitas was pronounced roughly [anˈtiːkʷɪtaːs]; Old French fronted and reduced it to [ɑ̃tikite]; Middle English restored a more Latinate stress, yielding modern /ænˈtɪkwɪti/ with antepenultimate stress typical of -ity nouns (compare vanity, gravity). The qu spelling retains the Latin labiovelar, now simplified to /kw/.

Semantic drift: the core movement is from quality (oldness) to period (old times) to privileged period (Greece and Rome) to object (what survives from that period). Each step is metonymic and historically motivated: Renaissance reverence narrowed the period; antiquarian collecting concretised it into things. Both senses now coexist, disambiguated by number: uncountable time versus countable things.

Key attestations: Cicero's antiquitas for Roman tradition; Isidore and medieval chroniclers for former ages; seventeenth-century auction catalogues listing antiquities; Gibbon's Decline and Fall; Pater's Renaissance, where classical antiquity is not a dead past but a reviving spirit.
