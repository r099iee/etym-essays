Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed, c. 3500 BCE)
*tragʰ- / *dʰregʰ-
‘to draw, drag, pull along the ground’
↓
Proto-Italic (reconstructed, c. 1000 BCE)
*traɣō
‘to pull, drag’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE)
trahere
‘to draw, drag’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
dētrahere
‘to pull down, take away, disparage’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
dētractiō
‘a pulling down, removal, disparagement’
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
detraccion
‘slander, disparagement, backbiting’
↓
Middle English (c. late 14th c.)
detraccion
‘speaking ill of others, slander’
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
detraction
‘the taking away from reputation; belittling’
↓
Modern English (current)
detraction
‘malicious disparagement; taking away from merit’

Note: Formed from the Latin prefix dē- ("down from, away from") + tractiō (noun of action from trahere, "to draw or drag", supine stem tract-). The semantic development moved seamlessly from physical subtraction (dragging an object away or pulling down a physical structure) to the figurative dragging down of someone's public dignity, honor, or worth.
