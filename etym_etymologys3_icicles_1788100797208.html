Introduction. Icicles is one of the most purely Germanic words in the language: every layer of it — the ice, the spike, the plural — comes down unbroken from the speech of the North Sea coasts, and its history shows how a folk word can survive intact while the science around it changes completely. The modern plural form sits at the end of a chain that begins in a reconstructed Indo-European root for “ice”, passes through a Germanic word that also meant “glacier”, and then, in a striking act of folk etymology, fuses two synonyms — “ice” and “icicle” — into one.

1. The Indo-European base: *yeg-

The second element of icicle goes back to a Proto-Indo-European root *yeg-, “ice”, which is reconstructed chiefly on Germanic and (possibly) Iranian evidence: the Germanic derivative *jekilaz and Avestan yaxa- (“ice, frost”), alongside which little else can be placed, since the Greek and Latin words for ice belong to different roots and cannot be shown to share it. This must be stated honestly: the Indo-European pedigree of the Germanic ice-words is thinly attested, and some handbooks prefer to treat *jekilaz as a Germanic formation of uncertain ultimate origin. What is certain is the Germanic stage: Proto-Germanic *jekilaz, “a piece or mass of ice”, “an icicle”, which produced Old Norse jǫkull (“glacier, mass of ice” — surviving in modern Icelandic jökull, “glacier”, and in the many Icelandic place-names in Jökull, such as the famous lagoon Jökulsárlón), Old High German forms of the ihhila type, and Old English gicel.

2. Old English: gicel and īsġicel

In Old English the plain noun gicel (masculine) meant “a lump or piece of ice” and, more specifically, “an icicle”; it is recorded in the vocabularies and glosses of the period. Alongside it stood the plain noun īs, “ice”, the ancestor of modern ice, which comes from Proto-Germanic *īsą, a word whose own pre-Germanic history is uncertain (it may be related to a root for “frost” or be an old substrate word; the cognates — Old Saxon īs, Old Norse īss, Gothic eis — agree closely). The decisive moment for our word came when the two were compounded: Old English īsġicel, literally “ice-icicle”, “icicle”, is attested before the Norman Conquest. The compound is a doubled, reinforced naming: the speakers of late Old English took their native word for the hanging spike and reattached the obvious word for ice to the front of it, the way one might say “rock-pebble”.

3. Middle English: isikel and its folklore

Middle English inherited the word in forms like isikel, isikil, ikkel, and ikyl (13th–15th centuries), the initial element eroded and re-amalgamated. The word appears in seasonal contexts — winter carols, proverbs, and the complaint-literature of cold — and folk belief attached weather-lore to growing icicles: many and long icicles meant a long, hard winter. In the 15th century isicle-type spellings appear as the unstressed final syllable weakens to the modern -icle, and by the 16th century the spelling icicle is standard: speakers now analyse the word as ice + -icle, a folk etymology that the older written record confirms was half-true — the -icle is really the eroded gicel.

4. The literary career (1590s onwards)

Shakespeare’s winter song closing Love’s Labour’s Lost (c. 1594–95) fixes the word in the canon: “When icicles hang by the wall”. After Shakespeare, icicle is a standard property of winter description in every century. Eighteenth-century naturalists studied icicle growth as part of the new science of cold — the growth of long, clear icicles was one of the curiosities of the Enlightenment’s ice-houses — and Romantic poets aestheticised the “diamond fringes” of eaves. Victorian prose, including Wilde’s The Happy Prince (1888), where “long icicles like crystal daggers hung down from the eaves of the houses” turns the icicle into a dagger of glittering urban poverty, made the word a shorthand for cold that is beautiful and dangerous at once. The image cluster (crystal, daggers, silver, diamonds) is remarkably stable across four centuries: an icicle is sharp as well as shining, which is exactly why Wilde’s simile works.

5. Modern technical and figurative drift

In the 20th century the word went scientific in two directions. Meteorology and engineering now study “icicle formation” as a hazard on aircraft, power lines, and roofs — icicle growth is a classic problem in the mathematics of freezing fronts, the “Stefan problem”, named for the physics of melting and solidification — while the decorative trades borrowed the shape wholesale: icicle lights, icicle embroidery, icicle glass, icicle-blue paint. The folk word has proved remarkably stable: its spelling, pronunciation, and core meaning have not changed since the 16th century, and the plural icicles, so central to winter scenery, carries unbroken the Old English compound’s double insistence: ice, and more ice.
