The biography of oddments begins not with a noun but with a point: the Proto-Germanic form *uzdaz, 'point, pointed thing,' whose deeper Indo-European ancestry has never been securely demonstrated. The oldest Germanic relatives all keep the physical sense: Old English ord 'spear-point, beginning, vanguard' (now obsolete, though it survives in place-names), Old Saxon ord 'point,' Old High German ort 'point, corner, extremity' (which shifted into the modern German word for 'place'), and Old Norse oddr, 'point of a weapon.' It is a North-West Germanic isogloss — a word native to that corner of the family and shared by no other Indo-European branch, which is why no trustworthy PIE root can be reconstructed for it.

In Old Norse the related oddi took the same word 'point' in a different direction: it came to mean 'triangle,' and from the figure of the triangle, with its single projecting vertex, the word acquired its crucial numerical turn — the 'odd' or third man, the extra party whose casting vote decides a tied dispute. Middle English borrowed odde around 1300 to mean a number that is not even, one that leaves a remainder of one when divided by two. From arithmetic the sense of 'surplus, extra, unmatched' spread to things — the odd glove, the odd coin, an odd job — and only in the sixteenth century did the modern sense 'strange, peculiar, singular' emerge and eventually come to dominate the word. The plural noun odds (sixteenth century) hardened the idea of imbalance into disparity and then into likelihood — 'the odds are against him' — while the phrase odds and ends (eighteenth century) fixed the everyday expression for miscellaneous bits that oddments would later echo.

In 1780 English formed oddment by attaching -ment to odd. The suffix is itself a long immigrant: Latin -mentum (ornamentum, fundamentum) made nouns of instrument, medium, or result; Old French carried it as -ment; and Middle English borrowed it with the Anglo-French vocabulary from the thirteenth century, where it soon became productive on native English bases (enjoyment, atonement, bewilderment). An oddment is therefore literally 'an odd thing,' a noun of result built on the ordinary pattern for nouns of result — in this case, the leftover that results from sorting, selling, or using a larger supply.

  • The coinage: the earliest recorded oddments are accounting and commercial — the surplus pieces of a stock, what remains after the main supply is gone; the single oddment is 'an odd article, a leftover, an extra.'
  • The plural wins: oddments, recorded almost immediately, fixed the word as a synonym of 'odds and ends': miscellaneous remnants of little value; the plural insists on a scatter rather than one curiosity.
  • The trade sense: in printing and bookbinding oddments became the technical name for leftover sheets, waste leaves, and end-matter of a book — a professional hardening of the 'leftover bits' idea, current into the twentieth century.
  • The register: daily conversation prefers 'odds and ends' or 'bits and pieces'; oddments survives in narrative and reflective prose with a faintly old-fashioned, whimsical flavour, still preserving the older sense of odd as unmatched and surplus — each item the third glove of a vanished pair.

In the context sentence, 'He found oddments in his pockets,' the word does exactly the work its history prepared: it names a scatter of trifling leftovers — a stray button, a pencil stub, an old coin — each too trivial to count as a possession, yet together the gentle clutter of ordinary living. The word's long journey, from a spear-point, through the triangle's odd projecting vertex, through the arithmetic oddness of numbers that do not divide evenly, to the surplus and the strange, and finally to the bookish noun for a leftover, is closed in a single plural: a pocketful of oddments, the odd bits that fit no set, each one a small monument to the unpaired and the extra, the surplus things of a life that the world has moved on from without noticing.
