- Old English (10th–11th c.): tumbian "to dance, leap, tumble" — the word is festive, acrobatic and admired; no idea of falling, only of agile turning
- 14th c.: Middle English tumblen keeps the entertainer's sense — "tumblers" are professional dancers and acrobats, crowd-pleasers at fairs — but the falling sense ("to fall head over heels") appears, and the word begins to connote clumsiness
- 16th–17th c.: "tumbler" multiplies — acrobat, pigeon that somersaults in flight, drinking glass that cannot be set down; the shared idea is now objects and creatures that turn over of their own accord
- 18th–19th c.: extended to great natural forces — cataracts and waves tumble; "tumbled" enters geological writing for rocks rolled and worn by water; financial journalism adopts "tumbled" for crashing prices
- late 19th c.: British slang "tumble to" = to understand, to catch on
- 20th c.: "tumble in the hay" — euphemistic-vulgar slang for a quick sexual encounter; "tumble dryer" makes the word a household appliance; "come tumbling down" remains the stock phrase for ruin
- Today: everyday in finance, geology, gymnastics and engineering; the old festive glee survives only in "tumbling" acrobatics, folk-song "walls come tumblin' down", and the name of a blogging platform
