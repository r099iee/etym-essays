Proto-Indo-European *keid- / *koid- "to set in motion, drive, strike, inflame" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; this PIE link is uncertain and debated — see note)
→ Germanic *haifstaz "violence, fury, fierce eagerness, contention" (reconstructed; attested in Gothic haifsts "strife, contention," Old Norse heifst "hatred, rage," Old English hæst "violence, fury")
→ Frankish (West Germanic) *haifst "violence, fury, urgency" (reconstructed, c. 5th–6th c.; borrowed into the Gallo-Romance speech of northern Gaul)
→ Old French haste "haste, speed, urgency" (12th c.)
→ Old French hastif "swift, hasty, rash, impetuous" (12th c.; adjective built on haste)
→ Middle English hasty / hasti "swift, rash, quick-tempered" (recorded early 14th c.; reinforced by the native Old English adjective hæstig "violent, hot-tempered")
→ Modern English hasty "done or acting with excessive speed; hurried" (present-day).

Note on the debate: The Germanic base *haifst- is securely attested across the family (Gothic, Norse, Old English, Old High German), but its deeper PIE ancestry is uncertain: the proposed root *keid-/*koid- "to drive, strike" is speculative, and some scholars treat the Germanic word as an isolated formation of unknown origin. The route into English is a famous two-way traffic: the native Old English hæstig existed, but the modern word hasty comes primarily from Old French hastif, whose haste the Franks themselves had earlier exported to France.
