**BATTER: the beating root that fed kitchen, court, and battlefield** BATTER descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *bhau- "to strike, beat, knock," which scattered striking-words across the ancient world. In the Celtic branch it produced Gaulish *battuere, whence Welsh bathu "to beat, mint" and Irish/Gaelic bata "staff, cudgel," and it was probably from Gaulish that Latin borrowed battuere — a word rare in literary Latin yet vigorous in Vulgar Latin, the everyday speech of soldiers, cooks, and labourers. The word thus travelled from prehistoric Europe into Rome through the Celtic frontier, entering Latin as a living, demotic verb of beating. **The Romance family and the march into English** From that Latin verb flowed the entire Romance family. Old French batre (11th c.) yielded abatre "to beat down" (English abate), bataille from Late Latin battualia, a fencing exercise (English battle), baterie "a beating" (English battery, originally a physical thrashing, later the legal term for unlawful striking, then an artillery unit, then an electrical device), and debatre / combatre (English debate, combat). The simple verb itself, Anglo-Norman batter, entered Middle English as bateren — a frequentative formed from Middle English batten "to beat" plus the iterative suffix -er, so that the word explicitly encoded the idea of beating again and again. Modern English BATTER therefore carries a fossilized grammar of repetition: to batter is to strike until something breaks. **The cooking noun and the sports noun** At the same time, the noun developed from Old French bateure "a beating": batter the cooking paste is literally "the beaten mixture" — flour, egg, and liquid beaten smooth — a sense that preserved the verb's oldest semantics in the kitchen. The sports noun is a different but kindred branch: English bat (the cudgel, c. 1200) entered from the Celtic striking family, and the baseball or cricket batter is its agent noun — the one who strikes. The two words, verb-batter and sport-batter, are distinct formations that ultimately nod to the same prehistoric root meaning "to strike." **From siege to law to battery** The verb fed such compounds as battering-ram (1610s), the massive beam swung repeatedly to breach a wall — the pure military embodiment of the word. In law, "battery" (from the same Old French baterie) became the unlawful beating of a person, paired with "assault"; in artillery, the battery became the unit of guns delivering repeated fire; and in the eighteenth century, when Benjamin Franklin arranged Leyden jars in series, the word battery was transferred to the electrical device that stores and delivers force. The chain of transfers is coherent: every step names a thing that stores or delivers beating force. **The modern social sense** In the twentieth century the word acquired its sharpest modern sense: from 1962, "battered" and "batter" entered the vocabulary of domestic abuse — battered wives, battered-woman syndrome — describing the repeated, destructive violence against a person that the verb has always meant. The word had always been available for this sense; the social movement of the 1960s and 1970s simply gave it institutional form. In the goblins' song, "Batter and beat!" the word's ancient violence is fully alive: it is the sound of an endless, crushing assault that intends to leave nothing standing, the same force the Romans heard in battuere and the English in the battering-ram.
