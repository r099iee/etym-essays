Every bale of hay preserves a verb. The word HAY descends from an ancient Indo-European root whose literal meaning was not 'dried grass' but the physical action of cutting: PIE *kewh₂-, 'to strike, hew, cut, forge'. At the root core, the semantic idea is the blow of a blade or a hammer — an act of striking that splits, trims, or fells. The Germanic branch carried this sense into a vigorous verb, Proto-Germanic *hawwaną, 'to hew, cut down', and from that verb was born the noun *hawją, literally 'that which is cut (down)', the mown harvest itself. The word thus began as a nominalization of the cutting act: hay is, etymologically, the cut stuff.

The noun emerged inside the Germanic word family in parallel with the verb. Proto-Germanic *hawją developed the sense of mown grass for feeding animals and descended through the North-West Germanic dialects into Old English hīeġ (West Saxon) and hēg (Anglian), forms shaped by i-umlaut out of *hawi. Alongside it the verb continued as Old English hēawan, giving modern hew. The two words — hay, the cut grass, and hew, the cutting — are doublets of a single root, and this ancient verb-noun pairing explains why the etymology of hay is inseparable from the axe.

  • Sound shifts: PIE *k- became Germanic *h- by Grimm's Law; the vowel of *kewh₂- developed into *-aw- in Germanic, and the i-umlaut triggered by the ja-stem suffix fronted the vowel of *hawi to give hīeġ; Middle English hei, hey, hay reflects the coalescence of the forms by the 15th century.

In Middle English the word settled as hei/hey/hay, and with the rise of enclosed farming and horse-drawn husbandry it became a staple of the agricultural lexicon. The semantic drift from 'cut' to 'cured forage' was complete: by Early Modern English hay named the product, not the action, and the phrase make hay while the sun shines (attested by the early 1600s) fused the drying need for sunshine with the proverb of seizing opportunity.

  • Semantic phases: (1) PIE *kewh₂- 'strike, hew'; (2) PGmc *hawwaną 'to hew' and *hawją 'the cut thing'; (3) OE hīeġ 'mown grass, hay'; (4) ME hay as commercial fodder; (5) Modern hay — feed, idiom, and synecdoche for rural life.

Today hay is a homely but layered word: agricultural commodity, equestrian feed, and a cluster of idioms — hit the hay, haywire, hay fever, that ain't hay — that carry the ancient 'cutting' root into modern colloquial English while retaining the scent of the mown field.
