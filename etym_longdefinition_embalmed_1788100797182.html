EMBALMED is the past participle of the verb embalm, "to treat a dead body with preservatives — historically balm, resins, spices, and aromatic oils — so as to delay or prevent decay". In the sentence that supplies this note — from Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince, where the Swallow describes the great King lying in his painted coffin, "wrapped in yellow linen, and embalmed with spices" — the word evokes the funerary grandeur of ancient Egypt, with its mummified Pharaohs and spice-laden wrappings. That is the primary, and for most readers the only, sense: the deliberate preservation of a corpse as a rite, a science, or a professional service.

Primary senses.

  • Ancient and ritual embalming: preservation of a body with balms, myrrh, aloes, natron, and cloth wrappings, as practised in Egypt from about 2600 BCE onward by priest-embalmers over a ritual period; the Bible's Genesis 50 recounts that Joseph "commanded his servants the physicians to embalm his father", and that "the Egyptians mourned for him threescore and ten days" — the oldest continuous textual tradition of the practice that the word names.
  • Modern mortuary embalming: since the mid-nineteenth century, the professional replacement of the body's blood with preservative fluids (originally arsenical solutions, later formaldehyde-based compounds) injected through the arteries, so that a body can be viewed, transported, and buried without decomposition. In the United States embalming is near-universal in funeral practice; in much of Europe it is rare or optional.
  • Metaphorical and transferred senses: anything "embalmed" is preserved unchanged, frozen, or kept artificially fresh — an "embalmed memory", an "embalmed institution", a landscape "embalmed in amber" or "embalmed in sentiment". The image is of something arrested mid-life, kept sweet-smelling but no longer alive; the connotation is usually gentle nostalgia or, in critical usage, sterile stasis.

Secondary senses and register.

  • In anatomy and museum science, embalmed describes specimens treated with fixatives for teaching or display; embalmed cadavers are the standard material of medical dissection.
  • In historical anecdote, "embalmed beef" was the notorious nickname of the tinned beef supplied to US troops in the Spanish-American War of 1898, alleged to be so over-processed and chemically treated as to resemble embalmed flesh — the word thereby gained a brief, bitter flavour of official negligence and corruption.
  • The participle functions adjectivally in fixed phrases — embalmed body, embalmed remains, freshly embalmed — and can be modified for time or manner: "skilfully embalmed", "newly embalmed".
  • In religious history, the related idea of bodily preservation (the "incorruptible" bodies of canonised saints, the relics of martyrs) kept the word close to piety for centuries: to say a saint lay "embalmed" was to say grace itself had preserved the flesh.

Semantic bridges. The core sense chain runs: (1) "to steep a body in balm or spices" — the word's literal anatomy, em- "in" + balm; (2) "to preserve a corpse from decay"; (3) "to preserve anything from change"; (4) "to keep something artificially alive and dead at once". Each step away from the funeral parlour keeps the perfume of sanctity and antiquity: an embalmed thing is fragrant, preserved, and sacred — or, in ironic modern use, preserved past the point of life.
