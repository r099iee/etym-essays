chukker (also chukka, chucker) is the name for one of the timed periods into which a game of polo is divided. In standard outdoor polo a match is normally made up of six chukkers, each lasting seven and a half minutes, with brief intervals between them; the team that has scored more goals at the end of the sixth chukker wins. The word is a borrowing from Hindi chakkar “circle, turn, round”, which goes back to Sanskrit cakra “wheel, circle”, and it belongs to the large family of English words — chakra, chakram, charkha, cycle, wheel — that descend from the Indo-European wheel.

Primary sense: a period of play. A chukker is not a fixed amount of action but a fixed amount of time. Play begins when the umpire throws in the ball, or when a goal has been scored and the teams line up again, and ends when the bell sounds. Because the clock does not stop for goals, penalties or injuries, a chukker has a brisk, continuous rhythm. Teams change ends after each goal, so the direction of play is constantly reversing, which reinforces the word’s faint sense of a “turn” or “round” — an echo of its Hindi source. In tournament play the number of chukkers can vary with the level of the competition, but six is the standard for outdoor polo; arena polo commonly uses four shorter chukkers.

Match structure and rules. Polo is played by two teams of four riders, each player carrying a long mallet and aiming to drive a small ball between the opponents’ goalposts. The chukker is the basic unit of the match, and much of the strategy turns on managing the horses across six of them: a player may change ponies between chukkers, and the best teams plan their string of ponies so that each horse runs only a limited number of chukkers. The interval between chukkers is short, usually a few minutes, and the half-time interval comes after the third chukker. Penalties are taken from set distances and can consume time within a chukker, but they do not extend it. The word therefore belongs to the technical vocabulary of the game as firmly as mallet, pony, goal and handicap.

The equestrian and colonial background. Modern field polo was developed in India, where versions of the game had been played for centuries in Manipur and neighbouring regions. British cavalry officers and tea planters encountered it in the nineteenth century and helped to codify and export it. The Calcutta Polo Club, founded in 1862, is often described as one of the oldest polo clubs in the world; the rules were standardised in the 1870s, and the game spread to England, Argentina, the United States and Australasia. Chukker entered English in this colonial sporting context, at the end of the nineteenth century, as a term for the period of play. It is thus a small linguistic souvenir of the British presence in India, alongside polo itself, pukka, tiffin and verandah.

Etymology and cognates. The Hindi word chakkar means a circle, a turn, a round, and also a spin or a dizzy spell. It descends from Sanskrit cakra, “wheel, circle, disc”, a word familiar in English through chakra (an energy centre in yoga), chakram (a circular throwing weapon), charkha (a spinning wheel) and the learned cycle. All go back to the reconstructed Indo-European form *kʷékʷlos, “wheel”, a reduplicated derivative of the root *kʷel- “to turn”. The same root lies behind Greek kyklos “circle” and English wheel, so chukker is a distant cousin of words that have nothing to do with sport. The English spelling with -er reflects the Anglo-Indian rendering of the Hindi pronunciation; the spelling chukka is closer to the Hindi form and is now the more common British spelling, especially in the compound chukka boot.

Secondary sense: “turn, round” in Indian English. In Indian English chakkar is a busy, everyday word: it can mean a turn or rotation, a short walk or detour, a round of a process, a dizzy spell, or a clever scheme. The polo sense is a specialised narrowing of this cluster: a chukker is one “turn” of the match. English has not borrowed the wider Hindi senses to any great extent, but the underlying image of circular motion explains why the same word can name a wheel, a spin, a stratagem and a period of play.

The chukka boot. The word has a second life in fashion. A chukka boot (or chukka) is an ankle-high boot, usually of suede or leather, with two or three pairs of laces and a relatively thin sole. It was originally associated with polo players, who needed light, flexible footwear around the stables, and it entered civilian menswear in the mid-twentieth century. The name is a direct transfer from the polo period: the boot was the kind worn for a chukka. In this compound the spelling chukka is standard, and many wearers have no idea that the word has anything to do with horses or India.

Figurative and journalistic use. Outside polo, chukker is occasionally used figuratively for a round, stint or session of activity, especially in sports writing or in loose journalistic prose: “the next chukker of negotiations”, “a tough chukker for the defence”. Such uses are self-conscious and depend on the reader recognising the polo term. The word has never become a general synonym for period, and it is not used in ordinary conversation outside polo and fashion.

Register and modern usage. Chukker is a technical term with a slightly old-fashioned, upper-class, equestrian flavour. It is fully current in polo commentary and in the polo industry, where its spelling and pronunciation are stable. In general English it is rare, and the variant chukka is more familiar because of the boot. The word should not be confused with chucker in cricket, which is a bowler whose action is judged illegal; that chucker comes from the verb chuck “to throw” and is unrelated.

  • Fashion sense: a chukka boot, originally polo footwear, now a casual classic.
  • Indian-English source sense: chakkar, a turn, circle, spin or round.
  • Figurative sense: a round or stint of activity, used self-consciously in journalism.
