Tom-cat is a young compound built from two very old words. It is first recorded in 1809, but to understand it fully we must follow each root — Tom and cat — separately, because the two strands of history meet only in the 18th century.

The cat half

The English word cat is ancient, inherited from Old English catt (male) and catte (female), recorded well before the 10th century. Old English in turn took the word from Late Latin cattus, which appears in writing around 350 CE in the agricultural writer Palladius, alongside the earlier feminine catta. Unlike most Latin animal words, cattus has no secure Indo-European ancestry: the domestic cat was a late arrival in Europe, and the word was borrowed along with the animal, probably from the Afro-Asiatic world. Proposed sources include Egyptian čaute, Nubian kadîs, and Berber kaddîska, but none is securely proven, and the OED marks the origin as uncertain. Because the cat was adopted into European life long after the PIE era, no PIE root exists for it — a rare case of a common noun with no Indo-European pedigree.

From Late Latin the word spread across the western languages — Germanic *kattuz, French chat, Spanish gato, Italian gatto — and English has kept the simple form cat unchanged for over a thousand years. Semantically it stayed equally stable: the domestic feline, mouser, and companion. What changed was the animal's reputation — from the practical farm mouser of the Middle Ages, through the witch's familiar and the unlucky crossing-cat of the early modern period, to the treasured pet of the 19th and 20th centuries — but the word itself never wavered.

The Tom half

Tom begins not in Germanic but in the Semitic world. It descends from the Aramaic word təʾōmā, “twin”, which entered Greek as Thōmâs because one of the twelve apostles, Thomas, was known by that epithet — traditionally understood as “the twin”. Greek Thōmâs passed into Latin as Thomas and so into English, where Thomas became one of the most common Christian names of the Middle Ages. By the 13th century the familiar short form Tom is attested, and the name proliferated in proverbs, ballads, and the generic “Tom” of everyday speech — so thoroughly that “Tom” came to stand for any ordinary man, as in “Tom, Dick and Harry” and the many trade and soldier names (Tommy, tom-tit, tom-turkey). It was this generic masculine value that made “Tom” available to name the male of an animal.

The meeting of the two

The decisive moment is 1760, when the anonymous London novella “The Life and Adventures of a Cat” appeared, telling the picaresque story of a male cat named Tom. The book was extraordinarily popular and was reprinted for decades, and it fixed “Tom” in the public imagination as the natural name for a male cat. Within half a century the compound tomcat is recorded (1809), and by the mid-19th century it is the standard everyday term for the male cat, complete with the folk knowledge of his behaviours — territorial spraying, fighting, roaming, and the loud yowling of the mating season.

In the 20th century the compound produces its own derivations. The verb to tomcat (“to tomcat around”) appears in slang for a man who roams in pursuit of women, transferring the cat's mating behaviour to human masculine prowling. The US Navy's F-14 Tomcat (in service 1974–2006) borrowed the name for its aggressive, tenacious fighter — a cultural echo of the tom-cat's tough, independent, street-wise image. In contemporary English the spelling splits between tomcat (common in American use) and tom-cat (common in British and literary use, as in the source context), but the meaning is stable: an adult male cat, especially the unneutered male with all his formidable behaviour. The compound thus unites a thousand-year-old cat word of uncertain oriental origin with a Semitic name meaning “twin” — history no one would guess from the burly, yowling creature in the alley, or from the “gigantic tom-cat purring” in the source sentence.
