The lexical history of blundering provides a classic illustration of how Scandinavian loanwords enriched Middle English with vivid physical and behavioral vocabulary, transitioning over centuries from literal sensory deprivation to multifaceted cognitive critique.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Germanic Cognates:

  • Indo-European Base: The root descends from Proto-Indo-European *bʰlendʰ-, meaning "to become turbid, grow dark, blend, blind, or wander in confusion". This versatile root is the ultimate source of a vast Germanic family of vision- and confusion-related words, including blind, blend, blunt, and blond.
  • Proto-Germanic Developments: Proto-Germanic formed the strong verb *blandaną ("to mix, mingle") and the adjective *blindaz ("blind, unseeing"), alongside weak formations connected with sensory confusion.

2. Old Norse Transmission and Middle English Emergence:

  • Old Norse Blunda: In Old Norse, the verb blunda meant "to close one's eyes, doze, or walk with closed eyes" (cognate with modern Swedish blunda, "to close one's eyes, ignore").
  • Middle English Adaptation (14th Century): During the 14th century, the root entered Middle English as blundren or blondren, taking on the frequentative suffix -er. It signified "to flounder blindly, stumble heedlessly, act in confusion, or stir things into disorder". In Geoffrey Chaucer's The House of Fame (c. 1380) and Troilus and Criseyde, the verb appears describing people who wander aimlessly or meddle blindly in affairs they do not understand.

3. Early Modern Expansion: From Movement to Cognitive Folly:

  • Renaissance Drama and Prose: During the late 16th and 17th centuries, the participle blundering gained immense descriptive currency. Playwrights used it to contrast stealthy, agile movement with loud, incompetent crashing through the dark.
  • The Noun Blunder (c. 1650s): In the mid-17th century, English back-formed the standalone noun blunder to denote a gross, stupid, or ridiculous mistake. Consequently, blundering acquired its modern double resonance: describing both physical stumbling in darkness and intellectual incompetence in governance, diplomacy, and science.

4. Literary Mastery in Tolkien's The Hobbit:

  • Atmospheric Panic in Mirkwood: In chapter 8 of The Hobbit (1937), Tolkien employs the verbal noun to capture the panic of Thorin's company when the magical elven feast vanishes, plunging them into pitch blackness: "After blundering frantically in the gloom, falling over logs, bumping crash into trees, and shouting and calling..."
  • Kinetic Intensity: Tolkien's use of blundering perfectly combines both the ancient sensory meaning (moving with blind eyes in the black forest) and the behavioral meaning (folly that alerts all the monstrous spiders of Mirkwood for miles around).
