cereals is the plural noun denoting cultivated grasses grown for their edible starchy seeds — wheat, rice, maize (corn), barley, oats, rye, millet, sorghum — and, by extension, the seeds themselves and foods made from them. In Durant's sentence it means grain crops as a dietary staple: cooking softened cellulose and starch so humans could rely on grain-seeds plus vegetables instead of raw foraged plants and hunted meat.

Botanically, cereals are members of the Poaceae (grass) family selected over millennia for large, threshable, storable caryopses. Nutritionally they supply carbohydrate (starch), some protein (gluten in wheat), B-vitamins, and fibre. Civilisationally they are the foundation of agriculture, cities, taxation, and writing — surplus grain can be stored, counted, and taxed.

  • Agricultural-botanical sense (core): The crop plants themselves: wheat fields, cereal cultivation, cereal yields. Includes true cereals (wheat, rice, maize, barley, oats, rye) and sometimes pseudocereals (quinoa, buckwheat, amaranth) by analogy, though strictly pseudocereals are not grasses. Durant uses this sense: turning to cereals = adopting grain farming.
  • Commodity-food sense: The harvested grain as bulk foodstuff: sacks of cereals, global cereal trade, cereal deficits. FAO statistics use cereals in exactly this way. Bridge: plant stands for its product by metonymy.
  • Nutritional-dietary sense: Grain-foods as a food group: whole cereals, refined cereals, cereal fibre. Dieticians contrast cereals with vegetables, pulses, meat — the same contrast Durant draws. Modern nuance adds whole vs. refined, glycaemic index, gluten.
  • Breakfast-cereal sense (chiefly American, 20th c.): Processed ready-to-eat grain foods: cornflakes, porridge oats, muesli. "He eats cereals for breakfast." Bridge: narrowing from raw material to branded product; unknown in Durant's 1930s civilisational history except as porridge.
  • Industrial-technical sense: Cereal chemistry, cereal science — milling, malting, brewing, baking. Barley for beer, rye for whisky, maize for ethanol are cereals in this industrial chain.
  • Singular vs. plural note: Cereal (singular) can mean one grain type (wheat is a cereal) or one serving (a bowl of cereal). Cereals (plural) normally means the class or multiple types. Durant's "cereals and vegetables" is the class plural paralleling vegetables.
  • Cultural-symbolic resonance: Bread, rice, and maize are civilisational emblems — staff of life, paddy culture, corn mother. To turn to cereals is to turn to settlement, seasons, granaries, and gods of grain (Ceres, Demeter).

In sum, cereals names the grass-seeds that made civilisation possible: storable solar energy in edible form. Cooking unlocked them by gelatinising starch and softening cellulose, as Durant notes; farming multiplied them; cities rose on their surplus. Whether as wheat in Mesopotamia, rice in China, or maize in Mesoamerica, cereals are humanity's chief reliance — then and now.
