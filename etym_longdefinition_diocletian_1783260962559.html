Diocletian (proper noun) — the conventional English name of the Roman emperor who reigned from 284 to 305 CE, the full Latin form of whose name was Caius Aurelius Valerius Diocletianus. He is remembered as the great reformer-emperor who ended the Crisis of the Third Century, created the Tetrarchy (the rule of four), reorganized the army, the provinces, and the coinage, and initiated the last and fiercest persecution of the Christians. On this card the name appears in Edward Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, where the Eastern court of the successors of the Western empire "still exhibited the splendour of Diocletian and Constantine" — a phrase that makes Diocletian a byword for late-imperial pomp and bureaucratic autocracy amid slow decay.

Sense 1 — The historical emperor and his reforms. Born Diocles in Dalmatia around 244 CE, of humble family, Diocletian rose through the army and was proclaimed emperor by his troops in November 284 after the murder of Numerian. His reign is famous for the administrative and military restructuring of the empire: the subdivision of provinces, the multiplication of imperial officials, the reform of taxation and the coinage, the strengthening of the frontiers, and above all the institution of the Tetrarchy in 293, whereby the empire was governed by two senior emperors (Augusti, Diocletian and Maximian) and two junior colleagues (Caesars, Galerius and Constantius). His Edict on Maximum Prices (301) attempted, largely in vain, to control inflation by fixing legal ceilings for prices and wages. He abdicated voluntarily in 305 — the first emperor to do so — retiring to his vast palace at Split (Spalatum) in Dalmatia, where he died around 311.

Sense 2 — The persecutor: the Christian register. In Christian memory Diocletian is above all the Great Persecutor. The Edicts of 303–304, issued under the influence of Galerius, launched the most systematic attempt to destroy Christianity the empire ever made: churches were demolished, scriptures burned, clergy imprisoned and executed, and Christians forced to sacrifice to the gods. The so-called "Era of Diocletian" (from his accession year 284) was used for centuries by the church of Egypt to count its martyrs. In this register his name carries a dark religious charge, and church historians such as Lactantius and Eusebius shaped the image of a tyrant that survived through the Middle Ages.

Sense 3 — Historiographical and symbolic senses. In the modern historical and cultural register, "Diocletian" is a synecdoche for the late Roman state itself: for centralized bureaucracy, ceremonial autocracy, and the managed economy. Gibbon treats him as the architect of the "despotism" of the later empire; later historians summarize his work as the transformation of the Principate into the Dominate, in which the emperor became an unapproachable, divine-court sovereign. The phrase "Diocletian's Tetrarchy" and "Diocletian's price edict" are standard terms in administrative and economic history, invoked as classic exemplars of wage-and-price controls and of collegiate imperial rule.

Sense 4 — Architecture and material legacy. The emperor's retirement palace at Split, Croatia, is the best-preserved imperial palace of Late Antiquity, its ruins now forming the historic core of the modern city. In architectural terminology his name also lives on in the Diocletian window (a window divided by two small columns and an arch, derived from the great arched windows of the Baths of Diocletian in Rome), and the Baths of Diocletian (Thermae Diocletiani) gave their name to the church of Santa Maria degli Angeli in Rome. The adjective Diocletianic is the standard scholarly term for the reforms, the persecution, and the era named after him.

Sense 5 — The personal name and its etymology. The name itself is Greek-built: Diocletian comes from Latin Diocletianus, from the Greek personal name Dioklēs (Διοκλῆς), "glory of Zeus", a compound of Dio- (the stem of Zeus) and -klēs ("glory, fame"). The imperial suffix -ianus simply marks "belonging to Diocles". The name thus encodes a Greek religious formula, "glory of Zeus", that the Christianizing later empire made a symbol of pagan autocracy — an irony visible precisely in Gibbon's pairing of Diocletian with Constantine, the first Christian emperor.
