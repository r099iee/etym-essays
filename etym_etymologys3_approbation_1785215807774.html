approbation entered English in the fourteenth century, borrowed from Old French approbacion, which in turn came from Latin approbātiō, "approval, assent, sanction". The Latin noun is the nominalisation of approbāre, "to assent to, to regard as good", a compound of the prepositional prefix ad- ("to, towards") and probāre, "to test, try, prove, demonstrate as good" — the very source of English prove, proof, probe, and probation. Behind probāre stands the Latin adjective probus, "good, honest, upright", attested from the early plays of Plautus in the second century BCE.

The deep root. Scholars reconstruct for probus a Proto-Indo-European compound, generally written *pro-bʰu-o-s, built from the adverb *per-/*pro- "forward, in front" and the root *bʰuH- "to grow, become, be" — the same "become/be" root that underlies Greek phýein "to grow" (whence physics, physiology), Sanskrit bhávati "he becomes" (whence Bhārata, the land of the Bharatas), Old English bēon "to be", and Latin fui "I was". The compound would mean literally "being in front", "standing out", hence "excelling", hence "good". This reconstruction is elegant and widely accepted, but the exact shape of the compound — and especially the proposed Sanskrit cognate prabhū "mighty, lord" — is debated; specialists treat the Indic link as possible rather than certain. The Latin-to-English chain itself, however, is entirely attested.

From testing to approval. The semantic heart of the word is the Roman idea that to approve is to prove. Probāre first meant "to test, to make trial of" — a man probatus was one who had been tested and found good — and approbāre meant "to apply the test and assent". The noun approbātiō accordingly meant the verdict of a satisfied judge: the passing of a thing after examination. This is why the word, alone among the approval family, still smells faintly of the courtroom and the assay: approbation is what a work receives after it has been put to the proof.

Life in English.

  • 14th–16th centuries. The word entered English in the 1300s with a strong legal and ecclesiastical flavour — the "approbation" of statutes, bishops, and licences — meaning approval that had authority behind it.
  • 17th–19th centuries. It broadened into the dignified general word for considered approval, flourishing in moral, theological, and parliamentary prose. "With the approbation of" became a standard phrase for weighty, considered endorsement; religious writers spoke of the approbation of God or of a good conscience; the British moralists (Hutcheson, Hume, Smith) made "approbation" a technical term for the sentiment by which we approve virtue. In the same era it was a staple of biographical encomium — the precise register of Mill's dedication, where Harriet Taylor's approbation is "my chief reward".
  • 20th century to today. Plain approval took over the ordinary registers, and approbation retreated into formal, academic, and deliberately elevated use, acquiring a slight archaism and dignity. Its antonym disapprobation shared the same fate.

Sound and form. The word entered Middle English as approbacioun and settled to approbation under classical Latin influence by the early modern period; the modern RP pronunciation stresses the -ba- and the final -tion syllable. The spelling preserves the Latin pp- and -ātiō, a small monument to the word's learned, bookish ancestry.

Mill's use. In the dedication to On Liberty (1859), approbation is deliberately chosen as the high, formal counterpart of incitement: she roused him, and her verdict was his reward. No word for casual liking would serve; the sentence needs the tested, weighty, almost judicial connotation of approbation — approval that could only come from the one mind whose sense of truth he trusted absolutely. The word's history, from Roman proving-ground to Victorian moral philosophy, is written into that single sentence.
