*apo- + *strebʰ-
"away, off" + "to twist, turn, wind"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th c. BCE)
ἀπό + στρέφω
"away" + "to turn, twist"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 5th c. BCE)
ἀποστρέφω
"to turn away, avert, turn back"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 4th c. BCE)
ἀποστροφή
"a turning away, rhetorical diversion"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. CE)
apostrophē
"a sudden turning aside to address"
↓
Middle French (c. 1540s)
apostropher
"to address rhetorically; mark with an elision"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1610s–1620s)
apostrophize
"to address by apostrophe; mark with an apostrophe"
↓
Modern English (17th c.–present)
apostrophized
"addressed directly (absent/object); punctuated"

Note: The Greek noun ἀποστροφή (apostrophḗ) was formed from the prefix ἀπό (apó, 'away, off') and the verbal noun στροφή (strophḗ, 'a turn, twist', also root of the poetic strophe). In rhetoric it meant turning away from the judges, while in printing it was adapted for the mark (') showing where an elided vowel had been turned away.
