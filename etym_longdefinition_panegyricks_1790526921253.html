Panegyricks (the archaic seventeenth- and eighteenth-century spelling of panegyrics, plural of panegyric) in the source sentence from Laurence Sterne's A Sentimental Journey (1768) refers to formal, lavish, and highly stylized public tributes, laudatory placards, or written encomiums posted upon doors to flatter influential patrons, hosts, or civic leaders. Sterne contrasts Panegyricks directly with Pasquinades (biting satirical lampoons), illustrating the dual sycophantic and libelous burdens of the conventional European tourist, who must paste flattering praises at one door while managing satirical barbs at another. In this eighteenth-century context, the archaic spelling with -ck- reflects contemporary printing conventions for Greek-derived nouns ending in -ic.

In general English lexicography, a panegyric (plural Panegyricks or panegyrics) is a formal public speech, oratorical address, or elaborate literary composition published in high, unreserved praise of a monarch, military hero, deceased statesman, saint, or institutional accomplishment. The term denotes praise that is public, structured, ceremonial, and often rhetorical or hyperbolic in character, as opposed to private, spontaneous compliments.

  • Classical Rhetorical Sense: Formal oratorical encomium delivered at public assemblies: In ancient Greco-Roman rhetoric, a formal oration delivered before a assembled multitude (such as the Olympic festivals or Roman senate) celebrating national virtues, heroic exploits, or imperial benefactions (e.g., Isocrates' celebrated Panegyricus; Pliny the Younger's Panegyricus addressed to Emperor Trajan). The semantic foundation lies in the Greek panēgyris, an all-inclusive national festival where public unity and collective ideals were ceremonially proclaimed.
  • Literary and Poetic Sense: Laudatory verse and courtly dedications: Elaborate poems, odes, or prose prefaces composed by court poets, dramatists, and scholars to celebrate monarchs, patrons, or aristocratic benefactors (e.g., John Dryden's Panegyrick to His Sacred Majesty on his coronation; seventeenth-century poets composing panegyrics for royal patrons). In this genre, rhetorical brilliance and classical allusion were deployed to justify political authority and secure financial patronage.
  • Modern Extended Sense: Any effusive, unrestrained, or extravagant praise: In everyday contemporary discourse, any speech, book review, testimonial, or article that heaps lavish, unqualified acclaim upon a person, artistic work, or technological breakthrough (e.g., the critic delivered an enthusiastic panegyric on the author's new symphony; the CEO's retirement speech turned into a prolonged panegyric).
  • Pejorative / Ironic Sense: Sycophantic or uncritical flattery: When used with skeptical, critical, or satirical inflection, the term implies obsequious adulation, whitewashing, or propaganda that ignores defects and exaggerates virtues (e.g., dismissing the official state biography as nothing more than a hollow panegyric; the author refused to pen a sycophantic panegyric for the corrupt regime).
  • Religious / Hagiographic Sense: Liturgical praise of saints and martyrs: In the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic traditions, formal sermons or homilies delivered on a saint's feast day commemorating their spiritual triumphs, ascetic virtues, and miracles for the edification of the faithful.

Stylistically, Panegyricks belongs to classical, historical, and literary registers. Its archaic -ck- spelling specifically evokes the Augustan age of English literature (Dryden, Pope, Swift, Sterne), when rhetoric, courtly patronage, and vicious political satire stood in constant, dynamic tension.
