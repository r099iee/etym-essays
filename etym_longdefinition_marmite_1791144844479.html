Marmite in the source passage (“a petite __________” of lice) is the French cooking vessel: a small covered pot, typically earthenware or metal, squat and lidded, used for slow simmering — and by metonymy the soup or stew cooked in it. A petite marmite is both the little pot and the classic clear soup petite marmite (beef broth with vegetables and marrow-bone, served with the pot’s contents as garnish) of bourgeois French cookery. Durant’s image is deliberately grotesque-precious: lice gathered in quantity, simmered like consommé, and devoured “with shouts of joy, as enemies of the human race” — revenge served as haute cuisine.

The word lives a famous double life in English. Alongside the culinary vessel (literary, food-historical: “a simmering marmite”), there is the trademark Marmite (capital M): the dark, salty yeast-extract spread invented in Britain in 1902, named precisely AFTER the little pot because it was sold in earthenware jars shaped like one. From the spread grew Britain’s best-loved metaphor of divisiveness: the “Marmite effect” (1990s advertising: “Love it or hate it”), now general slang — “he’s Marmite” means he splits opinion, no middle ground.

  • Vessel sense (the passage sense): “an earthen marmite,” “the stew bubbling in the marmite.” Squat, lidded, heat-keeping; the peasant and bourgeois workhorse before the stockpot. French marmite norvégienne (Norwegian/hay-box cooker) extends the idea to heat-retention cooking.
  • Dish sense: petite marmite (the soup), marmite dieppoise (Norman fish soup/stew) — pot-for-contents metonymy, exactly like “the kettle” for tea or “paella” for pan-and-rice.
  • Spread sense (trademark): Marmite — yeast extract, B-vitamins, umami depth, toast’s best friend or worst enemy. Lower-case marmite for the generic is resisted by the trademark owner but common in speech (“vegemite vs marmite” debates span the Commonwealth: British Marmite, New Zealand Marmite (sweeter), Australian Vegemite (different brand, similar idea)).
  • Metaphor sense: “Marmite politics, Marmite pop star” — anything polarising. Lexicographers record Marmite as adjective/noun of divisiveness (“a real Marmite character”). The metaphor needs no gloss in Britain; elsewhere it travels with explanation.
  • Grammar and form: countable (“two marmites”); attributive in marmite soup, Marmite jar, Marmite soldiers (toast strips). French feminine (la marmite); English genderless. Pronunciation: vessel /mɑːˈmiːt/ (near-French /maʁmit/); spread /ˈmɑːmaɪt/ in RP.
  • Polysemy bridge: pot → contents (metonymy) → branded spread named for the pot’s SHAPE (the jar honours the vessel) → divisiveness metaphor (the spread’s taste splits the nation). Each step is motivated; none is predictable — the classic chain that makes polysemy worth mapping.

For review, hold the pot in mind first: a squat lidded simmerer. The lice-soup joke, the fish stew, the yeast jar, and the love-or-hate slogan all hang from its two little handles.
