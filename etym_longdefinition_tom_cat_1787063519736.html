Tom-cat (also spelled tomcat) names an adult male domestic cat, specifically an unneutered male. The word carries with it a bundle of folk knowledge about male cat behaviour: the tom is bigger and more heavily built than the female, with a broader head, thicker cheeks, and a stronger scent; he patrols a large territory, sprays urine to mark it, fights other males for access to females, and, in the mating season, produces the famous loud yowling that has been likened — in the source sentence — to the rumble of a gigantic cat purring. Because the source context speaks of “a gigantic __________ purring”, the word contributes the idea of a large, powerfully built male cat.

Primary senses

  • A male domestic cat: the core meaning, recorded from 1809. Often contrasted with queen (the female cat) and kitten (the young cat).
  • An unneutered male cat: in modern cat-keeping, a distinction is drawn between the entire (unneutered) tom and the neutered male, and “tom-cat” is increasingly used for the entire male with its characteristic behavioural traits.
  • The behaviours themselves — tomcatting: a verb and gerund attested from the 20th century, meaning to roam or prowl about, especially in search of sexual conquest; applied to the cat first, then, figuratively and in slang, to a man who wanders in pursuit of women.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Slang — a womanizer: “to tomcat (around)” = to behave like a male cat in heat, pursuing sexual partners; usually mildly comic or disapproving, and it keeps the feline image of prowling, yowling, and territorial competition.
  • Military and aviation — the F-14 Tomcat: the US Navy's swing-wing fighter, named for the male cat's toughness, aggression, and fighting ability; the name carries the tom-cat's aura of power and tenacity.
  • In cat fancy and veterinary vocabulary: “tom” alone is the standard short form; “gib” is the dialectal word for a neutered male cat; “tomcat” is the informal everyday term for the entire male.
  • Literature and folklore: the tom-cat appears in proverb and story as a type of independent, self-willed male animal — the tom that owns the streets and answers to nobody, an image the source sentence's “gigantic tom-cat” exploits.

Why “Tom”?

The specific pairing of Tom with cat is widely credited to the anonymous 1760 novella “The Life and Adventures of a Cat”, whose hero is a tom named Tom; the enormous popularity of the book fixed “Tom” as the generic name for a male cat in the English-speaking world, and “tomcat” itself is first recorded in 1809. “Tom” had long served English as a generic name for an ordinary man (as in “Tom, Dick and Harry”), and it was already used for the male of other animals (tom-tit, tom-turkey), so the transfer was natural. The personal name Tom itself descends from Thomas, ultimately from the Aramaic word təʾōmā, meaning “twin”.

Semantic boundaries and register

Tom-cat differs from cat (which names the species or any member of it, male or female) and from feline (the formal, biological word) by specifying sex and, in practice, the masculine behaviours of roaming, spraying, fighting, and yowling. Queen names the female; mouser names a working cat by its job; moggy is the affectionate British slang for any ordinary cat. Tom-cat is informal but standard, at home in veterinary conversations, children's stories, and literary description alike.

Etymology in brief

Tom-cat is a compound of Tom (the generic male name, from Thomas, from Aramaic təʾōmā “twin”) and cat (from Old English catt, from Late Latin cattus). The word is first recorded in 1809. See the etymology fields for the separate chains of both roots and the full essay.
