- Old English era — stones are thrown, not measured: stān and þrāwan live as separate working words for missiles and hurling, with bowshot and spear-cast doing the measuring.
- Middle English — the cast is born: a stone’s cast appears for a short distance, weighing nearness by the arm’s throw in chronicles and devotional prose.
- Early Modern — scripture fixes it: translators render the gospel garden scene as about a stone’s cast, giving the phrase solemn biblical weight that lasts for centuries.
- 18th–19th c. — throw overtakes cast: a stone’s throw becomes the dominant colloquial form in novels and travel writing, while cast retreats into archaism and cricket.
- 20th c. — the everyday near-measure: guidebooks, journalism, and estate agents make it the friendly promise of convenience, rarely thrown, always near.
- Today — body-scale nearness in a metric world: still favoured by novelists like Tolkien for landscape pacing, warm and rural against kilometres and GPS pins.
