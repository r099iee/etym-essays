The sentence comes from the Foreword of a series volume, and it discusses a claim made by Ian Gordon in an earlier book of the series, The Movement of English Prose: namely, that the style and rhythm of English prose displayed "a throb of continuity over a millennium and more". The word is used figuratively, but with all its physical force intact. English prose is imagined not as a set of documents but as a living organism whose heart has beaten unbroken for over a thousand years. The sensory core of throb - the regular, insistent beating of a pulse - is transferred to literary history, so that continuity itself pulses beneath the surface of the language. The tone is vital, organic, almost affectionate: a throb is steady and reassuring, not violent; it is the deep rhythmic vitality that connects generation to generation, the sense that the language's heartbeat never stopped and never faltered. To say that a prose tradition "throbs" with continuity is to grant it life, warmth and a kind of loving constancy. Etymologically and phonetically, throb is a word of English origin: it appears in Middle English around 1400 as throbbe(n), "to beat, to palpitate", and its origin is recorded by the dictionaries as obscure, with the overwhelmingly likely explanation being that it is echoic - sound-symbolic, formed in English to imitate the dull repeated beat it names. It belongs to the native cluster of th- words for blows and impacts (thud, thump, thwack, thrash) and has no reconstructed Indo-European ancestor. PRIMARY SENSES OF THROB TODAY:
  • To beat or pulsate strongly and steadily (verb, intransitive), of the heart or pulse: the oldest and most central sense - "his heart throbbed with the effort"; the regular, forceful beating of living tissue.
  • To pulsate with pain (verb), of a wound, limb, head, or tooth: a throbbing ache is a dull, rhythmic, insistent pain that seems to beat in time with the pulse - "her head throbbed", "the wound throbbed through the night".
  • To pulsate rhythmically (verb), of inanimate things: machines, engines, music, bass lines, the hull of a ship - anything that produces or seems to produce a regular, vibrating beat; a city is said to "throb with life".
  • To be filled with intense emotion or activity (verb, figurative): "the hall throbbed with excitement", "a city throbbing with energy" - the transfer of the heartbeat image to collective emotional life.
  • A strong regular pulsation (noun): "the throb of the war-drum", "the throb of the engine" - a single beat or a continuing rhythm, concrete or mechanical.
  • A pulsation of pain (noun): "a dull throb in his leg" - the ache itself, counted in beats.
  • A pulse of emotion or vitality (noun, figurative): "a throb of joy", "a throb of nostalgia" - a sudden, felt surge of feeling; and, at the most abstract, our passage's "a throb of continuity", where a whole cultural tradition is granted the steady heartbeat of living continuance.
  • In compounds and derivatives: throbbing (adjective and noun - "a throbbing headache", "the throbbing of the drums"); throbby (informal, "pulsing, rhythmic"); heart-throb (16th-17th c. "a beat of the heart") and its modern heir heartthrob (20th c., especially the 1960s: a celebrity - most often a film star or pop singer - whose good looks and charm make the heart "throb").
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE: Because throb was coined as an echo of a beat, all its senses grow directly out of one central image: the regular, forceful pulsation of a living heart. The extension from heart to pain is natural - a wound or an inflamed limb pulses in time with the circulation - and from pain to machinery is equally natural, since an engine, a drum or a bass line reproduces the same insistent rhythm. The figurative uses - a city throbbing with life, a tradition displaying "a throb of continuity" - are all re-applications of the heartbeat to collective or abstract subjects, and they keep the physiological warmth of the original: to throb is to be alive. Against its near-synonyms, throb alone combines a living body, a strong steady rhythm and a hint of emotional intensity; pulse is neutral and quasi-technical, beat is generic, palpitation is rapid and anxious, vibration is mechanical and lifeless, thrum is a soft continuous droning, and drumming or pounding are heavier and more percussive.
