• Byway: Similar because both name minor secondary roads off the main routes; differs because byway is the general term (and in America a designated scenic route), while bye-lanes are specifically narrow enclosed lanes of the English countryside.
  • Byroad: Similar because both are secondary roads avoided by through traffic; differs because a byroad may still carry cars and be metalled, while bye-lanes suggest grass, ruts, and hedges too tight for confident driving.
  • Bypath: Similar because both are unfrequented side-ways for local users; differs because a bypath is usually a footpath or track, while bye-lanes are lanes — wider, often hedged, historically passable to carts and riders.
  • Country lane: Similar because both evoke narrow rural ways between hedges; differs because country lane is the broad affectionate term for any rural road, while bye-lanes are specifically the minor branches off it — lanes off lanes.
  • Back lane: Similar because both name the lesser way behind the main frontage; differs because a back lane serves the rear of houses, farms, or streets, while bye-lanes are independent minor through-lanes of the parish network.
  • Bridleway: Similar because both appear in Cobbett's list as ways the walker and rider own; differs because a bridleway is legally a horse-and-foot right of way (no carriages, now often no cars), while bye-lanes may be full if humble public roads.
  • Footpath: Similar because both belong to the walker's England that Cobbett celebrates; differs because a footpath admits feet only, while bye-lanes at least aspire to take a horse, a cart, or in dry weather a careful car.
  • Alley: Similar because both are narrow passages of secondary status; differs because an alley is urban and built-up (between houses, behind shops), while bye-lanes are rural and green (between fields, under hedges).
  • Boreen: Similar because both name tiny grassy rural lanes of the British Isles; differs because boreen is the Irish word (from boithrin, little road) with its own Gaelic music, while bye-lanes is the English compound of the same landscape.
  • Dirt road: Similar because both are humble unmetalled rural ways; differs because dirt road is the American term for any unpaved road however major, while bye-lanes are minor by definition — smallness, not surface, is their essence.
