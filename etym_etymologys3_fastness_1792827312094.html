The story of fastness is the story of one of English's strangest paradoxes: how the same word came to mean both holding still and moving quickly, both a mountain fortress and a laundry certificate. The thread that stitches fortress, knot, sprinter, and dyed shirt together is a single Old English adjective meaning firm.

The firm root: Proto-Germanic *fastaz meant firm, fixed, steadfast — what is tied and cannot slip. Its children spread across the Germanic languages: Old High German fasti, Old Norse fastr, Gothic fastan meaning to hold firm or to fast (abstain), German fest still meaning solid and festive-firm today. Old English fæst carried the full load: a fæst knot holds, a fæst friend stays, a fæst wall stands. Adding the noun suffix -nes (modern -ness) gave fæstnes: the state of being firm — and, by the easiest of extensions, the place that is firm, the fortified enclosure. Already in the ninth century the word meant both a quality and a castle.

How firmness learned to run: the famous shift needs retelling because it explains the modern speed sense. When Old English speakers said someone ran fæste, they meant he ran firmly, with resolve — hard and sustained. Over centuries the manner blurred into the pace: running hard became running fast, and by the later Middle Ages fast meant swift, leaving the old firm sense alive only in compounds like hold fast, steadfast, colour-fast. The noun fastness inherited both children of the split: it kept the ancient fortress and firmness senses intact while quietly gaining speed as one more meaning. No other common English noun houses such opposite twins so peacefully.

  • Sound shifts to notice: Proto-Germanic *a fronted to Old English æ in fæst, then flattened back to a in Middle and Modern English; the suffix -nes levelled to -ness; stress stayed firmly on the root; the southern long vowel in fast lengthened before s plus consonant, giving RP fahst while northern and American speech kept it short.
  • Semantic drift, stage by stage: firm and fixed (Germanic) → firmness as quality and as fortified place (Old English) → locked gates, tied knots, kept vows (Middle Ages) → plus swiftness through the fast paradox (early modern) → romantic mountain strongholds in literature (1700s–1800s) → technical dye permanence ratings (1900s) → fantasy's archetypal Dark Tower (Tolkien forward).
  • Literary attestations: Anglo-Saxon charters grant land with its fæstnes; border ballads sing of Highland fastnesses no army can take; Scott plants heroes in Highland and desert fastnesses; Tolkien, the professional philologist, restores the word to its full Old English dignity in Mordor; meanwhile textile standards quietly certify fastness to washing, to light, to rubbing on millions of labels.

Why one word holds opposites: English rarely lets a single form mean both still and swift, yet fastness does it without confusion because the senses live in different rooms: nobody hearing mountain fastness thinks of sprinters, and nobody reading colour fastness pictures battlements. The fortress sense survives precisely because it is archaic — modern speech would say stronghold — and archaism protects it like a museum case. Tolkien understood this better than anyone: by writing ancient fastness he summoned ten centuries of firmness in a single noun, and every fantasy fortress since stands in its shadow.
