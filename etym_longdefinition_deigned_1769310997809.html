deigned (verb, past tense of deign) — to have done something that one considers to be beneath one's dignity, doing it with deliberate, visible condescension; in older and more formal usage, to have graciously granted or vouchsafed something from a superior position.

Primary sense: condescension from a height

"He deigned to speak to the common people only when it suited his interests": the verb places the speaker on a higher rung of worthiness and portrays every contact with those below as a favour. To deign to do X is to do it while making clear that X is normally unworthy of you. The word is thus inherently evaluative and social: it requires a hierarchy of dignity — rank, merit, breeding, or moral standing — and it dramatizes the moment the superior steps down. In modern use the word is almost always ironic or critical: "he deigned to reply" usually means he replied with visible contempt or reluctance, and the sentence condemns his arrogance more than it praises his grace.

Archaic sense: gracious bestowal

In older English, especially in devotional and courtly poetry, deign meant to think it worthy or fitting of oneself to grant a favour — and often to grant it graciously and generously. "Deign to hear our prayer," "deign to accept this offering": here the word is humble before a higher power (God, a monarch, a patron) and means "condescend to receive or grant." This is the sense preserved in translations of the Bible and in liturgical language; it retains the root meaning of "to deem worthy" without the modern sneer.

Grammar and morphology

  • Defective verb: in modern English deign is used chiefly in the infinitive with "to" (deign to...) and in the past tense (deigned to...), especially with a following infinitive; the full present-tense paradigm ("I deign, he deigns") is rare and sounds archaic.
  • Form: "deigned" is the regular past tense and past participle, pronounced /deɪnd/, identical in sound to the archaic spelling "dained."
  • Negative-ironic construction: "would not deign to" — used to stress that someone treats an act as utterly beneath them ("he would not even deign to look at her").

Polysemy and semantic range

  • Neutral judgment (early sense): "to think worthy or fitting" — a morally neutral act of estimation, now obsolete.
  • Gracious condescension (older, courtly and liturgical): "to vouchsafe graciously," used of God, kings, and benefactors.
  • Haughty condescension (modern): "to do something beneath one's dignity," with contemptuous or ironic colouring — the dominant sense today.
  • Of things, by personification: "facts that do not deign to fit the theory" — a rhetorical flourish of literary prose.

Semantic bridge

The bridge runs through the Latin adjective dignus, "worthy." The verb dignārī meant "to deem (someone or something) worthy." When a medieval lord "deigned" to do a servant's task, he was announcing a judgment of worth: the act was beneath him, yet he judged it worth his attention as a favour. The modern contemptuous sense is the same act of judgment observed from below — what the lord calls graciousness, the observer calls arrogance.

In context

The card's sentence — "He deigned to speak to the common people only when it suited his interests" — combines the full modern value: a superior who descends only conditionally, when his own interests are served. The word makes the condescension audible and condemnable in a single verb: the conditional "only when it suited his interests" is precisely what turns deigning from grace into calculation.
