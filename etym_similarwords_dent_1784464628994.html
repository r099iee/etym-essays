• Indentation: Similar because both denote a hollow or notch pressed into a surface; differs because indentation is a formal, technical word (used of margins in print, coastlines, and notches) and does not necessarily imply impact, while dent is an everyday word specifically for a depression caused by a blow or pressure.
  • Depression: Similar because both name a low or sunken area in a surface; differs because depression is a broad, neutral term used in geology, medicine, and economics, whereas dent always implies a localized impact and is colloquial.
  • Ding: Similar because a ding is a small dent, especially in a car body; differs because ding suggests a minor, cosmetic mark and can also mean a bell sound, while dent is more general and can be deep or shallow.
  • Hollow: Similar because a dent is a kind of hollow; differs because hollow describes an empty concave space of any origin (natural or manufactured), while dent implies damage by impact.
  • Impression: Similar because both can name a mark pressed into a surface; differs because impression is a delicate, deliberate stamping or imprint, whereas a dent is an accidental or violent depression.
  • Notch: Similar because both are marks on a surface; differs because a notch is a deliberate V-shaped cut or groove, while a dent is an impact-induced hollow with no cutting involved.
  • Nick: Similar because both are small surface blemishes; differs because a nick is a shallow cut or scrape on the edge, while a dent pushes the surface inward without removing material.
  • Gouge: Similar because both are forms of surface damage; differs because a gouge is a deep groove or cut made by a pointed instrument, deeper and more destructive than a dent.
  • Dimple: Similar because both are small hollows; differs because a dimple is a charming, deliberate or natural indentation (of a chin or cheek), while a dent is an unwanted impact mark.
  • Dint: Similar because it is the historical ancestor of dent and shares the 'blow' family; differs because dint survives chiefly in the idiom 'by dint of' (by force of), preserving the older 'blow/force' sense, while dent names the resulting physical or figurative depression.
