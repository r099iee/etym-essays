cringing is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb to cringe. It describes the act of physically shrinking, ducking, or bending away in fear, or the emotional recoil of acute discomfort, shame, or servility. In the source passage, the dwarves crouch against the wall of a grassy terrace and cringing under boulders, hoping to escape the frightful eyes of the hunting dragon; the word conveys both their terrified posture and their abject helplessness before a power far greater than themselves.

  • Primary sense — physical recoil: To bend, crouch, or draw the body inwards in fear, as if bracing against a blow or an overwhelming threat. The posture is instinctive: the shoulders pull in, the head drops, the limbs fold close to the torso. A dog cringes beneath a raised hand; a person cringes in a doorway during a bombardment; a bird cringes in its cage when the cat approaches.
  • Secondary sense — servile submission: To behave with excessive, self-abasing deference before someone in authority, to fawn, to grovel metaphorically. In this older moral sense, a cringing courtier or servant humbles themselves for favour, mercy, or survival, sacrificing dignity in the process.
  • Tertiary sense — vicarious embarrassment: To wince inwardly at awkwardness, humiliation, or social discomfort, especially at someone else's folly. This is the foundation of modern cringe comedy, where an audience physically squirms while a character is painfully embarrassed on screen.
  • Register and connotation: Unfailingly negative. Whether the trigger is fear, sycophancy, or embarrassment, cringing implies a humiliating loss of composure, dignity, or self-possession. It is rarely used approvingly.
  • Grammar: Intransitive, taking prepositional complements (cringe at, cringe before, cringe away from). The participle functions both as a continuous verb form (was cringing) and as a prenominal adjective (a cringing figure), and it appears in causative constructions such as that makes me cringe.
  • Literary nuance: In heroic and fantasy prose (including the Tolkien text here), cringing is a word of anti-heroic fear: it contrasts with steadfast endurance and signals panic, self-preservation, and the collapse of composure under terror.
  • Contemporary extensions: Internet culture has re-nominalised the verb, so cringe is now also a noun ("that video is such cringe") and feeds compounds such as cringe-worthy, cringe culture, and second-hand cringe. The participle cringing remains the natural expression of the ongoing action or state of recoil.
  • Domain usage: In psychology and self-help writing, cringing describes anxiety responses and low self-esteem (a person who cringes at praise). In comedy and media criticism it names the defining audience reaction of awkward-comedy. In political commentary, cringing may describe national or cultural servility before greater powers. In none of these does the word lose its core meaning of withdrawing or diminishing oneself in response to something uncomfortable.

The historical bridge of the senses: The Old English ancestor cringan meant "to yield, to give way, to fall in battle" — warriors sank down in death. That downward, yielding motion is the key: the fall of the dying warrior became, by Middle English, the deliberate bowing of the subject, and then the instinctive crouch of the terrified. In the twentieth century the physical image was internalised into the emotional reflex of embarrassment, so that today a person can "cringe" with nothing visibly happening to their body at all. Every modern sense still encodes the ancient gesture of bending or shrinking away.
