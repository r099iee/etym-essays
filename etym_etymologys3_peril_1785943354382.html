A ROOT ABOUT VENTURING

The biography of peril begins with the idea of trying. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *per-, one of the hardest-worked roots of the entire family, whose central meaning is 'to lead or pass across, to go through, to venture'. From it flow two great rivers of words. The first is spatial: Latin per 'through', English for and fore, German ver-, the idea of passing across a space. The second is experimental: Latin peritus 'experienced, skilled', experiri 'to test' (the ancestor of English experience, experiment, expert), and periculum, 'a trial, a test, an attempt' - and only later 'danger'. The semantic bridge is the natural one of every ordeal: to put something to a trial is to expose it to harm. A periculum was originally the proof or test a thing was put to; the danger came in with the testing. The Romans ran the two senses side by side for centuries - periculum facere meant 'to make a trial' and also 'to run a risk' - until the dangerous sense won and the neutral 'trial' sense faded.

SOUND AND FORM: FROM PERICULUM TO PERIL

  • Latin periculum was formed from the verbal stem of *per- with the instrumental suffix -culum, which normally names an instrument or means (as in ferculum 'a dish for carrying', from ferre 'to carry'); the word literally names 'the means of trying' - the trial itself.
  • In the spoken Latin of the Empire the medial syllables began to erode: periculum, with its unaccented -i- and -cu-, was in Vulgar Latin compressed and reshaped.
  • Old French inherited the form as peril, with the -culum of the Latin having been reduced to the bare -l; the whole middle of the word collapsed into a single syllable. The English form, borrowed in the thirteenth century, is essentially the Old French peril unchanged.
  • The adjective perilous (Old French perilleus, from Latin periculosus 'dangerous') travelled alongside it, and modern English keeps the historic stress on the first syllable: /'per.El/.
THE PASSAGE INTO ENGLISH: THE AGE OF CHIVALRY

English took peril from Old French in the thirteenth century, and the word arrived already bearing the full Romance weight of mortal danger. Its earliest English record, around 1225 in the devotional work Ancrene Wisse, already uses it of the soul's danger and of physical jeopardy; the Ancrene Wisse puns on perils and perils of the flesh. The high medieval setting was decisive for the word's register: it entered English through the vocabulary of romance, chivalry, and religion, where adventures were perilous, journeys were perilous, and souls stood in peril of damnation. This is why peril has always felt more literary, more archaic, and more elevated than the humdrum danger: it came to England wearing armour and a halo.

THE DOUBLE CAREER: LAW AND LITERATURE

  • 13th-15th centuries: peril is standard in Middle English for physical danger, moral jeopardy, and damnation; Chaucer uses it freely, and the word is a fixture of romance.
  • 16th-17th centuries: the word enters the fixed legal and commercial formulae that survive to this day - 'perils of the sea' as an exemption in shipping and marine insurance, and 'at your peril' as a standing warning. Shakespeare uses peril for epic danger and for the precise risk of specific losses.
  • 18th century: maritime law fixes 'perils of the sea' as a term of art; the phrase names dangers of navigation - storms, rocks, shipwreck, piracy - that no human care can avert and for which no one is liable.
  • 19th century: the insurance industry systematizes the word: a 'peril' becomes the named cause of loss in a policy - fire, flood, theft, earthquake - so that peril develops a precise technical sense alongside its epic one. Adventure fiction (Conrad, Stevenson, the penny dreadfuls) keeps the high literary sense alive.
  • 20th-21st centuries: 'named perils' and 'all-perils' become standard insurance vocabulary; 'at your peril' remains a living idiom; and peril retains its place in epic, fantasy, and thriller prose as the strongest single-syllable word for mortal danger.
SOCIO-CULTURAL FACTORS

Peril's two modern faces - the courtroom and the insurance schedule on one side, the epic and the adventure on the other - are both inheritances from its double history. The Roman trial sense gave it the legal and technical career; the medieval romance career gave it the literary one. Modern risk management may prefer the antiseptic 'hazard' and 'risk', but when a realtor speaks of an 'all-perils policy' or a fantasy novel speaks of a 'perilous quest', they are both reaching for the same ancient word for putting oneself to the proof - and for what the proof may cost. The word has never lost the memory of its oldest meaning: to be in peril is, etymologically, to be being tested.
