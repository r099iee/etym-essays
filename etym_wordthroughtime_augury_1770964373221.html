- Antiquity (via Latin "augurium"): in Republican Rome, augury was the state-sanctioned art of the augurs — priests who read the will of the gods in the flight and cries of birds; a grave, institutional, quasi-religious practice that could decide wars and elections.
- Middle English (14th c.): borrowed through Old French as "augury", meaning "an omen or prophetic sign"; retains the sacred, foreboding weight of its classical origins.
- 16th–18th c.: the word widens beyond trained priests to any general portent, yet keeps an elevated, literary register tinged with destiny and ancient ritual.
- 19th c.: a favourite of Romantic and Gothic prose for signs that carry monumental, almost cosmic significance — reading the future in natural and human events alike.
- Contemporary: formal and archaic-sounding; used metaphorically for any sign or signal of things to come, while everyday speech prefers the plainer "omen", "sign", or "portent".
