• freedwoman: Similar because it names a woman in exactly the same legal transition from slavery to freedom; differs because of gender, and because Roman law used a separate form, liberta, with the same patronal obligations.
  • freedperson: Similar because it is the gender-neutral equivalent coined for modern usage; differs because it is recent, self-conscious and rare, and would be anachronistic in a historical narrative.
  • freeman: Similar because it sounds almost identical and also denotes a person who is free; differs sharply because a freeman holds the freedom of a city, borough or guild and was typically never enslaved at all.
  • free man: Similar because it asserts the absence of slavery; differs because it implies free birth or ordinary liberty and carries no reference to a manumitting owner or to a legal act of release.
  • ex-slave: Similar because it describes a person who was formerly enslaved; differs because it is journalistic and informal, defines the person wholly by the past condition, and ignores the legal relationship created by manumission.
  • emancipated person: Similar because emancipation is the general act that produces a freedman; differs because the phrase is abstract and administrative, and it is also used of minors and of civic emancipation, not only of slavery.
  • manumitted person: Similar because manumission is the precise legal procedure behind the status; differs because the phrase is technical and archaic in tone, foregrounding the act rather than the new social identity.
  • libertus: Similar because it is the Roman legal term for a freedman and the exact equivalent in the source’s world; differs because it is Latin, and it names the freedman specifically in relation to his patron, while libertinus names the status class.
  • freed citizen: Similar because manumission normally conferred citizenship; differs because it emphasizes the civic result and is not a standard English category — Roman freedmen had citizenship with disabilities, not full equality.
