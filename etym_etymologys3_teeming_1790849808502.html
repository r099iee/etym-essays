The adjective and participle teeming has a deeply rooted Germanic pedigree, evolving from an ancient Proto-Indo-European root for leading and drawing out into a central Old English concept of reproductive lineage, childbearing, and boundless biological fertility.

The deepest origin of the word is the Proto-Indo-European root *dewk-, meaning "to lead, pull, draw, or bring forth." This foundational root produced an immense array of words across the Indo-European family:

  • In Latin, it developed into ducere ("to lead" > English duke, conduct, produce, educate, deduce).
  • In Proto-Germanic, the root evolved via Grimm's Law into *teuhaną ("to pull, draw", whence German ziehen and Old English tēon), and produced the nominal derivative *taugmaz / *tōmiz ("that which is drawn out, a line of descendants, a brood, a harnessed line of draft animals").

In Old English, this nominal derivative appeared as tēam (c. 8th century):

  • Originally, tēam meant "a family, line of descendants, race, or a brood of young animals/children born together."
  • Simultaneously, because animals born together were often harnessed together to pull a plow or wagon, tēam also came to mean "a harnessed set of draft animals" (which evolved into our modern athletic and collaborative noun team).

From the substantive tēam, Anglo-Saxon speakers formed the causative weak verb tēman (or tīeman, c. 9th century), meaning "to bring forth offspring, bear young, propagate, procreate, or be pregnant with a child." In Middle English, this became the verb temen (attested throughout the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries), used widely in agricultural and biblical contexts to describe sheep, cattle, and humans bearing abundant progeny.

In the late sixteenth century (the Elizabethan era, c. 1580s), the present participial adjective teeming began appearing prominently in literature:

  • William Shakespeare used it with spectacular poetic potency, as in Macbeth ("What's the newest grief? / ...each minute teems a new one"), Richard II ("the teeming womb of royal kings"), and Henry V ("our fertile France, / Put up for her best garden of the world, / Our vineyards, fallows, meads and hedges, / Defective in their nurtures, grow to wildness, / Even so our houses and ourselves and children / Have lost, or do not learn for want of time, / The sciences that should become our country, / But grow like savages... while the teeming earth / Is all overgrown").
  • John Milton in Paradise Lost (1667) similarly celebrated the creation of the world when "the teeming Earth brought forth / Of Monsters, huge in bulk."

During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, teeming underwent two important semantic expansions:

  • In British regional dialects, the verb teem ("to pour out", partly influenced by Old Norse tœma "to empty, pour out") merged conceptually in popular imagination with biological teeming, giving rise to the widespread idiom "teeming with rain" (meaning raining in heavy, multiplying sheets).
  • With the rapid growth of nineteenth-century industrial centers, Victorian novelists (Charles Dickens, Elizabeth Gaskell) applied teeming to describe the packed, bustling streets, factories, and slums of industrial cities.
  • In modern critical theory (such as Roland Barthes's The Pleasure of the Text), teeming describes the boundless, uncontrollable proliferation of linguistic pleasures, desires, and textual meanings operating beneath and beyond rigid grammatical structures.
