Wards, the plural lock-hardware term of this card, is a technical specialization of one of the oldest and most productive word-families in English — the family of guarding and watching. Its biography is a story of how a single ancient notion of protection branched into custody, rooms, people, repelling blows, and finally the small metal ridges inside a lock that refuse every key except the one whose shape they were made to accept. 1. The deep root: *wer- and the two convergences The chain begins with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *wer-, a complex of attention and protection glossed roughly as "to cover, watch, guard, perceive." From it descend, across the Indo-European languages, words of watching and fearing and guarding. The Germanic branch developed it with great vigour: Proto-Germanic *wardaz "guard, watcher" and the verb *wardōną "to watch, guard" lie behind Old Norse vǫrðr (watchman, the -vǫrð of modern vǫrður), Old High German wart, and Old English weard. One caution is essential to an honest account: Indo-European also had a separate, homonymous root *wer- meaning "to turn," and that root produced the directional English suffix -ward / -wards (homeward, onward, toward) via the notion of turning toward a place. The guard-root and the turn-root converged in English spelling — both surface as ward / -ward — but they are different families, and the lock-word belongs firmly to the guard-root. This is precisely the kind of nuance that separates careful etymology from folk association. 2. Old English: weard, the guardian word Old English weard is attested from the eighth century with the meanings "guard, watchman, keeper" and, abstractly, "protection, custody." Beside the noun stands the verb weardian "to guard, keep watch." The word was productive: stig-weard "hall-keeper" gave modern steward; weard-setl named a watch-post. The ancient image is physical and personal — a person standing watch, keeping a place or a charge safe — and it is this image that the whole later family, including the lock-hardware sense, will extend. 3. Middle English and the branching of senses In Middle English the word settles as ward (and warde), covering custody, guardianship, and guarded places. From this base the later language multiplied institutional senses: the hospital ward is the guarded room where the sick are watched over; the electoral ward is the guarded division of a city; the legal ward is the person — most often a child — placed by law under a guardian's care or under the protection of a court (a "ward of court"). The verb to ward off keeps the root's defensive force: to turn aside or fend away a blow, a danger, an illness, an evil. Derived nouns — warder (a prison guard), warden (a keeper), wardrobe (from the guarded room where robes were kept) — all carry the same protective core. 4. The locksmiths' leap: from guards to wards The step that matters for this card is the transfer of the guard-image into hardware. Medieval and early modern locksmiths designed locks whose internal projections physically blocked any key not cut with matching grooves: the metal ridges themselves performed the work of a guard, refusing admission to the wrong key. The trade named them wards — the guards of the mechanism — and the corresponding grooves or notches cut into the key's bit came to be called wards as well, since they are the key's complement to the lock's projections. The sense is not a borrowing and not a separate word: it is the ancient guard-root applied to iron and silver architecture, the same word that guards people and rooms now guarding a bolt. 5. Sound and form: the plural does its work Phonologically the word is unremarkable — Old English weard, Middle English ward, Modern English ward — with the standard loss of the second element of the old diphthong-like cluster and the voicing of the final consonant in the plural (wards, pronounced /wɔːdz/). Morphologically, the plural is a plural of plurality: a set of ridges, "intricate wards," each one a separate guard inside the lock. The spelling merges with the directional -ward(s), as noted, but context selects the sense instantly. 6. The silver key of The Hobbit When Gandalf hands Thorin "a key with a long barrel and intricate wards, made of silver," the word carries both technical precision and the deep etymological flavour of guarding. The key is a small fortress of shape: its wards must answer the lock's wards before the Mountain's secret door will open. Tolkien, the philologist, uses the real vocabulary of locksmithing rather than a vague "magic key" label, and the reader who hears "wards" as genuine lock-work feels the wonder through the hardware — an object that is itself a watchman, admitting only the shape it was made for. The ancient root of protection has, across two millennia, found its way into the barrel of a silver key: a word that once named the guard at the door now names the ridges inside the lock that keep the door for its true key.
