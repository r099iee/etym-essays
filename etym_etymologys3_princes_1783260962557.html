PART ONE — The Indo-European root and the making of "first".

The story of princes begins not with royalty but with mere position. Behind Latin primus, "first", stands a Proto-Indo-European root *per-, meaning "forward, in front, through" — a spatial word before it was ever an honorific. In Sanskrit it gave purá "before"; in Greek pará "beside, alongside"; in Latin per "through" and porro "forward". The superlative sense "first" emerged when an old comparative-adjective stem *pri-, "before, in front", took the superlative suffix *-mo-, giving Proto-Italic *pri-mo- and then Latin prīmus, "first, foremost". This is the same *-mo- that builds summus "highest" and īmus "lowest", so that prīmus is exactly "the foremost", the one at the front of the line. The word appears throughout early Latin literature — Plautus and Ennius in the third and second centuries BCE — with a purely ordinal meaning: "the first man", "the first time", "the first thing".

At the same period the language had another energetic root at work: capere, "to take, seize, grasp", from PIE *kap-. This is the verb of physical seizure — of an enemy in battle (captus, "captured"), of a city stormed (capere urbem), of a vessel "containing" what it holds (capere, "to hold"). It is cognate with English have and German haben, and its Roman career runs through the whole vocabulary of taking: captiō "a taking", captīvus "captive", capāx "capacious".

PART TWO — The compound princeps and its Roman politics.

Out of prīmus and capere the Romans built the compound prīnceps, "one who takes the first share", hence "first in rank, chief". Roman etymologists already read the word this way: Varro glossed it as quasi primum capiens, "as it were, taking the first part", and the compound sits in the same mould as manceps, "the hand-taker", a bidder who literally takes goods in hand. For centuries prīnceps meant simply "leading man" — the leading citizen of a town, the founder of a family line, the originator of a custom. Then, at the end of the Republic, it was re-deployed as the least offensive word for autocracy.

Augustus, who had watched Julius Caesar be assassinated for accepting a crown, refused the titles of king and dictator and instead accepted the label prīnceps senātus, "first of the senate". The word's genius was that it was not a constitutional office at all — it was a precedence, a claim to be first among equals. The whole fiction of the early empire, the principate, rested on it: the emperor was not a monarch but the "first citizen". Gibbon's own sentence, "the splendour of Diocletian and Constantine", belongs to the later empire, by which time the principate had hardened into open autocracy — but the vocabulary of "firstness" persisted, and prīnceps remained the imperial title in Latin and Greek (the Greek hēgemōn, "leader", later basileus).

PART THREE — From Latin to Old French to English: the word goes royal.

When the Western empire collapsed and the barbarian kingdoms took its place, Latin did not die — it evolved. The church, the law, and the chancery all continued to write in Latin, and prīnceps was the natural word for the new kings: the rulers of the Germanic successor kingdoms — the very "German princes who reigned at Paris, Toledo, Arles, and Ravenna" of Gibbon's sentence — were called principes by the chroniclers, "first men" of their peoples. By the eleventh century the Old French form prince was a settled title for a sovereign ruler, and from Old French the word entered Middle English around 1200, in the romances and chronicles where a prince was any great ruler, king or otherwise.

Through the later Middle Ages and the Renaissance the word kept its width. A king could be called a prince; Machiavelli's Il Principe (1513) used "the Prince" to mean "the ruler" of a state, sovereign regardless of rank. The plural princes therefore names not just royal sons but the whole class of rulers — which is exactly how Gibbon deploys it, and how older English routinely did. Only in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, as constitutional vocabulary sharpened (king, emperor, duke, sovereign), did prince begin to narrow toward its modern job of denoting royal sons and rulers of small principalities. The word's modern breadth survives mainly in frozen phrases: crown prince, Prince of Wales, merchant prince, prince of the Church, prince of darkness.

PART FOUR — Semantic drift summarized.

  • Spatial to social: PIE *per- "forward" → Latin prīmus "first" — precedence conceived as position in a line.
  • Physical to political: Latin capere "to seize" → prīnceps "one who takes first place" — priority of rank imagined as a taking.
  • Republican to imperial: prīnceps "leading citizen" → the emperor's title of the principate — autocracy veiled as precedence.
  • Latin to Romance: prīnceps → Old French prince — the word rides the church, the law, and the chronicles into the vernacular monarchies.
  • Broad to narrow: "any ruler" → "royal son or prince of a small state" — constitutional modernization shrinks the sense, while metaphor keeps the old breadth alive.
  • English plural: prince + -s → princes — the ordinary plural naming the whole class of rulers in historical writing, as on this card.
