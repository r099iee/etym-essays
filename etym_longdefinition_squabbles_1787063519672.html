SQUABBLES is the plural of the noun squabble, and the word also functions as the third-person singular of the verb to squabble ("he squabbles"). A squabble is a petty, noisy, ill-tempered quarrel — an argument over trivial matters conducted with more heat than substance, usually with raised voices and little dignity. In the Tolkien passage above, the townspeople and the Wood-elves are said to have been friends "except for occasional squabbles about river-tolls": the word captures the small, recurring, half-comic bickering of neighbouring peoples over a minor commercial annoyance, friction that never grows into real enmity.

  • Primary sense (petty quarrel): a trivial, undignified dispute; a quarrel about something small conducted with noise and bad temper; e.g. "children’s squabbles over toys", "a squabble over the last slice of cake".
  • Verb sense (to bicker): "to squabble" is to engage in such quarrels, habitually or at the moment; the verb is typically used of children, neighbours, politicians, and animals; e.g. "the two firms squabbled over the contract".
  • Collective and abstract sense: the word can denote the general phenomenon of petty quarrelling — "endless domestic squabbles"; "political squabbles" are the minor, personality-driven disputes of politics that observers dismiss as beneath the dignity of the issues.
  • Animal usage: applied to birds and animals squabbling over food or territory — gulls, sparrows, squirrels; the image is of quick, noisy, unserious contention; e.g. "sparrows squabbling at the feeder".
  • Figurative / journalistic sense: in reporting, "squabbles" are the factional, small-scale conflicts of a party, family, or team — treated with dismissive amusement rather than alarm; the word signals that the matter is not serious.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The noun and verb appear together in English around 1600, of obscure origin — the usual view is that the word is imitative (echoic), reproducing the spluttering, bubbly noise of people arguing quickly and pointlessly; the Scandinavian dialect words skvabbel (Swedish, "a dispute, murmur") and skvabba (Norwegian, "to prattle, babble") are the nearest documented relatives and may be the source. The semantic character has been stable for four centuries: a squabble is always small, always noisy, and always slightly ridiculous. Unlike a quarrel or feud, a squabble cannot be a matter of life and death; the word itself laughs at its subject, and to call a dispute "a squabble" is to say that it is beneath serious concern.

Register and nuances. Squabble is informal, mildly comic, and slightly contemptuous. It emphasises pettiness of subject and noisiness of manner: the squabblers are behaving like children. It differs from argument (which may be rational), from quarrel (which may be serious), and from dispute (which is formal), standing closest to bickering, spat, and tiff in emotional tone. In Tolkien, the phrase "occasional squabbles about river-tolls" is gently humorous: the proud, ancient peoples of the river bicker like street traders over a tax, and the word keeps the reader smiling at their pettiness while affirming their underlying friendship.

  • Synonyms: quarrels, arguments, disputes, bickerings, wrangles, spats, tiffs, rows, altercations, scrapes, dust-ups, run-ins, falling-outs, disagreements, clashes, friction.
  • Emotional spectrum (least to most serious): tiff — spat — squabble — bickering — quarrel — row — altercation — feud.
  • Antonyms: agreement, accord, harmony, concord, peace, amity, reconciliation.

Word-family and grammar. The noun is countable (a squabble / squabbles); the verb is regular (squabble, squabbled, squabbling); the agent noun squabbler and the participle-adjective squabbling are in common use. In the Tolkien sentence squabbles is a plural noun; the identical form is used as a verb in "she squabbles with her sister".
