Hellenic begins with a tribal name. In the Iliad the Hellēnes are a people of Thessaly, followers of Achilles from Phthia and Hellas, and they are only one of several names for the Greeks alongside Achaeans and Danaans. The origin of the name is uncertain. Ancient genealogists made Hellen the son of Deucalion and the ancestor of all the Greek tribes, a tidy myth that explains the name by giving it a father; modern scholars have looked instead to a Thessalian locality, to the Dodonaean sanctuary and its Selloi, or to a pre-Greek substrate. What is clear is that a local name was generalised.

From tribe to nation. The Persian Wars of the early fifth century gave the Greeks a sharp sense of themselves as Hellēnes opposed to barbaroi. Herodotus opens his history with the question of why Greeks and barbarians fought; Aeschylus' Persians dramatises the contrast; Isocrates' Panegyricus urges a panhellenic expedition against Persia and defines Greekness by culture and education rather than by blood. In this period Hellas means the Greek world as a whole, and Hellēnikos means "Greek" in language, custom and art. The adjective thus acquired the cultural density it still carries.

Alexander and the Hellenistic age. Alexander's conquests spread Greek language and institutions from Egypt to Afghanistan, and the resulting era was later labelled Hellenistic. The term is modern: Johann Gustav Droysen coined Hellenismus in the 1830s to describe the fusion of Greek and Near Eastern cultures after Alexander, and English "Hellenistic" follows him. This created the useful but often blurred distinction between Hellenic (classical, Greek proper) and Hellenistic (post-classical, cosmopolitan, hybrid). The Koine Greek of the New Testament belongs to the Hellenistic world, which is why the reading that prompts this card can describe the New Testament's Greek as "the common Greek dialect of the Eastern Mediterranean".

Roman and Byzantine reception. Latin writers used Hellēnicus as a learned adjective for Greek things, while everyday Latin called the Greeks Graeci. The Roman elite admired Greek rhetoric, philosophy and sculpture even as Roman power conquered Greece; Horace's Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit ("captured Greece took captive her savage conqueror") captures the cultural dynamic. In the eastern empire the Greek-speaking inhabitants called themselves Rhōmaioi, "Romans", not Hellēnes, because Hellēn had acquired the meaning "pagan" in Christian usage. Only in the nineteenth century did Hellēn and Hellas return to favour as national self-descriptions.

English and the classical revival. English "Hellenic" is first recorded in the seventeenth century, in the learned vocabulary of antiquarianism and theology. It expanded in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries with the growth of classical scholarship, the Grand Tour, Winckelmann's history of ancient art, and the Romantic Philhellenism that supported the Greek War of Independence. Byron's death at Missolonghi in 1824 made "Hellenic" a word of political sympathy as well as scholarship. Matthew Arnold's Culture and Anarchy (1869) opposed "Hellenism" to "Hebraism" as the two poles of European culture, and Walter Pater's Plato and Platonism (1893) and Greek Studies (1895) popularised "Hellenism" as an aesthetic ideal. In art history, "Hellenic" describes the classical phases, while "Hellenistic" describes the dramatic, emotional and cosmopolitan art that followed Alexander.

The modern state and the language branch. The Greek state founded in the 1820s took the name Hellas, and the republic of 1974 is the Hellenic Republic. In linguistics, "Hellenic" is the standard label for the branch of Indo-European containing Ancient Greek, Koine, Byzantine Greek, Modern Greek and the modern dialects. The term is useful precisely because it is broader than "Greek": it can cover Tsakonian and Pontic, which are Greek in origin but not identical with the standard language.

Sound and form. Greek Hellēnikos passed into Latin as Hellēnicus, with the Greek suffix -ikos rendered as Latin -icus, and into English as Hellenic, with initial h- restored by learned convention. Modern Greek Ellinikós has lost the ancient aspiration and pronounces the vowel as /i/, a shift that separates the modern pronunciation from the English scholarly one. The English stress falls on the second syllable, /hɛˈlen.ɪk/, following the Latin penultimate pattern.

  • Semantic widening: one Thessalian tribe > all Greeks > Greek culture > the Greek language family > the modern state.
  • Semantic splitting: Hellenic (classical) versus Hellenistic (post-Alexander) versus Hellenism (cultural ideal or movement).
  • Religious revaluation: Hellēn meant "pagan" in early Christian Greek, then was reclaimed by the nineteenth-century nation and by modern polytheists.
  • Literary anchors: Homer's tribal name, Herodotus and Isocrates on Greekness, Droysen's Hellenismus, Arnold's Hellenism and Hebraism, and the Philhellene poetry of Byron and Shelley.

The word therefore carries a double inheritance: an ancient ethnonym that became a cultural ideal, and a modern scholarly label that keeps the classical and post-classical worlds apart. To call something Hellenic is always, in some measure, to make a claim about what Greece means.
