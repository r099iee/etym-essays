- Late 14th c. — Middle English adoption from Anglo-Norman: borrowed as tedious / tediose, conveying deep psychological weariness, painful distress, and heavy disgust, closely linked to theological acedia (spiritual torpor and weariness of life).
- 15th–16th c. — Early Modern rhetorical and literary shift: increasingly applied to protracted verbal discourse, pedantic sermons, and verbose books; used by Shakespeare and Renaissance humanists to describe long-winded speakers who exasperate their listeners.
- 17th–18th c. — Enlightenment & polite social critique: established as a refined conversational rebuke for social boredom, pedantry, over-elaborated etiquette, and repetitive labor, contrasting with neoclassical ideals of wit, brevity, and grace.
- 19th c. — Industrial and technological mechanization: applied to the grueling, repetitive, uncreative monotony of factory labor, administrative clerical drudgery, and academic transcription (as in Gombrich's commentary on realistic detail).
- 20th–21st c. — Contemporary workplace & digital parlance: universally used to describe soul-crushing bureaucratic paperwork, repetitive digital data tasks, sluggish buffering delays, or formulaic entertainment, universally denoting uninspired, time-draining monotony.
