1. The Indo-European root. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂welh₁- “to be strong, to have power” (Pokorny's *ual-). Its reflexes spread across the family: in Italic it gave Latin valēre; in Germanic it produced, via a dental-extended formation *waldaną “to rule, wield power”, the family of Old English wealdan (the ancestor of modern wield), Gothic waldan and Old High German waltan; in Celtic it underlies Old Irish flaith “lordship, sovereignty” and Welsh gwlad “land, country”; and in Baltic, Lithuanian valdýti “to rule”. The exact ablaut of the Germanic dental extension is debated, but the family's core sense — strength, power, rule — is secure.

2. Latin valēre. Latin valeō, valēre “to be strong, be well, prevail, be worth” is attested from the earliest literature (Plautus, 3rd–2nd century BCE). Its range is revealing: physical strength (valēre viribus), health (valētūdō), efficacy and worth (quantum valeat, “how much it is worth”), and the imperative vale “farewell!”, literally “be strong, be well”, the standard closing of Cicero's letters. The root's “worth” sense is crucial for the later story: in Latin, to be strong and to be worth something are the same verb.

3. Late Latin valor. From valēre was formed the abstract noun valor, valōrem “worth, value, strength” with the suffix -or (as in honor, amor). The word is recorded in Late Latin from the 4th century CE, and it is from this noun that the Romance languages built their words for both “value” and “courage”.

4. Old French: the chivalric pivot. Old French valor, valour (12th century) meant “worth, value, strength” and, increasingly, “courage”. The semantic pivot is feudal and literary: in the society of the chansons de geste and the romances, a knight's worth (valor) was demonstrated by his prowess in battle; worth and bravery fused into one idea. The word travelled to England with the Norman aristocracy, entering Anglo-French and then Middle English.

5. Middle English. English valour (spellings valour, valur, valowre) is attested from about 1300 in metrical romances and didactic verse, with both inherited senses: “worth, value, merit” and “courage, bravery”. For two centuries the two coexisted in the same texts.

6. The 16th–17th-century split. Early Modern English resolved the ambiguity by borrowing value (from Old French value, the past participle of valoir “to be worth”) for the “worth” sense, allowing valour to contract to “courage”. Shakespeare's usage shows the completed narrowing: valour is everywhere in his plays as martial courage — and in 1 Henry IV (1597) Falstaff delivers the famous ironic proverb “The better part of valour is discretion”, which has kept the word morally nuanced ever since: courage tempered by judgement, not blind recklessness.

7. Spelling and modern register. In the 17th century a Latinising spelling valor arose and became standard in American English, while British English kept valour (the -our spellings of honour, labour, colour follow the same Anglo-French scribal history). From the 18th century onward valour retreated into elevated diction — epic poetry, military ceremonial, eulogy — while ordinary speech adopted courage (from Old French corage, “heart”) and bravery (from Italian bravo, “bold”). Today valour survives in honours (Canada's Cross of Valour, 1972; the US Public Safety Officer Medal of Valor, 2001), in dignified literary prose, and in the proverb — a noble, slightly archaic word whose meaning is the fossilised value-system of medieval chivalry: a warrior's worth was his valour.
