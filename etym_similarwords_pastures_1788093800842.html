• meadow: Similar because both are grassland used in farming; differs because a meadow is mown for hay (or a wildflower meadow is left unmown), whereas a pasture is grazed, and meadow often implies moist, lowland, flowery ground.
  • field: Similar because a field is an enclosed piece of farm land that may be grazed; differs because field is generic — arable or any enclosure — while pasture specifies grazing land.
  • grassland: Similar because both denote grass-covered land; differs because grassland is a neutral ecological term covering prairies and steppes, not specifically land managed for livestock.
  • pasturage: Similar because it is a doublet of pasture from the same Latin root; differs because pasturage is a technical or legal term for grazing land or the right to graze, rarely used in speech.
  • lea / ley: Similar because both denote grassland or meadow; differs because lea is archaic and poetic, while ley (also ley pasture) is a specific agronomic term for temporary sown pasture in rotation.
  • paddock: Similar because a paddock is an enclosed grazing field; differs because a paddock is smaller and usually near stables or houses, often for horses, whereas pasture can be any scale.
  • range / rangeland: Similar because both are land grazed by livestock; differs because range is vast, unenclosed ranching country (especially in the American West), while pasture is comparatively small and managed.
  • savanna: Similar because savanna is grass-dominated land grazed by animals; differs because savanna is a tropical or subtropical ecosystem with scattered trees, entirely different climate and management.
  • common: Similar because common land is where villagers graze livestock; differs because a common is legally shared land, whereas pasture is usually private property.
  • greensward: Similar because both denote grassy ground; differs because greensward is literary and archaic and describes the grass surface itself, not the grazing function.
