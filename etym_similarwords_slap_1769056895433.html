• bang: Similar because it is the closest relative of slap in British idiom, meaning "directly, exactly" ("bang in the middle," "bang up against"); differs because bang pictures a loud, explosive strike—the sound of a door slamming or a gun—while slap keeps the flat, sharp contact of the palm; the two are so close that English fuses them in the doublet slap-bang (1786).
  • smack: Similar because it means "right, directly" in the same emphatic idiom ("smack in the middle," "smack against the wall"); differs because smack, commoner in American English, sounds harder and more deliberately flat, like a lipsmacking sound, while slap keeps the energy of the open hand; both descend from the same imitative cluster of blows.
  • flush: Similar because it describes even, immediate contact ("flush against the wall," "flush with the edge"); differs because flush is the carpenter's and stonemason's precise, passionless term for surfaces meeting level with no gap—it says nothing of impact—while slap insists on sudden, total, almost violent contact.
  • right: Similar because it denotes exact position ("right up against the gate"); differs because right is the neutral, colourless default that carries no image of impact, no emotion, and no physical metaphor, while slap is vivid, colloquial, and physical—you feel the palm.
  • straight: Similar because it can mean "directly, without deviation" ("straight into the wall"); differs because straight emphasises an unswerving path and motion towards a target, while slap names the resulting state of resting flush against something with nothing between—contact, not trajectory.
  • square: Similar because it can mean "directly, exactly, on the mark" ("square in the middle," "hit him square"); differs because square carries the geometry of evenness and fairness—of being level and honest—where slap is purely about sudden flat impact.
  • dead: Similar because it can intensify "exactly" ("dead centre," "dead on"); differs because dead means precisely, to the very point, with the sense of a fixed terminus, while slap adds the picture of a blow landing—impact rather than precision.
  • exactly: Similar because it denotes precise correspondence; differs because exactly is a word of measurement, correctness, and numbers, feeling out of place for physical contact, while slap is physical and spatial—objects flush against surfaces, not quantities agreeing.
  • cuff: Similar because it is a blow with the open hand, chiefly of the style of a slap; differs because a cuff is often a less emphatic, more admonishing tap aimed at the head or arm, whereas a slap is the full flat-handed strike, and the adverbial "slap" has no "cuff" equivalent.
  • clout: Similar because it is a hard blow; differs because a clout is a heavy, unglamorous thump that can be delivered with a fist or an object, while a slap is specifically flat-handed, and clout never develops the adverbial sense of "directly."
