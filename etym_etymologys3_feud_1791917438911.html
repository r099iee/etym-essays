Feud is a French-speaking import into English, but its roots are Germanic. The word descends from Proto-Germanic *faihīþō, “hostility, enmity”, a derivative of *faihaz, “marked, outlawed, hostile”. The same family is visible across the Germanic world: Old English fǣhþ “feud, hostility”, Old Saxon fehitha, Old High German fehida, modern German Fehde, and Dutch vete. The deeper Indo-European connection is usually made with *peyḱ-, “to mark, paint”, the root behind Latin pingere “to paint” and English picture: a person marked or stained was, in effect, an outlaw, and the hostility that followed was the feud. That semantic bridge is plausible and widely cited, but it is a reconstruction, not a documented record.

Germanic legal culture

  • In the early Germanic world the feud was not the absence of law but a recognised legal relationship. A wrong created a right of vengeance in the injured kindred, and the feud could be prosecuted openly and, in principle, honourably.
  • The alternative was composition: a payment, often called wergild (literally “man-price”), fixed by custom or by a court. The whole early history of law can be read as the long contest between these two remedies.
  • Feuds were subject to rules. Icelandic sagas describe formal declarations, the choice of a champion, truces, and arbitration; the feud had a procedure, however violent its substance.
  • Christianity and kingship attacked the institution from both sides: the Church offered penance and the peace of God, while kings claimed a monopoly on legitimate violence and punished private war as breach of the king's peace.

From faide to fede

Frankish *faihiþa passed into Old French as faide, with the sense “enmity, revenge, war”. After the Norman Conquest the word entered English as fede or feide, attested around 1300. Its arrival displaced, or rather absorbed, the native Old English fǣhþ, which died out of ordinary use. The Middle English word carried the full weight of the institution: a feud was a matter of kindred, honour, and enforceable custom, not merely a quarrel.

  • Medieval romance and chronicle use fede for the great baronial and clan vendettas, and for the private wars that the crown tried to suppress.
  • Legal and administrative texts distinguish the feud from felony: the one was a recognised state of enmity between parties, the other a crime against the king.
  • The spelling feud stabilised in the sixteenth century. The -eu- reflects the French diphthong and was probably reinforced by the unrelated legal word feud “fief”, which was much in fashion among Tudor lawyers.

The state against the feud

  • Tenth- and eleventh-century France saw the Peace of God and the Truce of God, which sought to protect non-combatants and to limit the days on which fighting was permitted.
  • The Holy Roman Empire issued Landfrieden — public peace ordinances — from 1103 onward, criminalising private warfare among subjects.
  • In England the king's peace, the growth of trespass and felony jurisdiction, and the decline of the duel gradually made the feud archaic as a legal institution.
  • By the seventeenth century a feud was something that happened in Scotland, Corsica, or the past — a marker of the primitive, which is exactly the tone it carries in the source passage about ordeals.

Romantic revival and modern metaphor

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century literature reimagined the feud as tragedy. The clan disputes of Walter Scott's Scottish novels, the family curses of Gothic fiction, and the operatic vendettas of the stage gave the word a romantic, fatalistic colour. In the twentieth century it migrated into politics and entertainment: the Hatfields and McCoys became the American archetype, Family Feud turned the phrase into a game show, and journalism adopted feud for any sustained quarrel between public figures. The modern word thus retains the old structure — two sides, long memory, reciprocal injury — while losing the kin obligation and the violence that once defined it.

The other feud: land tenure

English legal writing also uses feud for a fief: land held of a superior in return for service. This word comes from Medieval Latin feodum or feudum, which appears in eleventh-century documents and passes into Old French as fief. Its origin is genuinely disputed. One old proposal analyses it as Frankish *fehu-ōd, “cattle-property”, from *fehu “cattle, wealth” (the source of Latin pecunia and English fee) plus *ōd “property”; another seeks a link with Latin fides “faith” or with a Germanic word for a treaty. None commands consensus.

  • This homograph produced feudal, feudalism, feudatory, and the Scots law term feu, all concerned with land tenure and lordship.
  • It has nothing to do with blood revenge: the similarity of form is an accident of two different borrowing histories.
  • Because the collision causes confusion, modern historians generally write fief for the land and keep feud for the vendetta.

Phonology and modern use

The word is pronounced /fjuːd/, rhyming with mood; the Middle English fede would have yielded a different vowel, so the modern pronunciation follows the later French-influenced spelling. In contemporary English feud is most often a noun in journalism (“a long-running feud”), and the verb is almost always intransitive and continuous (“the feuding brothers”). Its emotional force remains strong: to call a disagreement a feud is to claim that it is old, personal, and irresolvable — a claim that participants and reporters alike find useful.
