- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Attested as *behealdan* (Class VII strong verb), meaning literally to hold by, grasp, maintain, protect, guard, or rule; gradually develops the derived mental sense of keeping in mind, paying heed to, and visually observing with care.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - Evolves phonologically into *behalden* / *beholden*; the older physical senses of grasping or defending territory begin to recede, while the specialized visual sense of looking upon with attention, wonder, or scrutiny becomes dominant; the past participle *beholden* diverges to express moral debt and gratitude.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c., King James Bible & Shakespeare) - Reaches the apex of cultural and spiritual resonance; famously used in the King James Bible to render Hebrew *hinneh* and Greek *idoú* ("Behold, I show you a mystery"), establishing "Behold!" as the supreme rhetorical command to witness divine miracles and solemn revelations; Shakespeare employs it extensively for dramatic grandeur.
- 18th to 19th Century - Retains an exalted status in elevated poetry, Romantic literature, and religious hymnody (Wordsworth, Keats, Shelley, Tennyson); specifically utilized by poets to express the sublime confrontation between the human soul and majestic nature or inner spiritual distress (as in Baudelaire's translated verse).
- 20th Century to Present - Mostly recedes from casual, everyday conversation in favor of common verbs like *look* or *see*, but remains powerfully entrenched in high literature, ceremonial address, epic cinema, and philosophical prose as an evocative verb of deep contemplation and solemn witnessing.
