The lineage of TONGS preserves the very metaphor by which the tool was first conceived. Everything begins with a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *denk-, "to bite, to clamp," whose descendants across the ancient branches show that the earliest speakers conceived gripping and biting as one action. In the Hellenic branch the root produced Ancient Greek daknein "to bite" (and dax "biting," which survives in the English learned coinage dacnomania, a morbid compulsion to bite); in the Indo-Iranian branch it yielded Sanskrit daśati "he bites." The same metaphor drives the Germanic branch: the tool that closes on an object the way jaws close on food was called *tangō, reconstructed to Proto-Germanic as literally "that which bites," and habitually used in the plural, *tangōz, because the instrument is made of two symmetrical legs, each a kind of jaw.

From this Germanic noun descend cognates across the whole northern family, and the near-identity of the forms proves the tool's antiquity: Old Saxon tanga (Low German tange), Old Frisian tange, Middle Dutch tanghe (Dutch tang), Old High German zanga (German Zange), and Old Norse töng (Swedish and Danish tång/tang, Icelandic töng). In Old English the word appears as tange, tong, or tanga, meaning "tongs, pincers, forceps" — the instrument for holding and lifting hot or awkward objects, above all at the smith's forge, where the Anglo-Saxon smith gripped his glowing iron with the two-armed tongs while the hammer fell.

  • The plural form hardens (Old to Middle English): because the tool is two limbs working as one, it was used in the plural from the earliest English, and by Middle English tonges/tanges had fixed the word as a plurale tantum: like scissors and shears, the tool of two halves is always spoken of in the plural. Modern tongs retains that shape; one says "a pair of tongs" exactly as one says "a pair of scissors."
  • The sibling word tang: the same Germanic stock, through the Old Norse cognate tangi "pointed end, prong, shank," gave Middle English tang — the shank of a knife or tool that is driven into the handle — literally "that which bites" into the handle. The two English words, tongs and tang, are thus siblings from the same "bite" root.
  • The forge and hearth through the centuries: tongs were a universal tool of medieval and early modern life — the smith's tongs for iron, the cook's and housewife's coal-tongs and sugar-tongs for the hearth and table. The tool's design changed little in a thousand years, and neither did the word.
  • The idiom "hammer and tongs" (1708): recorded from 1708, "to go at it hammer and tongs" means to work, fight, argue, or strive with all one's strength and violence. The image is exact: the smith hammering white-hot iron gripped in the tongs, at full force and full speed. Tolkien's goblins — "Hammer and tongs!" — swear by precisely this brutal, percussive energy, making the pair of forge-instruments a war-cry of iron and fire.
  • Modern continuations: the tool survives in every kitchen (cooking tongs, salad tongs, grill tongs), in the smithy, and in the idiom; the word's ancient "biting" image still flickers through the language, and the plural form still names the two-legged grip that reaches into fire.

Sound-shift notes: the Old English tange/tonge shows the ordinary West Germanic vowel, and the Middle English plural tonges led to the modern disyllabic /ˈtɒŋz/ (RP), with the final -s of the plural becoming an inseparable part of the word's identity. The remarkable stability of the form — the same tang-/töng-/zang- shape from Icelandic to German — is itself evidence of how ancient and how universal the tool is, and of how long the human mind has pictured a grasping tool as a pair of biting jaws.
