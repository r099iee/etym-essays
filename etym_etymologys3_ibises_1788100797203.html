1. Egypt: a sacred name in holy script. The word begins in Egypt as hb, written with the ibis hieroglyph and attested from the Old Kingdom (early 3rd millennium BCE) in religious and administrative texts. The bird was the emblem of Thoth, god of wisdom, writing and measurement, shown ibis-headed with a scribe’s palette; the ibis itself was kept about temple precincts, bred in later periods for the cult, and mummified by the tens of millions in great ibis catacombs — Saqqara and Tuna el-Gebel above all. Egyptian religion made the bird’s name a holy one: the same hieroglyph wrote “ibis” and the god’s name, so that the word itself was a piece of scripture.

2. Greece: from holy to curious. Greek borrowed the word as ἶβις (îbis). Herodotus (Histories, c. 430 BCE) is the first to dwell on the bird in prose: the ibis is sacred to Hermes (2.67, where the mummification of ibises is described as a mark of Egyptian reverence); the ibises destroy the winged serpents that the south wind brings against Egypt (2.75–76); and the priests treat the bird as a national treasure. Aristotle lists the ibis among the Nile’s birds in the History of Animals. In Greek the word sheds Thoth and takes on natural history: the famous bird is now a wonder of Egyptian zoology — serpent-eating, self-purging (the ibis was said to use its own bill to syringe water into its body and wash itself), utterly unhellenised — the very definer of the foreign and the ancient.

  • 3. Rome and the medieval library. Latin takes the word as ibis. Pliny (Natural History 10.87) repeats the self-purging lore at length; Ovid even names his curse-poem Ibis (c. 8–12 CE) after the Egyptian bird, making the ibis a symbol of biting, philosophic malice — the poem that attacks an enemy in and through a bird of ill omen. Late antique and medieval encyclopaedists (Isidore of Seville’s Etymologies, the bestiary tradition) pass the ibis to Christian Europe as a text-marvel and even a moral emblem — the bird that “washes itself with its own bill” became, for some moralisers, a figure of self-purging penance. The word reaches Middle English in exactly such a bookish stream: John Trevisa’s 1398 translation of Bartholomew the Englishman’s encyclopaedia records the earliest English “ibis” — a bird “of Egypt” known to readers only through books.
  • 4. Renaissance and the age of travel. The Renaissance naturalists restore the bird itself to Europe’s eye: Conrad Gessner’s Historia Animalium (1555) and Edward Topsell’s Fowles of Heaven (1607) describe the ibis with woodcuts and classical lore, and 17th-century travellers to Egypt — notably Pietro Della Valle — report the corridors packed with mummified ibises, objects that begin arriving in European cabinets of curiosities as marvels. The word now means “specimen” as much as “sacred bird”: the age of curiosity re-meets the age of faith.
  • 5. Linnaeus and the naming of the family. Linnaeus (Systema Naturae, 1758) placed the ibises among the stork-tribe in his genus Tantalus. Later ornithology split them out: the celebrated white African bird received the genus Threskiornis (G. R. Gray, 1842) — “the religious bird”, from Greek thrēskeia “worship” and ornis “bird” — and the family Threskiornithidae took its name from it. Scientific classification thus preserved, in a Greek compound, the Egyptian sanctity of the ancient hb: the museum drawer still carries the old religion in its label.
  • 6. Modern science, ecology and culture. In 2019 ancient-DNA analysis of Egyptian ibis mummies (Wasef et al., PLoS ONE) showed that most had been wild-caught for Thoth’s cult rather than bred — a flood of biological evidence that the “sacred” traffic was truly industrial. Meanwhile the name has gone global: the crested ibis of China and Japan is a conservation icon rescued from near-extinction in the 1980s; the glossy ibis has recolonised Britain after centuries; feral sacred ibises have established themselves in western France since the 1990s; and in Australia the white ibis has become the urban “bin chicken” of parks and tips — the sacred bird as rubbish-bin raider, a register shift that would have confounded a Herodotus. The plural “ibises” in modern prose may mean the Egyptian demigod’s bird, the conservation poster, or the car-park scavenger: one curved bill, three careers.
