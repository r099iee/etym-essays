- [Late 13th–14th c. Late Middle English] — Borrowed via Old French leüt from Arabic al-ʿūd, denoting the exotic, plucked stringed instrument brought back by crusaders and Moorish troubadours - [perception: an aristocratic, sophisticated instrument of courtly romance and poetic song]
- [15th–16th c. Renaissance Golden Age] — Reigned as the undisputed "king of instruments" across European courts, celebrating polyphony, humanistic balance, and personal virtuosic devotion - [perception: the supreme cultural symbol of refined education, celestial harmony, and courtly intimacy]
- [17th–18th c. Baroque Era & Subsequent Eclipse] — Expanded into large theorboed forms (theorbo, chitarrone) for basso continuo accompaniment before being gradually supplanted in salons and concert halls by the harpsichord and violin family - [perception: transitioned into an elite, nostalgic connoisseur's instrument, fading from mainstream performance]
- [19th c. Romantic Nostalgia] — Evoked in Romantic poetry (Keats, Shelley, Byron) and Pre-Raphaelite painting as a poetic relic of medieval chivalry and troubadour love, rather than an actively played instrument - [perception: a romanticized emblem of lost antiquity, poetic inspiration, and melancholic song]
- [20th–21st c. Early Music Revival & Musicological Taxonomy] — Resurrected globally through historically informed performance movements (e.g. Julian Bream, Hopkinson Smith) and adopted in ethnomusicology as the generic taxonomic category for all necked chordophones - [perception: revered as an authentic historic masterpiece of acoustic lutherie and an international musicological archetype]
