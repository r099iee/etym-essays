When Xue Pan calls for his roughs to 'get to work' on Feng Yuan, the plural noun names hired thugs: violent, lawless followers who supply the muscle for an arrogant young master's cruelty. In the story they are not colorful rogues but instruments of assault and intimidation, the blunt edge of privilege. They are men whose unrefined brutality can be summoned to beat, terrorize, and clear obstacles, and the word stresses coarseness of manners and morals together. These are people defined by jagged force rather than by craft, status, or ideology. The literary effect is to expose Xue Pan's power as something that outsources violence to disposable enforcers. DEFINITION, PRIMARY SENSE. As a plural noun, roughs (from the adjective rough) denotes violent, lawless men; rowdies, street thugs, or hired strongmen. The singular a rough, meaning a coarse or violent man, is attested from the early nineteenth century, and the plural roughs became the standard way to talk about gangs of street fighters and paid bashers in nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century prose and journalism, especially in British-influenced writing. The word always implies lack of refinement: these men are 'rough' the way unhewn stone or coarse cloth is rough, and the metaphor transfers surface texture to temperament and conduct. POLYSEMY. ROUGHS is a homographic plural with several unrelated technical uses, and the noun phrase the rough has further senses. The full inventory:
  • Violent, lawless men; thugs; rowdies (the sense of the card): a gang of roughs, hired roughs, street roughs. Dated but fully clear; period-flavored in modern prose.
  • The roughs (golf): the unmown, uneven strips of grass bordering the fairways, where a stray ball is hard to play. The golfing sense of rough as a noun dates from the early twentieth century, and its plural the roughs refers to the areas collectively.
  • Roughs (art, design, publishing): preliminary sketches or drafts, quick layout studies made before a finished piece. A rough is an unfinished first version of a drawing, advertisement, or cover.
  • Rough (noun, uncountable, various): broken or uncultivated ground (in rough land); the harsh or disagreeable side of things (the rough of it); the golfing rough used collectively; and figuratively 'a rough diamond' or 'diamond in the rough,' an unpolished person of good quality.
  • Rough-cut and rough draft: rough as adjective meaning 'approximate, unfinished, unpolished,' applied to a rough estimate, rough draft, rough sketch, or a rough-and-ready solution.
  • Related phrases built on the adjective: to sleep rough (to sleep outdoors without shelter), to rough it (to live without comforts), to rough someone up (to beat them). These keep the violence sense alive in informal English.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE. The bridge is a transfer from texture to character. The adjective rough first describes surfaces that are not smooth: shaggy hair, coarse cloth, undressed hide, uncultivated ground. By the fourteenth century it also describes what is harsh to experience or handle, including storms, seas, and speech. From 'unrefined surface' it moves to 'unrefined person': a rough man is one without polish in manner, education, or morals. The noun 'a rough' then names such a man outright, and the plural roughs collects them into a class of violent, lawless men. The whole chain is a slide down the scale of refinement, from texture to temperament to profession. COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER. Common collocations include a gang of roughs, hired roughs, street roughs, call for one's roughs, and send in the roughs. Register is informal to journalistic and historical-novelistic; the plural noun use is dated or period-flavored in contemporary prose but perfectly clear. It is not a legal term. In this context roughs efficiently brands Xue Pan's retinue as social jagged edges: men who have made a trade of roughness in both texture and temperament, set loose on Feng Yuan. The word is best avoided in formal academic prose, where thugs or violent men would be plainer.
