The colloquial verb and exclamation phut (most commonly encountered in the verbal idiom to go phut) signifies an abrupt cessation, breakdown, mechanical failure, or collapse into nothingness. In the context of the source sentence—drawn from P. G. Wodehouse, where Bertie Wooster notes that Uncle Tom Travers would sooner watch a hundred copies of Aunt Dahlia's magazine Milady's Boudoir “go __________” than part with his money—the term describes the catastrophic financial and operational demise of a publishing enterprise. The word captures ruin not as a grand, Wagnerian tragedy, but as a sudden, slightly ridiculous, deflated collapse, akin to a punctured balloon or a dying engine that emits a feeble pop before falling silent.

As an onomatopoeic noun and interjection, phut represents a dull, muffled, or flat popping sound—distinct from the sharp report of a gunshot (bang) or the resonant blast of a bomb (boom). It is the characteristic sound of a low-powered bullet striking soft earth, a suppressed firearm discharging, an internal combustion cylinder misfiring, or a pneumatic tyre giving up its air. From this acoustic profile, the word developed its extensive figurative life in late nineteenth-century Anglo-Indian English, rapidly permeating upper-class British colloquial speech.

  • Core idiomatic sense — to fail or break down abruptly (to go phut): Applied universally to appliances, vehicles, and mechanical assemblies that cease operating without advance warning. An alternator burns out, a vacuum tube blows, or a carburettor chokes: “the generator gave a slight shudder and went phut.”
  • Commercial, institutional, and project failure: Extended to business ventures, periodicals, theatrical productions, and administrative initiatives that go bankrupt or fold suddenly. In Wodehousian and interwar literature, magazines, theatrical revues, and speculative syndicates frequently “go phut” when financial backing evaporates.
  • Interpersonal and matrimonial collapse: Used with ironic understatement to denote the sudden disintegration of romances, engagements, alliances, or marriages: “their engagement went phut after the weekend party at Blandings.” The tone softens the social awkwardness through deliberate bathos.
  • Acoustic noun and adverbial usage: Designates the flat sound itself: “the bullet struck the sandbags with a dull phut”; “the cork popped phut into the tablecloth.” Here it retains its original nineteenth-century military attestation as recorded by Rudyard Kipling.
  • Spelling variants and graphic kin: While phut is the standard British spelling reflecting its Anglo-Indian transcription from Hindustani, English records numerous expressive cognates and orthographic cousins: fut, pffft, pfoof, and phuttt. However, phut remains the sole lexicographical entry recognized for the established verbal idiom go phut.
  • Sociolinguistic register and tone: Highly characteristic of British, Australian, and Commonwealth English of the late Victorian, Edwardian, and interwar periods. It belongs to the vocabulary of clubland, public schools, and country houses, offering an emotional cushion of stoic humour against financial ruin or domestic crisis.
  • Semantic bridge: The conceptual bridge moves from the physical acoustics of a puncture, misfire, or minor burst (where pent-up energy escapes with a muffled pop) to the sudden, irreversible deflation of complex plans, mechanisms, or commercial structures.

In contemporary usage, while somewhat vintage, go phut remains instantly intelligible across Britain and the Commonwealth, regularly surfacing in journalism, literary prose, and educated conversation whenever an author seeks to deflate self-important failures with wry humour.
