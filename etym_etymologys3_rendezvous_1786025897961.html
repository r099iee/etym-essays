RENDEZVOUS is one of the few English words whose entire biography can be read inside its spelling: a French imperative phrase, "rendez-vous", "present yourselves", lifted whole into English in 1590 and kept, like a pressed flower, in its original form. Because it is a compound, its history must be told as two interlocking stories - the story of rendez ("present") and the story of vous ("you") - and then the story of how the two came together.

1. The story of RENDEZ: from "give" to "present yourself". The deep ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *deh3-, "to give", one of the most productive giving-roots in the family: it lies behind Latin dare "to give" (source of English data, donor, tradition - the "handing over"), Greek didomi "I give", and Sanskrit dadati. In Latin the plain verb dare was strengthened by the prefix re- ("back", itself ultimately from the turning-root *wert-, so that reddere carries the sense "to turn back to the giver") to produce reddere, "to give back, to return, to restore, to render". Latin reddere passed into Old French as rendre, a verb of giving back, yielding, surrendering, and - crucially - of producing or presenting something. In medieval French, rendre acquired the reflexive use se rendre, "to betake oneself, to go, to present oneself": a soldier or a courtier "renders himself" to a place, going there of his own motion. The imperative plural of that reflexive, addressed to a group, was rendez-vous - literally "present yourselves!", "betake yourselves (there)!".

  • Ancient world: PIE *deh3- "to give" gives Latin dare and reddere, "to give back".
  • Medieval France: reddere > Old French rendre; reflexive se rendre "to betake oneself" develops; the imperative phrase rendez-vous is used to command troops and servants to assemble.

2. The story of VOUS: the making of a pronoun. The second element descends from the Proto-Indo-European pronoun stem *wos, "you", which in Latin produced vos, the plural "you". Roman formality extended vos to single distinguished persons (the imperial "we" of address), and this plural-of-address habit passed into French, where vous became the polite and formal "you" set against the familiar tu. In the phrase rendez-vous, the -vous is that pronoun: the command means strictly "you (plural or formal) present yourselves". The whole expression therefore encodes a piece of social history - a command in the respectful plural, the form used to address a body of men.

3. The frozen phrase becomes a noun and crosses the Channel. In sixteenth-century France the imperative phrase crystallized: rendez-vous was no longer merely an order but a thing - the place where troops were to assemble, and later any appointed meeting or gathering-place. English soldiers and diplomats, steeped in French military usage, borrowed it whole in 1590, and early English attestations are strictly military: the rendezvous is the assembly point where a dispersed force re-forms. From this narrow base the word broadened in English across the centuries, exactly as it had in French.

  • 1590s: military assembly point; "to rendezvous" is to bring forces together at an appointed place.
  • 17th-18th centuries: the place where people habitually gather - "a rendezvous for the fashionable"; a rendezvous of rivers or roads; the word loses its exclusively military feel.
  • 19th century: the romantic and secretive association grows: a rendezvous is a tryst, an assignation, a lovers' meeting, and the word enters the novel of manners and the detective story.
  • 20th century: spaceflight gives the word its most literal modern revival - two spacecraft performing an orbital rendezvous, meeting at an agreed point in space; logistics and emergency planning use "rendezvous point" for co-ordinated assembly.

4. Sound and register. The word retains its French pronunciation ("ron-day-voo") and, remarkably, its French spelling, including the hyphen, so that it stands in English like a miniature monument to the Norman and Renaissance prestige of French. Its plural rendezvouses and verb forms rendezvoused, rendezvousing naturalize it grammatically while the spelling stays French. On the card, "we will __________ with your army at first light" returns the word to its oldest English home: an officer commanding that two forces present themselves at an agreed place and hour - the exact command that sixteenth-century captains gave, in the same words, when they ordered their men to assemble. The history of rendezvous is thus the history of European military and courtly culture, compressed into a single hyphenated borrowing.
