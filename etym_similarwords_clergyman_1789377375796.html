• Priest: Similar because both refer to an ordained religious official who conducts worship; differs because priest carries a strong sacerdotal and sacramental connotation (offering sacrifices and mediating between God and man in Catholic, Orthodox, and high-church traditions), whereas clergyman is a broader social and denominational title common in Protestantism.
  • Minister: Similar because both denote an ordained Christian leader serving a congregation; differs because minister emphasizes service, preaching, and leadership especially in Protestant, non-conformist, and evangelical traditions (e.g., Baptist, Presbyterian, Methodist), whereas clergyman has a traditional association with the established Anglican church.
  • Pastor: Similar because both provide spiritual care to Christian believers; differs because pastor originates from the Latin word for shepherd (*pastor*), highlighting a warm, intimate, nurturing, and pastoral relationship of care over a specific local flock, whereas clergyman emphasizes formal ecclesiastical rank and societal status.
  • Vicar: Similar because a vicar is an ordained clergyman in charge of an Anglican parish; differs because vicar specifically denotes a priest who acts as a substitute or representative (*vicarius*) where the primary tithes were historically appropriated by an outside institution, receiving only the vicarial or small tithes.
  • Curate: Similar because both are ordained ministers; differs because a curate is specifically an assistant or junior clergyman appointed to assist the incumbent rector or vicar, often serving during their first years following ordination.
  • Rector: Similar because a rector is an ordained clergyman; differs because a rector historically held the full freehold and entire tithes (both great and small) of a parish, occupying the highest financial and legal status among parish clergy.
  • Parson: Similar because both denote an English parish clergyman; differs because parson (derived from Latin *persona*, representing the legal person of the church) is a warmer, slightly older or rustic colloquial term for the resident country parish priest.
  • Chaplain: Similar because a chaplain is an ordained clergyman; differs because a chaplain is attached to a specific secular or specialized institution (such as the military, a hospital, a prison, a school, or a royal household) rather than ministering to a geographic parish.
  • Preacher: Similar because clergymen preach sermons; differs because preacher focuses functionally on the act of delivering sermons and evangelizing, which in many traditions can be performed by non-ordained laypersons without formal ecclesiastical office.
