- [Compound formation (19th c.)] - "Foothill" (foot + hill) enters American English as a plain, utilitarian topographical term naming the lower hills at the base of a mountain range; entirely neutral and descriptive, carrying no emotional weight of its own.
- [Expansion through exploration & settlement (late 19th c.)] - Becomes a standard geographic descriptor in survey reports, travel writing, and settler accounts of the American West; acquires warm associations of transition — the threshold between tame, farmable lowlands and the wild, imposing peaks above.
- [Literary & scenic usage (20th c. onward)] - In prose (as in Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men) and scenic description the word gains a calm, expansive, almost majestic register; foothills now evoke golden rolling land, pastoral beauty, and the soft organic approach to dramatic mountains, while staying purely neutral in technical geography.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still chiefly a matter-of-fact label for the lower elevations of a mountain range; retains a mildly evocative, landscape-scenic flavour in travel and nature writing, but carries no negative or celebratory charge on its own.
