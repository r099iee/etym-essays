- 12th c. — Feudal French origin: emerges in Anglo-Norman and Old French as deservir, signifying to earn a fief, favor, or reward through faithful knightly or personal service.
- 13th c. — Middle English adoption: borrowed into English as deserven, retaining the feudal concept of earning wages or reward through devoted allegiance to a lord or king.
- 14th c. — Moral and theological expansion: in Chaucer and John Wycliffe’s biblical translations, deserveth expands to encompass moral desert before God—meriting divine grace or eternal condemnation.
- 16th – 17th c. — Renaissance and scriptural standardization: deserveth with the formal third-person suffix -eth becomes a staple of Elizabethan law, state papers, Shakespearean drama, and the King James Bible.
- 18th c. — Augustan prose and periodic rhetoric: while northern -s (deserves) dominates common speech, Samuel Johnson and his contemporaries deploy deserveth to impart classical balance and timeless moral authority.
- 19th c. – Present — Literary archaism: preserved in legal maxims, moral essays, and historical literature as an evocative marker of objective justice and enduring moral worth.
