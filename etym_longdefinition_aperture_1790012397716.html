aperture in its most general modern sense means an opening, hole, gap, or passage through which light, air, fluid, sight, or movement can pass. A dark aperture appeared in midair means a dark opening tore or manifested in empty space — a hole in the air itself. This core physical sense is neutral and broad: a cave aperture, a doorway aperture, an aperture in the clouds, a wound aperture. It stresses the open void rather than the material around it, and it sounds slightly technical or literary compared with plain hole or opening.

The dominant technical sense today is photographic and optical: the adjustable opening in a camera lens that controls how much light reaches the sensor or film. Photographers speak of wide aperture (low f-number like f/1.8, shallow depth of field, blurred background), narrow aperture (high f-number like f/16, deep focus), aperture priority mode, aperture blades, maximum aperture. Here aperture is not just any hole but a calibrated diaphragm, usually formed by overlapping metal leaves, whose diameter can be changed in stops. The semantic bridge from general hole to camera control is narrowing by function: the hole becomes an instrument. To open up the aperture means to brighten and isolate; to stop down the aperture means to darken and sharpen throughout.

  • General physical opening (core): a hole, gap, slit, or mouth in a surface, wall, body, or volume. An aperture in the roof, in the forest canopy, in midair. Literary and technical register; implies defined edge and throughput.
  • Photography — lens aperture: diameter of lens diaphragm, measured in f-stops (f/1.4, f/2.8, f/8). Wide aperture for portraits and night, narrow aperture for landscapes. Collocations: aperture priority, aperture ring, aperture blades, variable aperture. Central to exposure triangle with shutter speed and ISO.
  • Optics and instruments: aperture of a telescope, microscope, or eye — the clear diameter that admits light. Aperture stop is the physical ring that limits rays; numerical aperture (NA) measures light-gathering cone in microscopy and fibre optics; synthetic aperture radar (SAR) simulates a huge antenna by motion. Larger aperture generally means brighter image and finer resolution, limited by diffraction.
  • Anatomy and biology: a natural opening in the body or organism — pupil as aperture of iris, nasal aperture, the aperture of the larynx, shell aperture in molluscs (the main opening of a gastropod shell). Medical imaging: aperture of the pelvis, chest aperture. Neutral-descriptive, common in textbooks.
  • Astronomy and space: telescope aperture (8-inch aperture, 10-metre aperture), radio-telescope aperture, aperture synthesis. Also figurative sci-fi: a dark aperture in midair as portal, wormhole, rift. Your sentence uses this sense — space itself given an open mouth.
  • Architecture and archaeology: a doorway, window, oculus, or slit that admits light or passage. Medieval aperture, church aperture, fortress aperture. Often small by design for defence or light control.
  • Electronics and antenna theory: antenna aperture or effective aperture — the area that intercepts radio energy; aperture antenna (horn, slot, dish). Aperture coupling, aperture diffraction. Abstracted from hole to capture cross-section.
  • Figurative — opportunity or channel: a narrow aperture of opportunity, aperture for negotiation, aperture in the market. Means a limited opening in time or access through which action is possible. Borrows the visual logic of a small hole letting light through darkness; business and diplomacy jargon.

Grammatically aperture is a countable noun: apertures, apertured adjective (apertured mask), aperture as attributive modifier (aperture setting, aperture science). It has no verb form in standard use; you do not aperture something, you open or stop down an aperture. Register is formal-technical in science and photography, literary-elevated in fiction (a dark aperture appeared), never slang. Connotation is precise, almost clinical — to call something an aperture rather than a hole suggests measurement, edge, and purpose.

The bridge that unites all senses is control of passage. A hole in cloth lets air through by accident; an aperture lets light through by design or by defined geometry. Whether it is a camera iris, a pupil, a telescope mirror, a nostril, or a portal in midair, an aperture is where a barrier becomes permeable in a bounded way, and the size and shape of that bounded permeability determine what gets through.
