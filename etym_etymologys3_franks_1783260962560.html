PART ONE — An uncertain root: spearmen or free men.

The name of the Franks begins in a genuine etymological debate. Reconstructing backward from the attested Latin Francus (3rd century CE) and the Romance and Germanic forms, linguists posit a Proto-Germanic *frankō, but its meaning is disputed. The traditional school reads it as "javelin, spear", deriving the name from a weapon — so that the Franks would be "the spearmen", the lancers, a fierce warrior people named for their characteristic arm, much as the Saxons were named for the sax, a kind of knife or short sword. This fits the third-century sources, which describe the Franks as ferocious raiders and pirates on the lower Rhine, and it fits the wider Germanic habit of naming peoples by weapons or war-gear.

The rival school points to the remarkable later history of the name itself. In Late Latin, francus acquired the meaning "free" — because the conquering Franks, as the ruling class of Gaul, were the free, tax-exempt men of the land, in contrast to the subjected Gallo-Roman population. From that Latin francus "free" came Old French franc, English frank "free, candid", the verb frank "to stamp free of postage", and franchise. Defenders of the "free" etymology argue that the tribal name originally meant "the free, bold, fierce ones"; the medieval Frankish tradition itself, in the eighth-century Liber Historiae Francorum, glossed the name precisely as "free". Modern scholarship mostly favors the weapon theory for the earliest stage, treating "free" as a secondary development of the conquered-land social order — but the question is honestly still open, and both views are recorded.

PART TWO — Into Roman history: raiders, federates, and the name spreads.

Whatever the name meant, the Franks appear in Roman sources from the mid-third century as a feared presence on the lower Rhine and in the adjacent seas: they raided Gaul, crossed into Spain, and even appear in the sources as pirates. The Roman state answered with campaigns (the emperor Probus fought them in 277–278) and, more durably, with treaties: large numbers of Franks were settled as foederati, allied soldiers of Rome, a role that carried the tribe from the periphery into the machinery of the late empire. It is in this late-Roman world that the ethnonym hardened into its Latin form Francus / Franci, used by the panegyrics of the Tetrarchic age and by the fourth-century historians (Ammianus Marcellinus knows the Franci well).

The fifth century transformed the name utterly. As imperial authority in Gaul collapsed, the Salian Franks under their king Clovis conquered the Roman province and its barbarian competitors — defeating the Alemanni, the Burgundians, and the Visigoths — and Clovis's conversion to Catholic Christianity (about 496) bound the Frankish kingship to the Gallo-Roman church and aristocracy. The result was a new kind of polity: a Germanic kingdom that ruled a Romance-speaking, heavily Romanized population in alliance with Catholic orthodoxy, rather than against it. The name of the Franks now meant the free ruling people of the largest and most durable of the successor kingdoms.

PART THREE — Francia, the Carolingians, and the birth of France.

From the Merovingian kingdom the name grew into the concept of Francia, "the land of the Franks", which by the age of Charlemagne had swollen to embrace most of western and central Europe. Charlemagne's coronation as emperor in 800 made "the Franks" the name of the first great post-Roman empire of the West — a name that the papacy, the Byzantine court, and the Islamic world all used for the entire Latin Christian world. In Arabic the Franks became ifranj, in Persian farangi, in Greek phrankoi, and in the Crusading centuries (11th–13th) "Frank" was simply the word for any Western European, a usage that survived for centuries in the eastern Mediterranean and South Asia. The leap from a Rhine tribe to a universal name for the Latin West is one of the most remarkable semantic expansions in European history.

At home, the name underwent an even stranger development: it became the word for France and the French. Francia became Old French France, and the modern national name and the ethnonym merged. Meanwhile the Late Latin sense "free" produced the whole European family of frankness and franchise: Old French franc "free, noble", English frank "candid" (a "free-speaking" person), the verb frank "to stamp a letter free of postage", and franchise "the freedom of a city or trade". A single tribal name thus split into three modern strands: the historical Franks of the textbooks; the name France and the French people; and the adjective frank meaning free, open, and outspoken.

PART FOUR — The name in Gibbon and in memory.

In the historical tradition Gibbon inherits, the Franks are the vigorous, free, and warlike people who renewed Europe, and Gibbon pairs them on this card with the British kingdoms across the Channel: the "land of the Franks" is the continental Germanic homeland whose unconverted superstitions and ferocious freedom the Anglo-Saxons of Ida and Cerdic brought to Britain. The name thus carries a double charge in the passage — at once the founders of Christian France and the emblem of the unconquered pagan North from which, in the dark folklore Gibbon evokes, the spirits of the dead were ferried away at midnight.

  • 3rd century: Franci appear as Rhine raiders and pirates in Roman sources; settled as federates.
  • 5th–6th century: Clovis conquers Gaul; conversion to Catholic Christianity; Francia takes shape.
  • 8th–9th century: Charlemagne and the Carolingian empire make "Franks" the name of the Latin West.
  • Crusading age: ifranj / faranji become the generic word for Western Europeans in the Islamic and Byzantine world.
  • Modern: Franks is a purely historical ethnonym, while its derivatives France, French, frank, frankness, and franchise carry the old name into everyday vocabulary.

The biography of FRANKS is thus the biography of a name that meant both a weapon and a freedom, that named a tribe and then an empire, and that finally dissolved into the name of a nation (France) and the adjective for candid speech (frank).
