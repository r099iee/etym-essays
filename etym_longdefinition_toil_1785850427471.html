TOIL is hard, continuous, and exhausting physical or mental labour, and as a verb it means to work extremely hard or to move forward with great painful effort. In the reading passage the noun carries its modern core sense with full weight: "After decades of grueling toil in the underground mines, the elderly worker was finally able to rest" — the sentence pictures a whole working life compressed into the word, the grinding, unremitting, body-wearing labour of the mine. Toil is not ordinary work and not a mere task; it is the kind of effort that demands everything and gives back exhaustion, the labour of the labourer, the peasant, the miner, the drudge, and of anyone straining at a heavy task through fatigue.

SENSES AND USES

  • Core noun sense: hard, wearying, continuous labour — "the toil of the harvest", "a life of toil", "years of unceasing toil". The word names the whole burden of exhausting work, physical or mental, and often carries a note of dignity or pity for the one who toils.
  • Verb sense: to work extremely hard — "they toiled at the rock face", "she toiled over the manuscript for years"; to labour at something with persistent effort, with the emphasis on strain and duration.
  • Verb of movement: "to toil up / toil along / toil through" — to move with great painful effort, especially uphill or against difficulty — "they had to toil up the steep mountain pass in the freezing rain" (the example on this card); the verb of painful progress, as of a climber or a procession dragging itself forward.
  • The plural "toils" (a different word): snares, nets, or entanglements used in hunting, and by metaphor any trap that catches and holds a person — "caught in the toils of the law", "in the toils of love". This is an archaic or literary sense, normally plural, and comes from an entirely different root (see etymology).
  • "Toil and trouble": the famous idiom from Shakespeare's Macbeth (1606) — "Double, double toil and trouble" — in which toil, in the witches' chant, suggests the feverish, anxious labour of the spell along with its turmoil; the phrase has become a byword for oppressive, ominous labour.
  • Historical "toil" as battle and strife: in Middle English the noun could mean strife, contention, or battle — the labour of war — a sense that shows the word's origin in "struggling, dragging about" rather than in calm industry.
  • Modern discourse: "toil" survives in elevated, literary, and rhetorical prose about work — "the dignity of toil", "honest toil", "the toil of the working class" — and contrasts with the neutral, everyday "work" and the more formal "labour".
  • Register: toil is slightly elevated and literary; it is commoner in narrative, rhetoric, and poetry than in casual speech, where it has been overtaken by "work", "grind", or "slog", but it remains instantly understood and emotionally charged.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: modern English TOIL is actually the meeting-point of two distinct etymologies that fused in ordinary use. The labour sense descends from Anglo-French toiler, "to struggle, fight, drag back and forth", which came from Late Latin tūdiculāre, "to crush, beat, stir, agitate", itself built on Latin tūdicula, the pestle or machine for crushing olives, from tundere, "to beat, pound". The picture behind the labour sense is of beating and stirring — physical turmoil, dragging about, struggling against a resistant material — which is why the oldest English uses of toil carry the flavour of battle, strife, and violent exertion before the word settles into the steady grind of hard work. The hunting-snare sense ("toils") descends separately from Old French toile, "cloth, net, web", from Latin tēla, "woven cloth, web". In English experience the two words — the labour of struggling and the entanglement of a net — interwove, so that "toil" can suggest both the exhausting effort of a struggling worker and the trapped helplessness of a creature caught in a snare; both images colour the modern word's emotional weight.
