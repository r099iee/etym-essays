- [Late 14th century: scriptural glossing] - Middle English borrows the word through Old French and uses it chiefly for the expounding of a text, above all the explanation of scripture from the pulpit; its register is reverent, doctrinal, and homiletic — a preacher’s act of making the Word plain.
- [16th–17th century: learned and rhetorical sense] - Absorbed into scholarly discourse, “exposition” becomes the systematic setting-forth of a theme, theory, or doctrine — methodical, almost architectural, prized for lucidity and arrangement, and valued as the neutral counterpart of persuasion.
- [18th century: narrative criticism] - Adopted into the vocabulary of theatre and fiction, it names the opening portion of a plot that establishes setting, characters, and prior events before the conflict begins; the word now describes how a story is laid out as much as how an idea is laid out.
- [19th century: public spectacle] - A spectacular new sense appears: a large public exhibition of manufactures, artworks, and inventions, culminating in the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the great “world’s fairs”; the word swings from the lectern toward industry, display, and mass entertainment.
- [Contemporary sense] - The senses split and coexist: a formal, intellectual “exposition of an argument” in academic prose, a “trade exposition” in commerce, and the “exposition” of storytelling craft in literary study — today the word always means laying a subject open to view, whether before the mind’s eye or the public gaze.
