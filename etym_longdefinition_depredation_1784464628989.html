DEPREDATION (noun). Primary sense: the act of plundering, pillaging, ravaging, or laying waste; destructive spoliation, especially the systematic seizure of booty and the ruin of a place, a people, or a possession. It is almost always used of actions that are grave, recorded, and ruinous — the work of armies, raiders, and natural forces — rather than of casual theft. It is very often plural: "depredations" names a series of such acts over time. Polysemy — all senses:
  • Military and historical plundering — "the depredations of the Viking raiders", "piratical depredations". The earliest and still central sense; a noun of record for spoil-taking in war and by bandits, privateers, and pirates.
  • Ecology and wildlife damage — "depredation by wolves", "livestock depredation": the killing or damaging of livestock, crops, or game by wild animals. Standard terminology in conservation, farming, and wildlife management.
  • Natural and metaphorical erosion — "the depredations of time / age / climate / weather": the slow destructive action of impersonal forces on buildings, bodies, memory, or reputation.
  • Legal and formal register — in law, history, and ecclesiastical writing, depredation names the unlawful seizure of property and appears in formal accusations ("acts of piracy and depredation").
  • Plural as a catalogue — the plural "depredations" emphasizes recurrence and accumulation, turning single acts into a chronicle of ruin ("the depredations of the last decade").
Derived family: depredator (one who plunders; also, in ecology, a predatory animal), depredatory (adjective), depredate (verb, rare); and through the same Latin stock: prey, predator, predatory, predation, prehensile (Latin prehendō "to grasp" is built on the same PIE root *ghend- "to seize"). Usage and register: depredation is a high-register word. In ordinary speech speakers say "looting" or "damage" instead, reserving depredation for writing that is formal, historical, legal, or technical. The verb depredate is rare; the noun (especially the plural) and the adjective depredatory do the work. Characteristic collocations: "the depredations of war", "to suffer depredations", "acts of piracy and depredation", and in ecology "livestock depredation". Something depredates someone or something: armies depredate a country, wolves depredate a flock, time depredates a building — the grammar always points from an agent to a victim. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges: The Latin verb praedārī "to plunder, to prey upon" derives from praeda "booty, plunder, prey", itself from *prai-hedā — "that which is seized before (one)" — built on the PIE root *ghend- "to seize, take". The prefix dē- ("thoroughly, down from, away") intensifies the verb: dēpraedārī means to strip completely, to plunder out. The action noun dēpraedātiō "a plundering" gives the Romance and English forms. The bridge from human pillaging to animal predation and then to impersonal ruin runs along the shared idea of systematic taking and ravaging: first armies, then wolves, then time itself are said to commit "depredations". Tolkien's dwarves, speaking of "the depredations of dragons", use the chronicle register: Smaug as a historical catastrophe, the systematic stripping of a people.
