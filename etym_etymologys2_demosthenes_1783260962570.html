The name is a compound of two Greek elements, each with its own Indo-European chain, so the derivation is given for each element separately and then fused. CHAIN 1 — the "people" element: PIE *deh₂- (also *dā-) "to divide, share out, allot" (the root of dividing and sharing; also in Greek dáis "portion, feast", Sanskrit dáti "cuts, allots", and the root of English demon? No — English demon is from Greek daímōn, unrelated; the "divide" root is distinct) → Proto-Hellenic *dāmos "division, district" → Mycenaean Greek (Linear B, c. 1300 BC) da-mo "the district, the community" → Ancient Greek dêmos (δῆμος) "district, land-allotment; the people, the citizen body" (Doric form dâmos; source of democracy, democrat) → the first element of the compound Dēmosthénēs. CHAIN 2 — the "strength" element: PIE *segh- "to hold, prevail, conquer" (traditional derivation; the root also of Greek ékhō "I hold, have", Sanskrit sah- "to overcome, endure" with sahas "might", Avestan hazah "power", and the Germanic victory-root *seg- in Old Norse sigr, German Sieg, and English name-element sige) → Greek sthénos (σθένος) "strength, might, bodily force" → the second element of the compound Dēmosthénēs. Note on the debate: Robert Beekes treats sthénos with marked caution and allows the possibility of a Pre-Greek (non-Indo-European substrate) origin; the *segh- connection is the traditional and still standard account, but the word's history is not fully secure. FUSION: Ancient Greek Dēmosthénēs (Δημοσθένης) "strength of the people", a compound personal name already attested as a Greek name before the orator (the name was not invented for him) → Latin Dēmosthenēs "Demosthenes" → English Demosthenes, the Athenian orator (384–322 BC). Note on the person: the great orator is the most famous bearer of the name, but the name itself is an ordinary Greek aristocratic formation, "people-strength," of the same type as Sophokles "glory-of-wisdom."
