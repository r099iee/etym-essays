Tree-bark entered English around 1300 as a borrowing from Scandinavian settlers and traders. Old Norse had bǫrkr / börkr for tree covering, from Proto-Germanic *barkuz, itself probably related to birch — Old English beorc, Proto-Germanic *berkjon — from PIE *bhereg- to shine, bright, white, in reference to the pale bark of birches. The native Old English word for the same thing was rind, still preserved in orange rind, but rind retreated to fruit and cheese while bark took trees, likely because Norse timber and shipbuilding vocabulary carried prestige in the Danelaw.

Phonetically the change is minimal: Proto-Germanic *barkuz with a-stem nominative -uz lost its ending in Norse to börkr with rounded vowel from u-mutation, then English levelled it to bark with the northern short a. No Grimm shift obscures it; b, r, k remain stable across a millennium. Semantically it has barely moved: always the dead-plus-living sheath outside wood. What changed is use: medieval tanners needed oak bark for tannin, giving bark-bed, barkery, bark-bound; early modern apothecaries imported Peruvian Jesuit's bark for quinine and cinnamon bark as spice; American frontiersmen barked canoes and shingles.

  • Distinguish homonyms: bark to yelp is Old English beorcan, Proto-Germanic *berkan of imitative origin, with noun beorc; bark a ship is Middle French barque, Late Latin barca, possibly cognate with barge; neither shares *barkuz and their convergence in spelling is accidental.
  • Related forms: Low German borke, Dutch berk, birch, bright; all point to pale shining surface as naming motive.
  • Attestations: c.1300 tree skin in northern homilies; 1540s verb to bark meaning to strip; 1850 transferred to skin — to bark one's shin; forestry literature from Evelyn's Sylva 1664 onward elaborates bark thickness and grafting.
  • Socio-cultural arc: from peasant roofing and peasant shoes of bast, to industrial tannin and cork trade, to modern ecology where bark is biodiversity hotel and fire armour.

A nuanced debate concerns whether Proto-Germanic *barkuz is truly from *bhereg- bright or an independent substrate word for covering that later associated with birch by folk etymology. De Vaan, Beekes, and OED note probably related to birch but mark it probable, not certain, because the -k- extension is irregular. Etymonline follows the bright-birch link. What is undisputed is the Scandinavian route: Middle English bark replaces rind only after Norse contact, and cognates in Middle Dutch borke confirm a shared Germanic noun. If the deeper PIE link is doubted, the chain from Old Norse börkr to modern bark remains solid and fully attested.

Modern forestry retains bark as a diagnostic key: lenticel pattern, fissure depth, and peeling mode identify species in winter, while bark thickness predicts fire survival and beetle risk, keeping this Norse loan at the centre of woodland craft and science.
