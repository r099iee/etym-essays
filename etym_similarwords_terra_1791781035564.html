• earth: Similar because it renders the core Latin sense of the ground and the globe; differs because earth is the native English word and covers soil, planet and element in ordinary speech, while terra is a Latin citation confined to fixed phrases.
  • land: Similar because both oppose dry ground to water; differs because land is a plain countable English noun for territory or property, whereas terra is uncountable in English and literary in tone.
  • ground: Similar because both name the surface beneath the feet and can be figurative; differs because ground is the everyday word, including reasons and electrical earths, while terra carries classical colour and no such technical breadth.
  • soil: Similar because both denote the cultivated or fertile layer; differs because soil is a scientific and agricultural term for the substance, while terra denotes earth in general and appears in soil names only as a classifier.
  • terrain: Similar because both derive from Latin terra and refer to land; differs because terrain is a naturalised English word for the physical features of an area, especially for travel or military purposes.
  • territory: Similar because both can mean a country or region; differs because territory is a legal and political concept of bounded jurisdiction, whereas terra names the physical land itself.
  • mainland: Similar because both can translate the Italian sense of terra ferma; differs because mainland is a relative term defined against islands, while terra makes no such comparison.
  • globe: Similar because both can denote the whole Earth; differs because globe is a shape-based, geographical term, while terra is a Latin name used in astronomy and science fiction.
  • dry land: Similar because it is the literal sense of terra firma; differs because the English phrase is descriptive and unmarked, whereas terra imports the classical allusion.
