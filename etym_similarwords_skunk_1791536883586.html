• Polecat: Similar because both are small carnivorous mammals capable of discharging a pungent, foul-smelling secretion from anal scent glands when defending against predators; differs because the true polecat (Mustela putorius) is an Old World mustelid native to Europe and the wild ancestor of the domestic ferret, possessing a much weaker, localized musk rather than the specialized long-range chemical aerosol weapon characteristic of American mephitid skunks.
  • Badger: Similar because both are stocky, ground-dwelling carnivores featuring striking aposematic black-and-white facial striping and defensive scent glands; differs because badgers possess formidable digging claws, heavy muscular strength, and fierce physical combat capabilities, relying on subterranean burrowing and aggressive biting rather than chemical aerosol spraying.
  • Weasel: Similar because both share historical taxonomic affinity within the carnivoran superfamily Musteloidea and possess musky anal glands; differs because weasels are slender, hyper-agile, hyper-carnivorous predators built for entering rodent burrows and chasing prey at speed, lacking the skunk's deliberate, plodding gait and specialized defensive thiol artillery.
  • Scoundrel: Similar because both are utilized as sharp, disdainful personal insults targeting dishonest, untrustworthy, or dishonorable individuals; differs because scoundrel is a classic moral characterization denoting roguish or unprincipled conduct without the visceral, physical revulsion and stench-associated disgust inherent to calling someone a skunk.
  • Shutout: Similar because both describe a complete, decisive athletic defeat in which one team or competitor prevents their opponent from scoring; differs because shutout is a formal, neutral statistical term in sports like baseball, soccer, and hockey, whereas to skunk an opponent is an informal, colloquial, and often humiliating gaming idiom.
