Confer is a verb with two great branches that share one Latin root: (1) to consult together, to hold council, to exchange opinions with a view to a decision — ‘the doctors conferred at the bedside’; and (2) to bestow, grant, or lay upon — ‘to confer a degree’, ‘to confer a knighthood’, ‘to confer a favour’. Both branches mean a bringing-together: minds brought together in counsel, or honours brought and laid upon a recipient.

In the source sentence the word carries the first, consultative sense in its simplest Biblical dress: ‘they met together to confer.’ The parties assemble for the express purpose of joint counsel — to compare notes, weigh courses, and reach a common mind. The sentence's plainness is the point: no object, no ceremony, just meeting and mutual counsel, the oldest political act in the language.

Core meaning in detail: The consultative branch (confer with) is reciprocal and intransitive: two or more parties bring their views together, each bearing counsel to the common stock. The bestowing branch (confer on/upon) is transitive and one-directional: an authority bears an honour, degree, power, or benefit and lays it upon a recipient — universities confer degrees, sovereigns confer knighthoods, statutes confer rights. Grammar keeps the branches apart: with for counsel shared, on/upon for honours given. Both descend transparently from Latin conferre — com- (‘together’) + ferre (‘to bear, carry’) — to bring together, whether views or gifts.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To consult together, hold council (primary consultative sense, 16th c. onward): ‘they met together to confer’. The bridge is the Latin core conferre ‘to bring together’: minds and counsels brought into one room. Doctors, lawyers, and generals confer before they act.
  • To bestow or grant an honour, degree, right, or favour (primary transitive sense): ‘the university conferred the degree’, ‘the Queen conferred the honour’. The bridge is the same bringing-together pointed outward: the giver brings the gift together with the recipient — the honour and the head meet.
  • To compare views or statements (older literary sense, 16th–17th c.): ‘to confer passages’, ‘conferring testimonies’. The bridge is Latin conferre ‘to bring together for comparison’ — the ancestor of conference as comparison (compare the abbreviation cf., Latin confer, ‘compare!’). Scholars conferring manuscripts lay readings side by side.
  • To contribute or conduce (archaic): ‘this confers to our happiness’. The bridge runs from bringing-together to adding-unto: what is brought to the stock conduces to the result. The construction with to is now obsolete, replaced by conduce and contribute.
  • To take counsel with oneself — to reflect (rare, reflexive shading): ‘he conferred with himself’. The bridge turns the council inward: the mind meets together with itself. Mostly a novelist's flourish, but grammatically sound.
  • The imperative confer! / cf. (scholarly abbreviation): ‘cf. p. 42’ — Latin ‘compare!’, directing the reader to bring another passage alongside. The bridge is the comparing-sense frozen into citation furniture, used in every footnote in the world.

Register and connotation: confer is dignified, institutional English — senates, synods, sickrooms, and degree ceremonies. The consultative branch sounds grave and responsible (matters are conferred before they are decided); the bestowing branch sounds august and formal (honours are conferred, never merely given). Its noun conference has democratised the root — from summits to parent-teacher evenings — while the verb keeps its gown on. To confer is never hurried: the word insists on procedure, witnesses, and weight.
