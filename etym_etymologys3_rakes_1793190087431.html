The history of rakes is the history of hay itself. Long before English existed, Germanic farmers needed a light toothed implement to invert cut grass so that sun and wind could cure it into winter fodder, and the reconstructed Proto-Germanic *rakō- names exactly that tool — a wooden head set with peg teeth on a long ash handle, cheap to make and light enough to swing all day.

Old English meadows: The attested form raca appears in glossaries and estate records of the 9th–11th centuries, when haymaking was a customary labour service. Monastic granges and thegns’ demesnes measured the summer by the scythe and the rake: men mowed, women and children turned and cocked, and the reeve counted the cocks. The rake’s teeth then were carved pegs of hardwood, replaceable when they snapped — archaeology from waterlogged sites preserves such heads with their sockets worn smooth by straw.

Medieval harvest ritual: Under the manorial system haytime was communal and compulsory, announced by the church bell. Turning the swath with rakes fell to the whole village, and harvest songs and ale reckoned the rake among summer’s emblems alongside the scythe. Middle English rake passes unchanged in form while its spelling settles after the Norman Conquest, sharing pages with ploughs, harrows, and pitchforks in husbandry treatises.

Sound and spelling: The word’s phonetic journey is modest — Old English raca with its hard c passes through Middle English vowel adjustments to modern rake, the Great Vowel Shift lengthening the vowel while the final -e falls silent. The plural simply adds -s, unbroken from the earliest records.

Divergence and homonyms: In the 1500s a wholly separate career begins when rakehell — a hell-raising rogue, perhaps from rake in the sense of one who rakes hell for sins — clips to rake, giving Restoration comedy its beloved libertine. A third rake, the nautical slope of a mast, enters in the 1600s, likely from Scandinavian. Neither touches the tool’s meaning, yet all three share dictionary columns and confuse unwary readers.

Iron, empire, and suburbia: The 18th-century agricultural revolution puts iron teeth into garden rakes for seedbed work, while wooden hay rakes persist in meadows into the Victorian age. Emigrants carry the form worldwide — American garden catalogues, Australian vineyards, colonial parade grounds levelled for cricket. Then the 20th century mechanises haymaking with tedders and balers, and the hand hay rake retires to smallholdings, orchards, and heritage farms, while the spring-tine lawn rake conquers suburbia.

Literary witnesses:

  • Tusser’s husbandry verse (1573): rakes ordered among the tools the good farmer must own before haytime.
  • Pastoral poetry: from Milton’s rustics to Hardy’s hayfields, the rake signals honest summer labour.
  • Modern allotments: seed packets still instruct the gardener to cover seed with the back of the rake — the gesture unchanged in a millennium.

From monastic grange to suburban shed, rakes have gathered and levelled without changing their nature: teeth, handle, and the long pulling stroke of summer.
