Bollards, the plural of bollard, are in their harbour sense short, stout, ground-fast posts — cast iron, steel, stone, or timber — to which a vessel’s mooring lines are made fast, and by extension any squat guardian post that stands its ground against rope, hull, or wheel. To see them is to know at once that the ground is worked: ropes strain, chains clink, paint is chipped at rope-height.

In the source sentence they do their night-watch work in white paint: the white ______ near the water’s edge glimmered in the light of two lamps on high posts. Paired lamps above, paired emphasis below — white posts catching gold light at the brink where stone meets water. The colour is practical, not pretty: whitewash so boatmen, porters, and night-walkers see the obstruction and the mooring-point alike.

Core meaning in detail: The classic quay bollard rises knee- to waist-high, often mushroom- or T-headed, sometimes a pair of inclined posts (the double bitt), bedded deep in coping stone or concrete and backed by massive holding power. A ship’s line is passed round it in figure-of-eight turns so friction, not knots, takes the strain of tide and wind. Road and security cousins work by the same virtue — mass and rootedness — stopping vans rather than vessels, guiding feet rather than hawsers.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Quay mooring post (primary, from 19th c.): the iron or timber post on wharf or landing-stage to which ships make fast. This is the source sense and the dictionary headword.
  • Shipboard bitt or post (nautical): uprights on deck round which running rigging and mooring lines are belayed. The bridge is direct transfer: the quay’s furniture reproduced aboard to take the same strains.
  • Traffic and pedestrian bollard (modern street sense): short posts dividing footway from carriageway, guarding shopfronts and quaysides from vehicles. The bridge is function: from holding ropes to holding back wheels — the same squat refusal to move.
  • Security and anti-ram post (contemporary): steel-cored, concrete-filled guardians before embassies, stadia, and promenades. The bridge extends traffic duty into defence: mass plus depth defeats momentum, whether barge or lorry.
  • Light-bearing or decorative harbour post (extended): white-painted or lamp-topped bollards marking edges at night, as in the source. The bridge runs through visibility: the mooring post doubles as the edge-marker, paint and lamp making mass legible after dark.
  • River and canal relic (heritage sense): old timber or cannon-shaped bollards kept as character where ropes no longer come. The bridge is affection: obsolete work becomes ornament, and bollard becomes a museum word.

Register and connotation: bollards is workmanlike and reassuring — harbourmaster’s English, council-depot English. It promises that someone has thought about strain, tide, and human error, and has planted iron accordingly. White-painted, lamp-lit, they turn a dangerous edge into a cared-for edge; rusted and leaning, they warn that care has lapsed. Either way they are the punctuation marks of working waterfronts.
