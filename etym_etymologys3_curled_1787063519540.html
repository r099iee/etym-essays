The word curled possesses an intriguing phonological and semantic history that illustrates the powerful influence of North Sea trade, textile crafts, and metathesis in the shaping of English vocabulary.

1. Indo-European Foundations and Germanic Coiling Roots

The deep ancestral foundation of curl is the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ger-, signifying "to turn, to wind, to twist, to gather together." This root gave rise to a vast network of words across Indo-European languages dealing with roundness, gathering, and twisting:

  • In Latin, it produced curvus ("curved, bent"), corona ("garland, crown"), and circus ("circle, ring").
  • In Proto-Germanic, it generated several expressive adjectives and nominal forms beginning with velar plosives, specifically *kruslaz and *kurlaz ("curly, wavy, twisted, frizzled").

In continental West Germanic dialects, this gave rise to Middle Dutch krul ("a lock of hair, a spiral"), Middle Low German krulle, and Old High German krus (source of modern German Krause, "ruff, frill").

2. Middle English Borrowing and Consonantal Metathesis

The word was not natively attested in early Old English manuscripts (which preferred native terms like hringed or crisp). Instead, it entered Middle English during the 13th and 14th centuries through extensive mercantile and cultural interaction with Flemish weavers, Low German merchants, and Hanseatic traders in eastern English ports.

The earliest Middle English form was the adjective crul or crulle (c. 1300), meaning "having curly or frizzy hair." Geoffrey Chaucer famously employed this original form in the General Prologue to The Canterbury Tales (c. 1387) when describing the young Squire: "With lokkes crulle as they were leyd in presse" ("With locks curly as if they had been laid in a press").

During the 15th century, the word underwent systematic metathesis—a common phonetic process in English historical linguistics where the liquid consonant [r] swapped positions with the preceding vowel (shifting from crul / crulle to curl, mirroring similar historical metatheses such as Old English brid → bird, and thridda → third).

3. Functional Shift: From Adjective to Verb and Past Participle

By the late 15th and early 16th centuries, English speakers had converted the nominal and adjectival forms into an active weak verb: curlen / curl. The past tense and past participle curled was rapidly integrated into standard literary prose and poetry:

  • In Elizabethan drama and poetry, curled was used extensively to depict aristocratic fashions, such as the elaborate, heated-iron curling of hair, wigs, and ruffs.
  • Shakespeare used the term across diverse registers, famously in Othello (Act I, Scene 2), where Desdemona is described as rejecting "the wealthy curled darlings of our nation" (referring to fashionable, perfumed Venetian courtiers).

4. Physical, Postural, and Metaphorical Maturation

During the 17th and 18th centuries, the semantic field of curled expanded beyond human grooming to capture dynamic natural and physical phenomena. Writers described ocean waves that curled over reefs, leaves that curled under scorching summer suns, and ribbons of smoke that curled from cottage hearths.

Concurrently, the physiological phrase to curl up developed into a ubiquitous idiom for the comforting or protective posture of humans and animals withdrawing into a compact, restful ball. In 18th-century Scotland, the verb gave its name to the ice sport of curling, named after the distinctive turning motion imparted to the stones. Today, curled stands as a rich, multi-layered term bridging ancient Germanic twisting, Chaucerian portraiture, and modern physical comfort.
