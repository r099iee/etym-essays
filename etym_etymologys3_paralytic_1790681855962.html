The journey of paralytic begins in the Greek gymnasium and battlefield, where to loosen (lūein) was both to untie and to disable. PIE *leu-, to loosen, divide, cut apart — source also of English loose, lose, loosen, lysis, analyse, palsy via different routes — gave Greek lūein, to release, dissolve, acquit. Prefixed with para-, beside, amiss, alongside (from PIE *per-, forward, through, beside), it formed paralūein, literally to loosen beside or at the side, i.e., to disable one side of the body, to strike sideways. Hippocratic writers nominalised the result as paralysis, a loosening-aside, and adjectivised it as paralytikos, pertaining to such loosening. Galen systematised the vocabulary: paralysis for loss of motion or sensation, paralytikos for the person or remedy concerned, embedding the word in Western medicine for two millennia.

Late Latin borrowed the cluster wholesale as paralysis, paralyticus, and Christian Vulgate usage (the paralyticus let down through the roof to Jesus) gave it wide currency beyond physicians. Old French paralytique, perclus and Anglo-Norman mediation brought it into Middle English as paralitik, paralityk alongside the shortened native doublet palsy (from Old French paralisie with syncope), creating the enduring doublet palsy/paralysis and palsied/paralytic. Early Modern English regularised spelling to paralytic under Latin humanist influence, fixing stress on the penult and pronunciation with /tɪk/. Medical writers from Sydenham to Charcot refined senses: paralytic stroke, general paralysis of the insane, paralytic poliomyelitis, each anchoring the adjective to specific lesions, toxins, or infections.

  • Phonetic path: Greek upsilon /y/ Latinises to y then anglicises to /ɪ/; Greek kappa retains /k/ throughout; French loss of final -us and English re-Latinisation restore -ic /ɪk/; initial para- reduces to /ˌpærə/ with secondary stress in RP /ˌpærəˈlɪtɪk/, primary stress on -lyt- reflecting Greek quantity.
  • Semantic drift: from lateral loosening → unilateral palsy → any loss of voluntary motion → any causing of such loss (active/passive ambivalence) → figurative inhibition of will by fear or thought. The Hamlet bridge is decisive: Coleridge, Bradley, and modern psychology made paralytic hesitation the canonical example of thought sicklying action.
  • Socio-cultural context: asylums, neurology, and polio epidemics kept the literal sense urgent through 1950; disability-rights movements since 1970 have retired the noun a paralytic in favour of person-first phrasing, while management literature (analysis paralysis, 1960s) and self-help ironically revitalised the figurative adjective for affluent over-thinkers.
  • Literary attestations: Wycliffe's paralytic, Shakespeare's Hamlet ("thus conscience does make cowards" glossed by critics as paralytic conscience), Keats's "paralytic" numbness, Woolf's paralytic self-consciousness, and the source sentence's Hamlet gloss — each using neurology to authorise psychology.

Debated nuance: Indo-Europeanists dispute whether Greek para- reflects PIE *per- (through, beside) or a separate Aegean particle, and whether *leu- should be split into *leu- (loosen) versus *lewH- (cut); the OED notes the uncertainty without affecting the Greek-internal derivation, which is secure. For learners the morphemic story is sufficient and memorable: para- (amiss) + lys- (loosening, as in analysis, dialysis) + -ic (pertaining to) = pertaining to a loosening that leaves power disconnected, whether nerve from muscle or will from deed.
