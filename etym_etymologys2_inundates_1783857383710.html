Proto-Indo-European root *wed- (also *wédōr / *ud-, "water, wet") — reconstructed, no attested date. The root meant "water" and appears in the r/n-stem noun *wédōr (genitive *udnés, "of water"). Its descendants span the family: Greek húdōr (English hydro-, hydrate), Sanskrit udán, Latin unda and undāre, Old Irish uisce (source of whiskey), Germanic *watōr (English water). Proto-Indo-European zero-grade *ud- "water, wet" — the vowel-less form of the root used in Latin unda. Latin unda, undae f. "wave, billow, water" — attested 3rd–1st c. BCE (Plautus, Cicero, Virgil). Built on the zero-grade *ud- with an -n- extension; the exact formation (*ud-nā / *udn-ā) is debated but the root connection is secure. Latin undō, undāre "to rise in waves, to surge, to wave" — attested 1st c. BCE (from unda). The base verb of the compound. Latin in- + undāre → inundō, inundāre "to overflow, to flood, to cover with water" — attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE (Livy, Ovid, Pliny). The prefix in- here means "into, upon," giving "to pour waves upon." Latin inundātus, past participle "flooded, overflowed" (stem inundāt-) — attested in the classical period; this participial stem is what English borrows. English inundate (verb) "to overflow, to flood; to overwhelm" — first recorded in English c. 1620, borrowed directly from Latin inundātus / inundāre. The literal sense dominates at first; the figurative sense ("to overwhelm with a flood of things") grows through the 18th–19th centuries and becomes dominant in the 20th. English INUNDATES — third person singular present of inundate ("the river inundates the valley; the office is inundated with calls"). ◄ TARGET Note on debated/nuanced stages: the exact Latin formation of unda from PIE *ud- is not entirely settled — some scholars posit *ud-nā with a nasal extension, others an -n- infix or a thematised *ud-ro-; the root connection, however, is universally accepted. The PIE reconstruction *wed-/wédōr is standard (Pokorny, Mallory–Adams) but, like all PIE forms, inferred rather than attested.
