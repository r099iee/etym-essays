Pelting most commonly describes rain, hail, or sleet falling very heavily and striking surfaces forcefully and repeatedly, as if hurling countless small missiles. In Dickens's phrase pelting rain, it evokes a downpour that beats, stings, and rattles without pause or pity, too relentless to be entreated to stop. More broadly it is the present participle and adjective of the verb to pelt: to bombard, batter, or assail again and again.

At its core, to pelt means to strike repeatedly with thrown or falling objects. The objects may be literal — stones, mud, tomatoes, hailstones, raindrops — or figurative — questions, insults, emails, criticism. Pelting preserves the iterative, plural character of the action: not one blow but a shower of blows. A single stone hits; pelting is what a crowd, a storm, or an inbox does. This iterative force distinguishes it from near-synonyms like hit or strike, which can be singular, and from pour, which describes volume of liquid but not impact.

  • Pelting rain / hail / sleet (weather adjective): rain falling with unusual force and sting, beating on roofs, windows, skin. Example: pelting rain soaked them in minutes. Implies noise, slant, discomfort, reduced visibility. Stronger than heavy; heavy measures amount, pelting measures assault.
  • To pelt someone with something (transitive verb): to bombard a person or place with missiles. Example: the crowd pelted the stage with bottles. Extends to words: pelted with abuse, pelted with questions. Implies hostility or overwhelming volume from multiple sources.
  • To pelt down / pelt (intransitive, of rain): to fall violently. Example: the rain was pelting down. Chiefly British usage; American prefers pouring or pounding. Same striking force viewed from the sky rather than the target.
  • To pelt along / pelt down the road (intransitive, of people): to rush, hurry, run headlong. Example: he came pelting down the stairs. Older but still current in British narrative prose. The bridge is speed plus pounding feet, as if self-driven like hail.
  • Pelting as paltry / petty (archaic adjective): in Shakespeare pelting, pelting village, pelting officer means paltry, contemptible, trifling. Now obsolete except in scholarly notes, but explains why early dictionaries list pelting as mean. Probably a separate development from pelt as skin or trifle, not the rain sense, and should not be confused with the modern meaning.
  • Figurative bombardment: pelted with emails, applications, lawsuits, memes. Modern office and internet extension. Keeps the shower-of-missiles image while replacing stones with messages. Implies the recipient is passive under an uncontrollable influx.

Literary nuance matters. Dickens chooses pelting rather than heavy or pouring because pelting personifies rain as an assailant that cannot be reasoned with, yet still finds Scrooge colder. The word carries a faintly moral judgment: what pelts is unfeeling, mechanical, deaf to entreaty. In ordinary prose the same overtone survives: a pelting storm is not just wet but punishing; a pelting attack is not just critical but merciless. Register is vivid but not formal; it belongs to narrative, journalism, and speech rather than scientific meteorology, which would say intense precipitation or high rainfall rate.

To use it correctly, keep the plurality and the impact. Say pelting rain, pelting sleet, pelting hail for weather; say pelted with stones, rocks, eggs, insults, questions for assault; say came pelting after or off for rushing. Do not use it for a single blow, a steady gentle rain, or a flood without impact. Heavy rain wets; pelting rain stings.
