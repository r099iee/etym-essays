The biography of premonitory begins in the oldest strata of the Indo-European lexicon, in a small root that meant simply 'to think'. PIE *men- 'to have in mind, think' left descendants across the family: Sanskrit manas 'mind', Avestan manah 'mind', Greek menos 'mind, desire, courage', Latin mens 'mind', Gothic gamunds 'memory', Old English gemynd 'memory, mind' (the ancestor of English mind). The root lived on in Latin in two main dressed forms: the o-grade noun mens, mentis 'mind, intention', and a verb monere built on the same base, meaning 'to cause to think, to remind' — and from 'remind' it took one short, decisive step to 'warn, advise, admonish'. Every warning word in Latin grew from this point: monitus 'warning, admonition', admonere 'to warn, admonish' (source of English admonish), and monitor 'one who reminds or warns' (source of English monitor). The shift from 'make think' to 'make think of something in advance' was ready-made in the language.

To build the idea of warning beforehand, Latin compounded the simple verb with the preposition prae-, 'before, in front'. The result, praemonere, is attested in Classical Latin prose: Cicero uses it of foreseeing and forewarning, and the derived noun praemonitio 'a forewarning' appears in Late Latin and Ecclesiastical writing. From the participle and noun, Latin built the adjective praemonitorius 'belonging to a forewarning', and it is this adjective, carried into French as premonitoire, that crossed the Channel into English. The route is entirely bookish — a learned adjective borrowed into a learned register, not a word of ordinary street speech.

The English record begins in the 1580s, in the theatre. Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) uses premonitory of warning signs, and the word appears in the drama and devotional prose of the 1590s with a perfectly neutral colouring: a premonitory dream, a premonitory sign, a premonitory symptom. For three centuries it stayed a dignified, unremarkable adjective of formal and medical prose. It is a word with the flavour of the sermon and the treatise rather than the tavern. In medicine it found a technical home early, describing symptoms that herald the onset of an attack — premonitory shiverings, premonitory symptoms of fever — and in this clinical sense it has remained continuously in use, value-neutral and precise.

Sound and form:
  • The English word is a straight loan from French premonitoire: pre- + monit + -ory, with the stress falling on the second syllable (/pri'moniteri/ in RP), and the final -ory following the Latin pattern of -orius adjectives.
  • No sound-change obscured the loan; English kept the Latin/French skeleton prae- + mon- + -tori- + -us intact, merely respelling it premonitory.
  • The prefix pre-, from Latin prae-, keeps its transparent meaning 'before', which is what allows the word to be read at sight by any literate speaker.

Semantic drift:
  • Neutral 16th–19th century: 'serving to warn or indicate beforehand', with no colour of doom.
  • Darkening 19th–20th century: under the influence of premonition (which had long specialised toward dread and intuition of the unwelcome), premonitory attached itself to chills, signs of disaster, and intimations of approaching catastrophe, acquiring its modern ominous flavour.
  • Modern split: the ominous sense dominates everyday perception, while the neutral sense survives intact in formal and scholarly prose — exactly where Quirk's 1982 preface deploys it.

The word thus offers a compact lesson in how register protects an older sense. In colloquial use premonitory now points at approaching trouble; in the disciplined prose of the clinic and the lecture-hall it remains what it was in 1580 — an adjective for any sign that gives notice beforehand. The final stage on this card, PREMONITORY, is the same word the Elizabethans borrowed, its form essentially unchanged across four centuries, its two senses coexisting like two registers of a single instrument.
