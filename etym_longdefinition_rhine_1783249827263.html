Rhine. In this context — Macaulay's panoramic sentence that "from the Atlantic to the vicinity of the Rhine the Latin has, during many centuries, been predominant" — the Rhine is not scenery but a linguistic and civilisational frontier. The name functions as the geographical terminus of the Romance-speaking world: the river marks the easternmost line where Latin-derived tongues entrenched themselves permanently after the collapse of the Roman Empire, beyond which the Germanic speech of the Rhine's right bank prevailed. The tone is cartographic and impersonal, yet grand: a single sweeping glance over centuries and nations, in which the Rhine appears as a fixed, almost geological boundary — the invisible wall between the Roman and the non-Roman West. 'The vicinity of the Rhine' is carefully chosen: it gestures at the frontier zone rather than the riverbank, acknowledging that the language border meandered in a band around the great stream. Core senses
  • The river itself. The Rhine is one of Europe's great rivers, flowing some 1,230 kilometres from the Swiss Alps through Liechtenstein, Austria, France, Germany, and the Netherlands to the North Sea. It is the busiest waterway in Europe, a corridor of commerce from Basel to Rotterdam.
  • The civilisational frontier. In historical and linguistic writing, the Rhine is the boundary between the Romance and Germanic worlds — the line Macaulay draws between the Latin-speaking West and the Germanic East. This is the sense in the provided context.
  • The political shorthand. 'The Rhine' stands metonymically for the lands along it and the politics attached to them: the Rhineland, the Rhine frontier, the Rhine crisis of 1840, the Rhineland remilitarisation of 1936.
  • The Romantic icon. From the 19th century the Rhine became the supreme Romantic landscape — the Lorelei, the castles of the Mittelrhein, the legends celebrated in German poetry and English travel writing, Wagner's Das Rheingold.
  • As adjective and compound base. Rhenish (from Latin Rhenanus) is the learned adjective ('Rhenish wine'); Rhineland, Rhinegold, Rhine wine. In geography the name is a hydronym — a river-name — with the peculiar conservatism of such names.
Historical bridge The name Rhine is a Celtic hydronym. Roman authors report the river as Rhenus, behind which lies the Gaulish (Continental Celtic) word Rēnos, 'that which flows' — not originally a proper name but a descriptive common noun applied to running water, from the Indo-European root *h₃reyH- 'to flow, run, stream' (Greek rhéō 'to flow', Latin rīvus 'stream', Sanskrit rīyate 'flows', Old Irish rían 'sea, way', Proto-Germanic *rinnaną 'to run, flow', whence English run). The same naming pattern underlies the Rhône (< Latin Rhodanus). Germanic peoples borrowed the name as *Rīnaz, giving German Rhein, Dutch Rijn, and Old English Rīn; the modern English spelling Rhine re-Latinizes the -h- from Rhenus. Lexical neighbours
  • Danube formed the empire's northern line in the east but was far more multilingual and porous than the Rhine; in the formula 'Rhenus et Danubius' the two rivers framed the Roman frontier.
  • Loire and Garonne were internal Gaulish rivers, never frontiers — the linguistic border that matters for Macaulay runs far to their east.
  • Elbe marked a later, purely Germanic limit, not the lasting Romance frontier the Rhine became.
  • Rhenish is not a synonym but the learned, archaic adjective (from Latin Rhenanus), still heard in 'Rhenish wine'.
  • 'Vicinity of the Rhine' differs from 'banks of the Rhine': vicinity means the surrounding zone, deliberately imprecise, while banks pins the sense to the water's edge.
