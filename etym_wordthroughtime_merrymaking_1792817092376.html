- Old English — myrge, pleasant and agreeable: a gentle aesthetic word for delightful sounds, sights, and days, with no festivity yet attached.
- Middle English — mirie and to make mirie: cheerfulness becomes an activity, Christmas carols and ballads command listeners to be merry, and tipsiness joins the family.
- 16th–17th c. — golden age of the idiom: Shakespeare's clowns make merry, Merry England is christened as a nostalgic ideal, and merrymaking fuses into festive business.
- 18th–19th c. — respectable and rustic: merrymaking retreats to villages, harvest homes, and Christmas, while polite society prefers entertainment and amusement.
- 20th c. — literary fossil with journalistic second life: fantasy novels keep it for taverns while newspapers revive it for seasonal street crowds.
- Today — a nostalgia word: chosen to make celebrations feel hearty and timeless, slightly arch anywhere modern neutrality is wanted, warm exactly where tradition is wanted.
