- Late 14th c. — legal status and physical loss: enters Middle English from Old French and Latin, denoting a formal subtraction, material reduction in goods, or loss of royal honor.
- 15th to 16th c. — musical counterpoint and heraldry: acquires precise technical senses in Renaissance music for halving note values, and in heraldry for debasing or modifying a coat of arms.
- 17th c. — constitutional balance and philosophical physics: applied to political treatises concerning the curtailment of monarchical prerogative, and natural philosophy regarding the diminution of light, heat, or gravity over distance.
- 18th to 19th c. — rhetorical elegance and high criticism: becomes a staple of Augustan and Victorian prose (such as Ruskin, Burke, and Gibbon) for subtle, dignified losses of influence, reputation, or artistic majesty.
- 20th to 21st c. — jurisprudence and clinical pathology: primarily preserved in legal doctrine (diminution of value, diminution of responsibility) and medicine (diminution of cognitive or sensory acuity), alongside its enduring musical meaning.
