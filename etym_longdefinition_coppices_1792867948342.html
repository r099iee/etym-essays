Coppices, the plural of coppice, names in its primary modern sense small woods or dense thickets of trees and undergrowth, especially ones managed by the ancient woodland craft of cutting stems down to the stump so that a flush of new shoots springs up in their place.

In the source sentence the travellers walk in single file along hedgerows and the borders of coppices as night falls: the word paints exactly the fringing, half-wild woodland edge of the English countryside — low dense coverts of hazel, ash, and thorn where a party might find shelter or concealment, and where game, owls, and all the small rustling life of the night abide.

Core meaning in detail: A coppice in the strict forestry sense is not simply any small wood but a wood worked on a rotation: trees such as hazel, ash, chestnut, and willow are felled near ground level every seven to twenty-five years, and the stool — the living stump — sends up straight poles prized for fencing, thatching spars, charcoal, and firewood. The plural coppices therefore conjures both the physical thicket and the managed landscape: compartmented woods, rides between the panels, the sharp scent of cut hazel, and the sudden light let in where the cutters have been.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A small managed wood or thicket (primary noun sense, from c. 1400): a dense growth of small trees and underwood, whether actively coppiced or simply thick and low. The bridge from the verb is direct: the place is named for the cutting practised there.
  • The forestry system itself (abstract noun, coppice uncountable): as in to grow hazel in coppice or coppice-with-standards, where scattered mature oaks rise above the cut underwood. The bridge is metonymy: the product names the practice.
  • To cut back to the stump (verb, to coppice): woodmen coppice ash and hazel on rotation. The bridge runs backward from place to action: to make a coppice of a tree is to subject it to the coppice regime.
  • Young regrowth shoots (concrete plural use): the springy poles themselves, as when a countryman speaks of cutting the coppices for pea-sticks. The bridge is product-for-place: the shoots stand for the wood that bore them.
  • Literary shorthand for concealing woodland edge (figurative, Tolkien's use): borders of coppices as liminal cover — neither open field nor deep forest. The bridge is atmospheric: low dense growth at dusk suggests both shelter and the unseen, the perfect verge for travellers trying not to be seen.
  • Ecological habitat sense (modern conservation): coppices as biodiversity reservoirs — dormice, nightingales, fritillary butterflies — dependent on the light-and-shade cycle of cutting. The bridge is scientific redescription: foresters discovered that the ancient economy of the pole-wood was also an ecology.

Register and connotation: coppices is a soft rural word, loamy and English, smelling of leaf-mould and woodsmoke. It belongs to hedgerow England — to gamekeepers, charcoal-burners, and poets — and carries warmth rather than menace, though at nightfall, as in the source sentence, its edges darken with all the old suggestiveness of cover: what the thicket hides, it keeps. Gardeners use it technically, walkers sentimentally, and historians economically, for the coppice was for centuries the working poor's timber-yard.
