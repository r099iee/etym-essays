- [Old English: turf] - The word is native Germanic, naming the surface layer of earth bound together by grass roots — the living sod underfoot — and also a block of it cut free.
- [Early medieval: turf as building and fuel] - In treeless, boggy regions turf cut from moorland becomes a structural and heating material: turf walls, turf roofs, and dried peat-turf burned as fuel; the word spans the ground and what is dug from it.
- [19th century: 'the turf'] - 'The turf' becomes a stock metonym for horse-racing — the grassy course and the sport itself — giving the word a fashionable, sporting social register.
- [20th century: territory] - US slang extends 'turf' to one's own ground or sphere of control — gang territory, 'turf wars,' 'home turf'; British English also coins the verb 'to turf out' (to eject, from flinging someone out onto the grass).
- [Contemporary sense] - Pastoral and precise: the close-grazed, root-knitted living skin of a hillside, as in Tolkien's 'rabbit-cropped turf'; by extension, one's own domain or sphere of influence.
