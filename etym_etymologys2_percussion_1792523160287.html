Note on Latin phonological apophony and semantic development: Classical Latin phonologists (including Michiel de Vaan and Ernout-Meillet) point out that when the simplex verb quatere ("to shake, strike") was combined with prefixes (such as per-, con-, dis-), the stem vowel short -a- underwent regular Latin vowel weakening (apophony) into short -u-, producing percutere, concutere (ancestor of English concussion), and discutere (ancestor of English discussion). From the past participle stem percuss- (formed by regular assimilation of the dental cluster *-t-to *-ss-), Latin formed the abstract noun percussio. Notably, while the primary colloquial meaning was a violent physical strike or execution, classical rhetoricians (such as Quintilian in his Institutio Oratoria) specifically adopted percussio to designate the rhythmic tapping of the foot or finger that marked time and measured poetic meters—the exact semantic bridge that eventually gave rise to the musical classification of percussion instruments.
