Stings (/stɪŋz/), the plural noun form of sting, denotes sharp, pointed anatomical weapons, organs, or microstructures found in diverse animals and plants designed to puncture tissue and introduce venom, irritants, or acid; the acute, lancinating, or burning wounds and sensations produced by such punctures; or, by metaphorical transference, sharp edged weapons (such as swords and daggers), biting insults, pungent physical tastes, undercover police traps, or sharp psychological pangs.

In exhaustive lexicographical analysis, stings encompasses several biological, martial, sensory, psychological, and modern colloquial applications:

  • Biological and Entomological Envenomation Apparatuses: Specialized anatomical structures evolved across multiple phyla for predation and defense:
    • Hymenoptera (Bees, Wasps, Hornets, Ants): Modified ovipositors (the aculeus) located at the posterior abdomen, coupled with venom glands that inject melittin, apamin, phospholipase, or formic acid.
    • Scorpionida and Marine Biology: The sharp caudal telson (barbed stinger) of scorpions, the serrated caudal spines of stingrays (which introduce venomous proteinaceous mucus), and the microscopic cnidocytes/nematocysts of jellyfish (such as the Portuguese man-o'-war and box jellyfish).
    • Botanical Urtication: The hollow, silica-tipped trichomes (stinging hairs) of plants like the stinging nettle (Urtica dioica), which snap upon touch to inject histamine, acetylcholine, and serotonin.
  • Martial Weaponry and Literary Kenning: In heroic literature and fantasy, swords, daggers, rapiers, and bayonets carried at a warrior's hip are poetically characterized as artificial, deadly stings. Most famously, in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, Bilbo Baggins slays a giant spider in Mirkwood with his glowing Elven dagger, leading the panicked arachnids—unaccustomed to creatures carrying such sharp "stings at their sides"—to flee in terror, inspiring Bilbo to christen the blade Sting.
  • Somatic and Sensory Burning Sensations: Acute, lancinating, localized pain characterized by burning heat and lingering irritation:
    • Chemical contact: Antiseptic alcohol or iodine poured onto an open abrasion ("the sting of iodine").
    • Meteorological bite: Subzero blizzard winds and driving sleet lashing against exposed skin ("the cold wind's sharp stings").
  • Emotional, Moral, and Psychological Pangs: Intense, sudden emotional wounds caused by humiliation, rejection, or conscience:
    • Remorse and Conscience: "The stings of conscience" (the acute psychological torment of guilt).
    • Rejection and Defeat: "The bitter stings of unrequited love and public humiliation."
    • Sarcasm and Slander: Biting, acerbic remarks calculated to inflict maximum emotional distress.
  • Undercover Deceptions and Law Enforcement (Sting Operations): In modern criminal justice and investigative journalism, an intricate, covert deceptive operation designed to catch criminals, fraudsters, or corrupt officials in the act of committing an illegal transaction.
  • Scriptural and Literary Collocations:
    • "O death, where is thy sting?" (1 Corinthians 15:55): An iconic biblical exclamation triumphing over mortality.
    • "A sting in the tail": An unexpected, sharp, unpleasant surprise or twist at the very end of a story, speech, or transaction.
