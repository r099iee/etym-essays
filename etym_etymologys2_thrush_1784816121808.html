Linear derivation chain for THRUSH (bird sense): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *trozdo- (also *trosdo-, Pokorny; *trozdo- in de Vaan), reconstructed, c. 4000+ BCE: "thrush," an ancient bird-name, probably sound-symbolic (imitative of the bird's rattling, trilling song). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *thruskjon (also *thruskjan), c. 500 BCE–100 CE: "thrush." Stage 3 — Old English thrysce / thræsce, attested c. 8th–11th centuries: "thrush," with the parallel Old English throstle "song thrush." Stage 4 — Middle English thrusshe, attested 13th–14th centuries: "thrush." Stage 5 — Modern English thrush, 15th century onward: noun, the songbird of the family Turdidae. Parallel chain for the poetic doublet throstle: Old English throstle (attested 8th–11th c.) to Middle English throstel to Modern English throstle (poetic name for the same bird). Note on the medical homograph: the thrush of disease (candidiasis) is a DIFFERENT word, borrowed from Scandinavian (Old Norse / Danish troske, cf. Swedish torsk), and does not descend from *trozdo-. Debated / nuanced note: The exact PIE reconstruction varies among scholars (*trozdo-, *trosdo-, *trozdo-); the Greek strouthos (στρουθός) "sparrow" has been explained by some as a genericized development of the same bird-name (songbird to sparrow) and by others as a separate form — the connection is debated. Welsh drudwy "starling" shows a semantic shift within Celtic from thrush to starling.
