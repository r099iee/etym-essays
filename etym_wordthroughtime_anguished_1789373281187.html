- [Late 12th – 14th c.] - Middle English angwisshen enters from Old French angoissier (from Latin angustia 'narrowness, tightness') to denote bodily constriction, acute choking agony, or mortal pain - [connotation: physical suffocation, agonizing bodily torment, martyrdom]
- [15th – 16th c.] - Established in Early Modern English and biblical translation to describe profound spiritual despair, grief of the soul, and existential dread (e.g. Christ's agony in Gethsemane) - [connotation: deep spiritual ordeal, tragic lamentation, holy suffering]
- [17th – 18th c.] - Widely adopted in Jacobean and neoclassical drama for intense romantic despair, thwarted passion, and brokenhearted grief - [connotation: tragic pathos, romantic devastation, weeping lament]
- [19th c. – Victorian Era] - Becomes a hallmark of psychological realism (Dickens, Brontë, Eliot, Dostoevsky, Proust) to convey hidden moral conflict, closeted shame, and repressed psychological agony - [connotation: internalised guilt, brooding psychological depth, acute emotional crisis]
- [20th c. – Contemporary] - Enters modern philosophical and existentialist discourse (existential anguish / angoisse / angst) while remaining the universal descriptor for heart-wrenching emotional distress and tragic cries - [connotation: visceral empathy, existential dread, profound human grief]
