- 13th–14th Century: Chivalric Exploits & Heroic Chronicles - Derived from Anglo-Norman and Old French *geste* (from Latin *gesta*, 'deeds, heroic exploits'), initially denoting the recitation of heroic romances, chivalric poems, and martial chronicles (such as the *chansons de geste* celebrated across medieval Europe).
- 15th Century: Minstrel Evolution from Epic Deeds to Comic Entertainment - As wandering minstrels gradually shifted their repertoire from grand martial romances to humorous fables, bawdy anecdotes, and amusing dinner tales, *gest* transitioned semantically from a heroic deed into an entertaining story or merry joke.
- 16th Century: The Court Fool & Verbal Flippancy - By the Elizabethan era, *jest* had shed all heroic martial connotations to denote a witty quip, practical joke, or piece of buffoonery; *jesting* emerged as the participial adjective and verbal noun characterizing both the professional humor of the court jester and the habit of speaking non-seriously or scoffingly (immortalized by Francis Bacon in 1625: 'said jesting Pilate').
- 17th–18th Century: Enlightenment Sarcasm & Polite Sociability - Refined in neoclassical literature and satire (Alexander Pope, Jonathan Swift, Samuel Johnson) to differentiate sparkling, urbane wit from boorish buffoonery, frequently accompanied by moral warnings against 'jesting' with sacred theological truths.
- 19th Century: Archaizing Literary Color & Melancholy Wit - While largely replaced in common vernacular speech by *joking* and *kidding*, *jesting* was preserved in Victorian literature and historical novels (Walter Scott, William Makepeace Thackeray) as an evocative, slightly antiquarian term evoking Shakespearean fools, bittersweet irony, and royal courts.
- 20th–21st Century: Rhetorical Contrast & Cultured Allusion - Primarily deployed in elevated journalism, literary criticism, and philosophical discourse to designate cynical flippancy, ironic skepticism, or gallows humor in the face of grave existential or ethical dilemmas.
