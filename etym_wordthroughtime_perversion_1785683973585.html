- [14th century] - Borrowed via Old French from Latin perversio 'a turning upside down, overthrow'; first senses are theological — the turning of faith, doctrine, or a soul away from truth, a heresy or corruption of right belief.
- [15th–16th century] - Widens to any turning from the true, normal, or proper course: the perversion of justice, of laws, of a text's meaning — still a dignified moral reproach, 'to turn something against its own nature'.
- [18th–19th century] - Becomes the stock word for corruption and misapplication of sound things — 'perversion of justice', 'perversion of truth' in legal and moral rhetoric; strong but not sensational.
- [Late 19th–20th century] - Acquires the specific clinical and psychological sense (sexual perversion), which floods the word with taboo and salacious weight; the sexual meaning so dominates that it now colours every other use.
- [Modern register] - Heavily loaded and indignant: in 'a gross perversion of the ancient laws' the minister accuses the edict of being twisted, not merely neglected — but a modern reader unavoidably hears the sexual echo, making the word far more charged than its mild ancestors.
