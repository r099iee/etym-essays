STUPENDOUS descends from a single Latin chain: - PIE *steu- "to push, strike, knock, thump" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; recorded by Pokorny; cognates include the Germanic *staut- "to push" seen in German stoßen and Old English stēotan, and possibly English stub and stump) → Latin stupere "to be struck senseless, to be stunned, to stand motionless in amazement" (attested in Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE; the mental image is of a person knocked motionless) → Latin stupidus "stunned, dazed; (by extension) dull, foolish" [stupere + adjectival -idus] (attested 1st c. BCE; the source of English stupid) → Latin stupendus "to be wondered at, astonishing" [gerundive of stupere, literally "such as must be marvelled at"] (attested in imperial and late Latin) → English stupendous "of such magnitude, degree, or force as to astound the mind" (first recorded in the 1650s, taken directly from Latin) → Modern English STUPENDOUS. Sister branches of the same Latin family (not stages of stupendous): Latin stupor "numbness, astonishment" → English stupor (1690s); Latin stupefaciō, stupefacere "to stun, amaze" [stupe- + facere "to make"] → English stupefy (1590s) and stupefaction; in Romance, Italian stupendo and Spanish estupendo preserved the gerundive directly with the meaning "marvellous, wonderful." Note on debate: the Indo-European pedigree of stupere is not certain. The traditional account (Pokorny) connects it with PIE *steu- "to push, strike, knock"; modern handbooks (e.g. de Vaan) record stupere as of uncertain origin and allow that it may be an expressive or imitative formation without a secure Indo-European ancestor. No stage between any PIE root and Latin stupere is directly attested; the root is reconstructed on the strength of the wider "strike/stun" family.
