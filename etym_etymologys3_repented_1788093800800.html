1. Latin foundations

The line begins in Latin. The verb paenitēre (attested from the second century BCE) means “to cause regret; it is a grief to”, and its impersonal form paenitet stands at the centre of the classical idiom: paenitet mē “I repent” is literally “it repents me” — the sorrow is felt as something that happens to the speaker. The etymology of paenitēre is debated. Many scholars connect it with the adverb paene “almost, nearly”, on the speculative notion of “not quite (what one might wish)” — regret as the sense that something falls short — but paene itself has no safe Indo-European etymon. A second current associates the verb, at least secondarily, with poena “punishment, penalty, satisfaction” (Greek poinē, from PIE *kʷey- “to pay, atone”). This second association is historically real, because it shaped the Christian vocabulary: paenitentia “repentance, penance” is what an offender “does” (the Vulgate’s paenitentiam agere), and early Christian Latin pulled the whole word-group toward the sacrament of penance and its satisfactions. The safe summary: the verb is confined to Latin and its descendants, with no securely reconstructable PIE root; the attested history begins with the impersonal verb of regret in the second century BCE.

2. Romance reshaping

In Late Latin the sequence ae weakened to e, giving the Vulgar Latin *penitīre “to regret, be sorry”. Old French built on this the reflexive verb (se) repentir (attested by the eleventh century), formed with re- “again, in reverse” plus pentir “to feel regret” — literally “to feel regret back upon oneself”, i.e. to reconsider and be sorry. The Old French semantic core was broad: to feel sorrow, to change one’s mind, to turn back. From the same family come Old French repentance and penitence, the two caught in the medieval tension between inner sorrow and outward satisfaction.

3. English borrowing and the penitential age

English took the verb from Old French and Anglo-French in the early fourteenth century: Middle English repenten (attested c. 1300, e.g. in the biblical paraphrase Cursor Mundi), with the noun repentaunce soon after. The fourteenth century was the great age of the penitential system: the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) had made annual confession and penance the universal duty of the laity; the English vernacular sermons, Chaucer’s Parson’s Tale and Langland’s Piers Plowman all circle around contrition, confession and satisfaction. In this world “repent” is a demanding technical word: to repent is to be contrite in heart, to confess in mouth, and to amend in deed.

4. The Reformation controversy

At the Reformation the verb exploded into controversy. Erasmus and the humanists pointed out that the Greek of the New Testament is metanoia — literally “change of mind” — and that the Vulgate’s paenitentiam agite (“do penance”) had turned an inward conversion into a sacrament. Tyndale’s English New Testament (1526) deliberately chose “repent” to render metanoia, and the word became a banner of Protestant theology: repentance is the interior turning of the whole person toward God, not the payment of satisfactions. The King James Bible (1611) kept both the personal and the impersonal constructions — “the LORD repented of the evil” (Exodus 32:14), “it repented the LORD that he had made man” (Genesis 6:6) — preserving the older grammar at the very moment the language was discarding it.

5. Grammatical modernisation and secular broadening

Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries the impersonal “it repents me” died out of ordinary speech, replaced by the personal “I repent”. The verb also broadened: from the sixteenth century it freely applies to any regretted act, bargain or word, not only sin — “marry in haste, and repent at leisure” is recorded by the seventeenth century. By the nineteenth century the everyday sense “to regret” dominated; the religious sense retreated to sermons, prayer and formal prose while keeping its moral charge.

6. Modern position

  • Frequency has declined steeply relative to regret; repented now reads as formal, literary or religious.
  • In literature it carries real weight: in The Hobbit (1937), the line “the words of Thorin, of which he repented” refers to the dying dwarf-king’s retracted harshness — a usage that shows the word at full moral strength in modern prose.
  • The old impersonal structure survives only as a fossil in biblical diction and its pastiche; “he repented him of…” is now consciously archaic.
  • In secular psychology and self-help the underlying concept (metanoia as “turning”) has been revived as a word for transformative change, though “repented” itself keeps its rueful-to-remorseful range.
