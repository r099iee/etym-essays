- [c. 1300s–1400s] - Middle English 'lace' means a cord, thong, or snare: a purely functional binding for clothing, armor, and traps - [perception: utilitarian, humble, everyday]
- [15th–16th c.] - 'Lace' becomes braided or embroidered trimming on dress; gold and silver 'laces' mark rank and wealth on noble gowns, liveries, and military uniforms - [perception: ornamental, prestigious, regulated by sumptuary taste]
- [16th–17th c.] - Openwork needle and bobbin lace fabric triumphantly arrives: lace collars, cuffs, and ruffs advertise the wearer's riches and taste; a European lace industry rivals silk in value - [perception: luxury, artistry, status fetish]
- [18th–19th c.] - Mechanization (lace machines after 1809) makes lace cheap; with mass-made footwear, 'laces' (the plural, 'shoelaces') becomes the ordinary domestic meaning in daily speech - [perception: mundane, functional again]
- [19th–20th c.] - The figurative idiom hardens: 'laces and frills' = the ornamental extras, the refined embellishments of civilization, set against its necessities - [perception: elegant but perishable, the first luxuries lost in hard times]
- [20th–21st c.] - Verb spin-offs multiply: 'laced up' (boots), 'laced with' (a drink spiked with spirit or a text threaded with meaning), 'lace-curtain' (social striving) - [perception: either delicate craft or covert addition, depending on the sense]
