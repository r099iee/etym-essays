The history of grubbed, the past tense and past participle of grub, is the history of digging — literal, economic, and moral — from prehistoric soil-work to modern slang for food and drudgery. Its root lies in Proto-Indo-European *ghrebh- meaning to dig, bury, or scratch, a root that also feeds words like grave, groove, graft, and German graben to dig. The semantic core was never elegant: it meant scratching at earth with hands, tools, or snout.

In Proto-Germanic this became a family of grub/grab/groove verbs built on the gr-b sound cluster that imitates scraping and clutching. Old Norse had grubbla and Middle Dutch grobbelen, all suggesting fumbling, grubbing, rooting. By c.1300 Middle English attests grubben meaning to dig up roots, stumps, or weeds. Early citations concern clearing land: to grub up roots, to grub hedges. Wycliffe and later agricultural treatises use it for stump-clearing, hard low-status labour.

From land-clearing the sense naturally strayed in three directions, all attested by the 16th-17th centuries:

  • Physical digging and rummaging: to grub in the earth, to grub about a hut or tent for edibles, to grub worms for bait. This is the sense in your context — a woman grubbing about for food while man hunted.
  • Mean labour and drudgery: a grubber became one who does low digging work, then by extension any drudge or money-grubber. Shakespearean and later moralists contrast grubbing for money with noble work.
  • Larva and food: the grub-worm, the larva that burrows in soil, takes its name from the verb because it digs/bores. By the 17th century sailors' slang turns grub into food itself — what you grub up, what sustains you. Hence modern grub = meal, grubhub, grubby hands.

Phonetically the development is conservative. Initial gr- remained stable from PIE through Germanic. The vowel shifted between u and o in different dialects (grub/grob/grab), but standard English fixed grub with short /ʌ/. The past tense regularized as grubbed with doubled b and -ed, never developing a strong ablaut like dig/dug. The doubling preserves the short vowel in spelling.

Socio-cultural attestation shows a clear class register. Grubbing was never knightly or clerical work. In medieval manorial accounts, grubbing service was villein labour. In the King James Bible era, to grub is associated with poverty, foraging, and penitential toil. By the Industrial Revolution, grubber also named agricultural implements (grub-hoe, grubber-plough) for breaking fallow. American frontier usage kept the foraging sense alive: pioneers grubbed roots, miners grubstaked each other (supplied with grub/money for a share).

Literary attestations cement the humble, slightly pejorative colour. Dickens uses grubby and grubbing for money-obsessed counting-houses. Jack London uses grub for trail food. Tolkien uses grubbing orcs. In modern usage grubbed appears mostly in two frames: grubbed about/around/through meaning rummaged laboriously, and grubbed up/out meaning unearthed with effort. Both retain the image of bent back, hands in dirt.

Note on debated links: some older dictionaries proposed an Old French or Scandinavian borrowing for grubben, but the consensus now treats it as native Germanic from *ghrebh-, with Scandinavian cognates as parallel inheritances rather than sources. The connection to grave and groove via the same PIE root is secure; the connection to grumble is not and should be rejected.
