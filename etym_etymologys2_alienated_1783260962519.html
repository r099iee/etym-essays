Stage 1 — PIE *h₂el- “beyond, other, foreign” (Pokorny *al-; the “otherness” root of the family; no reliably reconstructed Indic reflex — a Sanskrit cognate should not be forced — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE). Stage 2 — PIE stem *h₂élyos “other” → Latin alius, alia, aliud “other, another” (the everyday Latin word; the Germanic reflex of the same stem is Old English elles, modern English else). Stage 3 — Latin alienus “of another, belonging to another, foreign, strange” (alius + -ēnus; attested in classical Latin). Stage 4 — Latin alienāre “to make another's, to transfer ownership of, to estrange, to make foreign” (alienus + -āre; a technical verb of Roman property law; attested in classical Latin). Stage 5 — Latin alienātus, past participle, “made foreign, estranged, transferred” (the participle of alienāre). Stage 6 — Old French aliéner (from Latin alienāre) → Middle English alienaten (a learned loanword of the 15th c.; the noun alienation, from Latin aliēnātiō, was borrowed earlier, in the 14th c.). Stage 7 — Modern English alienate, with the past tense and past participle ALIENATED (Macaulay's “errors ... alienated a loyal gentry”). NOTE: the twin adjective Latin alienus also gave, through Old French, English alien “foreign, a foreigner” (extended to “extraterrestrial” only in 1953 science fiction) — a separate loan from the same Latin stage.
