WHOOPED is the past tense and past participle of whoop, meaning to utter loud, wild, high-pitched shouts or cries of excitement, triumph, or derision — a short, sharp, rising cry that cuts through noise and carries across distance. In Tolkien's sentence the goblin-drivers whooped as they drove the captured dwarves along with cracking whips: the word catches the high, jagged, bestial cries that burst from them in savage high spirits, fusing the hunting cry with the bully's taunt. Where the crowd behind laughed and stamped, the drivers shout — and the sound itself is the point, for whoop is the most purely sound-symbolic word in the group. Its very spelling still echoes the noise.

Etymologically, whoop has no Indo-European ancestry; it is an imitative (echoic) formation, descended from the medieval French hunter's halloo, and its meaning has never drifted far from its sound. From the hunting field it spread into war-cries, celebration, medicine (whooping cough), and even a bird's name (whooping crane).

Core senses in detail:

  • Primary — to shout (verb): To utter a loud, high-pitched, excited cry, especially of triumph, derision, or exultation; to shout loudly. "The hunters whooped in triumph upon sighting their quarry."
  • Primary — the cry (noun, whoop): The cry itself — a short, sharp, rising shout; also the convulsive crowing gasp of whooping cough.
  • Extended — war-cry (war-whoop): The fierce, exultant battle cry of warriors, especially in American frontier usage (1761).
  • Extended — the disease (whooping cough): Pertussis, named 1739 for the patient's characteristic crowing "whoop" between coughing fits.
  • Extended — the bird (whooping crane): The great white North American crane, named 1791 for its far-carrying call.
  • Extended — celebration (whoop it up): To make a disturbance, to celebrate boisterously (1881); whoopee (1845) for unrestrained revelry, whence whoopee cushion (1931).
  • Extended — the past participle (WHOOPED): The goblin-drivers whooped — they shouted in wild, exultant, derisive high spirits as they drove the captives along with cracking whips.

Semantic bridge and rationale:

  • Sound is the sense: The word imitates the cry it names; every extension (war-whoop, whooping cough, whooping crane, whoopee) simply attaches the distinctive sound to a new context in which that sound is heard.
  • From hunt to war to celebration: The medieval hunter's halloo became the war-cry, then the cry of triumph and festivity; the contexts changed, the sound did not.
  • Medical and zoological naming: Whooping cough and whooping crane are named by ear, from the symptom and the call — proof of how vividly the word preserves its echoic identity.
  • Register: The word is vivid, energetic, and informal; it is more festive and shaped than plain shouting, and carries a half-war-cry excitement.

Modern usage contexts:

  • Games and sport: Fans whoop with joy; the word is the natural exclamation of sudden triumph.
  • Hunting and the frontier: The war-whoop and hunting whoop survive in historical and western writing as the cry of attack or the kill.
  • Medicine: Whooping cough (pertussis) is the everyday name of the disease; whooping is its defining sound.
  • Ornithology: The whooping crane is among the most famous endangered birds, named for its call.
  • Colloquial celebration: "Whoop it up," "make whoopee," "whoopee cushion" — all descend from the same cry of joyful noise.

In short, a whoop is rhythmic, exultant, and almost festive — and in a cruel context it reveals delight in the chase itself, exactly the savage high spirits of the goblin-drivers as their whips crack behind the running captives.
