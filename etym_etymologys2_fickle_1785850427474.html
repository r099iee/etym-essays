Derivation chain for fickle: 1. Proto-Indo-European *peig- 'evil-minded, hostile, treacherous' (traditional reconstruction, Pokorny; posited as the ultimate base of the Germanic word for deceitfulness. The reconstruction is plausible but NOT universally accepted — many handbooks treat the Germanic base *fik- as having no secure PIE ancestor and record the deeper origin as unknown or debated). 2. Proto-Germanic *fik- 'to deceive, to be cunning' — base of the verb *fikaną 'to deceive, to move deceitfully' and of the adjective *fikala- 'deceitful, cunning' (both reconstructed from the Old English and Low German evidence, not directly attested). 3. Old English ficol (adjective) 'deceitful, treacherous, cunning, crafty' (attested from c. 1000, e.g. in Ælfric's homilies; the companion verb fican 'to deceive, to cheat' is also attested; the -ol suffix is the Germanic quality/instrument suffix). Note: a homograph ficol 'fickle, unsteady' may already carry the nuance of quick restless motion, and some scholars connect the base *fik- with the idea of rapid flicking movement (compare the English verb flick and the flicker of a flame); this motion-based etymology is one of the debated alternatives to the 'deceit' etymology. 4. Middle English fikel (adjective) 'false, treacherous, unstable, inconstant' (12th–15th c.; the semantic shift from 'actively deceitful' to 'unreliable, inconstant' is complete by the 13th c.). 5. Modern English fickle 'changeable, inconstant, unreliable, especially in affection or loyalty' (sense established in the 16th c.; spelling with -ck- standard from the 17th c.) — the exact target word FICKLE. Note on debated points: the PIE root *peig- is traditional but not securely demonstrated, and the deeper ancestry of the Germanic family may simply be unrecoverable; an alternative school connects *fik- to the notion of rapid flicking motion, which would make 'changeable, unsteady' the original meaning rather than a softened 'treacherous'; and the Old High German verb ficken 'to rub, to move back and forth' is sometimes adduced as a cognate, but its connection is uncertain, and its modern German vulgar sense is a separate later development. Each of these points is genuinely debated rather than established.
