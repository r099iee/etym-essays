PLEAD: To make an earnest, urgent, and often humble appeal, or to argue formally in support of a cause, especially before a court or other authority. In the card's sentence — "He would __________ with the council to spare her life, laying his whole reputation on the strength of his entreaty" — the verb is charged with desperation and sacrifice: the man will "plead with the council to spare her life," risking his entire reputation on the strength of his entreaty — so that to plead here is to stake everything on persuasion. The tone is intense and supplicatory, at once legal and emotional; it blends the formality of the courtroom with the beseeching of a loved one, and it makes the council itself, not a bench of judges but an assembly of men who must be swayed, the tribunal before which the appeal is made. Core features of the concept.
  • Earnestness and urgency: to plead is to ask with feeling and persistence, never casually; the word implies that much is at stake.
  • The double register — legal and emotional: plead straddles the courtroom (to plead a case, to plead guilty) and the heart (to plead with someone for mercy); it is the only word of the request-family that does both.
  • Structured appeal: unlike "beg," pleading is a reasoned, structured entreaty — it presents a case, marshals arguments, addresses an authority that must be convinced.
  • Register: formal in its legal sense, intense and dramatic in its emotional sense; the word belongs to law, rhetoric, and solemn narrative.
Polysemy and related senses. The verb divides into two well-demarcated registers, each with its own senses:
  • Sense 1 — forensic (legal): to argue a case before a court, to present a claim or defence in law; "to plead a case," "to plead before a bench," "to plead the statute," "to plead a defence," and, above all, the procedural formula "to plead guilty" / "to plead not guilty" (to answer a charge). This is the original English sense, borrowed from Old French legal vocabulary in the thirteenth century.
  • Sense 2 — supplicatory (emotional): to make an earnest appeal for a favour, mercy, or another's cause; "to plead with someone," "to plead for mercy," "to plead for clemency," "to plead for forgiveness," "to plead for another's life." This is the sense of the card's sentence.
  • Sense 3 — to put forward as an excuse (legal-derived): "to plead ignorance," "to plead poverty," "to plead illness" — to offer a reason or excuse, formally stated, in defence of one's action; the sense is the legal one extended to everyday self-justification.
  • Sense 4 — to argue in support of a cause (rhetorical, general): "to plead the cause of the poor," "to plead the refugees' case" — to advocate on behalf of a person or cause before any authority; the neutral bridge between the courtroom and the heart.
The noun "plea" (a request or a formal statement in court), the noun "pleadings" (the formal documents filed in a lawsuit), and the adverb "pleadingly" all belong to the same word-family. The verb's power is its union of the two registers: the same word that argues before a judge begs before a human being. Set against its near-synonyms.
  • Beg is urgent but often abject, the posture of a suppliant with nothing to bargain with; to beg is to ask from need, to plead is to present a case.
  • Entreat and implore stress passionate request but lack plead's legal frame — one implores a person, but one pleads a case.
  • Supplicate is lofty and religious, the language of prayer and of the altar.
  • Petition is dry, written, and administrative, submitting a document rather than pleading in person.
  • Appeal is neutral and procedural, pointing upward to a higher court or authority, without the emotional intensity of pleading.
  • Beseech is archaic and intense, close to plead's emotional register but without the courtroom structure.
Only "plead" carries the double force of heartfelt entreaty and courtroom argument at once, which is exactly why it fits a scene in which a council must be swayed by a mixture of reason and feeling. Collocations and register. The emotional register gives "plead with someone," "plead for mercy," "plead for clemency," "plead for forgiveness"; the forensic register gives "plead guilty," "plead not guilty," "plead a case before a bench," "plead the statute," "plead ignorance," and the noun formations "plea," "pleadings." The verb's register is formal in law and intense in narrative; it collocates with nouns of authority ("council," "bench," "court," "jury," "judge") and with nouns of desperate hope ("mercy," "clemency," "forgiveness," "pardon," "life"). In modern English it remains a living verb in both registers — every criminal court uses "plead guilty," and every desperate lover "pleads for mercy" — and its double life is the reason it is more dignified than begging and more urgent than appealing.
