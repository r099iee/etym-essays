The English noun revelation, whose plural revelations is the card form, enters the language as a religious term and only gradually becomes an everyday word for any startling disclosure, and that journey from altar to newsroom structures its whole history. Its Latin ancestor revēlātiō is itself a Christian-Latin coinage built on the classical verb revēlāre, to draw back a veil, and it first denotes God uncovering hidden things to human eyes: prophecy, apocalypse, the manifest will of heaven. When Middle English writers borrow it as revelacioun around 1300, it still carries this numinous weight, naming visions granted to saints and above all the biblical Book of Revelation, the Apocalypse of John.

Three large developments then secularise and pluralise it. First, the Reformation and early modern Bible culture put the word in every parish church and household, so that by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries revelation names not only John’s visions but any doctrine held to be divinely revealed, set against reason and natural religion in the great revealed-versus-natural theology debates. Second, Enlightenment and Romantic writers extend the veil metaphor to the mind itself: sudden insight, the lifting of ignorance, the moment when nature or conscience discloses itself. Third, nineteenth- and twentieth-century journalism, biography, and science writing convert it into a workhorse of public prose, where revelations — now typically plural — means documents brought to light, memoirs disclosing secrets, or experimental findings that overturn assumptions.

  • Sacred origin: Late Latin revēlātiō among Christian authors (Tertullian, Jerome’s Vulgate) denotes God’s unveiling of truth; the veil image echoes biblical language of parted curtains and opened heavens.
  • Medieval apocalypse: in Middle English the word’s capitalised use, Revelation(s), fixes itself to the last book of the New Testament; Wycliffite and later translations cement the association between the noun and prophetic vision.
  • Reformation theology: sixteenth-century divines oppose revealed religion to natural religion; revelation becomes a technical term for truths unknowable by reason alone, such as the Trinity or grace.
  • Phonetic stability: the form changes little from Latin through Old French to English; the stress settles on the penultimate syllable and the plural simply adds /z/, preserving the Latinate grandeur that keeps the word weighty even in secular use.
  • Romantic inward turn: poets and essayists from Wordsworth to Emerson use revelation for moments of imaginative or moral illumination, shifting the veil from heaven’s curtain to the mind’s own covering.
  • Modern pluralisation: twentieth-century usage strongly prefers the plural — revelations about a scandal, a diary’s revelations, scientific revelations — because the word now catalogues successive disclosures rather than one absolute unveiling; corpus data shows this plural, journalistic sense dominating contemporary prose.
  • Literary attestation: from Julian of Norwich’s Revelations of Divine Love (c. 1395, the first book in English certainly authored by a woman) through Blake’s prophetic books to modern headlines promising ‘startling revelations’, the noun has never lost its thrill of the hidden made manifest, which is exactly the force it carries in the source sentence about verbal style yielding extraordinary disclosures.

In the given context — play and competition admitted into the study of style producing extraordinary revelations — the word operates in its mature modern sense: not divine visions but intellectual breakthroughs, findings that feel like veils drawn back. The etymology remains audible beneath it: something was covered, and now it is not, and the reader experiences the pleasure of sight restored.
