1. Deep Etymological Analysis of Constituent Roots

Sanskrit plavaga (प्लवग) is an upapada-samāsa (dependent determinative compound) formed from two ancient Proto-Indo-European roots, each possessing a profound historical pedigree:

Root 1: plava- (प्लव) — "Leaping, Floating, Swimming"

The first element derives from the Sanskrit verbal root plu- (present plavate, class 1 Ātmanepada), meaning "to float, swim, bathe, submerge, hop, leap, or surge forward." This descends directly from Proto-Indo-European *pleu- ("to flow, float, swim, fly, run"), one of the most prolific kinetic roots in the parent language:

  • Greek Cognates: Greek pleō (πλέω, "to sail, float"), plootos ("swimming, navigable"), and ploion ("ship, vessel").
  • Latin Cognates: Latin pluere ("to rain"), pluvia ("rain"), and pulmo (from *plumo, "lung," literally the light, floating organ).
  • Germanic Cognates: Proto-Germanic *fleutaną, yielding Old English flēotan (Modern English float, fleet), Old English flōwan (Modern English flow), Old English flēogan (Modern English fly), and Old English flōd (Modern English flood).
  • Slavic & Baltic Cognates: Old Church Slavonic pluti ("to float, sail"), Russian plyt' (плыть), and Lithuanian pláuti ("to wash, rinse").

In Vedic Sanskrit, plu- originally focused on aquatic movement (floating on water or sailing in rafts, plava). However, early speakers observed a direct kinetic analogy between floating effortlessly upon turbulent water and bounding smoothly through the air or leaping between swaying tree branches, prompting a swift semantic generalization to saltatorial movement.

Root 2: -ga (ग) — "Going, Moving, Traversing"

The second element -ga is a root-affix (kṛt suffix) derived from the verbal root gam- (present gacchati), meaning "to go, move, depart, walk." It traces back to Proto-Indo-European *gʷem- ("to step, go, come"):

  • Greek Cognates: Greek bainō (βαίνω, "to go, walk, step"), basis ("step, base").
  • Latin Cognates: Latin venire ("to come," from *gʷm-ye-), yielding English advent, convene, venture.
  • Germanic Cognates: Proto-Germanic *kwemaną, yielding Old English cuman (Modern English come).

When compounded as an agentive second member (Pāṇinian upapada), -ga signifies "one who goes or moves in the manner of the preceding element," as seen in khaga ("sky-goer, bird"), uraga ("breast-goer, serpent"), and bhūga ("earth-dweller").

2. Compounding Mechanics and Semantic Synthesis

Under Pāṇini's grammar (Aṣṭādhyāyī 3.2.38: gamaḥ supi / antātyantādhvāna-dūrapārasarvānanteṣu ḍo vaktavyaḥ), the root gam- loses its nasal coda and takes the zero-grade agentive form -ga when compounded with a preceding dependent noun or adverb. The compound plava-ga literally signifies "one whose motion consists of leaps" (plavena gacchati iti plavagaḥ).

This synthesis bridged the physical act of swimming/floating with aerial leaping. In the eyes of the Vedic and Epic poets, a monkey navigating the high forest canopy does not walk on earth; it "floats" across the sea of green foliage, leaping across chasms like a ship riding waves. Hence, plavaga became the supreme poetic descriptor for arboreal locomotion.

3. Epic and Classical Attestation: From Vālmīki to Kālidāsa

In the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, plavaga functions as an indispensable heroic title. When Hanumān prepares to leap from Mount Mahendra to Lanka in the Sundarakāṇḍa, the text repeatedly hails him as plavagarṣabha ("bull/champion among leapers"). The term emphasizes his divine ability to transform the kinetic energy of a leap into a cosmic flight that crosses the ocean.

In classical courtly poetry (Kāvya), master poets like Kālidāsa (in the Raghuvaṃśa) and Bhāravi (in the Kirātārjunīya) exploited the semantic polysemy of plavaga through poetic puns (śleṣa). A single verse could simultaneously describe the stormy monsoon season where frogs (plavaga) croak and jump, the dense forests where monkeys (plavaga) chitter and leap, and the cloudy night sky where Saturn (plavaga) traces his fateful astrological arc.
