- 15th Century (Late Middle English, c. 1400–1450) - Borrowed from Old French *frequentacion* and Latin *frequentātiō*, initially appearing in ecclesiastical chronicles and moral treatises to denote habitual visiting of holy places or, conversely, sinful consorting with wicked company.
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Early Modern Era) - Expands in legal, educational, and courtly discourse (Francis Bacon, King James translations) to denote regular social intercourse, companionship, and attendance at royal courts, universities, or religious assemblies.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Reaches high literary prestige in English essayists and salon culture (Addison, Steele, Samuel Johnson) as the standard term for the deliberate cultivation of refined company, intellectual clubs, and literary conversation.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely used in social reform, sociology, and biological treatises to describe physical patterns of resort (e.g., frequentation of public libraries, taverns, or natural habitats), while retaining elevated literary status in historical prose.
- 20th Century to Present - Becomes an elegant, scholarly, and slightly archaic literary term, favored in high criticism (especially translations of French literary theory and Proustian studies) to describe the deep, patient, iterative immersion of oneself in an author's mind or body of work.
