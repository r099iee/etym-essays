The story of anvils is the story of metallurgy itself, and to trace it is to trace how humans learned to impose durable shape upon the mineral world. The plural form adds only the productive Modern English plural suffix -s to the singular anvil, whose lineage runs without break from Old English anfilt back to a Proto-Germanic compound meaning "on-beater" or "that upon which folding/beating happens". Every sound change, every spelling wobble, and every metaphorical extension records a stage in the social prestige of the smith.

In Proto-Germanic the compound *anafaltiz joined *ana "on, upon" with a base *falt- connected to folding and beating. This transparently describes function: the work is laid on it and beaten. Old English preserves this clearly as anfilt, anfealt, onfilt, attested in glosses and in Ælfric's vocabulary lists around 1000 CE where it glosses Latin incus. The initial vowel alternation an-/on- reflects unstressed prefix weakening, common in Old English compounds. The -filt element already shows devoicing and vowel reduction.

  • Old English to Middle English (800-1400): The Norman Conquest did not replace the native smith word with a French one; the forge stayed English. Forms shift from anfilt to anveld, anvelt, anvild under two pressures: vocalisation of /f/ to /v/ between vowels, and lowering of unstressed /i/ to /e/. The Chaucerian period shows anvelt in craft ordinances. Final -t remains firmly pronounced.
  • Late Middle to Early Modern (1400-1650): Loss of final -t in rapid speech and reanalysis gives anvil, anvile. Caxton's prints and later Shakespeare's era fix anvil. The plural at this stage is anvils with voiced /z/, fully regular. The Great Vowel Shift does not greatly affect the short stressed /æ/ which remains stable as /æ/ in southern England.
  • Phonetic consolidation (1650-1800): RP settles on /ˈænvɪl/ with clear stress on the first syllable, second vowel reduced to /ɪ/. The plural /ˈænvɪlz/ adds syllabic voicing assimilation. No further stem change occurs; the word is phonologically frozen because its referent - a 50-200 kg block of wrought iron with steel face - is itself frozen in form.

Semantically, the anvil never loses its concrete core, but it massively gains symbolic load. In early medieval law codes the smith and his anvil stand at the edge of the village, both essential and slightly feared. By the Viking Age the anvil is linked to myth: Wayland the Smith, Thor's hammer work, and later St Dunstan who famously seized the Devil's nose with his tongs upon his anvil. The phrase "on the anvil" meaning "under consideration, being shaped" appears by the 16th century, calquing Latin in incude esse. This metaphor treats ideas as hot iron.

  • Industrial apotheosis: In the 18th-19th centuries the London-pattern anvil with horn, heel, hardy hole and pritchel hole becomes standardized. Dictionaries from Johnson (1755) to the OED define it with loving technical detail. The anvil becomes emblem of Labour, engraved on trade union banners, pennies, and war memorials to industry.
  • Modern extensions: Meteorology borrows anvil for the anvil-shaped top of a cumulonimbus cloud (anvil cloud, anvil head). Anatomy borrows it for the incus bone in the middle ear, still called the anvil. Idioms multiply: "between hammer and anvil" for being caught between two forces, "to hammer out on the anvil" for laborious creation. The plural anvils in the source context - "shaped stones into a museum of arms and implements: hammers, anvils..." - preserves the oldest sense: stone anvils of the Neolithic, before iron existed, used as passive hammering bases.
  • Literary attestations: Beowulf's forge scenes imply the anvil without naming it in modern form; Middle English romances name anvelt in armour-making; Longfellow's "The Village Blacksmith" (1840) - "Under a spreading chestnut-tree / The village smithy stands; / The smith, a mighty man is he" - fixes the anvil at the moral centre of honest labour; Tolkien's Noldor and modern fantasy return it to myth.

Thus anvils carries a continuous line from PIE preposition to village forge to thundercloud: a small compound that never needed replacement because no better name for "the thing we beat things on" was ever found.
