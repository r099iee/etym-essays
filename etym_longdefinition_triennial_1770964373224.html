triennial (adjective and noun) is a calendrical word built, like a set of nesting Russian dolls, from two ancient roots: tri- "three" and annus "year." In its primary and dominant sense it means "occurring every three years" — that is, an event or occasion that repeats at three-year intervals. It also carries a secondary, less common sense, "lasting for a period of three years," which is grammatically older in Latin (Latin triennis "of three years' duration") though rarer in modern English. In the literary context of this card, the word names a great periodic examination that takes place once every three years — an institutional landmark in the life of the scholar, long anticipated and heavily invested in, and the word therefore carries a formal, bureaucratic, epochal tone: it announces rarity, structure, and a fixed rhythm imposed on time by institutions.

The full polysemy and usage range can be set out as follows:

  • Sense 1 — Occurring every three years (the dominant modern sense). The adjective is applied to events scheduled on a three-year cycle: triennial examinations, triennial festivals, triennial conferences, triennial elections, triennial reviews. The word emphasizes not merely the fact of recurrence but the interval — the accumulated rarity and anticipation that three years' worth of waiting creates. This is the sense in the card's context, where the "next triennial" is a rare, structured window of opportunity.
  • Sense 2 — Lasting for three years (technical, chiefly historical). The older Latin-derived sense "of three years' duration" survives in formal and historical writing: a triennial office, a triennial term of service, a triennial lease. This sense treats the word as a duration adjective, parallel to "biennial" meaning "two-year-long" and "annual" meaning "year-long." In everyday modern English this sense is rare and sounds legalistic.
  • Sense 3 — The noun: a triennial event, publication, or celebration. By ellipsis the adjective becomes a noun: "the triennial" is a festival, examination, meeting, exhibition, or anniversary that recurs every three years. The card's context ("the next _______") uses the word precisely this way, as a substantive referring to a specific recurring occasion.
  • Sense 4 — Institutional and legal usage. The word has a strong bureaucratic history: in Britain the Triennial Acts (1694, renewed 1716) limited parliaments to a maximum of three years; the ecclesiastical world has triennial visitations and triennial councils; universities administer triennial examinations and reviews. In all these settings "triennial" is a technical term of administration, signalling legally or institutionally fixed three-year cycles.
  • Sense 5 — Analogical and metaphorical extension. Following the model of "biennial" (the standard botanical term for a two-year plant life cycle), "triennial" is occasionally used by analogy for anything spanning a three-year period — a triennial plant, a triennial report cycle, a triennial rhythm of life. It may also be used loosely and figuratively of anything felt to recur "about once every three years," though purists reserve it for exact three-year intervals.
  • Sense 6 — Register and grammar. The word is formal, learned, and administrative. In casual speech, native speakers overwhelmingly say "every three years" rather than "triennial." The adjective is not inflected; the noun is countable ("a triennial," plural "triennials"). It is easily confused with triannual, which means "occurring three times in a single year" — an important boundary to mark, since the two words sit on opposite sides of the same number "three."

The historical semantic bridge. The word was born in the administrative precision of the Roman world, which needed a vocabulary for exact recurring periods of governance, taxation, and religious observance. Latin triennium named a period of exactly three years; the adjectival formations triennis and (in later Latin) triennalis converted that period into a description of things occurring every three years. English, in the seventeenth century, re-formed the word directly on the Latin model with the suffix -al, and from the beginning it was a word of institutions: parliaments, universities, churches, and learned societies. The semantic history is therefore not a story of dramatic shifts but of steady institutionalization: a precise Latin measure of time became an English bureaucratic adjective, then a noun for the recurring event itself, and finally a general — though still formal — word for anything that follows a three-year rhythm.

Boundary comparisons. The nearest neighbours form a strict family. Annual means every year (or lasting a year); biennial means every two years (or lasting two years); triennial means every three years (or lasting three years); quadrennial means every four years; quinquennial means every five years; septennial every seven years. Perennial is a different creature entirely: it means lasting or recurring throughout the year and year after year, without any fixed interval, and is famous as the botanical term for plants that live for many growing seasons. The genuine trap-word is triannual ("three times a year"), which shares the "three" element but inverts the temporal meaning. Each member of the family is identical in formation and differs only in the number locked into its prefix — a beautifully regular paradigm in which "triennial" occupies the third slot.
