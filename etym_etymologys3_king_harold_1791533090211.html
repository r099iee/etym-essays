King Harold is not a word whose meaning drifted; it is a name whose meaning was fought over. The two elements of the phrase have their own histories — king from a Germanic word for the head of a noble lineage, Harold from a compound meaning “army-ruler” — but the phrase as a whole became historically charged only because of a single day in October 1066. Its evolution is therefore a case study in how a proper name can become a moral and political symbol.

“King”: the title

The English word king descends from Old English cyning, from Proto-Germanic *kuningaz. The usual analysis connects the first element with *kun- “family, kin” and the suffix *-ing- “descended from, belonging to,” so that a king is originally “the man of the noble kin” — a leader chosen from a royal lineage rather than a ruler defined by territory. Cognates include Old Frisian kening, Old Saxon kuning, Dutch koning, German König and Old Norse konungr. The word is thus older than any English state and carries the archaic assumption that legitimacy is inherited.

By the eleventh century the title had acquired the full range of Christian and legal associations: anointed, crowned, law-giving, coin-bearing. Harold’s kingship was made by election and consecration, not by heredity alone, which is precisely why the Norman case against him had to be framed as perjury rather than usurpation.

“Harold”: the name

Harold comes from Old English Hereweald, a transparent compound of here “army” and weald “power, rule” — “army-ruler,” a name of the same type as Herewulf and Hereberht. Its Norse cognate Haraldr was extremely common in Scandinavia and in the Danelaw, and the two forms merged in Middle English. The name was borne by kings of Denmark and Norway before it became the name of an English king, and it remained in use after 1066, though it never again belonged to a reigning monarch.

1066: the making of the phrase

Harold Godwinson was crowned on 6 January 1066, in a kingdom already ringed by enemies. His reign compresses the whole military history of late Anglo-Saxon England into ten months: a forced march north, a victory at Stamford Bridge, a forced march south, and a defeat at Hastings. The speed of the campaigns and the completeness of the disaster are why the phrase “King Harold” became a narrative unit — it contains a beginning, a climax and an end.

Norman propaganda and English memory

The Norman account, elaborated by William of Poitiers, William of Jumièges and the Bayeux Tapestry, presents Harold as a perjurer who swore on relics to support William’s succession and then took the crown for himself. In this version his death is a divine verdict. English memory moved in the opposite direction. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle treats him as the legitimate king and laments the fall of “the best men of England”; twelfth-century writers such as William of Malmesbury and Henry of Huntingdon soften his image; Waltham Abbey, which he founded, promoted his cult-like memory. The arrow-in-the-eye motif, first attested in the twelfth century, turns his death into a martyrdom and gives art a single unforgettable image.

The legend of the survivor

Medieval and early modern folklore insisted that Harold had escaped. A version current in Chester made him an anchorite; other versions sent him to Germany or Devon. The legend has no contemporary support, but it is historically eloquent: a king who leaves no tomb is a king whose story can be rewritten. The same impulse later produced the romantic Harold of Victorian painting and the “if only” Harold of counterfactual history.

Victorian hero and modern case study

Nineteenth-century nationalism made Harold a tragic English patriot, and the 900th anniversary of 1066 in 1966 fixed him in the heritage industry. Modern scholarship has shifted the emphasis from personality to contingency: the battle was close, the logistics were fragile, and the English kingdom was rich and administratively sophisticated. Harold emerges less as a doomed romantic figure than as a competent ruler caught between two invasions.

Attestations and afterlives

  • Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (D and E), s.a. 1066 — the earliest English narrative of the campaign and Harold’s death.
  • Bayeux Tapestry, c. 1070s — the visual sequence from Harold’s oath to his death, including the disputed arrow.
  • William of Malmesbury, Gesta Regum Anglorum (c. 1125) — a more sympathetic English-Latin account.
  • Shakespeare’s King John and later history plays do not stage Harold, but the Elizabethan chronicle tradition keeps him in the national story.
  • Bulwer-Lytton’s Harold, the Last of the Saxon Kings (1848) — the Victorian novel that fixed the romantic image.
  • 1966 anniversary exhibitions and the modern re-enactment at Battle — the phrase as public heritage.

Why the phrase still carries weight

“King Harold” is a small phrase with a large burden. It names the last crowned king of an older England, the man whose death made the Norman conquest possible, and the counterfactual that English history has never quite stopped imagining. Every use of the phrase — in a textbook, a pub sign or a re-enactment — inherits the argument between the Norman perjurer and the English martyr, and chooses a side, often without knowing it.
