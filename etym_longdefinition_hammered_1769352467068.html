hammered (verb, past participle / adjective) — struck, beaten, driven, or shaped by repeated blows of a hammer; the past tense and past participle of the verb to hammer. In its most literal sense it describes the action of a blacksmith, carpenter, or builder: a nail is hammered into wood, metal is hammered into shape, and a stone-paved or earthen path is hammered hard by the constant passage of people, as in the source sentence — the ground is beaten down and compacted into firmness by the repeated tread of many feet. That context sense is subtle but real: "hammered hard" does not mean struck with a tool at all, but pressed, pounded, and compacted until it is firm, the way a footpath or a road becomes beaten solid by traffic. The word thus carries a double life: the sharp, deliberate blows of a hand-tool on one side, and the slow, cumulative pounding of pressure on the other. Primary senses:
  • Struck or hit with a hammer — "he hammered the nail into the wood," "the blacksmith hammered the red-hot iron."
  • Shaped, forged, or decorated by hammering — "hammered copper," "a hammered silver bowl," "hammered metalwork," describing both the process and the finished, textured surface it produces.
  • Beaten down, compacted, or driven flat by repeated impact — "a path hammered hard by many feet," "the road was hammered smooth by carts."
Secondary and modern senses — the polysemy in full:
  • Slang, heavily intoxicated: "we got hammered last night" — drunk or high. The semantic bridge is the sensory metaphor: the pounding of the forge becomes the pounding in the head, and heavy drinking "hammers" the senses until the drinker is as flat as a beaten nail. This is now the most frequent informal sense.
  • Finance and markets: "the stock got hammered," "the company was hammered by the crash" — a security or business hit hard, sold off heavily, or financially battered. The image is of a severe, repeated beating delivered by the market.
  • Sport and competition: "the home team got hammered 5–0" — decisively, humiliatingly defeated. The metaphorical beating transfers physical pounding to scoreboard dominance.
  • Criticism and pressure: "she was hammered by the press," "hammered with questions" — subjected to relentless attack, questioning, or blame; the blows are verbal.
  • Music: a "hammered dulcimer" is a stringed instrument played by striking strings with small hammers, and in guitar technique a "hammer-on" is a note sounded by hammering a finger onto the fret — a technical jargon use of the verb.
  • Commerce and argument: "hammer out" means to reach an agreement through long, hard negotiation — to pound a deal into shape, exactly as a smith pounds metal into final form; "hammer home" means to drive a point in with force, like driving a nail home.
  • Law and auctions: "sold under the hammer" — an object knocked down at auction, where the auctioneer's gavel (a small hammer) seals the sale.
Historical semantic bridge. Every secondary sense is a metaphor of the same physical act. The blacksmith's hammer, the oldest image, supplies the violence (beaten, battered, hammered by the market), the shaping (forged, hammered out), the compaction (a path hammered hard), and even the intoxication (the drunk is hammered flat, the head pounds like a forge). The word has never lost its percussive core: wherever it appears, from a jeweller's bench to a stock exchange floor, something is being struck, and struck repeatedly.
