Grudge operates as both a potent transitive and intransitive verb and a pervasive psychological noun in English. As a verb, it means to give, concede, grant, or allow something reluctantly, unwillingly, and with deep inward resentment, or to resent another person’s possession, enjoyment, or acquisition of some advantage. As a noun, it designates a persistent, smoldering, long-cherished feeling of ill will, bitterness, resentment, or rancor harbored against someone on account of a real or perceived past grievance, insult, or injury. Rooted in ancient onomatopoeic sounds representing guttural grumbling in the throat, the word captures the visceral physical tension of withholding generosity and nursing grievance.

In the source text from Samuel Johnson’s The Rambler (No. 144, 1751), the word appears in its classic early modern transitive verbal sense: ‘They grudge not the whiteness of the sepulchre, because by no honor they can bestow upon it can the senseless corpse be rendered an object of envy; but they are niggardly of the reputation which contributes to happiness, or advances to fortune.’ Johnson brilliantly contrasts the ease with which people grant honors to the dead against the bitter parsimony with which they treat the living. To grudge not the white marble of a tomb means that people feel no painful reluctance or resentment when spending praise on a corpse, precisely because a dead body cannot feel joy or provoke jealousy. In contrast, they begrudge every ounce of reputation to living competitors who might actually prosper from it.

Polysemic Senses and Functional Domains:

  • 1. Reluctant Concession and Begrudging Bestowal (Primary Transitive Verb Sense): To grant, yield, pay, or concede something with palpable unwillingness, annoyance, or resentment (e.g., ‘no one could grudge him his hard-earned retirement’ or ‘she grudged every penny she was forced to pay in taxes’). The subject feels that the recipient does not deserve the benefit, or resents parting with the resource.
  • 2. Deep-Seated Resentment and Cherished Rancor (Primary Noun Sense): A persistent, chronic state of bitterness, animosity, or grievance held against a specific person, group, or institution over time. Typically framed with verbs of enduring physical containment—such as to harbor a grudge, to nurse a grudge, or to hold a grudge—implying that the grievance is carefully fed and sheltered within the psyche rather than resolved.
  • 3. Competitive Rivalries and Athletic Clashes (Modern Sports Sense): Used in the compound noun grudge match: a sporting contest, fight, or professional confrontation between bitter rivals whose intense antagonism is fueled by past insults, disputed victories, or personal hostility rather than mere athletic competition.
  • 4. Archaic Intransitive Murmuring and Grumbling (Historical Sensory Sense): To grumble, murmur, mutter discontentedly, or complain under one’s breath against authority, fate, or hard work. This was the earliest Middle English sense, directly reflecting the word’s sound-imitative origin.
  • 5. Archaic and Medical Lingering Symptoms (Historical Pathology Sense): In early modern medical texts, a grudge was an archaic term for an early warning sign, slight twinge, premonitory ache, or stubborn lingering trace of an illness—such as ‘a grudge of an ague’—analogous to a smoldering fever that has not yet broken out into full sickness.
  • 6. Psychological and Cognitive Patterns: In modern cognitive psychology and therapy, grudge-holding is recognized as an active cognitive distortion characterized by rumination, perceived moral injury, and resistance to emotional reconciliation, creating prolonged physiological stress in the harborer.

Register and Connotations: Grudge possesses an earthy, blunt, and visceral character. The phonetics of the initial voiced velar plosive /ɡ/ coupled with the short central vowel /ʌ/ and voiced postalveolar affricate /dʒ/ create a heavy, churning acoustic profile that mirrors the feeling of physical resentment. When used as a verb (to grudge), it carries a sharp moral critique of stinginess and petty envy; when used as a noun (a grudge), it conjures the image of an emotional trap where ancient offenses are preserved in cold storage.
