Every wave in the word undulates has been rolling for six thousand years. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *wed-, the ancient name for water and wetness, the same root that fills English water, Russian voda, Greek hydor, and Latin unda. Latin unda meant wave and flood at once, the sea in motion, and from it the Romans formed the affectionate diminutive undula, a little wave, the kind that laps rather than drowns. Medieval Latin then coined undulatus, furnished with little waves, for wavy ornament and anatomy, and 17th-century English natural philosophers borrowed the whole family to name the motion their new instruments were revealing.

The borrowing was no accident of fashion. When English thinkers argued over the nature of light, they needed a verb for what waves do, and to undulate, first recorded in the later 1600s, supplied it: light undulates, said Christiaan Huygens and his English readers, against Newtonians who said it flies as particles. The noun undulation became a technical term of physics, and the adjective undulatory labelled a whole theory of the universe. Sound, too, was an undulation of air, heat an undulation of invisible fluids, and the cultivated English reader learned to see the world as a pond permanently rippling.

From physics to feeling:

  • Sound and light, 17th–18th c.: undulation names wave propagation in treatises; dictionaries define undulate as to move as waves, always with water as the first illustration.
  • Landscape and art, 18th–19th c.: landscape gardeners praise undulating grounds, painters undulating drapery, and novelists undulating hills; the verb escapes the laboratory for the picturesque.
  • The wave theory triumph, early 19th c.: Thomas Young and Augustin Fresnel prove light undulates, and the word rides the most successful physics of its century into every textbook.
  • Medicine and music, 19th–20th c.: physicians describe undulating fevers that rise and fall, and musicians undulating tones; the waveform on the oscilloscope screen makes every fluctuation visible as a literal wave.
  • Modern ubiquity: from peristalsis to stock charts, from eels to easeful prose rhythms, undulates in its third-person form marks any subject, snake, surface, or voice, that has taken water as its model of motion.

Phonetically the word kept its Latin shape almost intact: un-du-late with the stress on the first syllable, the final -s of undulates merely marking present-tense agreement, he undulates, it undulates. The soft vowels and liquid consonants perform the meaning, and teachers of rhetoric have long cited it as onomatopoeia of the learned kind. Semantically it never strayed far from water: every extension, to sound, to markets, to moods, passes through the visible picture of the pond in the source sentence, the little wave that taught a root meaning water how to become a verb meaning to move beautifully.
