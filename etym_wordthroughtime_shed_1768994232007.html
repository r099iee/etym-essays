shed
- c. 1200 Middle English shedden — "to cast off, spill," from the older Germanic sense "to divide, separate": to shed tears or blood is to let them part from the body.
- Later Middle and early Modern — the "divide" sense fossilizes in watershed (19th c.) and the "shed" of the hair (the parting), while the everyday sense turns to natural casting-off: skin, leaves, hair, horns, antlers.
- 19th-century American dialect — the participial fossil "get shed of" survives in frontier speech as a blunt, final "get rid of": to part from something decisively and forever.
- Contemporary — the standard verb means an easy, natural, almost effortless separation (a snake sheds its skin, a tree its leaves, a man his tears); the noun "shed" (a lean-to hut) is a different word and a standing caution.
