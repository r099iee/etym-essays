- c. 1300 — toilen "to pull or drag about, to struggle, contend" — borrowed from Old French toillier "to stir, entangle"; the early sense is physical wrestling and strife, not yet steady work.
- 14th–15th c. — the sense shifts from "tug, struggle" to "labour hard, work continuously" — toil becomes the honest word for grinding effort; the noun toil means both "labour" and "strife".
- 16th–17th c. — toiled is the past of the hard-working labourer; the word is used sympathetically of peasants, soldiers, and craftsmen, and morally of Adam's curse of work; Shakespeare's "toil and trouble" (1606) fixes the phrase forever.
- 18th–19th c. — "the toiling masses" enters social and political writing; toil gains dignity as the honest labour of the poor, opposed to the leisure of the rich.
- 19th–20th c. — toil stays rural and manual; by the industrial era "toiling" is the word for factory and field labour alike, carrying weight and exhaustion.
- 21st c. — toiled is literary and slightly old-fashioned: a writer still says "they toiled up the mountain" for effortful struggle, but everyday speech prefers "worked hard"; the word retains the muscular, straining colour of its French ancestor.
