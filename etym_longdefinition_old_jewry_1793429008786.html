Old Jewry is a historic street in the City of London, situated in the Ward of Coleman Street, running northwards from Poultry (the eastern continuation of Cheapside) to Gresham Street (formerly Cateaton Street). Historically, it constitutes one of London’s most storied and culturally resonant micro-geographies, having functioned from the eleventh century until 1290 as the principal residential, financial, and religious quarter of the Anglo-Jewish community following their invitation to England by William the Conqueror. In literary and cultural history, Old Jewry stands as a powerful socio-economic signifier, marking an address of affluent, respectable mercantile commerce, medical apprenticeship, and civic authority.

In Jonathan Swift’s Gulliver’s Travels (1726), the street serves an essential sociological function in establishing Lemuel Gulliver’s professional baseline. After completing his medical studies at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, and his surgical apprenticeship under Mr. James Bates in Leyden, Gulliver returns to London, marries Miss Mary Burton, and settles in Old Jewry to practice surgery. In the late seventeenth century, Old Jewry was a prestigious City address, populated by substantial merchants, respected apothecaries, and successful surgeons catering to the wealthy commercial elite of the financial district. Gulliver’s subsequent financial decline is traced through an explicit downward topographical migration: when his master Bates dies and business falls off, Gulliver is forced to remove first to Fetter Lane (a more crowded, artisan and printing district near the Inns of Court) and finally down to maritime Wapping, where he hopes in vain to scratch together a living among common merchant seamen before financial desperation compels him back to sea. Old Jewry thus symbolizes the comfortable bourgeois apex of Gulliver’s domestic life before his fantastic wanderings begin.

Primary and Secondary Meanings across History and Modernity:

  • The Specific Toponymic Thoroughfare (Primary Modern Sense): The literal one-way street in the EC2 postal district of the City of London. Today, it is lined with sleek neoclassical and modern commercial offices, high-end investment funds, corporate law firms, and consultancy practices, standing directly adjacent to the Bank of England and the historic guildhalls of the Worshipful Companies of Grocers and Mercers.
  • The Medieval Jewish Ghetto and Anglo-Jewish Archetype (Historical Cultural Sense): A metonym for the medieval Jewish experience in England between the Norman Conquest (1066) and the Edict of Expulsion (1290). During this period, the Jewish community was designated as the direct chattel of the Crown under the *Carta Judaeorum*, serving as essential royal financiers. The Great Synagogue of London stood at the northeast corner of Old Jewry until its seizure and destruction following Edward I’s expulsion order.
  • Shorthand for Dissenting Theology and Radical Politics (18th–19th Century Sense): The home of the famous Old Jewry Meeting House, one of the most prominent Presbyterian and Unitarian Dissenting chapels in London. It was here on 4 November 1789 that the moral philosopher and mathematician Dr. Richard Price delivered his legendary sermon *A Discourse on the Love of Our Country*, celebrating the French Revolution and asserting the natural right of peoples to cashier their governors for misconduct—a speech that directly provoked Edmund Burke to write his foundational conservative manifesto, Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790).
  • Metonym for City of London Law Enforcement (Civic Administration Sense): For more than 180 years, from 1839 until 2020, Number 26 Old Jewry served as the headquarters of the City of London Police force. In British administrative and journalistic parlance throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, ‘Old Jewry’ was frequently employed as a metonym for City police commissioners and financial fraud detectives, analogous to ‘Scotland Yard’ for the Metropolitan Police.
  • Literary Signifier of Commercial Ambition and Urban Realism: In early modern drama, Old Jewry regularly represented the bustling nexus of London credit, trade, and domestic comedy. Ben Jonson chose Old Jewry as the setting for the London revision of his masterwork Every Man in His Humour (1598), utilizing its streets and taverns (notably the Windmill Tavern) to depict the eccentricities, humours, and anxieties of the rising merchant class.

Register and Connotations: Today, the name Old Jewry carries a solemn historical weight coupled with elite financial authority. Unlike generic street names, it serves as a living linguistic monument to medieval London’s demographic shifts and religious history, evoking both the tragedy of medieval state-sponsored expulsion and the subsequent commercial rebirth of the City.
