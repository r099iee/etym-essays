TRIUMPHS is the plural of TRIUMPH. In the source passage, Gibbon's famous sentence from The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the word names the formal Roman ceremony: "The seven first centuries were filled with a rapid succession of triumphs". Here "triumphs" refers not to abstract victories but to the specific ritual processions by which the Roman state celebrated a victorious general's return to the city - the highest military honour in the Roman Republic, a civic-religious pageant that paraded the general, his troops, captives, and spoils up to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitol. The plural and the phrase "rapid succession" together convey frequency and momentum: conquest as habit, celebration as the Republic's pulse. PRIMARY SENSE (the Roman ceremony)
  • The Roman triumph (triumphus): The formal procession granted by the Senate to a victorious general (imperator), who entered Rome in a chariot, his face painted red, preceded by his troops, his captives, and the spoils of war, and proceeded to the Capitol to sacrifice to Jupiter. It was the Republic's most coveted honour, reserved for great wars won by a commander of consular rank.
  • Victory or achievement (extended sense): From this institutional core the word broadened to mean any notable victory or signal success - a military victory, a diplomatic coup, a great accomplishment. "A triumph of engineering", "a triumph over adversity".
  • Exultation and joy (extended sense): A further extension yields the meaning "exultant joy or elation": "he shouted in triumph", "a note of triumph in her voice". The semantic bridge is the celebratory, victorious feeling that accompanies a great success.
SECONDARY AND TECHNICAL SENSES
  • The lesser "ovation" (technical distinction): In Roman constitutional usage a "triumph" (triumphus) was strictly distinguished from the lesser "ovation" (ovatio), a smaller ceremony granted for less important victories. The word "triumph" therefore carries precise legal-institutional meaning in ancient history: not every victory earned a triumph; it had to meet criteria of scale, enemy type, and the general's rank.
  • Triumphal vocabulary (technical/historical): The word generates a technical cluster: "triumphal arch" (a monument erected to commemorate a triumph), "triumphal procession", "triumphal entry", and "triumvir" - though the last, meaning "one of three joint rulers", is a different Latin formation (from "tres" "three" + "vir" "man") and must not be confused with "triumph".
  • Figurative and journalistic use: In modern journalism and everyday speech "triumph" is the default word for any outstanding success or moral victory ("a triumph for science", "the underdog's triumph"), usually losing all reference to the Roman rite. "Triumphs" in the plural is common in retrospective surveys ("the triumphs of modern medicine").
  • Technical use in ancient historiography: For historians, "triumphs" are a countable, documented institution: the Fasti Triumphales (the recorded list of triumphs) are primary evidence for Roman expansion, and the source passage's "rapid succession of triumphs" is a compressed allusion to exactly this record.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES The word entered English from Latin "triumphus" (via Old French "triumphe"), and the Latin word itself was borrowed from Greek "thriambos", the name of a hymn or procession in honour of Dionysus. The bridge is spectacular: what began as a Bacchic procession - a religious parade with song, in honour of the god of wine and ecstasy - became in Rome the state procession honouring a conquering general, and the religious cry of the Bacchic rite ("thriambos!") became the shout of victory. The Roman ceremony kept something of the ritual flavour of its Greek ancestor: the triumphing general wore the costume of Jupiter, was crowned with laurel, and was accompanied by musicians and singers, as in a divine procession. The extension from "Roman ceremony" to "any great victory" is then natural: the ceremony was the visible embodiment of victory, so the word came to stand for victory itself, and finally for the elation victory brings. CONTEXT IN THE PASSAGE In Gibbon's sentence the plural "triumphs" is deliberate: Rome's first seven centuries are measured not in battles but in celebrations, and the "rapid succession" of triumphs marks the Republic's rhythm of ceaseless conquest. The word therefore functions as both a literal reference to the Roman institution and a compressed image of imperial momentum, against which Augustus's moderation ("relinquishing the design of subduing the whole earth") is dramatically contrasted. GRAMMAR AND COLLOCATION "Triumph" is a countable noun (plural "triumphs") and also a verb ("to triumph over"). Standard collocations: "to celebrate a triumph", "to earn/obtain a triumph", "to grant/refuse a triumph", "triumphal arch", "triumphal procession", "a succession of triumphs", "in triumph". The adjective "triumphal" (relating to the ceremony: "triumphal arch") must be distinguished from "triumphant" (feeling or expressing victory: "a triumphant shout"), a distinction worth preserving in careful usage.
