Downs, the plural of down in its hill sense, are rolling open uplands of chalk grassland — smooth green whaleback ridges without hedges or woods, grazed by sheep and crowned with ancient hill-forts, dewponds, and chalk figures.

In the source sentence the word is the payoff of the geology beneath it: upon a bed of chalk, the downs always afford good sheep food. The sentence is a small lesson in downland farming — thin chalk soils grow short sweet turf, sheep crop it close, and the treeless openness that looks bare to a stranger is exactly what makes the pasture reliable. The speaker states it as a general truth, always, the confidence of someone who knows the hills.

Core meaning in detail: The downs proper are the two great chalk ranges of south-east England — the North Downs running from Hampshire to Kent, and the South Downs across Sussex to Beachy Head — with outliers on Salisbury Plain, the Chilterns, and the Wessex chalk. Their turf, grazed for millennia, is a close sward of fescues, salad burnet, wild thyme, and orchids, supporting the famous Southdown breed of sheep and, in sporting history, the racecourses of Epsom and Goodwood. Writers from Kipling, who loved his Sussex downs beyond all other ground, to W. H. Hudson have treated them as the distilled essence of open England.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Rolling chalk uplands (primary sense, from Old English): the grassy hills themselves — the downs of Sussex, Kent, and Wessex. The bridge to every other sense is this landscape: smooth, open, sheep-grazed high ground.
  • The Downs as proper noun (capitalised): the North Downs and South Downs as named ranges, now a National Park and Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty; also the roadstead called the Downs off the Kent coast, sheltered water between the Goodwins and the shore. The bridge is naming: the most famous examples take the capital letter.
  • Downland pasture and its products (transferred): downland mutton, the Southdown sheep breed, down wool. The bridge is produce: the hills lend their name to what they feed, as champagne names the wine of its chalk.
  • Downland as habitat (modern conservation): chalk grassland treasured as England's richest wildflower sward — orchids, blues, and burnets — maintained only by grazing. The bridge is ecology: the sheep food of the source sentence is now the botanist's treasure, and the same grazing keeps both alive.
  • To be distinguished — the other downs (homographs, different roots): eider down and goose down, the soft under-feathers for quilts, from Old Norse dúnn; and the adverbial down — down the hill, down with the king — from Old English dūne, off the hill. These share spelling and sound but not blood: the hill word is Celtic, the feather word is Norse, and confusing them is the classic trap the dictionary warns against.

Register and connotation: downs is deeply English and deeply nostalgic — chalk, sheep-bells, larks, and sea-winds in a single syllable. Estate agents, poets, and racegoers all reach for it: the word sells cottages, crowns verses, and names classic races run on turf that has felt hooves for three thousand years. Unlike the grander mountains or the wilder moors, the downs promise gentle openness — walking country, readable at a glance, where the source sentence's sheep are always, reassuringly, well fed.
