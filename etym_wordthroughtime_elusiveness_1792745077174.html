- [Late 17th c. Early Modern English] — Derived from the newly adopted adjective elusive (which arrived via Neo-Latin ēlūsivus), initially appearing in theological tracts and metaphysical poetry to denote the slippery nature of demonic illusions, spiritual deceptions, and the difficulty of pinning down heretical doctrines — [perception: marked by suspicion and theological wariness; seen as a deceptive, evasive trick of false reasoning or demonic shape-shifting]
- [18th c. Enlightenment & Natural Philosophy] — Extended into philosophical epistemology (Locke, Hume) and natural history, characterizing subtle natural fluids (phlogiston, caloric, animal magnetism) and the frustrating boundary of empirical knowledge where first causes constantly evade human observation — [perception: viewed as a profound intellectual challenge; an empirical hurdle demanding finer scientific instruments and sharper inductive logic]
- [19th c. Romantic & Victorian Era] — Embraced by Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Shelley, Keats) and Victorian novelists as a supreme aesthetic virtue, naming the transcendent, half-glimpsed beauties of nature, the evanescent flashes of poetic inspiration, and the delicate emotional states that resist crude industrial quantification — [perception: celebrated as an evocative, sublime quality of beauty, spirit, and romantic longing]
- [20th c. Modernism & Modern Science] — Formalized across theoretical physics (quantum uncertainty, wave-particle duality, dark matter), psychoanalysis (the elusive unconscious drives and dream fragments), and high modernist literature (Proustian memory, Virginia Woolf's fleeting consciousness) — [perception: recognized as a fundamental structural feature of the universe and human consciousness rather than a mere temporary obstacle]
- [21st c. Contemporary Global English] — Pervasive across popular literature (Tolkien fantasy tropes of stealthy woodland folk), technological discourse (the elusiveness of artificial general intelligence, cyber-security threats, dark web actors), and wellness culture (the elusive pursuit of work-life balance) — [perception: an evocative, refined marker for goals, truths, or states of being that remain perpetually aspirational and resistant to final capture]
