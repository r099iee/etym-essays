SHIRK is the verb for sly, cowardly, or indolent avoidance of a duty, responsibility, or labor — the sneaking abandonment of an assigned task rather than the open refusal to do it. The word carries a built-in sneer: to shirk is not merely to rest but to evade, to slip away from obligation by laziness, fear, or trickery. In the goblins' slave-driving song, Nor dare to shirk, the word is pure menace: the captives are forbidden even the surreptitious evasion the verb describes, threatened with punishment while their captors feast and laugh. The tone is that of an overseer — contemptuous, watchful, and warning that no prisoner may slacken under the masters' eyes.

Historically, shirk entered English in disreputable company: its early senses included "to practice fraud," "to live by one's wits," and "to sponge as a parasite," and it is probably related, through the German Schurke "rogue, scoundrel," to the predatory shark. That criminal residue still flavors the modern verb, which is why shirking always feels shabbier than mere laziness — it is evasion with a hint of dishonesty and cowardice.

Core senses in detail:

  • Primary — to evade duty (verb): To avoid or neglect a duty, responsibility, or share of labor out of laziness, fear, or indolence. "He shirked his chores."
  • Primary — to avoid work (verb, with "off"): To slip away from work or obligation; colloquially "to shirk off" or "to shirk it."
  • Extended — to slink or move evasively (now archaic/rare): In late-16th and 17th-century use, to go slyly, sneak, or slink away to avoid something; the physical sense from which the moral sense grew.
  • Extended — to live by fraud (archaic): In the 17th century, to gain a living by trickery, sponging, or swindling; the parasitic sense, now obsolete.
  • Derived noun — shirk (archaic): A needy parasite, a disreputable sponger; the 17th-century noun recorded c. 1633.
  • Derived noun — shirker: One who evades duty or work; recorded 1799, used especially of soldiers and workers who dodge their obligations.
  • Derived noun — shirking: The act of evading duty; common in "shirking of responsibilities," "shirking work."

Semantic bridge and rationale:

  • From physical slinking to moral evasion: The earliest recorded senses are physical — to move evasively, to sneak away — and the moral sense grew by metaphor: the man who dodges his duty is sneaking away from obligation as he would from a pursuer.
  • The rogue's residue: The association with German Schurke "rogue" and with English shark "swindler" loaded the word with dishonesty from the start; shirking therefore implies not just failure but underhandedness.
  • Register: The word is informal-to-neutral but morally loaded; in work and military contexts it is a term of contempt for the malingerer or the coward.

Modern usage contexts:

  • Workplace: "shirking responsibility," "a shirker" — the standard term for the employee who dodges his share of the work.
  • Military: Historically applied to soldiers who avoided service or dangerous duty; shirking one's duty in wartime became a serious moral charge of cowardice.
  • Academic: "shirking studies," "shirking homework" — common informal usage for students avoiding work.
  • Political rhetoric: "shirking one's duty to the nation" — a moralizing charge levelled at those who avoid civic obligations, especially wartime service.
  • Economics and game theory: "shirking" is a technical term for a worker exerting less effort than contracted when effort cannot be observed — the central principal-agent problem in the theory of the firm.

In short, SHIRK uniquely combines the ideas of cowardice, dishonesty, and the stealthy abandonment of one's task — precisely the crime the goblins forbid their prisoners from attempting, and the offense modern speakers still accuse the lazy, the fearful, and the malingering of committing.
