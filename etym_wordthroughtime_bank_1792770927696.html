- Early Middle English (c. 12th–13th c.) — Inherited from Old Norse *bakki (and cognate with Old English *banc), denoting a natural slope of earth bordering a river, ditch, or road, or a raised shelf of elevated ground — [perception: essential geographical and agricultural boundary marker of rural landscape].
- Late Middle English (14th–15th c.) — Extended to artificial earthen ramparts, defensive fortifications, ditch dikes, and garden terraces designed for military protection or agricultural boundary demarcation — [perception: pragmatic earthwork engineered for security, property rights, and flood control].
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — Coincided with the borrowing of the separate homonym bank (financial repository, from Italian banca, "moneylender's bench"); meanwhile, the topographical sense expanded to vast maritime sand shoals ("The Grand Banks") and meteorological cloud formations ("a bank of fog") — [perception: stable topographical boundary existing alongside the new merchant banking institution].
- 19th Century Industrial & Railway Era — Deeply integrated into civil engineering to describe railway cuttings, canal dikes, and highway earthworks where cuttings were carved into hillside banks (as in Tolkien's road cutting at Bag End) — [perception: indispensable feature of industrial infrastructure and pastoral roadsides].
- 20th to 21st Century — Extended dynamically into aviation (banking an airplane along an inclined curve, mirroring an earthen banked roadway) and technology (banks of batteries, memory banks, banks of switches) — [perception: versatile foundational term spanning physical topography, aerodynamics, and digital architecture].
