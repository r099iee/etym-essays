- Late 13th c. (c. 1300) — Anglo-Norman importation: entered Middle English as grevous from Old French grief; originally used to describe oppressive physical burdens, exhausting toil, and ruinous feudal taxes.
- 14th–15th c. — Physical & emotional broadening: expanded into chivalric literature and religious homilies to denote agonizing physical wounds ("a grevous wounde") and deep, inconsolable grief.
- 16th c. (Reformation & Elizabethan era) — Liturgical and dramatic entrenchment: codified in Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer to express profound moral contrition ("grievous sins") and immortalized in Shakespearean tragedy ("a grievous fault" in Julius Caesar).
- 19th c. (Victorian era) — Criminal common law codification: formally established as a statutory term of art in the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 for Grievous Bodily Harm (GBH), denoting really serious bodily injury.
- 20th–21st c. — Formal legal and elevated literary register: maintained strictly as a marker of solemn gravity in appellate jurisprudence, historical assessments of tyrannical oppression, and poignant descriptions of irreparable loss.
