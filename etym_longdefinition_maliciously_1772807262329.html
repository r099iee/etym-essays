In the courtroom scene of Honglou meng, "The usher smiled maliciously." The adverb does not describe open violence; it colours a small social gesture with ill will. Malice is the desire to harm, humiliate, or take pleasure in another's discomfort. Here the smile is the delivery system: the usher knows something Yu-cun does not, and his amusement is edged with superiority and spite. In the literary context the single adverb exposes a power inversion — servant over magistrate — through hidden knowledge of a "monstrous" case. The tone is cold, knowing, and morally corrosive rather than explosively angry: a quiet, deliberate pleasure in the other's discomfiture. Core definition. Maliciously (adverb) means in a manner characterized by malice or ill will; with intent to do harm; deliberately, out of a wish to injure or cause distress. It is the adverbial form of malicious ("showing malice; done with intent to harm"), from the noun malice ("ill will, the desire to harm"). The adverb modifies actions, gestures, speech, or looks, marking them as motivated by ill intent: "smiled maliciously," "maliciously spread rumors," "maliciously damaged property." Polysemy and senses:
  • Moral-psychological sense (core): with ill will, with deliberate unkindness — the everyday sense covering hostile gestures, smiles, whispers, and actions.
  • Legal sense: in tort and criminal law, acting with wrongful intent or recklessness as to harm — maliciously damaged, malicious prosecution, malicious falsehood, malicious mischief. Here "malice" is a term of art for the mental element of certain wrongs.
  • Rhetorical / literary sense: signalling knowing, superior amusement at another's misfortune (Schadenfreude), as in "smiled maliciously" — the sense of the novel.
  • Technical-IT sense (recent): "maliciously crafted" — of files, code, or requests deliberately designed to harm or exploit, an extension of the legal-moral sense into computing.
The historical bridge: all senses run through the Latin malus "bad, evil." Malice names moral badness directed at others; maliciously marks the manner or motive of an act as flowing from that ill will. The legal sense formalises the everyday one: where the law punishes or compensates, it must identify when an act was done "with malice" — deliberately and with intent to harm — which is why the adverb is at home both in the courtroom and in the moralising novel. Synonym boundaries:
  • spitefully: similar in acting from ill will; differs because spitefully emphasizes petty personal resentment and retaliatory smallness, while maliciously can cover broader, more deliberate harm.
  • malevolently: similar in intending harm; differs because malevolently is heavier and more abstract or gothic ("wishing evil"), while maliciously can describe a specific knowing act or gesture.
  • viciously: similar in aggressive harm; differs because viciously stresses cruelty or a savage manner and often physical or verbal attack, not a composed, knowing smile.
  • wickedly: similar in moral badness; differs because wickedly can be playful or moralizing and lacks the precise legal-moral weight of maliciously.
  • nastily: similar in unkind manner; differs because nastily is colloquial and thinner, lacking the formal malice-of-intent component.
  • cruelly: similar in causing hurt; differs because cruelly stresses inflicting suffering, while maliciously stresses the ill-will motive even in small gestures.
Collocations and register. Common collocations: smiled / grinned / whispered maliciously, maliciously damaged, malicious prosecution, maliciously false, acted maliciously, malicious mischief, maliciously crafted (of files or code). Register spans law (where malice is a term of art for wrongful intent) and elevated narrative; in everyday speech it remains available but marked as stronger and more formal than meanly or nastily. In the novel, the collocation with smiled is especially damning: the face performs friendliness while the adverb convicts the motive — a single word that turns a servant's smile into evidence of deliberate ill will.
