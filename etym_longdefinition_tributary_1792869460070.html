Tributary names, in its primary modern sense, a stream or river that flows into a larger one, feeding it with its waters: the brook into the river, the river into the Mississippi, the thousand springs into the one Road.

In the source sentence an old saying holds that there is only one Road, like a great river, with its springs at every doorstep and every path its tributary. The metaphor turns geography into philosophy: every lane, errand, and wandering footpath pours its travellers into the single current of the journey, just as every hillside rill pours into the valley river, and no starting point stands outside the watershed of the Road.

Core meaning in detail: Hydrologists distinguish the main stem from its tributaries by discharge and length, mapping dendritic networks where first-order brooks join into mightier orders until the trunk river reaches the sea. A tributary keeps its name to the confluence and loses it after, the Cherwell remaining itself until it marries the Thames at Oxford, then sailing onward under Thames's name. The word takes the smaller stream's point of view with quiet pride: to be tributary is to contribute, to belong to something greater without ceasing to be oneself.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Feeder stream (primary modern sense): any brook, river, or rill joining a greater flow. The bridge is Rome's tribute-word turned to water: as subject peoples paid tributum into the treasury, so lesser streams pay their waters into the trunk river, contribution made geography.
  • Tributary state or nation (historical): a people paying tribute to a greater power, as the khanates to China or the border kingdoms to Rome. The bridge is the original Latin sense, tributarius, liable to tribute: politics before rivers, the word's birthright meaning.
  • Tributary payments and gifts: tribute itself rendered as adjective, tributary offerings, tributary praise. The bridge keeps the money-sense alive inside ceremony: flowers laid at a memorial are a tributary stream of honour.
  • Anatomy — tributary veins (medical): small veins emptying into larger trunks on the way to the heart. The bridge is the body as watershed: Harvey's circulation mapped blood like rivers, and the metaphor has perfused textbooks ever since.
  • Figurative contributors (literary): tributary paths, tributary ideas, tributary notes feeding a biography. The bridge is the source sentence itself: anything that pours itself into a greater current earns the name, and essayists, preachers, and fantasy guides have drawn on it ever since Rome.

Register and connotation: tributary is dignified and generous — atlases, histories, memorial addresses — flattering the small with purpose and the great with gratitude. Rivers gain majesty from counting their tributaries, a thousand named givers; small streams gain meaning from the trunk they serve. The old political sense could sting, marking subjection, but the river sense has washed it nearly clean: today to call a path or an idea tributary is to praise its contribution, every doorstep's spring acknowledged as the great river's wealth.
