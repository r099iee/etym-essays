- Late Antiquity (4th to 6th Century) - Formed in Late Latin patristic theology (*interminabilis*) by Church Fathers (Tertullian, Augustine, Boethius); utilized in an exalted, positive metaphysical register to define divine eternity and the boundless, limitless life of God.
- 12th to 14th Century - Emerges in Old French and enters Middle English (notably in Chaucer’s translation of Boethius, c. 1380); retains its formal philosophical definition of divine boundlessness while gradually appearing in scholastic disputes.
- 15th to 16th Century - Spreads into Early Modern English literature and humanistic rhetoric; begins acquiring hyperbolic rhetorical usage to describe tedious human disputes, protracted legal litigations, and endless ecclesiastical debates.
- 17th to 18th Century - Completely solidifies its modern pejorative register of temporal weariness and exasperation in Enlightenment prose, travel diaries, and polite essays (Swift, Johnson, Gibbon), describing boring lectures, long roads, and endless ceremonies.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes a ubiquitous staple of realist fiction (Dickens, Eliot, Melville) to dramatize bureaucratic labyrinths (the Chancery court in *Bleak House*) and sprawling landscapes.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues in modern literary criticism, existentialist literature, and everyday discourse; famously employed by Proustian scholars to describe the physical "interminable strips" of corrected galleys and the vast, cyclical temporal design of *In Search of Lost Time*.
