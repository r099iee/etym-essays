The rootWord LACQUER-WORK is a compound of two elements, each with its own derivation chain. No PIE ancestry is attested for the first element (an Indo-Aryan Wanderwort); the second element is native Germanic from PIE. CHAIN 1 — the LACQUER element (non-PIE, Indo-Aryan → Westward trade route): Sanskrit lakṣā (लक्षा) “lac; the red resinous secretion of the lac insect” (attested in classical Sanskrit; precise dating uncertain, pre-medieval) → Persian lāk (لاک) “lac, the red gum” (borrowed from Sanskrit along trade routes; date uncertain, medieval) → Arabic lakk (لَكّ) “lac” (loan from Persian; medieval) → Portuguese laca, lacre “lac; sealing-wax” and Spanish laca (16th c., via Indian Ocean and overland trade) → French laque “lacquer”, with older form lacre (16th c., via Mediterranean trade) → English lacquer “a kind of varnish” (1570s, via obsolete French lacre); English lac “resinous substance” (c. 1660); English lake “crimson pigment” (1610s, via French laque) → English compound lacquer + work (the craft and its objects; compound coined in English) CHAIN 2 — the WORK element (PIE, native Germanic): PIE *wérǵom “work, labour” (from the root *werǵ- “to do, work”; reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *werką “work” (reconstructed) → Old English weorc, worc “work, deed, labour” (attested from c. 8th c.) → Middle English werk, work “work, labour, deed” (12th–15th c.) → English work “activity involving effort; the product of labour” (modern) → English compound lacquer + work = LACQUER-WORK “objects coated in lacquer, or the craft of coating them” (compound attested from the 17th–18th c., when Asian lacquer ware entered English collections) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The traditional pairing of Sanskrit lakṣā “red dye/resin” with lakṣa “one hundred thousand” (whence English lakh) rests on the vast swarms of lac insects needed for the dye; modern scholars treat this as folk etymology and note the two senses may be independent developments. (2) The origin of Sanskrit lakṣā itself, beyond Sanskrit, is unverified (possibly a Dravidian or substrate term); never map this word to a PIE root. (3) The exact Old/Middle English date at which the textile sense of work (in lacquer-work, i.e. the “-work” of craft) crystallised is not separately attested; the compound follows the general pattern of craft nouns in -work (needlework, woodwork).
