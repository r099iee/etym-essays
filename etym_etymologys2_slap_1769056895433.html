Single linear chain from the earliest reconstructable form to the present rootWord slap: 1. Imitative Germanic base *slapp- (sound-symbolic; no Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed) — "a flat, sharp blow; the sound of an open hand striking a surface." The family is best treated as echoic rather than inherited. 2. Middle Low German slappe / Middle Dutch slappe (14th century) — "a blow, a cuff, a slap." The continental imitative form that supplied English. 3. Middle English slappe / sclappe (early 14th–15th centuries) — noun, "a blow, a smack, a cuff." Borrowed into English from the Low German/Dutch form. 4. Early Modern English slap, noun (16th century) — "a blow with the open hand." The verb to slap, "to strike with the open hand," is recorded from c. 1600, derived from the noun. 5. English slap, adverb (1670s) — "suddenly, directly, full upon, exactly at/against" — the sense of this card, in "slap up against the gate." The adverbial use is a conversion from the noun/verb: because a slap makes immediate, complete contact, the word came to mean exactly that contact in space. Emphatic compounds built on it include slap-bang (1786) and slapdash (1670s). Note on nuance/debate: the base *slapp- is imitative and therefore has no derivational history before it; its continental relatives (German Schlappe, "defeat, setback," earlier "a slap, a blow") show the same base specializing in parallel but independent directions. What is attested and certain is the English chain from Middle English slappe to the modern adverb—all stages beyond the base itself are securely recorded.
