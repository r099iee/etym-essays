Core sense. Imperishable means "not perishable": that which cannot decay, die, rot, be destroyed, or pass away. It is the negative of perishable (liable to spoil or die) formed with the Latin negative prefix in- ("not"). The word is most at home in elevated, literary, and solemn contexts: imperishable fame, imperishable songs, imperishable memory, imperishable glory — things that outlast death and time itself.

1. Primary senses.

  • Of abstract things (dominant use): fame, honor, memory, love, art, truth, the soul — "incapable of being forgotten or extinguished". An "imperishable song" is one that will be sung forever; an "imperishable name" is one that will never be erased. This is the sense in the source context: the Master of Lake-town declares the dragon-slayer "worthy of many imperishable songs" — songs so durable that no age will let them die.
  • Of material things: physically indestructible or decay-proof — stone, bronze, gold, certain plastics; here "imperishable" is a near-synonym of "indestructible" and "durable".
  • Of food and supplies (practical jargon): in retail, logistics, and emergency management, "imperishable" (usually as the noun "imperishables" or the synonym "non-perishables") means food that keeps without refrigeration — canned, dried, salted, or preserved goods. A disaster-relief appeal asks for "imperishable food items". This sense is a 20th-century narrowing of the material sense.
  • Theological and philosophical use: the soul, the spirit, or the divine is imperishable — incapable of corruption or annihilation. Here the word overlaps with "incorruptible" and "immortal"; the King James Bible speaks of the resurrection body as "incorruptible" (1 Corinthians 15), the religious register of the same idea.
  • Rhetorical/literary sense: "imperishable" also means "so valuable or beautiful that it deserves to last forever" — a value judgment as much as a prediction: an imperishable masterpiece, an imperishable achievement. The word says: this deserves immortality.

2. Semantic bridge. Perish (via Old French, from Latin perīre, "to go through, pass away, die") gave perishable, "liable to pass away or spoil". Negating it with in- produces "not liable to pass away". The word was born, in the early 19th century, precisely when Romanticism was busy declaring that art, fame, and love transcend death; its early life is almost entirely in that idealistic register. Only later did it descend into warehouses (imperishable goods) and relief lists (imperishable foods), while keeping its poetic glow for songs and memories.

3. In translation and religion. Where the King James Bible says "incorruptible" (1 Corinthians 15, of the resurrection body), later translators reaching for the same idea have used "imperishable"; in comparative religion and philosophy the word renders Sanskrit akṣara ("imperishable, indestructible" — an epithet of the highest reality) and Greek áphthartos ("incorruptible"). In every case the core image is the same: something that no process of decay, time, or destruction can touch.

4. Register and collocations. Literary, formal, solemn; common collocations: imperishable fame/glory, imperishable memory, imperishable songs, imperishable soul, imperishable foodstuffs. It is rarer than everlasting or undying in speech; writers reach for it when they want weight and dignity. The word-family: perish, perishable, nonperishable, imperishability (the noun), and the intensifier use of perishing ("perishing cold"). Note the register split: "imperishable" applied to fire, hell, or wrath is bleak; applied to a person's inner qualities it is almost always admiring.
