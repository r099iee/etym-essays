Ceylon is the former European name for the island now called Sri Lanka, and its story runs from an ancient Indian lion legend through Portuguese, Dutch and British colonial reshaping. The root lies in Sanskrit Siṃhala, traditionally analysed as siṃha lion plus suffix -la or alia abode, meaning dwelling-place of lions or lion-people. Pali chronicles call the island Sihala or Tambapanni, copper-palmed, and trace the Sinhalese to Prince Vijaya, said to be descended from a lion, giving the lion flag that still flies today. Linguists note that siṃha itself has no secure Indo-European etymology and may be a borrowing from a Central Asian or Dravidian word for lion, fitting because lions never inhabited the island and the lion here is totemic and royal rather than faunal.

Greek Taprobane and Latin Taprobana, from Pali Tambapanni via Greek transmission, was the classical name used by Ptolemy and Pliny. Arabic traders called it Serendib or Sarandib, from Sanskrit Suvarnadvipa or Siṃhaladvipa, giving English serendipity via Walpole. When the Portuguese under Lourenco de Almeida reached the island in 1505, they adapted local Sinhala Sinhale or Sihala into Ceilao, spelled Ceilão with nasal diphthong. The Dutch, who seized coastal forts in 1658, wrote Ceilon or Ceylon, and the English, who took the island in 1796 and made it a crown colony in 1802, standardised Ceylon.

  • Lion legend: Sanskrit siṃha to Siṃhala creates an ethnic and island name from a royal beast emblem, blending mythic ancestry with territorial identity.
  • Pali transmission: Siṃhala to Pali Sihala shows Middle Indo-Aryan de-aspiration and simplification, the form used in Buddhist chronicles.
  • Classical detour: Tambapanni to Greek Taprobane shows how the island had parallel copper-land and lion-land names in antiquity.
  • Arabic bridge: Siṃhaladvipa to Serendib shows Persian-Arabic phonetic reshaping and the route by which the island entered Western romance vocabulary.
  • Portuguese refashioning: Sinhala Sinhale to Ceilao shows Lusitanian nasalisation and loss of initial sibilant cluster.
  • Dutch-English fixing: Ceilao to Ceilon to Ceylon shows northern European spelling with C-e-y and anglicised pronunciation with initial s.
  • Post-colonial replacement: Ceylon to Sri Lanka in 1972, meaning resplendent island, restores a Sanskrit-derived indigenous official name.

Phonetically Portuguese Ceilao, pronounced say-lao with nasal ending, became Dutch say-lon and English si-lon or say-lon, spelled Ceylon with ey for the long a-i diphthong. The spelling C- rather than S- reflects Portuguese orthography. Semantically the word narrowed from an ethnic label for lion-people to a purely geographic and then colonial-administrative label for the whole island and its products.

Culturally Ceylon accumulated layers of prestige. Portuguese Ceylao meant cinnamon, the island's fabled spice. Dutch Ceylon meant fortified cinnamon monopoly and elephant trade. British Ceylon, 1796 to 1948 colony and 1948 to 1972 dominion, meant tea, Ceylon tea becoming a global brand, sapphires, and a model crown colony with Kandy, Colombo and Galle. Writers from Chekhov to Leonard Woolf evoke Ceylon as tropical Eden and imperial burden. After independence in 1948 and the republic in 1972, Sri Lanka replaced Ceylon officially, yet Ceylon tea, Bank of Ceylon and Ceylon sapphire preserve the old name in commerce. In older histories like the source sentence, Veddahs of Ceylon simply locates the people in the familiar colonial geography contemporary readers knew.
