Dyak (modern standard Dayak) is an exonym built from an Austronesian directional. Proto-Austronesian *daya meant toward the interior, upriver, away from the sea — reconstructed by Blust and Trussel in the Austronesian Comparative Dictionary from reflexes across Taiwan, Philippines and Borneo (e.g. Kenyah daya upstream, Lun Dayeh people of the upper rivers). Proto-Malayo-Polynesian retained *daya as inland. In coastal Brunei and Melanau Malay this became daya (land, interior) and dayak (up-country, interior people), applied by Muslim coastal Malays to the non-Muslim riverine and hill peoples upstream, without reference to any single tribe.

Dutch colonial ethnography in Kalimantan fixed Dajak in the early 1800s for administrative classification; English travellers and missionaries borrowed it as Dyak/Dayak, first recorded 1830–40, Merriam-Webster 1836. Collins still glosses Malay dayak as savage / upcountry from darat land, preserving the pejorative coastal view. Pronunciation settled as /ˈdaɪæk/ (also /ˈdaɪək/), plural Dyaks or collective Dyak.

  • Pre-colonial: Directional noun: Daya simply means upriver. To call someone orang daya is to say upriver person, like highlander. No ethnic content.
  • 1830–1900: Colonial umbrella: James Brooke (White Rajah of Sarawak), Dutch officials and missionaries use Dyak for 200+ groups — Iban (Sea Dyak), Bidayuh (Land Dyak), Kayan, Kenyah, Ngaju, Murut — united only by being non-Malay, non-Muslim interior longhouse dwellers, rice farmers, hunters, once headhunters. Travel books sensationalize Dyak headhunting, longhouses for 100 families, and omen-bird religion, fixing a fierce-savage stereotype.
  • 1900–1960: Anthropological refinement: Scholars stress Dyak has no precise tribal meaning (Britannica, EBSCO). Each river has its own dialect, law (adat), and territory. Iban migration, Kayan stratification into chiefs/commoners/captives, and conversion to Anglicanism/Catholicism diversify the picture.
  • 1960–present: Reclaimed identity: In Indonesian Kalimantan Dayak becomes a proud pan-ethnic political identity (Dayak Unity Party, Dayak customary councils) versus coastal Malay dominance; in Malaysian Sarawak/Sabah it narrows locally to Iban + Bidayuh. Spelling Dayak displaces Dyak/Dajak. Population ~2.2 million on Borneo across three nations.

The semantic drift is thus deictic → social → political: from where you live (upstream) to what you are not (not coastal Muslim) to who you choose to be (Indigenous Bornean). No PIE is involved; the Austronesian root *daya is the ultimate etymon. The beeswax barter vignette in the source — a Dyak wandering a bazaar with wax seeking profitable exchange — captures the 19th-century colonial-economic gaze: interior producer entering coastal Malay/Chinese markets where forest products (beeswax, rattan, hornbill ivory) meet salt, cloth, guns and beads in non-monetary rates that textbooks use to illustrate primitive money.

Literary attestations include Conrad's Borneo tales, Somerset Maugham, and countless missionary memoirs where Dyaks appear as naked-to-the-waist warriors in war-coats. Modern usage prefers Dayak; Dyak survives in older books and in the prompt's early-20th-century economics text (likely Beveridge/Withers-style money history citing bazaar barter).
