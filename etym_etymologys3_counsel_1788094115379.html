Counsel is one of the great Norman-French imports into English — the vocabulary of government, law and wisdom that the Conquest carried across the Channel. Its Latin ancestry, however, is older than Rome's empire, and the details of that ancestry are a famous puzzle in Latin etymology.

The Latin base. The word descends from Latin consilium, “deliberation, consultation, advice, plan, judgment”. Consilium is a derivative of the verb consulere, “to consult, to deliberate, to take counsel” — the same verb that gave Rome its consul, the magistrate whose very name means “the one who takes counsel”. In classical usage consilium is a word of public and private wisdom alike: the Senate deliberates in consilium, a general holds a council of war (consilium habere), and Cicero, the great theorist of deliberation, uses the word constantly for reasoned judgment as opposed to impulse. Plautus and Terence already attest the verb in the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE.

The debated etymology. The origin of consulere is genuinely uncertain, and honest dictionaries say so. The most widely repeated proposal derives it from com- “together” plus a root *sel- “to take, to gather” — making it “to gather (thoughts) together” — and connects the root with Greek heleîn “to take, seize”. A rival view ties the verb to sedēre “to sit”: to “sit together” in deliberation, which would make consulere a cousin of considere “to sit down”. Neither etymology commands universal assent, and some handbooks decline to reconstruct beyond Latin itself. The chain given in etymologyS2 therefore marks the PIE stage as proposed and debated rather than established.

The French centuries. Latin consilium developed in Gallo-Romance into Old French conseil — the form shows the palatalisation of Latin -li- before a vowel, so that consilium becomes conseil with the familiar French -eil ending (compare soleil from soliculum, sommeil from somniculum). The word entered English in the 12th century, after the Norman Conquest, as conseil, later counsel, covering advice, deliberation, plan and intention. It is a word of the court and the castle: in medieval romance and chronicle, kings take counsel, keep counsel, and are undone by bad counsel. The Romance cognates — Spanish consejo, Italian consiglio, Portuguese conselho — preserve the same Latin base across the whole of western Europe.

The great split: counsel and council. A second Latin word, concilium “assembly, meeting” (from com- + calāre “to call”, so literally “a calling together”), also passed into Old French, where its form converged with conseil. Medieval English therefore had one sound and one spelling, “counseil”, doing duty for both “advice” and “assembly”. From the 14th century the spelling began to differentiate: council for the body of people (and for the Church's ecumenical councils), counsel for the advice and the adviser. The distinction was firmly fixed by the 17th century and remains a classic spelling trap today — and the source passage's “this counsel” plays exactly on the older, unified world in which the word had not yet split.

The legal sense. Because the word meant both “advice” and “adviser”, the law adopted it early: the barrister who advises and speaks for a client is “counsel”. English legal usage treats the word as both singular and plural — “counsel for the defence”, “two counsel appeared” — and the American Sixth Amendment's guarantee of “the Assistance of Counsel for his defence” (1791) gave the word constitutional rank. In British usage, “King's Counsel” (KC, formerly Queen's Counsel) is an honour conferred on senior barristers, the modern descendant of the medieval serjeants-at-law.

Religious and literary life. The religious register is equally deep. The Authorized Version names Christ “Wonderful, Counsellor” (Isaiah 9:6), and Catholic moral theology distinguishes the “evangelical counsels” — poverty, chastity and obedience — the higher, optional perfections advised beyond the commandments; from this idea English formed the idiom “a counsel of perfection”, advice so ideal it is practically impossible. Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde is almost a treatise on counsel: the whole tragedy turns on counsels given, kept and betrayed, and the poet repeatedly urges his readers to keep their “conseil” secret. Shakespeare's characters give, take and keep counsel on every page.

The modern turn. In the 20th century the verb took a new direction. “Counselling” ceased to mean merely giving advice and came to name the professional practice of helping people through personal, emotional and psychological difficulties — the movement associated with figures such as Carl Rogers from the 1940s onward. A counsellor today is more likely a therapist than a barrister. The noun meanwhile remains a pillar of legal language, and in literary prose of the kind the source passage exemplifies, “counsel” still carries the full medieval weight of deliberation and wisdom.
