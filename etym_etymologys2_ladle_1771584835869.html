PIE *kleh₂- / *klā- "to put, lay out, spread out flat, arrange" (reconstructed; the root of laying and arranging, c. 4th millennium BCE) -> Proto-Germanic *hlathana- / *hladana- "to load, heap up, stack" (c. 5th–1st c. BCE; attested in all branches of Germanic — Old Norse hlaða "to pile up, to lade a ship", Old Saxon hladan, Old High German hladen, Gothic hlaþan) -> Old English hladan "to load, heap up, burden; to draw up water" (attested from the 8th c.; uniquely in English the verb also meant "to draw up water", to fill a vessel by scooping) -> Old English hlædel "a thing for lading; a scoop, ladle" (late Old English; formed from hladan + the instrumental suffix -el, the same suffix that makes a tool-name from an action-name; glosses Latin antlia "a pump, scoop for drawing water", 10th–11th c.) -> Middle English ladel "ladle, a scoop for drawing liquid" (12th–15th c.; the form without h-, h-dropping in onset clusters) -> English LADLE (modern form, from the 15th c.) Note on the root: the equation of the Germanic "lade" family with PIE *kleh₂- is standard in the handbooks, but the Old Norse and Gothic evidence supports a simpler vocalism, and some scholars reconstruct a Germanic-only root *hlaþ- without committing to a precise PIE ancestor. Latin clādēs "destruction, calamity" is sometimes printed beside this family, but modern scholarship (de Vaan) analyses it with the "strike" root *kelh₂-; the connection is therefore disputed and best set aside. The English sense development is unusual: alone among the Germanic languages, Old English used the "load" verb also for drawing up water, and it is precisely that sense that produced the tool-name hlædel. No PIE stage beyond *kleh₂- can be securely reconstructed for the -el suffix, whose origin is a Germanic instrumental morpheme.
