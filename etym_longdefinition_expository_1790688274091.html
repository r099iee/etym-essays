Expository is an adjective meaning relating to, or intended to explain, describe, or inform — setting forth facts, ideas, or procedures clearly and systematically. “Forms of expository discourse” means genres of writing and speech whose purpose is explanation: textbooks, manuals, news reports, scholarly summaries, and lectures, as opposed to stories that entertain or arguments that persuade.

Central senses in current use:

  • Rhetorical / pedagogical (core sense): designed to explain or inform. An expository essay analyzes, defines, classifies, or compares; an expository paragraph has a topic sentence, evidence, and explanation. Teachers contrast it with narrative (telling a story), descriptive (painting a scene), and persuasive/argumentative (convincing). Example: “an expository passage on how vaccines work.” Neutral in tone, prized for clarity, accuracy, and logical order.
  • Theological / homiletic: (expository sermon, expository preaching) working systematically through a biblical or other authoritative text, verse by verse, drawing out meaning. Evangelical churches advertise “expository ministry” to signal fidelity to scripture rather than topical moralizing. Register is insider-ecclesial; outside it the term can sound technical.
  • Legal / formal: occasionally, an expository statement or affidavit is one that lays out facts without argument. Drafters speak of the “expository part” of a judgment (recital of facts) versus the ratio decidendi. Rare in general prose but alive in legal stylistics.
  • Musical / narratological extension: critics borrow the term to describe passages that establish background rather than advance plot — “an expository scene,” “expository dialogue” in film (characters telling each other what the audience needs to know). Often mildly pejorative: “too expository” means clunky info-dumping, telling instead of showing.

Nuances and near-boundaries:

  • Expository vs. explanatory: near-synonyms, but explanatory stresses making intelligible (an explanatory note), while expository stresses systematic setting-forth as a genre or mode. All expository writing is explanatory in aim, but not every casual explanation is expository discourse.
  • Expository vs. expositive: variant forms; expositive is rarer and more formal, sometimes preferred in theology (“expositive preaching”). Most style guides accept both but expository dominates in education.
  • Expository vs. didactic: expository aims to inform; didactic aims to instruct morally. A manual is expository without being didactic; a fable is didactic without being purely expository. Confusing them makes neutral information sound preachy.
  • Connotation drift: because schools associate exposition with exams and rubrics, expository can imply dryness (“a flat expository style”) versus vivid or playful prose. The source sentence exploits this: purpose enters through expository forms, but style is more than information-delivery; persuasion, play, and display also drive language.

In short, expository labels the informative pole of language. To call discourse expository is to say its contract with the reader is “I will make this clear and orderly,” whether the text is a recipe, a textbook, a sermon through Romans, or the opening crawl of a film that sets out who is who.
