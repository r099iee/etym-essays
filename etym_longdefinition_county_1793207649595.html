County is, in its essential modern sense, a large district into which a country or state is divided for government: the unit that organises courts, police, roads, rates, and records between the parish or town below and the nation or state above. In the source sentence Cobbett rides through Surrey and declares that this county of Surrey presents a greater contrast to the traveller’s eye than any other county in England — rich Thames-side meadows at one end and bleak heath at the other — using the word in its classic English sense of the historic shire, with its own character, pride, and politics.

Core meaning in detail: The English county is the old shire under its Norman name: Surrey, Kent, Yorkshire, each with a county town, and in later centuries a county council, a county constabulary, and a county cricket club to fly its flag. Across the Atlantic the same word built America’s local map: nearly every state is tiled with counties — Los Angeles County, Cook County — which run the courts, the jails, the land registry, and the sheriff’s office. The machinery differs, but the idea is constant: the county is the piece of the country small enough to be governed and large enough to matter, the middle layer where the citizen actually meets the state.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The English historic shire (primary British sense, from the 13th c.): the county of Surrey, a Surrey man, county families. The bridge is conquest: the Normans renamed the Anglo-Saxon shire a county because it was, or resembled, the domain of a count — and the French name stuck to the English soil for eight centuries.
  • The American unit of local government (primary US sense, from the 17th c.): Orange County, county seat, county clerk, county sheriff. The bridge is colonisation: English settlers carried the county system to Virginia and Massachusetts, and the republic tiled the continent with it until Texas alone holds 254.
  • The domain of a count (original feudal sense, now historical): the territory ruled by a count or earl, as a dukedom is ruled by a duke. The bridge is the root itself: Latin comes, companion of the prince, became the title count, and his comitatus became the county.
  • The county as institution — jail, court, hospital (metonymic, chiefly US): a night in county, county hospital, to take it to county court. The bridge is metonymy: the building where county justice and county charity happen lends its name to the punishment and the cure.
  • Compounds of pride and pastime (modern British): county town, county set, county cricket, the county show. The bridge is identity: the county is the largest unit English people feel affection for, so its name stamps the team, the fair, and the social circle — to be county is to be rooted, established, and faintly grand.
  • County lines (sinister modern slang, 21st c.): the drug-supply routes running from cities into provincial towns. The bridge is grimly literal: gangs carrying drugs across county boundaries are named for the lines they cross, and the police term became the crime’s own title.

Register and connotation: county is solid, respectable, faintly nostalgic English — the vocabulary of maps, signposts, and cricket commentators. Cobbett wields it as a unit of comparison and complaint, weighing shire against shire the way a farmer weighs beasts at market. In America it is plainer and more official: the county is where you pay your property tax, serve your jury duty, and — if unlucky — spend your night in the cells. Few words wear their history so quietly: every county still whispers count, the Frankish warlord whose riding became Everyman’s address.
