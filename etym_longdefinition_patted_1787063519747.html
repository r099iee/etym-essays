PATTED is the past tense and past participle of the verb pat, meaning to touch or strike lightly and gently with the flat of the hand or something flat, and, by extension, to shape, flatten, or press something with such gentle repeated taps. In the supplied context - "they praised him and patted him on the back" - the verb is used in its idiomatic sense: the gesture of tapping someone on the back as a sign of approval, congratulation, or encouragement, and in the same breath as the figurative "pat on the back" (praise). The dwarves' gesture is at once physical and symbolic - they physically pat Bilbo's back and symbolically congratulate his success.

Primary senses

  • To tap gently with the flat of the hand: a light, usually repeated and rhythmic touch meant to soothe, comfort, show affection, or call attention. "She patted the child's head." "He patted the dog."
  • To shape or flatten by patting: to press loose material into a flat or compact form with the hand - patting butter into a mould, patting dough or a snowball into shape. "Pat the meat into four flat burgers."
  • To pat down / on: in modern idiom, "to pat down" is to search a person's body with flat hand-passes (airport security); "to pat on the back" is to praise or congratulate.

Secondary, idiomatic, and extended senses

  • To pat (someone) on the back - to praise: the fixed gesture of congratulation, transferred from the action to the meaning. "They pat him on the back and promote him."
  • To pat oneself on the back - to self-congratulate: often with a hint of smugness: "He pats himself on the back for his restraint."
  • To pat (something) into place or down: gentle pressing for grooming or arrangement - patting down hair, patting a pocket (feeling for contents by tapping). "He patted his pockets for the keys."
  • Of rain or sounds (poetic): "the rain patted against the window" - light, repeated soft impacts; a natural extension of the verb to non-human agents.
  • Rare causative of motion: "to pat (someone) on the way" - to send off with a light tap (now rare).

Semantic reasoning and historical bridges

The word's history is short and physical. The verb "pat" (late 16th century) is almost certainly imitative - the sound of a light blow - and sits in a family of English sound-words ("tap", "rap", "clip", "flap") that echo their action. From "strike lightly" the word developed the sense "soothe by light strokes" (17th century), and from "stroke" it developed "shape by pressing" (18th-19th century, in cookery and craft). The idiomatic back-pat - the gesture of approval - is attested from the mid-19th century, and the fully figurative "a pat on the back" (praise) from the 1880s. Each stage is a small step along a path of increasing softness: the word's whole career moves from a blow toward a caress and then toward an abstract blessing.

Register and nuance

  • Neutral and common in literal uses (animals, children, comfort, cookery).
  • Warm and approving in the idiom "pat on the back".
  • Slightly condescending in "pat yourself on the back" if overused - self-congratulation is viewed with mild suspicion in modern workplace culture.
  • The participle "patted" in narrative prose is almost always tender, approving, or practical - never violent.
  • In Tolkien, "patted him on the back" folds the physical gesture into the dwarves' grand, comic promises ("put themselves and all their families for generations to come at his service") - the small warm pat contrasting with the enormous formal pledge.
