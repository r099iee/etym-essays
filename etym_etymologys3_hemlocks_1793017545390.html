The most remarkable thing about the word hemlock is that nobody knows where it comes from. For a plant that killed Socrates and haunted two thousand years of literature, its English name stands oddly alone: Old English hemlic and hymlice have no certain cognates in any other Germanic language, no agreed division into parts, and no accepted Proto-Indo-European root. Scholars have guessed at compounds and lost elements, but the responsible verdict remains unknown origin, a small famous island in the lexicon.

The Greek shadow: The plant's fame, paradoxically, comes from a language its name never touched. The Athenians executed Socrates in 399 BCE with kōneion, a draught of poison hemlock, described by Plato with clinical calm: numbness rising from the feet, the cup-bearer watching, the philosopher's last joke about owing a cock to Asclepius. When English writers from Chaucer onward moralised about hemlock, they meant their own ditch weed but borrowed Athens for gravity. The word thus carries two histories at once: a native English name of unknown birth, and a classical death that gave it meaning.

Herbals, witches, and doctors:

  • Anglo-Saxon leechbooks: remedies already warn and prescribe, hemlock poultices for swellings alongside cautions that the plant is stark poison within.
  • Medieval herbals: the plant joins the cold poisons, opposed to hot venoms, prescribed in minute ointment doses and feared in every other.
  • Tudor murder literature: pamphlets and plays make hemlock the poison of choice for secret killing, crowned by the witches' cauldron, where hemlock digged in the dark seasons the broth.
  • Enlightenment medicine: Viennese physicians experiment with measured coniine preparations against tumours and spasms, reviving the Greek idea of poison as tool.
  • Modern toxicology: coniine is isolated and its ascending paralysis mapped, and hemlock becomes the textbook example of mistaken identity among foragers.

The American namesake: In the 1700s colonists noticed that the crushed foliage of the great eastern conifer Tsuga canadensis smelled faintly like the Old World poison weed, and transferred the gloomy name to a harmless timber tree. The tanning industry then made hemlock bark famous: its tannins cured leather by the million hides, and whole mountainsides were stripped. So the same word came to mean, across one ocean, the ditch poison of Socrates, and across another, the shade tree of cathedral forests and the foundation of an industry.

Sound and spelling: From hemlic through hemlok and humlok to hemlock, the word's music barely changes: the heavy first syllable, the swallowed second. The modern -lock spelling invites false parsing as a lock that bars, and poets have been glad of the accident, but no authority derives it from any locking. Sometimes a spelling is just a coincidence that poetry adopts.

Why the mystery endures: Plant names of the deep countryside often predate literacy and leave no paper trail; hemlic may be a fragment of a lost compound whose other half died with the dialect that coined it. The obscurity suits the plant: a killer with lookalikes for cousins and a nameless past, recognised everywhere and explained nowhere, fading each summer on spotted stems exactly as the source sentence finds it.
