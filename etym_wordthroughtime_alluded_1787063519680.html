- 1530s - English adopts allude from Latin alludere; the past form alluded first means “jested at, mocked, made sport of” — a hostile, playful sense.
- Late 16th c. - The sense shifts under French influence toward “referred to indirectly, touched upon in passing”; alluded loses its mocking edge and becomes a verb of polite or sly reference.
- 17th-18th c. - Grammarians fix the collocation allude to and sharply distinguish allude (indirect) from mention and refer (direct); the verb becomes a precise tool of rhetoric and criticism.
- 19th c. - In the age of the novel and of classical education, alluded thrives in literary criticism; to be “alluded to” suggests cultural weight, since only well-known texts and figures can be pointed at obliquely.
- 20th-21st c. - Alluded is common in journalism, academia, and diplomacy, often with an evasive or delicate tone (“she alluded to the rumours”); the verb’s old jesting colour survives only in the noun allusion and the adjective allusive.
