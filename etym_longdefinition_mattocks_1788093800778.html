Mattocks are heavy two-handed digging tools consisting of a long wooden handle and an iron or steel head mounted at right angles to the shaft, the working face being a broad, flat, adze-like blade; on many designs the opposite end carries a second, narrower blade or a short pick-point. The mattock is swung in a chopping arc so that the blade bites vertically into the ground; its weight and leverage allow it to break up hard, stony, or root-bound soil that a spade cannot penetrate, to grub out tree stumps and roots, and to cut through turf and clay.

Primary sense — agriculture, building, and excavation: In husbandry the mattock is the classic tool of land clearance: it lifts turf, severs roots, breaks clods, and opens trenches in gravel and boulder clay. Ditchers, hedgers, grave-diggers, road-makers, miners, and archaeologists have all used it; in archaeology it survives as a standard excavation tool, heavier than the trowel and gentler than the pick. The ancient distinction, still observed in tool catalogues, is that a pickaxe has two pointed ends for breaking rock, whereas a mattock has at least one broad cutting blade for grubbing and cutting.

Secondary senses and subtypes:

  • Grubbing (or cutting) mattock: both ends bladed (an adze blade at the front, a narrower axe-like blade at the back), made for root-cutting and turf-work; also called a "grub axe" in some regional dialects and catalogues.
  • Pick mattock: an adze blade at one end and a pick-point at the other, for alternating digging and rock-breaking — the nearest relative of the pickaxe, and the form most easily pressed into service as a weapon or pry-bar.
  • War and improvised use: heavy-headed tools of this class have repeatedly been drafted as weapons; in Tolkien's The Hobbit the dwarves of the Iron Hills fight with mattocks at the Battle of Five Armies, and the historical analog is the siege and fortification work of medieval armies, where agricultural heads doubled as pikes or picks when iron was scarce.
  • Figurative use: rare but real: "to mattock something out" means to hack away at a problem or obstacle by brute, sustained effort; the tool's name can stand metonymically for relentless heavy labour, as in the pairing "axes and mattocks" for the whole kit of rough ground-work.

The plural form and its semantic bridge: the source context uses the plural "mattocks", the plain instrumental-collective plural of the tool word — it denotes a squad's set of such implements (as in medieval estate inventories and in biblical English, where "axe and mattock" appear as paired equipment). Nothing shifts in sense; the plural simply stretches the tool word from one implement to a batch of them, which is exactly how plural tool words work in English.

Register and neighbours: the word is a plain, workaday lexeme — neither slang nor poetic — that reads today as rural, traditional, and pleasantly archaic in prose, which is precisely why fantasy and historical authors reach for it. Distinguish it from the hoe (light, often one-handed, blade set at an angle for shallow weeding), the spade (foot-driven, blade in line with the shaft), the shovel (for lifting loose material), and the adze (blade set crosswise but used on wood, swung downward), and from the pickaxe, which is all points and no blade.

Literary and biblical salience: the King James Bible (1 Samuel 13:20) fixed the word in English memory as an emblem of hard agrarian labour — "all the Israelites went down to the Philistines, to sharpen every man his share, and his coulter, and his axe, and his mattock" — and Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) codifies it plainly as "a kind of instrument to break the ground with".
