Impassioned is the adjective of burning earnestness: filled with passion, animated by intense feeling, and uttered or held with fervent conviction. An impassioned speech shakes the room; an impassioned defender refuses detachment; and in the source sentence the absence of impassioned allegiances is praised, because the study of orality and literacy should encourage reflection rather than kindle partisanship.

In the source sentence the word carries its standard modern sense of zealous emotional commitment to a cause or theory: allegiances that are impassioned burn with loyalty, defend their heroes fiercely, and resist counter-evidence. The author warns that knowledge of orality-literacy contrasts does not normally generate such allegiances, and the remark is itself a plea for cool inquiry. Here impassioned is faintly cautionary: feeling that honours a lover or an anthem can distort a scholar, and the sentence prefers curiosity to crusade.

Core meaning in detail: Morphologically impassioned joins the prefix im- (in, into) to passion plus the participial -ed, literally put into passion or endowed with passion. It almost always modifies speech, advocacy, or belief: impassioned plea, impassioned debate, impassioned supporter. The passion in question is noble rather than sensual in modern use, closer to zeal and ardour than to romance, though older texts let the amorous sense breathe more freely.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Filled with strong feeling and animated by fervour, especially in speech or writing (primary modern sense, from 17th c.): an impassioned address to the jury. The bridge is direct: to be put in passion is to speak as feeling dictates, loudly, swiftly, and from the heart.
  • Holding intense conviction or zealous loyalty to a cause, person, or theory (the source sense): impassioned allegiances, impassioned defenders of the faith. The bridge extends fervour from the moment of utterance to the habit of the heart: the speaker who burns once becomes the partisan who burns always.
  • Marked by barely controlled emotion, trembling or blazing with earnestness (descriptive, literary): impassioned eyes, an impassioned letter. The bridge moves the fire into the body: voice, gaze, and handwriting all show the pressure of feeling that will not stay cool.
  • Moved by love or desire in older and poetic use (archaic shade, 17th–19th c.): an impassioned lover. The bridge remembers that passion once named suffering love above all, from Christ's passion to the lover's, before the word widened toward zeal in general.
  • Suffering or enduring in the oldest theological sense via passion (obsolete, preserved only in etymology): Christ's passion as anguish borne. The bridge is the Latin root passio, suffering: to be impassioned was once, ghost-like, to be plunged into suffering, before feeling itself became the whole meaning.
  • Rhetorical praise-term for sincerity and moral seriousness (journalistic): her impassioned campaign for clean water. The bridge turns heat into virtue: where coolness might suggest indifference, passion certifies that the speaker genuinely cares, and reporters award the adjective as honour.

Register and connotation: impassioned is dignified and sympathetic, seldom a sneer. To call a speech impassioned is usually to admire its force even when doubting its logic; to warn against impassioned allegiances, as the source does, is to respect feeling while reserving judgement. The word belongs to courts, parliaments, pulpits, and reviews, where earnestness is currency. Its shadow is excess: the impassioned may persuade the heart while clouding the mind, and every tribute to passion carries the quiet reminder that reason has its rights.

Collocations and grammar: classic frames are impassioned speech, plea, appeal, defence, debate, letter, supporter, advocate, and the verbs deliver, make, launch into plus object. It rarely premodifies cool nouns: one does not write an impassioned shopping list, and the mismatch is itself a joke about disproportion.
