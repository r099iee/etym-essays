Echoic / onomatopoeic base — no secure single PIE root; the word is sound-imitative. A proposed PIE *kleng- "to resound" (Pokorny) is DEBATED and may merely be a shared echoic template. - Proto-Germanic *klinganą "to ring, resound" (reconstructed; c. 1st millennium BCE–CE) → Old English clynnian / clynian "to sound, resound, make a ringing noise" (attested from the Anglo-Saxon period) → Middle English clinken "to ring, give forth a sharp metallic sound" (attested early 14th c.) → Middle English clink (noun) "a sharp ringing sound, as of metal or glass" (attested c. 1400) → reduplicated form clink-clink "a sharp light metallic ringing repeated in sequence" (echoic doubling for iteration; the doubled form is the modern lexical item) → **CLINK-CLINK** (Modern English). Note on the chain: the Old English verb is often listed as clynnan/clynian, but some scholars treat English clink as an independent Middle English echoic formation rather than a direct continuation of the Old English word; the two are certainly cognate in the echoic Germanic set, which also includes Old High German klingan "to ring, sound" (Modern German klingen, Klang), Middle Dutch klinken, and Middle Low German klinken, all pointing to the same reconstructed base *klinganą. The Old English forms in cly- / Old Norse klingja etc. show the familiar y/i and g/k alternations of this family. The connection of the whole set to a PIE root is a matter of debate; the OED treats the verb as echoic and gives the "to resound" reconstruction only tentatively. Sibling echoic forms in the same family (not stages in this chain, but same sound-symbolism): clank (attested 1610s), clang. Separate branch — the Clink prison: the gaol at Clink Street, Southwark, London (attested from the 1510s) gave slang "the clink" meaning prison; the place-name is popularly derived from the sound of chains or bolts, i.e. it is a proper-noun offshoot of the same echoic base, not a stage in the derivation of clink-clink itself.
