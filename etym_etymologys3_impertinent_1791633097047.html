The historical evolution of impertinent illustrates how a specialized term of Roman physical mechanics and scholastic logic underwent semantic moralization to become an indispensable descriptor of human social relations.

Indo-European Roots and Latin Morphology:

The word is an ancient compound rooted in two Proto-Indo-European morphemes:

  • The prefix *per-, denoting spatial or temporal transit ('forward, through, beyond').
  • The verbal root *ten-, meaning 'to stretch, extend, hold', which produced Greek teinein ('to stretch') and tonos ('tension, tone'), Sanskrit tanoti ('stretches'), and Latin tenere ('to hold, keep').

In Latin, the combination of per- and tenere formed the verb pertinere. Its original physical sense was 'to stretch out to, extend as far as, reach', describing physical boundaries that reached a river or mountain range. In Classical Latin, Cicero and Caesar extended this physical reaching into abstract conceptual relations: a topic or law 'reached out to' or 'pertained to' (pertinere ad) a particular matter. The present participle pertinens ('pertaining, belonging, applicable') became standard vocabulary for relevance.

Late Latin Negation and Scholastic Jurisprudence:

In Late Latin (attested around the fourth century CE), the negative prefix in- (assimilated phonetically to im- before the bilabial plosive /p/) was attached to pertinens, generating impertinens (genitive impertinentis). It was coined explicitly to denote that which does not reach, belong, or relate to the matter under consideration. In medieval canon and civil law glosses, an argumentum impertinens was an argument that failed to touch the core legal issue—an immaterial point that judges were instructed to dismiss without consideration.

Anglo-Norman Transmission and Middle English Usage:

Following the Norman Conquest, the French development impertinent entered the bilingual realm of English legal proceedings and scholastic philosophy. By the late fourteenth century, it appeared in Middle English literary and philosophical texts. Geoffrey Chaucer, in The Canterbury Tales (in the Clerk's Prologue, c. 1386), used it strictly in its logical sense: 'hir thyng, which that ye clepen [call] an impertinent sentence', meaning a digression or irrelevant statement that did not advance the narrative. John Gower and John Lydgate similarly employed it to describe treatises containing matter alien to the declared subject.

Renaissance Semantic Metamorphosis: From Irrelevance to Insolence:

During the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, the term underwent its decisive semantic shift. In an intensely status-conscious society, conversational boundaries were rigid. If a social subordinate—such as a servant, apprentice, or child—interrupted a discussion with remarks that 'did not pertain' to their place or duty, the interruption was viewed as an offensive transgression. Shakespeare captured this transitional moment: in The Tempest (1611), Prospero asks Miranda if his tale is 'impertinent' (meaning irrelevant or rambling), while in King Lear (1605), Edgar observes Lear's mad ramblings as 'O, matter and impertinency mix'd! / Reason in madness!'

By the mid-seventeenth century, the transgression had fully transferred from the topic to the person. Samuel Johnson's Dictionary of the English Language (1755) formalized this dual character, defining the adjective as first 'Of no relation to the matter in hand; contrary to reason' and second 'Intrusive; meddling; foolishly trifling'. Throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, novelists such as Samuel Richardson, Jane Austen, and William Makepeace Thackeray turned impertinent into the quintessential weapon against those who failed in due deference or committed social intrusions.

Modern Bifurcation and Aesthetic Application:

In modern contemporary English, the word maintains its dual legacy. In common parlance, it retains its sharp, aristocratic rebuke of insolence and nosiness. In Anglo-American jurisprudence, it rigorously retains its 600-year-old scholastic definition of immateriality. Meanwhile, in sophisticated literary criticism, such as Rudolf Arnheim's mid-century aesthetic writings, the two streams merge harmoniously: condemning inappropriate psychological reductions as both intellectually out of place and offensively disrespectful to the autonomous integrity of visual art.
