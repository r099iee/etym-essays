The evolution of imagistic spans Roman ancestor masks and modernist manifestos, showing how a word for wax portraits became a slogan for poetic revolution. Classical Latin imago meant a likeness, portrait, echo, or ghost, most solemnly the wax death-masks of ancestors carried in funeral processions and kept in atria as lineage and law. From imago Latin derived imago, imaginari, to picture to oneself, and imitari, to copy, all circling mimesis. Phonologically imago passed with little change into Old French image for statue, idol, and picture, losing final o and softening g to zh, giving Middle English image by Chaucer's time for both carved idol and mental picture. The Great Vowel Shift and French stress left RP /ˈɪmɪdʒ/ with initial stress and palatal ending, a learned yet fully nativized shape.

Medieval culture charged image with theology, icons, eidola, and idols were debated as windows or traps for devotion, while memory arts taught monks to store scripture as imagines in mental palaces. Renaissance rhetoric made image a term for simile and metaphor, the image as ornament that makes speech see. Romanticism inwardized it further to the creative imagination itself. Then in 1912 Ezra Pound, H.D., and Aldington in London tearooms weaponized it, demanding direct treatment of the thing, no superfluous word, and rhythm of musical phrase. They called themselves Imagistes, then Imagists, publishing Des Imagistes and Amy Lowell's anthologies, praising haiku, Chinese ideograms, and Greek hardness. Adjective imagist named the poet, imagistic named the method, first in reviews of the 1920s for hard, clear, superposed pictures.

  • Classical to medieval: imago as ancestor mask and legal likeness to holy icon and mental phantasm, sacred prestige and suspicion of idolatry intertwined.
  • Rhetorical stage 1500s-1800s: image as figure of speech, stock of similes in epic and sermon, then Coleridgean imagination as creative power, image becoming mind's eye itself.
  • Modernist codification 1912-1930s: Imagism as anti-Victorian economy, red wheelbarrow, metro petals, cantos superposition, imagistic clarity as ethical as well as aesthetic, honesty against rhetoric.

After Imagism dissolved, imagistic generalized from school to style and then to cognition. Mid-century New Critics praised imagistic density in Donne and Hopkins retroactively, film theorists spoke of imagistic montage, and cognitive psychologists of the 1970s contrasted imagistic and verbal coding in memory, Paivio's dual-coding theory giving laboratory prestige to pictures in the head. To strive for imagistic clarity in a world of words, as in the source, thus invokes both Pound's severe cure for logorrhoea and a universal desire to see through verbiage. What began as wax faces of Roman fathers ends as a contemporary compliment for any prose that still lets light fall on a real thing.

Pedagogically this lineage matters for prose as well as poetry, because teachers who ask students to prefer concrete showing over abstract telling are repeating Pound without knowing it. When composition handbooks praise imagistic clarity, they echo the Imagist rules of directness and economy, urging writers surrounded by floods of ready-made phrases to return to things seen, heard, and touched. The computer screen intensifies that possibility, allowing pictures, clips, and spaced lines alongside words, so that imagistic no longer means words alone but words staged among images. What began as wax ancestor masks thus ends as a contemporary media ethic, let the eye verify what the tongue asserts.
