- Early 16th Century (Tudor Period) - The noun *by* or *bye* meant a secondary way, side course, or subordinate matter (contrasted with the "main"); the legal phrase *upon the by* meant in passing or secondarily.
- Early 17th Century (Jacobean Era) - The prepositional phrase *by the by* begins appearing in drama and essays (such as Francis Bacon's works) meaning "in passing, casually, or parenthetically".
- 18th Century (Augustan & Georgian Eras) - The phrase becomes ubiquitous in epistolary correspondence and novels (Henry Fielding, Samuel Richardson, Jonathan Swift), solidifying the spelling *by-the-bye* with hyphenation.
- 19th Century (Victorian Golden Age) - Reaches its zenith in polite literature; used frequently by Jane Austen, Charles Dickens, and Oscar Wilde (as in *The Devoted Friend*) as a polished conversational trope for topic-shifting and false casualness.
- 20th Century - Gradually displaced in everyday vernacular English by *by the way*, retreating into a distinctly literary, nostalgic, or self-consciously erudite British register.
- 21st Century - Retained in historical drama, classic literature study, and high-register prose, immediately evoking Victorian charm, theatrical wit, or subtle rhetorical misdirection.
