The story of moor begins in water. Its deepest known ancestor is the reconstructed PIE root *mori-, meaning a body of water — lake, mere, sea — the same root that gives English mere and marsh, Latin mare, and German Meer. To call high dry heather by a water word sounds backwards until the Anglo-Saxon farm is pictured: to the first English speakers, what mattered was not altitude but use, and both the drowned fen and the sour hill were one thing, land that defeated the plough. So the Germanic descendant *mōraz named waste as such, wet or high, and Old English mōr kept that double vision, glossing marsh in one charter and barren hill in the next.

Centuries of enclosure then pulled the senses apart. As the low fens were drained, banked, and renamed marsh and fen, the high wastes kept the old noun and specialised it: by Chaucer's century more increasingly meant the heathered uplands, and by the Tudor surveys it is the moors of Yorkshire and Devon — unenclosed commons of grazing, peat digging, and grouse — that carry the word. Sound changes were gentle along the way: the long vowel of mōr rounded and shortened into modern moor, rhyming now with door rather than more, while the spelling wobbled between more, moore, and moor until print fixed it.

Stages of the journey:

  • PIE waters (before 2000 BCE): *mori- spreads across the dialects as the word for standing water and sea — the wet root of everything to come.
  • Germanic waste (c. 500 BCE): *mōraz narrows the water word to waste ground, marshy or barren — the farmer's antonym of field, not the sailor's sea.
  • Old English double ground (5th–11th c.): mōr in charters and poems (it stands glowering in Beowulf as the haunt of Grendel) covers fen, hill waste, and wilderness alike.
  • Middle English parting (12th–15th c.): lowland senses drain away to marsh and mosse; more climbs to the uplands and stays there, gathering heather as it goes.
  • Early modern commons (16th–18th c.): manor courts record moors as common of pasture and turbary — unenclosed, stinted, quarrelled over, essential to upland survival.
  • Romantic sublimity (late 18th–19th c.): Scott, the Brontës, and later Doyle's Hound make the moors the theatre of passion and terror; the waste becomes beautiful precisely because it cannot be tamed.
  • Sporting estate and heritage (19th c.–today): grouse-moor management burns heather in rotation for birds; then national parks, carbon accounting for peat, and reservoir protection give the ancient waste startlingly modern value — the carbon locked in a Pennine bog now worth more, tonne for tonne, than the sheep above it.

Literary attestation crowns the journey. From Grendel's mor to the Brontës' wind-roaring heights to Tolkien's North Moors — the near wilderness where hobbit gossip plants its walking tree — the word has never lost its northern cold. The capital M of the North Moors is only a mapmaker's courtesy; beneath it lies twelve centuries of waste, weather, and wonder, still unenclosed in the imagination whatever the Common Agricultural Policy pays for the grazing.
