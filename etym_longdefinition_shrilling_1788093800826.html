Shrilling is the present participle and gerund of the verb shrill, meaning the production of a high-pitched, piercing, penetrating sound, or such a sound itself. In the source passage from Tolkien's The Hobbit, the "shrilling of the trumpets" of Lake-town joins the twanging of bows to drive the dragon Smaug's wrath "to its height, till he was blind and mad with it": the word captures the thin, bright, cutting quality of trumpet calls ringing over water and wood.

The word family is Germanic and imitative: Middle English shrillen (c. 1300) "to sound with a high, piercing note" is probably an echoic formation related to Middle Low German schrellen/schrillen "to resound, sound sharply" and Middle High German schrelen. The very phonetics of the word — the voiceless palato-alveolar fricative /ʃ/, the vibrating /r/, the high front vowel /ɪ/ and the prolonging /l/ — enact a thin, bright, constricted sound, which is why the family has been so stable across seven centuries.

1. Primary senses — sound production

  • Of instruments: to sound with a high, piercing note (trumpets, fifes, whistles, bagpipes). "The shrilling of the trumpets" is the classic literary collocation.
  • Of voices and animals: to cry out, scream or sing in a high, piercing tone (children, people in distress, birds, insects such as cicadas).
  • Of the wind and objects: to make a sharp, whistling, penetrating noise as air passes over or through something (a shrilling gale, a shrilling kettle, a shrilling siren).

2. The adjective and adverb — the quality of sound

  • Shrill (adj., late 14th c.): high-pitched and piercing, penetrating and often unpleasant to the ear; "a shrill whistle", "a shrill voice".
  • Shrilly (adv.): in a shrill manner.
  • In phonetics and acoustics, "shrill" describes sounds of high fundamental frequency with strong high-frequency energy — strident, bright, cutting timbres.
  • In ornithology, many bird calls (of swifts, terns, waders) are routinely described as shrill; the word is a staple of natural-history writing.

3. Figurative and modern senses

  • Shrill rhetoric/criticism/headlines (20th–21st c.): strident, insistent, alarmist or excessive in tone — an extension in which the piercing quality of the sound stands for the aggressive insistence of the message.
  • The bridge: a shrill sound forces itself on the ear, cannot be ignored and grates; a "shrill" argument or editorial likewise demands attention by sheer vehemence and is felt as grating, exaggerated or unpleasantly intense.
  • In modern media discourse the adjective is frequently applied (often controversially, and often to women's voices in public debate) to criticism perceived as emotional rather than reasoned — a usage linguists have analysed as gendered and pejorative, since the same intensity in a male voice is more often called "passionate".
  • In music criticism, "shrill" denotes over-bright, edgy tone production, especially in the high registers of strings, brass or voice.

4. Summary of the semantic bridge

Every use of shrilling — literal or figurative — trades on the same acoustic signature: high pitch plus piercing intensity plus inescapable penetration of the ear. From the trumpet calls of epic and romance, through the naturalist's bird notes and the engineer's whistling steam, to the strident voice of modern polemic, the word has never stopped describing sound that cuts through every other sound and will not be ignored.
