- [Classical Latin] positum/positus — neutral past participle "placed, set, situated"; physical and institutional placement, not yet an English verb of argument.
- [c. 1640s–1700] Early Modern English posit — enters scholarly English as "to place, set down; to assume as true for the sake of argument"; strongly Latinate, rare outside learned circles; perception: technical, rigorous, slightly foreign.
- [18th–19th c.] Enlightenment & positivist eras — increasingly tied to systematic philosophy and science; to posit is to make axioms transparent; connotation of intellectual honesty rather than dogma when used carefully; can also sound dogmatic if overused.
- [Early 20th c.] Analytic philosophy & social science — formula "X posits that…" becomes a standard expository device in papers and textbooks; the form posits signals third-person summary of a theory's foundations.
- [Late 20th–21st c.] Academic & quality journalism — still high-register; occasionally mildly mocked as jargon in popular style guides, yet indispensable in precise argumentative prose; perception: careful, formal, premise-aware rather than merely "says" or "thinks."
