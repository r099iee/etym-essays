The biography of farthing starts with the number four. Its deepest ancestor is the PIE root *kwetwer-, the word for four that echoes through Sanskrit catvaras, Greek tessares, Latin quattuor, and Germanic four itself, one of the most stable numerals in the family. From this root Germanic built its ordinals and fractions with methodical regularity: *fedwōr is four, *fedurþa- is fourth, and Old English fēower and fēorða carry the set cleanly into the age of Alfred. The farthing is simply the fourth made little.

Old English fēorðing first means any fourth part, not yet a coin: wills divide hides into feorthings, bakers divide loaves, and the word belongs to surveyors and stewards as much as to moneyers. The diminutive suffix -ing does affectionate work here, turning the severe fraction into a handy small thing, exactly as shilling, penny's companions, and farthing form a family of coin-diminutives. Sound change then does its slow levelling: the diphthong simplifies, the th voices and devoices by position, and Middle English ferthing settles toward the modern spelling with a, a vowel shift shared with far's own history.

The coin career begins in the 13th century with the cut penny. Because the mint struck only pennies, small change was manufactured at the counter by cutting pennies into halves and quarters along the cross stamped on the reverse, and the quarter, round or ragged, was the farthing. Edward I regularised the piece as a round coin, and from then until decimalisation the farthing shadows every monetary reform: silver under the Plantagenets, base issues and private tokens under the Tudors and Stuarts, regal copper in the 17th century, and finally the handsome bronze farthings of 1860 with seated Britannia, replaced in 1937 by the beloved wren reverse that collectors still treasure.

Each metal tells a social story. Silver farthings were easily lost and constantly clipped, so small that chroniclers joke about swallowing fortunes; copper farthings were farmed to patentees whose monopolies Parliament attacked as oppression of the poor; bronze farthings rode the great Victorian expansion of small commerce, pricing buns, matches, and tram stages. Cobbett writes at the hinge of this history, when the farthing still bought something and therefore still measured ruin: his farmer who cannot keep a single farthing for rent is not missing a curiosity but missing bread.

Semantic and social landmarks:

  • Anglo-Saxon fractions: feorðing quarters lands and loaves in charters, the accountant's word before it is the minter's.
  • Medieval markets: cut and round silver farthings buy ale, candles, and alms, the smallest lawful price.
  • Stuart monopolies: farthing patents enrich courtiers and enrage the Commons, making the tiny coin a symbol of corruption.
  • Victorian bronze: the 1860 bronze issue and the 1937 wren make the farthing the nation's best-loved small change.
  • Cobbett's protest: Rural Rides turns farthing arithmetic into political economy, proving by pence that taxation eats the farm.
  • 1961 withdrawal: inflation renders the coin valueless, the mint calls it in, and the farthing passes from till to proverb.

Literary attestations are everywhere because moralists love minimums. Chaucer's friars deal in farthings, Shakespeare's fools would not give a farthing for oaths, the Authorised Version's two mites become two farthings in English purses, and Dickens counts farthings to prove both honesty and poverty. The idioms preserve the coin's ghost with precision: not worth a farthing for worthlessness, to the last farthing for exact repayment, and every farthing for exhaustive accounting.

Today no till holds farthings, yet the word refuses to die. Collectors grade wrens and Britannias, historians price medieval wages in farthings, and speakers who never held one still deny worth to the farthing. From PIE *kwetwer- through Old English fēorðing to the bronze wren, the thread is unbroken division into four, and the smallest fourth still teaches the largest lesson: that fortunes are lost not in pounds but in farthings, one single coin at a time.
