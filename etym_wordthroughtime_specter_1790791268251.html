- 1600s - *Early Modern English*: Borrowed from Middle French *spectre*, denoting a visible, disembodied ghost, apparition, or supernatural phantom in drama and demonological tracts.
- 1700s - *Enlightenment & Rationalist Prose*: Gradually broadened from literal demonology to describe psychological delusions, optical illusions, and figurative phantoms of the imagination created by superstition or fever.
- 1840s - *19th-Century Politics & Marxism*: Famously elevated by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels ('A spectre is haunting Europe...') into an ideological metaphor for looming, latent socio-political forces that terrify the status quo.
- 20th c. - *Modern Journalism & Geopolitics*: Ubiquitously adopted across media and political commentary as a standard idiom for menacing existential risks ('the specter of inflation,' 'the specter of nuclear annihilation').
- Late 20th–21st c. - *Critical Theory & Hauntology*: Conceptualized by Jacques Derrida and cultural theorists as the persistent haunting of the present by the unresolved ghosts and unfulfilled promises of past eras.
