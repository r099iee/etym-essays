Pericles (proper noun) — the name of the Athenian statesman and general (c. 495–429 BCE) who led Athens during its greatest age — the period of the Delian League's transformation into an empire, the building of the Parthenon, the maturing of democracy, and the flourishing of drama, history, philosophy, and art that later generations called the "Age of Pericles". In Walter Pater's sentence the name stands for exactly that: an era "productive in personalities, many-sided, centralised, complete", used as the golden benchmark against which the age of Lorenzo de' Medici is measured.

Primary sense — the historical statesman. Pericles dominated Athenian public life from roughly 461 BCE to his death in the plague of 429 BCE, after the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War. Under his leadership Athens extended her maritime empire, completed the Long Walls, and rebuilt the Acropolis; the Parthenon and the great sculptures of Phidias belong to his programme. The fourth-century historians record him as the champion of radical democracy — payment for jury service, citizenship law of 451/450 restricting citizenship to children of two Athenian parents — and, later, as the man who led the city into the catastrophic war with Sparta. His Funeral Oration, as recreated by Thucydides (History 2.35–46), is the most famous single speech in Greek political prose — the classic statement of Athenian values, later echoed by Lincoln at Gettysburg.

Second sense — "the Age of Pericles" (period-label). Since antiquity the name has labelled a golden age. In the nineteenth century, especially in the criticism of Matthew Arnold and Walter Pater (and in German philhellenism), "the age of Pericles" became shorthand for the classical ideal: a society at the height of artistic and intellectual achievement, "the very crown of Greek life". It is this sense Pater uses — an era superbly furnished with personalities.

Third sense — the name as type and title.

  • The type of the great democratic statesman. In political rhetoric "a Pericles" or "Periclean leadership" denotes eloquence, vision, and command of a free people.
  • Shakespeare's play.Pericles, Prince of Tyre (printed 1609; now attributed to Shakespeare with George Wilkins) made the name a stage figure — a romance of shipwreck and reunion that survives in repertory.
  • The adjective Periclean (with the rarer noun Pericleanism?) belongs to art history and political science: "Periclean Athens", "Periclean democracy".
  • Modern namesakes. The name Pericles has been borne by scholars, politicians, and even a Brazilian composer and a Spanish Civil War-era politician; it remains a living, if uncommon, given name, especially in Greece and Italy (Pericle).

Name meaning. Greek Περικλῆς (Periklēs) is a noble compound: περί (peri), "around, exceedingly, far" + κλέος (kléos), "fame, glory" — hence "far-famed", "surrounded by glory", the perfect heroic name for the man ancient writers made the embodiment of Athenian glory. The pattern (-klēs, "renowned") recurs in the Greek onomasticon: Themistocles ("famed for justice" — traditionally), Sophocles, Heracles, Pericles' own rival Cleon (from the same κλέος stem reversed).

Cultural register. The name is canonical cultural knowledge. It is not an NPC: Pericles is one of the supreme figures of Western history, a keeper in any vocabulary deck, and the card treats him as such — biography, name-etymology, and afterlife all required.
