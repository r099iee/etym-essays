- 16th Century (c. 1530s–1580s) - Enters Early Modern English from Middle French *férocité* and Latin *ferōcitās*, initially carrying a dual classical connotation: both savage wildness and high-spirited, haughty boldness or stubborn courage that defies subjugation.
- 17th Century - Becomes anchored in natural philosophy, anatomical texts, and hunting chronicles to describe the natural *ferocity* of untamed carnivores (lions, wolves, bears), contrasting wild beastliness with domestic animal gentleness and Christian decorum.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Extends into political theory, historiography, and military chronicles (Edward Gibbon, David Hume) to describe barbarian invasions, battlefield collisions, and the uncontrolled passions of human nature unmoderated by civil law and Enlightenment manners.
- 19th Century (Romanticism to Victorian Era) - Broadens metaphorically to characterize the sublime fury of nature (polar storms, ocean tempests) and begins appearing in critical literature to characterize uncompromising artistic expression and polemical debate.
- 20th Century to Present - Established across educated English as an evocative term for relentless drive, athletic intensity (ferocity on the pitch), searing intellectual critique, and uncontrollable elemental force, standing alongside its original predatory denotation.
