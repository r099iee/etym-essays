- 17th c. — curt (borrowed around 1620, from Latin curtus via Middle French court, 'short') enters as a neutral word for 'short, brief'; the adverb curtly follows in the same century, meaning simply 'in brief terms.'
- — 18th–19th c. — The Latin root's edge asserts itself: curtus meant 'cut short, docked, maimed,' and curt/crually come to describe speech deliberately clipped of politeness and padding — brevity that carries impatience.
- — Contemporary: curtly fuses brevity with dismissiveness. It is never merely brief; it is pointedly, rudely brief — a sentence snapped off, trimmed to the stump, communicating that the speaker will not waste words on the obvious.
