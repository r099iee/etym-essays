- Old English 'cleofan' - the strong verb for splitting or rending apart, native to the heroic vocabulary of sword and axe; in Old English poetry the blade that cleaves is an instrument of fate.
- Middle English - 'cleven' survives as the plain word for splitting wood or flesh, but the rise of Scandinavian 'split' and native 'cut' begins to crowd it from everyday speech.
- 16th-19th century - the split-sense of 'cleave' retreats into elevated, archaic registers: the Bible ('cleave the altar'), epic, and verse; it becomes the verb of ceremonial or heroic division rather than of daily carpentry.
- Contemporary - 'cleave' is strongly literary and archaic; it lives on in song, saga, and solemn diction, so that its current form instantly signals a deliberately ancient, epic voice — as when a dwarf-sworn blade is wished to cleave goblins again.
