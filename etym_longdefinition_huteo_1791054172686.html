huteo (noun, lower-case in English borrowing; plural huteo nuts) is a Tahitian name for a fish-poison tree and especially its stupefying nut, pounded and washed into streams or lagoons to narcotise fish so they float and can be gathered by hand. In the source sentence it appears in the phrase huteo nut, paired with the hora plant as the two Tahitian intoxicating mixtures. Modern botanists generally identify it with Barringtonia asiatica (sea putat, fish-poison tree) or a close congener, though older books leave the Linnaean identification vague.

Full sense inventory, given obscurity:

  • Tahitian fish-poison nut (primary ethnobotanical sense): The seed, rich in saponins, whose grated pulp stuns fish. Example: They beat the huteo nut to foam in the pool. This is the only securely attested English sense.
  • By extension, the tree itself: Travellers write huteo tree for the Barringtonia that bears such nuts — a large coastal tree with square fruit that floats oceanic dispersal. Usage is rare and usually glossed.
  • By extension, the pulp mixture: The intoxicating mixture prepared from the huteo nut — grated kernel mixed with water, sometimes with hora leaves — poured to drunk fish. This is metonymy (nut for preparation).
  • No independent modern senses: Unlike narcotic or stupefy, huteo has developed no slang, technical, or figurative meanings in English. It does not mean any poison generally, any nut generally, or drunkenness generally. Its meaning is locked to the Tahitian fishing practice.

Domain detail is anthropological and botanical. Ethnography: Fish stupefaction (ichthyotoxic fishing) is pantropical: Derris root in Amazonia and Melanesia, Tephrosia in Africa, Verbascum in Europe, Buckeye in North America. Tahitian use was communal and festive, damming a stream section at low water, praying, pounding nuts, then scooping floating fish with baskets — protein without hooks or nets. Botany: Barringtonia asiatica is a littoral tree to 20 m, with large white pom-pom flowers, square fibrous fruit, and seeds containing barringtonin and saponins toxic to gills but harmless to mammals when cooked fish are eaten (poison washes out and heat degrades it). Pharmacology: Action is narcotic to fish (gill membrane disruption, hypoxia) rather than true anaesthesia; fish appear drunk, leisurely, floating — Durant's adjectives. History of the passage: Will Durant's Our Oriental Heritage (1935) uses huteo and hora to illustrate how food quest generated polity; the sentence compresses missionary reports into world-history colour.

Bridges and confusions must be stated plainly. The nut-to-tree-to-mixture extensions are ordinary metonymy. The deeper bridge — why one nut stupefies — is chemical ecology: littoral trees evolved fish-toxic seeds to deter predators, humans exapted them. Learners may confuse huteo with:

  • hutu / futu / utu: Cognate Polynesian names for the same Barringtonia complex; spelling variants, not different plants.
  • hora plant: The co-mentioned Tahitian fish-poison, possibly Tephrosia or Pittosporum; a companion, not a synonym.
  • hutu (Maori breadfruit ferment): Unrelated homograph in New Zealand contexts.

Grammar: countable noun, almost always with nut, tree, or pulp (a huteo nut, huteo nuts). No verb (*to huteo) or adjective (*huteoic) is established. Register is exotic-archaic, appearing only in historical anthropology, reprints of Durant, and ethnobotanical footnotes. Capitalisation is lower-case in English; Tahitian sources may write Huteo initially. Pronunciation in RP is approximated as hoo-TAY-oh, since Tahitian /u, e, o/ map onto English long vowels. Rarity is extreme: outside this card, most readers will never encounter it, which is precisely why mnemonic and similar-word scaffolding matter.
