- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) — Attested as the strong verb tredan (past træd, past participle treden), denoting the physical action of walking, trampling, or crushing underfoot, as in treading the winepress or pressing corn.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) — Evolved into treden; expanded into zoological senses (the mating behavior of fowl) and religious allegories of crushing sin and the devil under Christ's feet.
- Early Modern English (16th–18th c.) — Solidified in dramatic literature (Shakespeare, Milton) and immortalized in 1711 by Alexander Pope's proverb "Fools rush in where angels fear to tread" (An Essay on Criticism); noun specialized in carpentry for the flat horizontal board of a staircase step.
- 19th c. (Industrial Revolution) — Applied to mechanical contraptions like treadmills (originally penal and agricultural grinding wheels driven by stepping workers) and early tracked wheels.
- 20th–21st c. — Universalized in automotive and heavy transport engineering for the grooved outer tread of rubber tires and military caterpillar tank tracks, while preserving its iconic metaphorical idiom of stepping gingerly in fraught situations.
