- [Old English storc (“the stiff-legged one”)] - A plain, homely bird-name in use for over a thousand years, coined for the bird's rigid, deliberate gait; a thoroughly everyday word of fields and rooftops with no folklore attached yet.
- [Middle English stork] - Unchanged in form and sense; a familiar rural word for the large wading bird that nested on roofs and chimneys, already a companion of human settlements.
- [Renaissance–early modern English] - The bird becomes an emblem of loyalty and domesticity — returning to the same nest year after year — and folk belief in the creature's homely virtue quietly grows around the name.
- [19th-century folklore] - European tradition consolidates the baby-bringing myth (“a visit from the stork”); the word acquires a nursery, fairy-tale connotation of birth and new beginnings.
- [Modern register] - An everyday, neutral bird-word with a rich folkloric layer; “the stork brings babies” is a recognised euphemism, while in natural-history writing the plural storks and the family name Ciconiidae mark the formal scientific register.
