reproved — the past tense and past participle of the verb to reprove: to reprimand, censure, correct, or express disapproval of someone, ordinarily in a mild but firm manner and with the express aim of amendment rather than punishment. In the specific literary moment on this card the word is a speech tag — “You’ve been hearing too many native fairy-tales,” reproved Bellman — and it performs the entire characterisation of the speaker in a single verb. Bellman does not shout or rage at Maspic; he administers the quiet, authoritative correction of a confident expert who treats Martian folklore as a superstition to be gently scolded out of existence, then reasserts the claims of terrestrial science. Reproof, as the word has always carried it, is the mild firmness of a person who corrects without raising the voice — patient, condescending, tinged with intellectual superiority, and aimed at the listener’s improvement rather than his humiliation.

“Reprove” is not exhausted by the simple gloss “criticise”. A genuinely exhaustive account must track the polysemy that has accumulated around the word since the fourteenth century, when it entered English with a double face that modern dictionaries still record in layered order.

  • Primary sense — censure or rebuke with a view to amendment: To speak to a person, or about an act, with authoritative disapproval intended to correct. “Reprove” is gentler than rebuke (which can be sharp and public), more measured than scold (which is often emotional and nagging), and more personal than censure (which is a formal judgment, often of an institution or a publication). The core gloss — “to rebuke; to chide; to reproach” — hides the crucial refinement that the word aims at the mending of the offender.
  • Secondary, now archaic sense — to disprove, refute, or convict of error: In Middle English and Renaissance usage to reprove also meant “to prove a claim false, to convict an argument or person of error.” A preacher reproved a heresy; a scholar reproved a faulty text; a lawyer reproved a false witness. This is the etymological core of the word — the re- (“back, against”) + prove (“test, prove”) — and it survives today chiefly in theological register (“reprove the world of sin”) and in the noun reproof, “a refutation,” which lingers alongside the common “a rebuke.”
  • The reproving voice of conscience — a theological and moral extension: In devotional writing of the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, reprove and reproof describe the inner accusation of conscience and the corrective speech of God. The Holy Spirit “reproves” the heart; a friend’s reproof is valued as a gift of truth (“Faithful are the wounds of a friend,” Proverbs 27:6). This extension explains why reprove feels warmer than rebuke: it presupposes a relationship in which correction is an act of love.
  • The morphological sense — “to prove again” (etymological wordplay): Because the word visibly contains prove, popular etymology reads reprove as “prove again,” as in the card’s mnemonic of the teacher re-proving the wrong sum at the blackboard. This is technically a false analysis of the historical derivation (the Latin reprobāre meant “reject, condemn,” not “prove again”), but it is semantically productive and worth recording: it captures the truth that reproof is a demonstration, a laying out of the mistake so the doer can see and mend it.
  • The wider prove family and its modern split: The verb is the sibling of prove, approval, approbation, probation, probate, probable, probity, and — via the Latin past participle — reprobate (“a person rejected as bad, depraved”). The split is instructive: approve and reprove are the two directions of the same testing action, one saying “tested and found good,” the other “tested and found wanting.”
  • Register and contemporary usage: In present-day English reprove is distinctly literary, biblical, and old-fashioned. A modern speaker says scold, tell off, call out, or rebuke; choosing reprove signals a deliberate, patrician mildness. It is the verb of parents and tutors in Victorian novels, of judges in formal prose, and — exactly as here — of a calm superior correcting a naive listener without ever raising his voice. In formal and biblical contexts it remains fully standard: “the judge reproved the counsel for the outburst.”

Collocations complete the picture: reprove a child, reprove a fault, reprove error, a gentle reproof, reproof and correction, to reprove sternly / mildly / kindly. The word is transitive — one reproves a person (or, archaically, a fault “for” / “of” / “in” a person) — and its noun reproof and adjective reproving (hence reprovingly) carry the same mild-firm balance. In the cave passage, the choice of reproved over any plainer verb quietly establishes the social and intellectual hierarchy of the scene: Bellman is the reprover, Maspic the reproved, and the whole exchange is staged as a lesson in which Martian “fairy-tales” are corrected by terrestrial science.
