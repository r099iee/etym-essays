Balkan is a proper name that has become a common adjective and, in derived form, a metaphor. Strictly it denotes the Balkan Mountains — the range known in Bulgarian as Stara Planina, "the Old Mountain", which crosses Bulgaria from the Serbian border to the Black Sea — and, by extension, the Balkan Peninsula, the whole of south-eastern Europe south of the Danube and the Sava. In English the word is used as an attributive adjective ("the Balkan states", "Balkan history"), as a noun in the plural ("the Balkans"), and as the base of a family of politically charged derivatives (balkanisation, balkanise, Balkanism).

Primary sense: the mountain range. The Balkan Mountains run some 560 kilometres across northern Bulgaria, rising to Botev Peak (2,376 m). The range was the classical Haemus (Haimos) of Greek geography, and it formed the natural northern wall of the Byzantine Empire against the Bulgars and later the Ottoman frontier. The name in this strict sense is still current in Bulgarian, Serbian, Turkish and English geographical writing; "the Balkans" as a bare plural, however, almost always means the peninsula, not the mountains.

Secondary sense: the peninsula and its region. The Balkan Peninsula is bounded by the Adriatic, the Ionian, the Aegean, the Sea of Marmara and the Black Sea, with its northern limit conventionally drawn at the Danube–Sava line. It contains, in whole or part, Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, Kosovo, Albania, North Macedonia, Greece, Bulgaria, Romania and the European part of Turkey. The definition is contested: Croatia and Slovenia prefer to describe themselves as Central European, Romania is sometimes excluded, and Greece is sometimes treated separately because of its Aegean and classical identity. "Southeastern Europe" is the compromise term favoured by many scholars and by the European Union.

Tertiary sense: the geopolitical metaphor. Because the peninsula was for centuries the meeting point of empires — Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman, Habsburg, Russian — and because its modern history includes the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, two world wars, and the violent break-up of Yugoslavia, "Balkan" acquired a metaphorical charge. To call a situation "Balkan" is to suggest fragmentation, entrenched ethnic rivalry, backwardness, corruption or picturesque disorder. The derivatives make the metaphor explicit:

  • balkanisation — the fragmentation of a state or region into small, mutually hostile units; first attested in English around 1918 and popularised in the interwar years.
  • balkanise (also balkanize) — to divide in that way, used of countries, institutions, industries and even software architectures.
  • Balkanism — the scholarly term, after Maria Todorova's Imagining the Balkans (1997), for the Western discourse that exoticises and denigrates the region, analogous to Edward Said's "Orientalism".
  • Balkanic — a rarer, more neutral adjective used in linguistics and geology.

Linguistic sense: the Balkan Sprachbund. In linguistics, "Balkan" names one of the most famous contact areas in the world. The Balkan Sprachbund is a group of genetically unrelated languages — Bulgarian, Macedonian, Romanian, Aromanian, Albanian, Greek, Serbian, Romani and others — that have converged grammatically through centuries of coexistence. Shared features include the postposed definite article (Bulgarian čovekŭt, "the man"), the loss of the infinitive and its replacement by subjunctive constructions, the future formed with a particle from "want", the merger of genitive and dative, and a distinctive evidential mood. The term Balkan linguistics was established by Kristian Sandfeld's Linguistique balkanique (1930).

Technical and geographical notes. The peninsula is not a single tectonic unit; the "Balkan" orogen is a fold-and-thrust belt linked to the Alpine–Himalayan system, and geologists speak of the "Balkanides". In ecology, "Balkan" identifies a floristic and faunal province with high endemism. In demography and politics, "Western Balkans" is a recent EU coinage (post-1999) for the states of the former Yugoslavia plus Albania that are not yet EU members — a bureaucratic label with no physical geography behind it.

Register and caution. The word is not a slur, but "Balkan" as a metaphor is widely felt to be condescending, and serious writers use it with irony or in quotation marks. The people of the region rarely call themselves "Balkan" in ordinary speech; they use national or city identities, or the neutral "Southeastern European".
