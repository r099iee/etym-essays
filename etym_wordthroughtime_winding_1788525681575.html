- [Old English: c. 800–1100] - windende / windung - The concrete action of turning, twisting, plaiting, braiding cords, or whirling weapons - [perception: physical Germanic labor of spinning, weaving, and bound coils]
- [Middle English: c. 1150–1450] - windinge - Expanded to describe the continuous spooling of thread onto bobbins, winding sheets (shrouds for burials), and the twisting architectural contours of stairs - [perception: associated with domestic textile manufacture, religious burial rituals, and masonry]
- [Early Modern English: c. 1500–1700] - winding - Enters clockmaking with the invention of coiled mainsprings; heavily used by Renaissance poets to describe winding streams, labyrinthine thoughts, and serpentine paths - [perception: elegant, meditative, and mechanical precision]
- [Industrial & Victorian Era: c. 1750–1900] - winding - Heavily adopted in mining (winding engines/shafts), textile mills (mechanical bobbin winders), and electrical technology (motor coils) - [perception: industrial power, steam mechanics, and romantic picturesque landscapes (as in Wilde's prose)]
- [20th–21st Century] - winding - Universal term in electrical engineering (stator/rotor windings), electronics (magnetic transformers), computer science (unwinding call stacks), and everyday descriptions of scenic, curved roads - [perception: versatile descriptive participle spanning heavy engineering, high-tech systems, and pastoral geography]
