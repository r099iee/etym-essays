Drought — a prolonged period of abnormally dry weather, with little or no rain, during which water supplies, rivers, lakes, and soil moisture fall to levels that damage crops, livestock, and human activity; by extension, any prolonged and painful absence or shortage.

The word is entirely native to English — an abstract noun formed from the adjective "dry" — and it names one of the oldest and most dreaded facts of the rural world. A drought is not a single dry day or a dry week; it is dryness prolonged until it becomes a disaster: the ground cracks, the pasture turns brown, streams shrink and vanish, wells fail, and the waters of lakes sink, exposing old shorelines and even the ruins of drowned towns. It is precisely that last image that Tolkien uses: "the rotting piles of a greater town could still be seen along the shores when the waters sank in a drought."

  • Primary sense — meteorological drought: a period of substantially below-normal rainfall over a region, long enough to constitute a weather hazard; measured against a region's normal precipitation.
  • Agricultural drought: a period when soil moisture is too low for crops and pasture, so that plants wilt, harvests fail, and cattle starve; historically the direct cause of famine. This is the sense that made "drought" a word of terror in the agrarian past.
  • Hydrological drought: the fall of river flows, lake levels, and reservoir storage below normal — the sense in the Tolkien sentence, where the sinking waters expose the old piles. Hydrological drought is the delayed, accumulated face of rainfall shortage.
  • Socioeconomic drought: the modern, policy-oriented sense in which water shortage begins to injure economies and communities — restricting irrigation, industry, power generation (hydroelectric), and public supply.
  • Figurative and extended senses: "a drought" of anything is an abnormal, prolonged lack: "a drought of ideas", "an innovation drought", "a goal drought" (a player or team scoring no goals for a long stretch). The word also carries an old religious image of spiritual dryness — "a drought of grace", the parched soul — an allegory present in English since the Middle Ages.
  • Register: drought is today a scientific and journalistic word of the first importance, central to climate reporting; but it has never lost its ancient emotional weight of famine and ruin, so it remains simultaneously technical and doom-laden.

Semantic reasoning and history: the word began as the plain abstract noun for "dryness" (Old English drūgaþ, formed from "dry"), covering any dryness of ground, air, or body. As rainfall became, for an agricultural people, the decisive fact of survival, the word specialised: Middle English "droughte" is already a dry period of weather. The scientific age subdivided the concept into meteorological, agricultural, hydrological, and socioeconomic droughts, but each is still the same thing — dryness gone on too long. The figurative senses ("drought of goals") simply reapply the pattern: a stretch of time in which the normal supply fails to come. The spelling "drought" preserves a ghostly older consonant (the "gh" was once pronounced as a fricative), and the dialect word "drouth" is its northern and Scottish twin.
