- 16th to early 17th Century: Exclamatory interjection and hot-blooded youthful frolic - Used as a jubilant shout of wonder, surprise, or lusty joy, transitioning in Elizabethan drama (notably Shakespeare's Hamlet) to signify impetuous passion and hot blood in youth.
- 18th Century: Folk-etymological reanalysis from interjection to temporal noun - Syntactically reanalyzed through association with 'high day' (festival, holy day), shifting from an expressive cry to an abstract noun denoting a period of vigorous youth or bloom.
- 19th Century: Victorian nostalgic descriptor for lost eras of imperial and industrial grandeur - Widely adopted in historical essays, novels, and biographies to contrast the vanished prosperity and romance of bygone eras with contemporary realities.
- 20th to 21st Century: Standard retrospective designation for past peaks of popularity, technology, and culture - Ubiquitous across sociology, business, music, and memoirs to denote the definitive flourishing peak of obsolete machines, trends, or personal careers.
