Tantallon belongs to the oldest layer of Scottish toponymy — a name older than its castle, older than Scots, and probably older than Gaelic in Lothian — whose language cannot be fixed with certainty. The headland itself was occupied in prehistory; a Bronze Age burial and Iron Age occupation underlie the medieval masonry. But the name first enters record only with the Douglas fortress: William Douglas's charter of the mid-14th century, royal summonses of 1374-1380, and the chroniclers' Tantallon and Tantalloun. Medieval scribes spell by ear, with Tan-, Tal-, Dun- variants that hint either at a Brittonic fort-word or at scribal confusion, and no contemporary gloss explains it. Unlike transparent names such as Edinburgh (Edwin's fort) or Stirling (place of strife, whatever the root), Tantallon arrives opaque.

Place-name scholars therefore proceed by elimination. Lothian before c. 600 spoke Brittonic (the language of the Gododdin, akin to Welsh); after c. 600-900 it was contested between Northumbrian Old English and Gaelic, with Norse raids on the coast. A Brittonic origin would expect a first element like din (fort, Welsh din) or tan (fire, Welsh tân) plus a second element like tal (forehead, end) or a stream-name. A Gaelic origin would expect dun (fort) or teine (fire, beacon) plus talamh (ground) or lon (meadow). Victorian antiquaries cheerfully proposed Teine-tal-mhon (fire hill of the moss) as a beacon-station opposite the Bass, and guidebooks still repeat it. Modern surveyors — Mac an Tàilleir, Taylor, Dixon — mark all such divisions with an obelus: the stress-pattern and the early Tan- (not Dun-) tell against dun, the supposed beacon-function is unattested, and no Gaelic *tanntalon compound is paralleled elsewhere. The responsible statement, followed here, is that Tantallon is of uncertain, probably Brittonic or Gaelic, origin, first securely attested in the 14th century as the Douglas castle-name, and not to be segmented with confidence.

  • Castle-building and Douglas centuries (1350s-1528): William Douglas raises the great curtain wall in dressed red sandstone from the local quarry; his descendants the Earls of Angus entrench there. The name migrates from rock to fortress to lordship: the Lord of Tantallon, to hold Tantallon, to lie in Tantallon. Royal artillery twice batters it (1491, 1528) without erasing the name; instead the sieges fix it in chronicle and ballad as the House of Angus against the Crown.
  • Scott's resurrection (1808): Marmion Canto 5 makes Tantallon Hold a set-piece: Marmion and his train wind o'er the bending of the hill to its gate, are received by Angus, and hear the Douglas rebuke of invasion. Scott had visited the ruin in 1807; his notes quote the 1528 siege. The poem sells 2,000 copies on its first day, and Tantallon passes from charter-Latin into drawing-room English. Ruskin's father read it aloud; the boy believed the garrison still stood.
  • Empty-castle shock (Ruskin childhood, 1820s-1830s): the family tours — Tantallon, Stirling, Loch Katrine — produce the sentence that is our source. The stones are there; the men are not. Victorian travel-writing generalises the feeling: ruins prove both the truth of romance and its loss. Tantallon becomes a moralised landscape, industry versus chivalry, present versus past.
  • Preservation and science (1850s-present): photography, Ordnance Survey (spelling stabilised as Tantallon), state guardianship in 1924, consolidation, excavation, and seabird ecology all keep the name current but transfer it from ballad to heritage panel. Pronunciation stays Scots /tænˈtælən/ even when southern visitors rhyme it with alone.

Phonologically the modern form shows Scots reduction: first vowel /æ/, stressed second /æ/, final -on to /ən/. The double ll is orthographic and does not lengthen the consonant. No regular sound law can be traced because the pre-14th-century form is unknown; to invent one would be guesswork, and the protocol forbids it. The history of Tantallon is therefore a history of a castle and a poem, not of a root.
