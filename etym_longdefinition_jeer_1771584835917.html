Jeer names a taunting cry of mockery and derision, raised loudly at a person or group the crowd wishes to humiliate. In the verse preamble that introduces Bao-yu in Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng (The Story of the Stone), the word enters at the exact point where the world passes judgment on him: "Yet not a whit cared he how folk might jeer!" After a catalogue of his delinquencies — "harum-scarum," "doltish," "acts outlandish," "nature queer" — the couplet gathers the social response into one noisy, contemptuous verb. A jeer is never quiet and never private: it is the roar of a crowd declaring that someone stands outside the fold, the loud laughter and shouted taunts that punish non-conformity. The nuance of the passage turns on the word's collision with its opposite, care. Bao-yu is explicitly indifferent — he does not care "a whit" — so the jeer is framed as external, impersonal sound that fails to reach him. The poet is saying not that Bao-yu is untouched because he fails to hear the mockery, but that he is untouched because he refuses to grant the crowd any authority to judge him. The jeer thus becomes the emblem of conventional society doing what conventional society always does, and of the eccentric spirit's power to tune that noise out entirely.

The word has a small but precise family of senses:
  • Verb, intransitive (with at): to make rude, mocking, usually loud remarks at someone — "the crowd jeered at the losing team," "he jeered at the speaker." This is the primary modern use, and the sense in the poem's "how folk might jeer!"
  • Verb, transitive (rare, now chiefly literary): to jeer a person, to taunt or deride them directly, or to jeer someone off a stage, down from a platform, out of the room — the crowd "jeered the player from the boards."
  • Noun: a rude, mocking shout or remark — "a jeer from the gallery," "jeers and catcalls," "jeers of derision." The noun nearly always implies collectivity and noise.
  • Derived forms: jeering (adjective and noun, mocking/derisive), jeeringly (adverb), jeerer (the person who jeers) — all attested from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Boundaries of the word. Jeer must be set against its neighbours in the vocabulary of mockery. A taunt is aimed and verbal — a specific insult hurled at a specific person — whereas a jeer is often a wordless or near-wordless collective noise, a howl or hoot of contempt from many throats at once. Scoff and deride express contemptuous disbelief, frequently at an idea or a claim rather than a person; they can be a curled lip or a sarcastic remark and need no crowd. Sneer is the antonym of jeer in manner: a sneer is silent, private, and contemptuous — a look or smile that asserts superiority without a sound — while a jeer is loud, public, and performative. Gibe (or jibe) is a witty, sneering remark, cleverer and more pointed than a jeer; and hoot and boo name the pure noise of a hostile audience, without the articulate mockery of a taunt. The boundary that matters is voice and collectivity: jeers come from crowds and are meant to be heard by everyone; sneers are single and silent. To jeer is to make mockery theatrical.

Collocationally, jeer appears in a small, stable set of frames. The verb is almost always intransitive with at, and the noun runs in the twin forms "jeers and catcalls," "jeers and hoots," or "jeers of derision." People are "met with jeers," "greeted with jeers," "drowned out by jeers," or "sent off with jeers" from a stage, a courtroom, or a football ground. Register-wise the word is informal-to-neutral but strongly vocal: it belongs to the crowd, the mob, the audience, the press, and the assembly, and it retains an archaic-literary sheen in verse. It is precisely the right word for a poem because it is both onomatopoeically vivid and socially precise — it tells us the sound (a harsh, shouted cry), the attitude (contempt), and the social event (public humiliation of one person by many) in a single syllable. In a modern sentence such as "the crowd jeered him off the pitch," the word still performs exactly the work it does in the Stone's couplet: it turns individual difference into public spectacle, and measures one person's indifference against the noise of the many.
