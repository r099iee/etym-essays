confute means to overcome an argument, opinion, or person by proof: to show conclusively that a claim is false, mistaken, or untenable. One confutes a theory, a fallacy, an accuser, or an opponent — with facts, logic, or evidence. It is stronger than dispute or deny, which merely oppose, and equivalent in force to refute, with which it is often paired: to confute and refute. In the source sentence, terms difficult to confute in the abstract are terms so vague or circular that they cannot even be decisively disproved in theory — they evade refutation by saying too little to be wrong.

The verb governs both things and persons. To confute a proposition is to disprove it; to confute a person is to silence or overcome them by disproving what they maintain. Older use allowed a wider sense, to confute as to bring to naught, to check, to restrain — to confute evil counsels — but modern usage restricts the word to intellectual overthrow. The adjectives confutable (capable of being disproved) and inconfutable (rare, indisputable) and the noun confutation (the act or instance of confuting, a formal refutation) belong to the same formal register.

  • Logical sense — to disprove: to demonstrate the falsity of an argument by counter-evidence or by exposing fallacy. To confute a hypothesis with data, to confute idealism, to remain unconfuted. This is the dominant modern sense.
  • Rhetorical sense — to silence an opponent: to overcome a person in debate by refutation. He was publicly confuted. Slightly archaic in tone; modern debate prefers debunked, demolished, or shot down.
  • Obsolete sense — to check or restrain: 16th-century writers could confute pride, error, or rebellion in the sense of holding back or quelling. This sense, closest to the Latin original, has died, leaving only the refutational meaning.
  • Confuse with confound / confuse (warning): confute is not confuse. Generations of students have written confute for confuse and vice versa. To confute is to prove wrong; to confuse is to mix up; to confound is to overthrow or to mix thoroughly. The three share con- but diverge completely in object and outcome.

Register is formal, academic, and slightly archaic. Confute lives in philosophy, theology, law, and literary criticism — Bentley confuted Collins, Bradley confuted James — where refute is now the everyday equivalent. Its very rarity gives it force: to reach for confute rather than refute or disprove signals classical learning and finality. Typical frames are to confute decisively, to confute utterly, to attempt to confute, difficult to confute, easy to assert but hard to confute. The last frame is exactly the author's point: abstractions that cannot be confuted are not strong but empty — unfalsifiable rather than true.

  • Confute vs refute: effectively synonymous today, but purists reserve refute for successful disproof and rebut for counter-argument; confute has always implied success — a confutation that fails is a contradiction in terms.
  • Confute vs rebut / counter: rebuttal answers arguments; confutation destroys them. Rebuttal leaves the field standing; confutation clears it.
  • Confute vs debunk: debunking exposes pretension with flair and publicity; confuting proves error with logic and evidence, often quietly.

In sum, confute is refutation at its most complete: not to disagree, not to doubt, but to close the case — to leave the opposed opinion with nowhere left to stand, at least in the abstract.
