pang (noun) names a sudden, sharp, piercing spasm of pain — physical or emotional — that strikes without warning, is intense for a moment, and then passes, though it may recur. The word's essence is brevity and point-like intensity: a pang is the opposite of a dull ache or a lingering sorrow. In the literary context — “A pang of fear and loss, like an echo of Gollum's misery, smote Bilbo, and forgetting even to draw his sword he struck his hands into his pockets” — the word captures the instantaneous, darting stab of empathy that pierces Bilbo when he glimpses Gollum's endless misery: the fear and loss are not a settled grief but a quick, involuntary spasm of the heart, so sharp that it suspends even self-preservation for a moment. The comparison to an echo is apt: like sound rebounding, the pang arrives suddenly, strikes, and dies away, leaving a residue of sorrow. The nuance of pang is its brevity and its point-like intensity, and the tone of the sentence is of sudden moral insight registered at the level of physical sensation: pity as a wound. The word belongs to a slightly literary register and pairs naturally with conscience, hunger, childbirth, loss, regret, and longing in fixed collocations.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings — the word is sharply polysemous between the physical and the moral:

  • Physical pang (16th c. onwards) — a sudden, sharp, piercing spasm of bodily pain, above all the intermittent spasms of childbirth: “the pangs of labour” (recorded from the 1580s). It is also used of the cramps of hunger: “the pangs of hunger”. The physical pang is brief, point-like, and intensely localized.
  • Moral and emotional pang (16th c. onwards) — a sudden stab of feeling: “pangs of conscience” (1530s) for the mind's pinching remorse; pangs of guilt, regret, love, jealousy, loss, longing, and nostalgia. This is the dominant modern use, and it crosses freely between body and mind: the moral pang is felt as a physical twinge in the heart or stomach.
  • Collective and plural use — pangs in the plural suggests a repeated, racking series of spasms, especially of childbirth or hunger, as in “the pangs of the starving city”. The singular names one sharp instant.
  • Figurative extension in modern idiom — “a pang of nostalgia”, “a pang of envy”, “a pang of guilt” — the brief, sharp reawakening of a feeling, distinct from a settled or chronic emotion. The word is chosen precisely when the emotion is sudden, involuntary, and short-lived.
  • Obsolete and dialectal senses — the earlier English form prange (mid-15th c.) meant “a sharp, pinching, throbbing pain”; English dialect kept prang, “a sharp pain” and “to pinch”. The relative prong, “a pointed projecting spike” (15th c.), is probably the same Low German word for a pinching instrument.


Among near-synonyms, each shades differently:

  • twinge: similar in a sudden, brief, sharp pain; differs because a twinge is slighter and more fleeting, and rarely carries pang's full emotional depth or fixed collocations like “the pangs of hunger”.
  • spasm: similar in a sudden intense contraction of pain; differs because a spasm stresses involuntary muscular action and may last long, whereas a pang is short and point-like.
  • stab: similar in a sudden piercing pain; differs because a stab is more violent, localized, and physical, with none of pang's easy crossing into moral feeling.
  • throe: similar in a violent pang; differs because a throe is prolonged and severe, associated with childbirth and the death-agony, where a pang is brief.
  • ache: similar in being felt pain; differs because an ache is dull, continuous, and diffuse — the exact opposite of pang's sharp, brief, piercing suddenness.
  • qualm: similar in a sudden pang of misgiving; differs because a qualm is restricted to scruples and the conscience, while pang ranges freely over physical and emotional pain.


In register, pang is now slightly literary and old-fashioned, though still perfectly current in the collocations above. It is the noun English reserves for the sudden, involuntary, piercing arrivals of pain and feeling, and its pinching, squeezing etymology (see below) remains faintly audible in the way a pang feels like a clamp on the flesh or the heart.
