Basque (RP /bɑːsk/, also /bæsk/) is an English noun, adjective and proper noun naming the people of the western Pyrenees and their language. It is a borrowing from French Basque, itself from Gascon Occitan basc, from Latin Vascō, Vascōnēs, the name of a pre-Roman tribe of the region. The word is capitalized in all its ethnic and linguistic uses; the historically related garment noun basque is usually lower-case.

1. The people. As an ethnonym, Basque denotes a member of the Basque people (Euskaldunak in their own language), who live in the western Pyrenees on both sides of the Franco-Spanish border. The historical Basque Country (Euskal Herria) comprises seven provinces: Biscay (Bizkaia), Gipuzkoa, Álava (Araba) and Navarre (Nafarroa) in Spain, and Labourd (Lapurdi), Lower Navarre (Nafarroa Beherea) and Soule (Zuberoa) in France. The population of the Basque Country is more than two million, and a large diaspora, especially in Latin America and the United States, preserves the name. The adjective Basque describes anything relating to this people, its territory or its culture.

2. The language. Basque is also the English name of Euskara, the language of the Basques and the most famous language isolate in Europe. It has no demonstrated genealogical relationship to any other living language, and it is generally regarded as a survival of the pre-Indo-European linguistic layer of western Europe. The extinct Aquitanian language, attested in personal names and short inscriptions in south-western Gaul, is widely thought to be related to or ancestral to Basque, though the evidence is fragmentary. Basque is agglutinative, marks ergative-absolutive alignment, has a rich case system and no grammatical gender, and uses a vigesimal numeral system. Its six main dialect groups are Biscayan, Gipuzkoan, Upper Navarrese, Lapurdian, Lower Navarrese and Souletin; the standard language, euskara batua, was codified from the late 1960s by the Royal Academy of the Basque Language (Euskaltzaindia). The first printed book in Basque, Bernard Etxepare's Linguae Vasconum Primitiae, appeared in 1545, and a substantial literature, from Axular's Gero (1643) to modern writers such as Bernardo Atxaga, continues today.

3. The adjective and cultural senses. In English, Basque functions as a relational adjective with a wide cultural range:

  • Geography and politics: the Basque Country, the Basque provinces, Basque autonomy, Basque nationalism.
  • Culture and sport: Basque cuisine (pintxos, salt cod, txakoli), Basque pelota (a family of court games, including jai alai), the Basque beret (txapela), Basque dancing and Basque singing.
  • History and anthropology: Basque whalers, Basque shepherds, the Basque-Icelandic pidgin recorded in seventeenth-century whaling contact, and the Basque diaspora.
  • Linguistics: Basque as a language isolate, Proto-Basque, the Vasconic hypothesis, and the Basque substrate in Romance toponymy.

4. The garment sense. A separate but historically connected noun, usually written basque, denotes a woman's fitted bodice or corset-like undergarment that extends from the bust or shoulders to the hips. It entered English in the nineteenth century from French basquine, a garment associated with the Basque region. The lower-case spelling distinguishes the garment from the people and language, although both ultimately point to the same place name.

5. The semantic bridge. The path from tribe to language to adjective to cultural product is a familiar pattern in ethnonyms: a name for a people becomes the name of the language they speak, then a general adjective, then a label for things culturally associated with them. The garment sense is a separate branch, a French regionalism that migrated into English fashion vocabulary. The modern political sense, Basque as a marker of identity and autonomy, is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century development, but it rests on the much older ethnic name.

6. Usage and register. In careful English, Basque refers to people, language and culture; Euskara or Euskera is the preferred name for the language in Basque and in much scholarly writing. Euskaldun means a Basque speaker, literally "one who has Basque". The Spanish forms vasco and vascuence and the French basque are the immediate continental relatives. The English pronunciation varies between /bɑːsk/ and /bæsk/; both are accepted in RP, and the word is homophonous with bask in the second pronunciation. The capitalized form is standard for the ethnonym and glossonym; the garment is normally lower-case.

7. Technical and modern uses. In linguistics, Basque is a standard example of a language isolate and of a surviving pre-Indo-European language. The Vasconic hypothesis extends the Basque family to Aquitanian and to a postulated substrate in western Romance; it is plausible but not proved. In genetics and anthropology, Basque is often used in discussions of European prehistory, sometimes with overstated claims about purity or continuity. In tourism and gastronomy, Basque has become a brand: Basque cheesecake, Basque cider houses, Basque design. These commercial uses are extensions of the ethnic adjective, not new senses of the underlying name. The political history of the word, from the Carlist wars through the Franco dictatorship to the ETA conflict and the 2018 dissolution of ETA, has given Basque a strong association with questions of identity, language rights and autonomy.

8. Register and sensitivity. The word is neutral in English, but in Spain and France it can be politically loaded. Some Basque speakers prefer euskaldun for a person and euskara for the language, reserving vasco or basque for official or external contexts. The English adjective is not derogatory, but careless use of Basque as a synonym for separatist or terrorist is both inaccurate and offensive. The garment sense is entirely separate and stylistically neutral in fashion writing.
