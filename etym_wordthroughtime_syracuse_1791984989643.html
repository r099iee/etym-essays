- 16th c. — English readers meet Syracuse in translations of Thucydides and Plutarch, where it is chiefly the place that destroyed the Athenian expedition; the name belongs to the rhetoric of tragic overreach.
- 17th–18th c. — The Grand Tour and antiquarian travel make it a station in Sicily; Goethe's Italian Journey, written up after his 1786–88 travels, describes the fountain of Arethusa and the papyrus beds and fixes the romantic image.
- 19th c. — Malaria, rural poverty and emigration reduce the name to a provincial curiosity, though classical scholars keep it alive through the study of the siege and of Archimedes.
- 1943 — The Allied landings in south-eastern Sicily put Syracuse briefly back on the front pages, as a point on a military map rather than a city of legend.
- 21st c. — UNESCO status, the Greek theatre festival and the Baroque rebuild of Ortigia turn the name into a destination for cultural tourism, while “Syracuse” without qualification increasingly means the city in New York State.
