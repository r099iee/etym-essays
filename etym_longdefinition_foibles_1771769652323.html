In the passage from which this card is drawn — "Not least was his inexhaustible sense of wonder and his tolerant curiosity about the foibles of the human race" — the word "foibles" names the small, almost endearing cracks in human character: the petty vanities, quirks, and minor failings that mark people as human rather than heroic. The surrounding phrase, "tolerant curiosity about the foibles of the human race," frames these weaknesses not as moral indictments but as objects of amused, scholarly interest. The tone is gentle and observational: the subject of the sentence is cast as someone who watched mankind's little faults with wonder rather than severity, so the word carries warmth, mild irony, and a humane distance. It is the characteristic word of the essayist and the biographer who loves human beings precisely for their imperfections — Montaigne's essayism, Addison's Spectator papers, and the tolerant comedy of the eighteenth-century moralists all live in this word. At its core, a "foible" is a minor weakness or eccentricity in a person's character: a small fault of temperament or habit that is too slight to be called a vice and too personal to be called a mere error. Foibles are the little things that make a person recognizable — the harmless vanity, the superstition, the endearing stubbornness, the comic preference. To catalogue someone's foibles is not to condemn them but to sketch them, to draw the outline of their humanity. The word is nearly always used with affection, indulgence, or at worst gentle mockery; it sits at the opposite pole from words that describe serious moral failure. The word repays polysemous attention because its history is a series of meaning-shifts from a physical object to a moral abstraction:
  • The fencing term (the original literal sense): in the technical vocabulary of swordsmanship, the "foible" is the weaker half of a sword blade — the part from the middle to the point, which is less stiff and less strong than the forte, the strong half near the hilt. A fencer parries with the forte and attacks with the foible; the word is borrowed from French, where foible (now faible) means "weak," so the foible is literally "the weak part" of the blade. This is the sense that survives in the dictionaries of fencing and in historical discussions of swordplay.
  • The transferred sense, weakness of character (17th century): the metaphor transfers the "weak part of the blade" to the "weak part of a person" — the point in a character at which it bends, yields, or breaks. This is the sense that has become the ordinary one: a foible is a moral or temperamental weak point, a minor failing of judgment, temper, or will.
  • The softened modern sense, eccentricity / endearing quirk (18th century onward): from mere weakness the word slides toward the charming or amusing eccentricity — the harmless oddity that one smiles at. "Everyone has their little foibles" is the register: not blameworthy faults but endearing imperfections. In this sense the word is usually plural (foibles) and always affectionate.
  • Figurative extension to things: by extension, "foible" can name the weak or vulnerable point of any system, argument, institution, or plan — "the foibles of the legal system," "the foibles of human nature" — where the human sense shades into "the inherent weakness of the thing."
  • Professional register, literary use: in biographical and critical writing, "foibles" is a standing word for the minor human weaknesses that biographers record with affection and humor — the "human foibles" that make great figures approachable. The collocations "human foibles," "personal foibles," "the little foibles," and "everyone's little foibles" are all standard.
The semantic bridge between the fencing sense and the moral sense is the notion of weakness and bending. In the blade, the foible is the part that bends most easily, the part least able to resist pressure; in the character, the foible is the point that bends most easily, the part least able to resist temptation, vanity, or habit. The image is exact and physical: just as the sword gives way at its foible, so the person gives way at theirs. The word therefore never describes a crime or a serious vice — those are faults of the "forte," the strong part of the blade, willed and rooted — but only the small places where the metal of the character is thin, where it yields with a smile rather than with a decision. Register distinguishes "foible" sharply from its neighbors. A "vice" is a settled, habitual immorality — serious, blameworthy, sometimes depraved. A "flaw" is broader and more neutral, usable of character, of objects, of arguments, and it can be structural or even tragic (a "fatal flaw"), where a foible is always minor and endearing. A "failing" can be serious — a failing is any falling short, from a small lapse to a grave dereliction — whereas a foible is by definition small. A "shortcoming" stresses practical inadequacy, a failure to meet a standard of performance, while a "quirk" stresses eccentricity of manner with no moral content at all. A "defect" is a structural or physical imperfection, cold and objective. "Frailty" is the word for human weakness in general, with a biblical and tragic ring, whereas "foible" is the word for the weakness one half expects and half smiles at. The whole family maps a gradient from the grave to the amused, and "foible" sits at the amused end — the word of the tolerant observer, exactly as it appears in the sentence on this card, where the "foibles of the human race" are the object of an inexhaustible wonder.
