- Roman — poured together and ruined: Latin confundere means to mix by pouring and, by extension, to destroy and bring to ruin — mixture as annihilation.
- c. 1300 — English borrows the mixture: confounden means to mix up, to mistake one thing for another, and to overthrow enemies — confusion and defeat in one verb.
- 14th–16th c. — theology's favourite: Babel's tongues, confounded pride, confounded popery — preachers wield it against enemies of every doctrine.
- 17th c. — the curse softens into use: confound him becomes the respectable man's damn, bleaching toward mere irritation.
- 18th–19th c. — comedy claims it: stage grumblers and novelists' uncles cry confounded at weather, servants, and visitors — damnation in carpet slippers.
- Today — two settled halves: confounded by (perplexed, formal-neutral) and confounded + noun (that confounded thing, comic-period grumble) — mixture of mind and mildness of curse.
