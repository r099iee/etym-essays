Hiccups (singular hiccup) are involuntary, spasmodic contractions of the diaphragm followed by sudden closure of the glottis, producing the characteristic “hic” sound. In ordinary usage the plural names an episode of such spasms; figuratively it names small, temporary interruptions in a process, plan, or system.

1. The physiological sense

A hiccup begins when the diaphragm contracts abruptly and the vocal cords snap shut a fraction of a second later. The inrush of air is stopped, and the closure produces the familiar sound. Episodes are usually brief and harmless, often triggered by eating too quickly, carbonated drinks, sudden temperature change, alcohol, or emotional excitement. The medical Latin term is singultus, literally a sob or catching of the breath.

  • Mechanism: diaphragmatic spasm plus glottic closure.
  • Common triggers: rapid eating, fizzy drinks, spicy food, alcohol, stress, temperature change.
  • Usual course: self-limiting, lasting minutes.

2. Clinical classification and serious causes

Physicians divide hiccups by duration: transient (under 48 hours), persistent (48 hours to one month), and intractable (over one month). Persistent or intractable cases can signal underlying disease — gastro-oesophageal reflux, diaphragmatic irritation, central nervous system lesions, metabolic disturbance, or certain drugs. In the source passage, Bertram is “highly liquid” and gives “a couple of hiccups,” a comic signal of drunkenness rather than pathology.

3. The figurative sense

Since the twentieth century “hiccup” has been used metaphorically for a minor glitch or temporary interruption: a hiccup in the negotiations, a hiccup in the supply chain, a few hiccups during the launch. The metaphor is apt because a hiccup is sudden, brief, involuntary, and usually harmless. In business and journalism the plural “hiccups” is a favourite way of downplaying problems without denying them.

  • Minor setback: “the project had a few hiccups.”
  • Technical glitch: “a hiccup in the software.”
  • Market wobble: “a temporary hiccup in demand.”

4. Spelling: hiccup versus hiccough

The modern spelling hiccup is echoic and transparent. The variant hiccough, which appeared in the seventeenth century, is a mistaken respelling by analogy with cough; it does not reflect the word’s history and is now chiefly British and formal. Both are pronounced /ˈhɪkʌp/; hiccough is not pronounced like cough. The plural hiccups follows the regular English pattern.

5. Verb and grammatical behaviour

As a verb, to hiccup means to make the sound or to suffer an episode: “he hiccuped loudly.” The participle hiccuping (also hiccupping) is common. The noun is countable: “a hiccup,” “several hiccups.” In figurative use the plural is especially frequent because setbacks tend to come in batches.

6. Semantic bridge

The literal and figurative senses share four features: suddenness, brevity, involuntariness, and relative harmlessness. Those features make the word a natural metaphor for any small disruption that interrupts but does not derail. The comic potential is also shared: hiccups are faintly ridiculous, and so are many organisational mishaps.

7. Register and collocations

  • Common collocations: a fit of hiccups, a bad case of hiccups, the hiccups, a few hiccups, hiccups in the process.
  • Register: neutral and informal in the literal sense; journalistic and managerial in the figurative sense.
  • Avoid the misspelling hicup; the standard forms are hiccup and the older hiccough.
  • “The hiccups” can name a persistent attack, as in “he’s got the hiccups.”
