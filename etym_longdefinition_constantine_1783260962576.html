Constantine (prop. n.) — the name, borne most famously by Flavius Valerius Constantinus, Constantine the Great, Roman emperor from 306 to 337 AD. He is celebrated as the first Christian emperor: he legalised Christianity after his victory at the Milvian Bridge (312), confirmed toleration in the Edict of Milan (313), convened the first ecumenical Council of Nicaea (325), and refounded Byzantium as his new eastern capital, Constantinople (330). In this card's context, "the splendour of Diocletian and Constantine" denotes the late-imperial court at its most magnificent — the court that still exhibited, even in a slowly fading civilisation, the pomp of the ancient world.

Primary sense (the historical emperor). The name in historical writing normally means Constantine the Great, son of Constantius I Chlorus, proclaimed emperor at York in 306, ruler of the whole empire from 324. His reign marks the turning point at which Christianity passed from a persecuted minority faith to the favoured and eventually dominant religion of the empire — the decisive event of the "Constantinian shift."

Polysemy & secondary modern meanings:

  • The "Constantinian shift" (theological and historiographical term) — modern scholarship and church history use "Constantinian shift" for the fusion of church and state under and after Constantine: the church exchanged its persecuted status for imperial patronage and, in consequence, absorbed the empire's power structures. The semantic bridge from the personal name to this abstract noun is metonymy: the emperor's name stands for the entire era and its consequences.
  • Constantinian dynasty and imperial title — the name was borne by later rulers (Constantine II, Constantius II, Constans) and became a dynastic emblem; in Byzantine usage it descended through Constantine V to Constantine XI Palaiologos, the last emperor, who died defending Constantinople in 1453. The name thus frames the whole life of the Byzantine Empire — its foundation and its fall.
  • Constantinople and city naming — the capital he founded bore his name: Greek Kōnstantinoúpolis (Κωνσταντινούπολις), "city of Constantine," now Istanbul; "Constantinople" is the standard English historical form. Semantic bridge: the emperor's name passed into geography, and the city-name in turn became shorthand for the Eastern Roman/Byzantine world.
  • The Arch of Constantine, churches, and monuments — the triumphal arch in Rome (312–315), the Lateran and the great basilicas he built; "Constantinian" is the adjective for his architecture, his legislation, and his religious policy.
  • Constantine in religious polemic and legend — in Christian tradition he is "equal to the Apostles" (Greek Orthodox usage) and the bringer of peace after persecution; in Reformation and Enlightenment polemic he is sometimes the corruptor of the pure gospel through his entanglement of church and state. The Donation of Constantine, a forged 8th-century document purporting to grant the papacy temporal power, made the name a battlefield of Renaissance and Reformation scholarship (exposed by Lorenzo Valla, 1440).
  • Given name and namesakes — a common given name across the Christian world (Konstantin in Slavic and Greek, Costantino in Italian, Constantino in Spanish/Portuguese), reflecting the emperor's standing as the first Christian sovereign; also place-names (e.g., Constantine in Algeria, formerly Cirta).
  • Modern cultural resonance — "Constantine" in film and literature (e.g., the film Constantine, 2005) plays on the name's occult and imperial grandeur; historians routinely use "the Constantinian era" for the fourth century and its church-state synthesis.

Etymology in brief and semantic bridge. The name derives from Latin Cōnstantīnus, "belonging to Constans," formed on cōnstāns "standing firm, steadfast" (cōnstāre "to stand together, hold firm," from con- "together" + stāre "to stand"; PIE *steh₂- "to stand") plus the suffix -īnus. The emperor's name thus literally means "the steadfast one," and the virtue of constantia (steadfastness) — prized in Roman moral thought — is built into the word. That etymological core of firmness resonates through the historical reputation: Constantine the unshaken, the founder who fixed the empire's religion and its capital. The name's journey from a common Latin adjective of moral praise to the emblem of Christian empire, and then to a city, a dynasty, and a historiographical turning-point, is the fullest example on this card of a proper name hardening into an epoch.
