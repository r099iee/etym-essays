The history of "feverishly" is the history of the word "fever" wearing three successive coats. The chain runs: Latin febris → Old English fēfor → Middle English fever → the English adjective feverish (14th century) → the English adverb feverishly (late 17th century). Each stage is attested and each represents a genuine semantic event.

1. The Latin root febris and the Indo-European fire. Latin febris ("fever, the burning sickness") is widely, though not universally, derived from the PIE root *dʰegʷʰ-, "to burn, to be hot" — the same root that yields Latin fovēre ("to warm, cherish"), Sanskrit dahati ("burns"), and, via "the burning one", the Old English word for day (dæg). If this etymology holds — and the phonology and semantics fit — then the heat of fever is conceptually continuous with the heat of the sun and the hearth across the whole Indo-European-speaking world. The alternative minority view connects febris with fervēre ("to boil, seethe", from *bʰeru-), which would make fever the "boiling" of the blood. Both derivations agree on the core intuition: fever is fire in the body. The Romans, whose medicine was deeply Greek and humoral, regarded febris as a divine affliction with its own goddess — the deified Febris had temples in Rome where the sick left votive offerings — a superstition that underlines how central the concept was to ancient life and how old the association of fever with a burning, quasi-supernatural heat really is.

2. The journey into English: Old English fēfor. English did not inherit a native Germanic word for "fever"; the Anglo-Saxons spoke of hriþ (shivers) and used fever-compounds, and could also say brȳne (a burning, inflammation). The word fēfor (also fēfer) was a learned borrowing from Latin febris, travelling through the school and the monastery to appear in the medical and herbal glossaries of the 10th–11th centuries, where "fever" is treated as one of the four humoral disorders inherited from classical medicine. The borrowed form shows typical Old English reshaping — Latin febris, an i-stem with an unusual ending, was rebuilt as the masculine a-stem fēfor — and it coexisted with native descriptive compounds such as fēforlīċ ("feverish"), so that the sick Anglo-Saxon was already described in the vocabulary that would eventually yield both "fever" and "feverish".

3. Middle English fever and the humoral world. By the 14th century the word appears as fever, fevir, feure in Chaucer, in the surgical and medical writings of John of Arderne, and in monastic herbals. Medieval medicine inherited Galen's system in full: fevers were classified by their period (quotidian, tertian, quartan) and explained as putrefied humours boiling in the blood. The word remained strictly clinical — phlegm, chills, sweating, crises — and its figurative potential stayed dormant for centuries. One crucial 14th-century development is the adjective feverish itself, formed with the native suffix -ish ("inclined to, of the nature of"); it initially meant only "afflicted with fever".

4. The figurative turn: 16th–18th centuries. The Renaissance, with its renewed humoral psychology, began to re-describe emotions as fevers of the soul. "Feverish" migrates from the body to the mind — "feverish dreams", "feverish passions", "feverish imagination" appear in the prose and drama of Shakespeare's age, and Elizabethan tragedy repeatedly images passion as a burning sickness that medicine cannot cure (Macbeth's anguished question about the "mind diseased" belongs to the same humoral world in which fever and frenzy were one family). When the adverb feverishly is coined in the late 17th century, it is born already carrying both registers: physicians use it literally, poets metaphorically. The age of the coffeehouse and the financial bubble filled periodical prose with "feverish" speculation, so that by 1700 the word was already an instrument of social description as much as of medicine.

5. The Victorian consolidation. The 19th century did the decisive work. Industrial journalism, the sensation novel, and the new sciences of energy and heat all conspired to make "feverish activity" a staple phrase. Newspapers described markets in "feverish excitement", crowds in "feverish haste", and the age of steam itself as "feverish motion". Charles Dickens, the great anatomist of urban nerves, repeatedly deploys fever imagery for the restless city — the fevered streets, the feverish pursuit of wealth — and the adverb feverishly became the natural intensifier of 19th-century effort: work that burns like a sickness. Meanwhile the literal sense remained vivid: the great fever hospitals of the early 19th century (the London Fever Hospital among the first) kept "feverish" clinically honest, and the century that perfected the thermometer gave the word a measurable object — the fever chart with its spiking line.

6. The modern word. In the 20th–21st centuries the balance tilted decisively toward the figurative. Antibiotics and vaccines removed fever from everyday mortal dread, while the pace of industrial, commercial, and creative life made "working feverishly" a permanent fixture of the language — "crews worked feverishly to restore power", "she wrote feverishly through the night", "fans searched feverishly for tickets". The medical sense survives in clinical prose, but the adverb's default habitat is the metaphor. Its emotional temperature, however, is unchanged: something of the old humoral fire, the ancient conviction that intense effort heats the blood beyond the body's calm, still glows inside every "feverish" sentence — a three-thousand-year-old metaphor that still runs at fever pitch.
