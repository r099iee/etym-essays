The compound gaily-dressed is a small museum of English semantic history: each of its two components carries a long and distinct biography — one reaching back to Frankish speech and a word that would become, centuries later, one of the most contested adjectives in the language; the other descending from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “to rule” that somehow ended up naming the act of putting on clothes.

Part one: gaily — from “swift” to “cheerful” to “bright.” The history of the adverb begins in the darkness of Frankish speech, with a reconstructed adjective *gāhi meaning “swift, sudden, impetuous.” Its reflexes in Old High German (gāhi, “quick, sudden”) show that Germanic speakers used the word for speed and vehemence; yet the word that entered Romance was, astonishingly, a word of joy and colour. In Old Provençal and Old French the adjective gai meant “joyful, merry, light-hearted” and, by easy extension, “splendid, brightly coloured” — a connection that modern English still half-heartedly preserves in “gay colours” and “gay apparel.” Exactly how a word of speed became a word of joy is debated: some scholars see the leap through the notion of high spirits (gaiety as liveliness, quickness of mood), others suspect the Romance colour-sense developed independently among the troubadours. What is certain is that the Middle English gay, borrowed in the 12th–13th century, carried “joyful, light-hearted, splendid, bright” as its full meaning, and that the adverb gaily (attested from the 14th century) crystallized “cheerfully, brightly, showily.” In the 18th and 19th centuries, “gaily” was a standard intensifier of appearance in poetry and fiction — gaily painted boats, gaily singing crowds, gaily dressed maids — with an entirely positive colouring: brightness of hue married to brightness of spirit.

Part two: dressed — from “to rule” to “to clothe.” The second component descends from Proto-Indo-European *reg-, “to move in a straight line; to direct, to rule.” In Latin this became regere, “to rule, direct, guide, set straight,” and from it the Romans built the prefixed verb dirigere, “to set straight, to arrange,” whose past participle directus meant “straight, arranged, directed.” In the post-classical period, a derived verb directiare (“to direct, set straight, arrange”) passed into Old French as drecier, which meant “to set up, raise, arrange, put in order” — and, crucially, began to mean “to put in order” in the most ordinary domestic sense: to prepare, to array, to make ready. Middle English borrowed the verb in the 13th century as dressen, and by the 14th century the “arranging” sense had settled into its modern core: to clothe, to attire a person, to array oneself. The semantic journey is almost comic in its indirection — a verb meaning “to keep the world straight and ordered” gradually narrowed to the particular ordering act of putting clothes on a body — but it is a textbook example of how Latin administrative vocabulary was democratized and domesticated in medieval French and English. The past participle dressed (“clothed, attired,” 14th century onward) is the exact form the compound requires.

The compound: when gaily met dressed. Hyphenated compounds of adverb plus participle (“gaily-dressed,” “finely-dressed,” “richly-clad”) are an old pattern of English attributive adjectives, and “gaily-dressed” flourished in the 18th and 19th centuries, the age in which the two components’ meanings aligned perfectly: bright colour (gaily) + festive attire (dressed) = cheerful, showy clothing. In Victorian fiction, gaily-dressed crowds, gaily-dressed children, and gaily-dressed maids appear in street scenes, festivals, and moments of household display — the word almost always signals prosperity and social order made visible, exactly as it does in the translated Chinese scene of this card, where the bright dress of serving-girls proclaims the wealth and good management of a great clan mansion. The compound is thus a period phrase: semantically transparent, socially informative, and unselfconscious in its cheerfulness.

The modern collision and the word’s retreat. No history of this word can end without the 20th-century revolution. As gay, from about the 1920s–1960s, acquired its primary modern association with homosexuality (a development itself rooted in older senses of sexual freedom and looseness), the adverb gaily and its compounds withdrew from neutral use. Writers and speakers, aware of the collision, began to avoid “gaily-dressed,” which now sounds dated, precious, or deliberately historical; it survives chiefly in period fiction, in translations of older literature (as here), and in nostalgic or ironic registers. The semantic bridge of the original word — bright clothing as the outward sign of joy — has been severed for most modern readers, who must consciously re-historicize the adjective to feel its cheerful colour. The compound remains grammatically transparent and perfectly intelligible, but its cultural resonance is now that of a museum piece: a faithful witness to the bright, cheerful, self-confident dress of an older age, and to the strange power of one small word (gay) to recolor an entire family of phrases.
