Cringed, the past tense and participle of cringe, names the flinch of the whole body before something feared, painful, or shameful: to shrink back, crouch, or cower, bending away from a blow, a scolding, or a memory. In its oldest sense it is purely physical — the body folding to present a smaller target — and in its modern extension it is inward, the wince of embarrassment we feel at someone else's folly.

In the source sentence the prisoner whined and cringed under pressing, rubbing his long hands and licking his fingers as if remembering some old torture. The verb does double duty: it reports the visible cower — the crouch of a creature expecting to be struck — and it diagnoses the moral posture, servile and evasive, of one who hopes that shrinking will deflect further questioning. The rubbing hands complete the portrait: guilt, fear, and old pain made flesh.

Core meaning in detail: To cringe is to contract the body defensively: shoulders hunch, head ducks, knees bend, the whole frame yielding sideways or downward from the threat. Grammarians distinguish the transitive causative, now archaic (to cringe the body), from the everyday intransitive (he cringed at the blow, she cringed at the memory). The past form cringed freezes the flinch as fact — he cringed — whether the trigger was a raised hand, a dentist's drill, or, in the newest use, a second-hand embarrassment so acute the witness feels it in the spine.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Cowering physically before threat or pain (primary sense, from c. 1200): the dog cringed from the stick, the prisoner cringed under questioning. The bridge from bending is literal: the body yields and folds, offering the smallest possible surface to harm.
  • Behaving servilely, fawning (early figurative extension): cringing courtiers, a cringed and fawning manner — flattery as a full-body posture. The bridge carries the physical crouch into social hierarchy: the inferior bends before the superior exactly as the body bends before the blow.
  • Wincing inwardly at embarrassment — cringe as noun and adjective (modern, late 20th c.): that interview was pure cringe, cringe comedy, I cringed so hard. The bridge moves the flinch inside: the same contractive reflex now answers social pain — someone else's awkwardness — rather than physical danger, and the noun names the feeling itself.
  • Yielding or giving way structurally (archaic, rare): old writers speak of cringing boughs and cringing walls, meaning bending under weight. The bridge is the earliest root sense — simply to bend — surviving in inanimate subjects before the fear-shades crowded it out.
  • Contracting with cold or discomfort (marginal): travellers cringed in the wind, sleepers cringed under thin blankets. The bridge is postural: the body folds against cold as against blows, and the same verb serves both weathers.

Register and connotation: cringed is pitiless — narrators deploy it to strip dignity from its subject, and the source sentence is no exception. Where flinch can be involuntary and honourable, cringe implies a moral verdict: the cringer is servile, evasive, or ridiculous, and the modern noun sharpens the knife by making the observer, not the sufferer, the judge. Pity survives only in the torture-memory reading, where the cower testifies to old pain rather than present baseness.
