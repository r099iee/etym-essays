The biography of slops begins not with waste but with motion. The Proto-Indo-European root *sleubh- means "to slip, slide, glide," a verb for the effortless, slightly dangerous movement of a thing losing its grip on a surface — a foot on wet stone, a cloak slipping from a shoulder, a liquid slithering out of its container. The Germanic branch carried this root forward as *sleupan-, "to slip, creep, slide," and from an early date the family split into two directions that still govern the modern words: the motion sense (to slip, to creep) and the result sense (that which has slipped out of its proper place).

From slipping to spilled

The split is partly grammatical — an ablaut difference between the full grade *sleubh- and the o-grade *slaub- — but it becomes fully semantic. English slip keeps the pure motion: a foot slips, a secret slips out, a person slips away. But Old English also had sloppe, "dung, slime, mire," built on the o-grade, and here is the first decisive shift: from "that which slides" to "filth that has slid out of place." The root's own logic does the work — anything that slips from the body, from the pan, from the bucket becomes waste — and slop is that spillage given a noun. Middle English sloppe broadened to "a puddle, a muddy place, slush," and the noun slop came to mean any watery mess or liquid refuse.

The plural takes charge

It was the plural that carried the word into its most intimate territory. From the 16th century onward, slops settled into the household as the name for the collected liquid waste of daily life: dishwater, the washings of kitchen and sickroom, and above all, in the language of nursing, the soiled water and bodily refuse emptied from beside a bedridden patient. The plural is essential — it marks recurrence, the day-in, day-out rhythm of intake and evacuation around a convalescent — and it is precisely this sense that the passage on the card deploys when the tavern keeper's wife "carries out his slops." In old domestic and medical writing the word is entirely unembarrassed: a slop-basin, slop-pail, or slop-jar was standard furniture of a sickroom, and to "carry out the slops" was plain, necessary labour — the unglamorous underside of care.

The clothing homograph

Meanwhile the same root, following the same image of things that "slip on," produced a second English word. Middle Low German slop, "a loose outer garment," was borrowed in the late Middle Ages; English slop came to mean a loose mantle and then, most durably, the baggy breeches worn in the 16th and 17th centuries — Shakespeare's "slops" are the wide trunk-hose of the day. In the 17th century the plural was taken up by the navy: "slops" became the ready-made garments — breeches, shirts, stockings — sold to sailors from the ship's stores, so that the word acquired a second, institutional life at sea while the other slops was being emptied at bedsides ashore. The two words are historically distinct loans from the same root, converging by accident of sound.

Sound-symbolic sprawl

Around both words the sound-symbolic frame sl- + a liquid vowel generated a whole echoic family: slush, slosh, slobber, slither, sludge, and the verb to slop. The frame itself says "wet, sliding, mess," and it has allowed the family to keep producing new members whenever English needs a word for careless wetness. Sloppy, first "wet and muddy" in the 19th century, extended to "careless, slovenly," and then — memorably — to "maudlin, embarrassingly sentimental," the emotional equivalent of liquid slopped out of its container.

Modern and institutional retreat — and return

In the 20th century the word retreated from public speech as institutions supplied neutral vocabulary: sewage, wastewater, effluent, liquid waste. Slops survives in dialect, in deliberately blunt literary usage, and in nursing memory; its very flatness has made it attractive to writers who want honesty without euphemism. And the 2020s produced a striking figurative revival: "slop" is now the colloquial name for low-quality, mass-generated digital content — AI-produced text and images that flood feeds — a new application of the oldest intuition that careless excess is waste. The card's sentence sits at the end of this long history: a word that means "what slips away or is slipped on," doing the unglamorous, honest work of naming what a sickroom actually produces, day after day, until the patient is well enough to leave.
