PIE *oi-no- "one, single" [reconstructed; c. 4500-2500 BCE; cognates: Sanskrit eka "one," Greek oine "the ace on a die," Old Irish oen "one," Germanic *ainaz, whence English one] > Latin unus "one" [attested from the 3rd century BCE] > Latin unio, unionis "oneness, unity; a single pearl" (noun) and unire "to make one, to unite" (verb) [Classical Latin, 1st century BCE] > Late Latin dis- + unire > disunire "to separate, to sever, to disunite" [Late Latin, c. 4th-6th century CE] > Old French desunir "to disunite" and the noun desunion "separation, discord" [c. 12th century] > Middle French desunion "disunion, rupture of alliance" [14th-16th century] > English DISUNION "state of separation, discord; lack of agreement" [first recorded late 16th century] Notes: the prefix dis- descends from an Indo-European deictic element meaning "in two directions, apart" (also seen in Greek dia); its exact PIE reconstruction is debated. The English noun was borrowed from French rather than coined natively, and its specifically political sense - "the feared breakup of the Union" - crystallized in American English of the 18th-19th centuries, long after the word entered the language.
