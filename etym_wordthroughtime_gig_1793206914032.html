- c. 1300 — a whirling toy: Middle English gigge is the spinning top, all hum and wobble, a nursery word for spin itself.
- 1500s — a flighty creature: gig stretches to anything giddy and skittish, unkindly including flighty young women; the spin becomes a judgement of character.
- 1791 — the fast little carriage: on improved turnpike roads gig becomes the light two-wheeled trap of doctors, dealers, and dashing young men; speed with a dash of showiness.
- 1820s — Cobbett's scorn: radical rural writers make the gig the emblem of town polish that cannot touch real country — useless in bye-lanes, the wrong machine for England.
- 1920s — the jazzman's night: Black American musicians' slang turns gig into a one-night musical engagement; the brevity and dash survive, the horse is gone.
- Today — the unit of precarious work: from jazz clubs to food delivery, a gig is any short paid job, and the gig economy is built from the word; the carriage lives on only in museums and novels.
