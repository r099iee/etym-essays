In the literary line "The artist is his conveyance, not his companion," the noun is metaphorical rather than vehicular: the artist is the medium or carrier through which an idea, vision, or higher intelligence is transported to the audience — not a personal fellow-traveler sharing the journey as an equal. The tone is coolly functional, almost mechanical. It strips the artist of companionable intimacy and assigns him the role of instrument: a means of transmission. The contrast with companion is deliberate; conveyance implies delivery of cargo, not shared experience.

Core meaning: the action or process of transporting someone or something from one place to another; by extension, any means of transport, and in law, the formal act or instrument by which property is transferred from one owner to another.

Senses and uses:
  • Act of transporting — the action or process of carrying people, goods, or information from one place to another: "the conveyance of goods by rail," "the conveyance of messages."
  • Means of transport (a vehicle) — any vehicle or mode of travel: "a horse-drawn conveyance," "a public conveyance"; historically a carriage, coach, ship, or ferry.
  • Legal transfer of property — the formal act, and especially the deed or instrument, by which title to real or personal property passes from one owner to another: "a deed of conveyance," "the conveyance of the property was completed yesterday."
  • Figurative / medium sense — whatever carries an idea, message, feeling, or spiritual influence to an audience: the artist as conveyance of vision or inspiration; language as a conveyance of thought.
  • Communication / transmission — the conveying of news, information, or meaning, close to "transmission" and "communication": "the conveyance of information."
  • Historic sense: escort or conduct — the older meaning of being escorted or conducted along a way, preserved in the verb's ancestry (Old French convoier, "to escort, accompany on the way") and flickering under the modern senses.
Polysemy and semantic bridges:
  • The bridge from "carrying" to "vehicle" is metonymy: that which performs the act of carrying comes to name the means — the carriage, ship, or bus becomes "a conveyance."
  • The legal sense is a specialization of the medieval Latin-influenced vocabulary of transfer: to "convey" property is to carry it over from one hand to another; the document that performs the carrying is the "conveyance." The law preserves the word's oldest, most literal machinery.
  • The figurative medium sense exploits the same imagery: an idea is "carried over" to an audience, so whatever carries it — an artist, a book, a language — is a conveyance. In the card's line, the artist is reduced to this carrying function, deliberately contrasted with the intimacy of a companion.
  • The historic "escort" sense (conducting someone along the way) links to convoy: a convoy is ships or vehicles escorted together along a route — the same root with the same prefix.
Boundary contrasts:
  • Vehicle — can be literal transport or a figurative medium, but is often more concrete or technical; it lacks the formal, legal-historical weight of conveyance.
  • Medium — stresses the channel of communication (paint, language, air) without the motion-of-carrying image.
  • Carrier — a bearer (of disease, messages, or goods), more logistical and rarely a person-as-instrument in elevated prose.
  • Transport — names the act or system of moving people or goods, rarely the person-as-instrument or the formal legal deed.
  • Transmission / transfer — emphasize the movement or handover itself rather than the means or the vehicle.
Collocations: means of conveyance; public conveyance; conveyance of property; deed of conveyance; mode of conveyance; the conveyance of information. Register splits: in everyday modern English the transport sense sounds slightly formal or dated ("horse-drawn conveyance"); in law it remains fully technical; in elevated literary prose the metaphorical use — person or art as the conveyance of mind or spirit — keeps a precise, slightly archaic dignity that suits critical or aesthetic argument.
