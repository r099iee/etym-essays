• facade: Similar because both refer to an outward surface that can conceal an underlying truth; differs because facade is an architectural metaphor (the front exterior of a building) emphasizing a deliberate, constructed, and often grand physical barrier erected to impress onlookers, whereas semblance is an abstract, delicate appearance or faint likeness that can occur naturally or unintentionally.
  • guise: Similar because both denote an assumed or external aspect; differs because guise emphasizes an intentional costume, disguise, or adopted role assumed to conceal identity or motive (e.g. under the guise of an inspector), while semblance refers to the visible likeness or fractional degree of a condition itself.
  • veneer: Similar because both suggest a thin, superficial surface layer covering something inferior; differs because veneer is a woodworking metaphor implying a paper-thin layer of fine wood glued over cheap timber, carrying stronger connotations of social shallowness or artificial polish.
  • simulacrum: Similar because both denote an image, likeness, or representation of something; differs because simulacrum is a heavy philosophical and critical term (from Jean Baudrillard) denoting a hyper-real copy or representation that replaces reality and possesses no genuine original referent, whereas semblance is an ordinary, flexible literary term for outward show or mimesis.
  • pretext: Similar because both can mask a hidden reality; differs because pretext is specifically an ostensible, fabricated justification or legal excuse offered to conceal the true motive for an action (e.g. using a border incident as a pretext for war), rather than a visual appearance.
  • likeness: Similar because both denote visual similarity or resemblance to an original; differs because likeness is inherently positive, neutral, and truthful, denoting an accurate portrait or recognizable family resemblance without implying falsity or cosmetic fragility.
  • masquerade: Similar because both involve false outward appearances; differs because masquerade invokes a carnival ball of costumed revelers, emphasizing an active, dramatic, and often collective game of deceit or social pretense.
  • mimesis: Similar because both relate to representational likeness in art; differs because mimesis is a technical philosophical concept in aesthetics (dating to Plato and Aristotle) describing the theoretical imitation or representation of nature in art, whereas semblance refers to the concrete visual appearance itself.
