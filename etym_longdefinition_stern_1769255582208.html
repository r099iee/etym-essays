stern (adjective; also a distinct noun): severe, strict, uncompromising, or grim in manner, appearance, tone, or discipline — a person, face, look, voice, law, or measure that shows no indulgence and will not bend.

1. Primary sense — severe and unrelenting

As an adjective, stern describes severity and firmness, especially in the exercise of authority and discipline:
  • of persons: "a stern judge," "a stern father" — someone who does not soften, waver, or show indulgence;
  • of expressions and features: "a stern face," "a stern look" — a hard, unsmiling, forbidding appearance;
  • of speech: "a stern voice," "a stern warning" — cold, firm, emphatic;
  • of rules, laws, and measures: "stern laws," "stern punishment," "stern measures" — harsh, exacting, unforgiving (exactly the sense in the source context, where Narnia's laws are stern and Miraz is a cruel man).
Key features of the primary sense:
  • Firmness without warmth: sternness is severity that does not yield to emotion or plea.
  • Association with authority: teachers, captains, judges, rulers, and parents are proverbially stern.
  • Not necessarily unjust: a stern ruler may be fair but hard; the word carries coldness, not corruption (contrast cruel, which adds malice and enjoyment of suffering).

2. Related and extended senses of the adjective

  • Stern opposition — resolute, unyielding resistance ("he faced stern opposition").
  • Stern reality / stern facts — the hard, unsparing, inescapable truth of a situation.
  • Sternness — the abstract noun for the quality.
  • Comparative/superlative: sterner, sternest.
  • Compounds: stern-faced, stern-visaged, stern-willed; and, in American English, stern-wheel (of a steamboat) and stern-chaser (a gun mounted at the rear of a vessel).

3. Secondary sense — the nautical noun (homograph, different etymology)

As a noun, the stern is the rearmost part of a ship, boat, or aircraft. "From stem to stern" means the whole length of a vessel. Semantic bridge and caution: this noun is an unrelated English word that happens to share the same form as the adjective. The adjective descends from a root meaning "stiff, rigid" (see etymology), while the nautical noun descends from a Germanic word for "steering" — the stern is the back end, where the steering gear (the rudder) was placed. There is a third, equally unrelated word: sternum, the breastbone, from Greek stérnon. These three must never be confused in etymology; they are homographs only.

4. Register, synonyms, and usage notes

  • Modern everyday speech tends to prefer strict (neutral) or harsh (harsher); stern carries a formal, slightly old-fashioned dignity and a clear moral temperature.
  • Distinguish the temperature: strict = rule-following; severe = harsh in degree; stern = grim, unyielding, forbidding in manner.
  • In literature, stern is the classic epithet of authority figures, divine judgement, the elements, and the sea.

5. Polysemy summary

  • (1) Adjective: severe, strict, uncompromising, grim — primary and only related sense family.
  • (2) Noun (homograph): the rear of a ship — a separate word from the steering root.
  • (3) Sternum (breastbone) — a further unrelated homograph from Greek.
  • The semantic bridge of the adjective is stiffness: a stern person or law is one that does not bend, exactly as a stiff body does not bend.
