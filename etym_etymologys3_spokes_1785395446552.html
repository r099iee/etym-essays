Spokes descends from a root whose first meaning was 'point'. The Proto-Indo-European root *spey-, 'sharp, pointed', extended with a -k- suffix as *spey-k-, is the ancestor of a wide family of pointed things across the western branches of the family. In Latin it yielded spīca, 'ear of grain', the pointed head of a wheat stalk, borrowed into English as the botanical and astronomical spica and, through Old Provençal espiga, as the homely spigot, a pointed wooden peg; a related Latin form spīna, 'thorn', gave English spine. The root shows no established reflex in Indo-Iranian, so there is no Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia branch to draw — a silence the chain records honestly rather than papering over. In Latin and elsewhere the common thread is unmistakable: a pointed thing, something that pierces or projects.

It is in Germanic that the root achieved its most exact specialization, and there it split into two sibling nouns. The form *spaikōn designated a splinter or pointed stick and, by an early specialization within a wheel-using culture, 'the spoke of a wheel', the pointed rod driven between hub and rim. The noun is shared across the Germanic languages — Old English spāca, Middle Dutch speke (Dutch speek), Old High German speihha (German Speiche, which also means 'radius bone') — so the wheel-spoke already had its name in the common Germanic era, well before the separate English and German records. Old English spāca passed straight into Middle English spoke, recorded from the thirteenth century, and into modern English spoke, whose plural spokes is the ordinary English plural of a word that has not changed its meaning in over a thousand years: the radial member of a wheel.

The sibling Germanic form *spīkō, 'spike, nail', produced English spike and was borrowed into early Slavic, where Russian спица (spítsa) still means both 'spoke' and 'knitting needle' — a Germanic wheel-word that went east and survived in Slavic long after its cousins had evolved. The two Germanic forms show the same root idea ('pointed rod') diverging into a wheel-part and a nail; English keeps both, so that spike and spoke remain visibly related in sound and sense.

The modern history of spokes is the history of the wheel itself. The wooden spoked cart-wheel was for millennia the standard object the word named; medieval and early modern English added the idiom 'to put a spoke in someone's wheel', from jamming a bar into a wheel to stop a cart, giving the word a metaphorical life meaning 'to thwart'. Then came the bicycle age. In the late nineteenth century the heavy wooden wheel gave way to the tensioned wire wheel, and spoke acquired its most familiar modern referent — the thin, tightly strung wires of a bicycle wheel — while the word's sense widened again to any regular radial pattern: spokes of light, spokes of a web, the 'hub-and-spoke' model of modern transport and networks. The false etymology of spokesman (from spoke, past tense of speak) shows how deeply the wheel-word has colonized the modern imagination.

Key stages and attestations:

  • PIE (reconstructed) — *spey- 'sharp, pointed', extended *spey-k-; Latin sisters spīca (ear of grain), spīna (thorn); no Indo-Iranian reflex.
  • Proto-Germanic (reconstructed) — *spaikōn 'splinter, pointed stick; spoke' and sibling *spīkō 'spike'; the wheel-spoke is already named in the common Germanic era.
  • Old English (pre-1000) — spāca 'spoke of a wheel', unchanged in meaning ever since.
  • Middle English (13th c.) — spoke; dialect use for a ladder-rung widens it briefly.
  • 16th-17th c. — idiom 'put a spoke in someone's wheel' recorded, giving the word its obstructive metaphor.
  • 19th-20th c. — the bicycle age remakes the word: wire spokes, spoked wheels, radial metaphors, hub-and-spoke networks.

Socio-culturally, spokes is a craftsman's word that survived every revolution of transport — cart, wagon, carriage, bicycle, aircraft — because the wheel kept needing a word for the rods that hold it true. In Tolkien's simile, the dwarves themselves are craftsmen of wheels, and the image of branches as spokes measures the tree by a wheelwright's eye, importing mechanical order into the panic of the night. From a PIE 'point' through a Germanic 'splinter' to the bicycle and the network, the plural spokes has always meant the same thing: regular rods radiating from a centre, holding the circle together.
