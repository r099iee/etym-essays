The history of stout is the biography of an ideal: what one age admires in a man — pride, then bravery, then sheer bodily solidity — the word obediently follows. It begins among the Germanic tribes, where a reconstructed adjective *stultaz meant proud and stately: the bearing of a chief, chest out, head high. When the Franks carried this word into Gaul, Old French shaped it into estout, and there it acquired the particular flavour of the age of chivalry — bold to the edge of fierceness, proud to the edge of arrogance. An estout chevalier was magnificent and slightly dangerous, and the chroniclers use the word for warriors, rebels, and storms with equal approval.

Middle English borrowed it around 1300 in exactly this sense. For two centuries stout in England meant brave and valiant and little else: stout knights, stout blows, stout resistance. The semantic bridge to the modern meaning was the medieval imagination itself, which pictured courage as a physical property — the brave man was broad-chested and thick-limbed, and so, by slow degrees, the word for the spirit became the word for the frame that housed it. By Shakespeare's day both senses lived together: a stout fellow might be courageous, or broad, or — most often — both at once, since the age saw no contradiction there.

The early modern centuries then worked the word outward from people to things. Anything that shared the strong man's virtues could be stout:

  • Stout objects (1600s): stout shoes, stout ships, stout oak — the adjective of English practicality, praising whatever survives weather and work. A stout wall is a wall with a stout fellow's shoulders.
  • Stout drink (1700s): London porters asked the brewer for the strongest vat, the stout porter, until the adjective detached and became a noun — a pint of stout — now immortalised by Guinness and drunk worldwide by people who have never met a stout knight.
  • Stout denials and efforts (1800s): abstractions went stout too — stout opposition in Parliament, stout resistance on the field — keeping the old bravery sense alive in metaphor long after bodies had claimed the literal one.
  • Stout sizes (Victorian era): as industrial tailoring standardised clothing, stout became the trade euphemism for larger figures — stoutwear departments, stout gentlemen's outfitters — chosen precisely because the word still sounded like praise.

Phonetically the word barely moved across seven centuries — estout to stout is little more than the loss of a French prefix vowel — which is why it feels so solid in the mouth: a single blunt syllable ending in a stopped t, exactly the shape of the thing it names. Its German cousins kept the older pride sense — modern German stolz still means proud — so that the family between them preserves the whole journey from pride to bravery to bulk. And in Tolkien's Shire the oldest sense survives intact: Farmer Maggot, stout fellow, is the free Frankish warrior reborn as a mushroom-guarding farmer — fierce to trespassers, loyal to friends, broad as his own barn door, the ancient Germanic ideal in a smock.
