In this scene from Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), the word twitched carries a precise, miniature drama. The white cat by the pond "sat very, very still," and her stillness is the entire point of the sentence: the narrator twice stresses how motionless she is, so that the single betraying detail — the tip of her tail "twitched as if it were alive" — stands out in perfect relief. A twitch is exactly the right verb for a predatory cat: it is quick, small, localized, and above all involuntary, a muscle-spark the animal cannot suppress. Because the motion is confined to the tail-tip and over in an instant, it escapes Peter's notice while being unmistakable to the reader; Potter uses the word to build dramatic irony, a quiet warning that this graceful creature is a living hunter.

Primary sense. To twitch is to make a short, sudden, convulsive movement — typically of a small body part (a tail, an eye, a lip, a nose, an ear, a hand). The verb is intransitive in this sense ("her tail twitched") and also transitive ("to twitch a muscle"). The corresponding noun, twitch, names the movement itself: a sudden involuntary jerk or muscular spasm, especially a brief, localized one. Potter's twitched is the simple past tense of this verb, formed by the regular weak suffix -ed.

Polysemy and extended senses:

  • Transitive plucking (older sense): in Middle English and surviving dialect, twitch means to pluck, pull sharply, or snatch at something — e.g. "twitching weeds" out of the ground; this deliberate-pulling sense is the ancestor of the modern involuntary one.
  • Physiology / medicine: a muscle twitch is a single brief contraction of a muscle fiber or small group of fibers (clinically a fasciculation), distinct from a sustained spasm or tremor.
  • Birdwatching jargon: twitching (1950s) is the hobby of traveling great distances to see rare birds; a twitcher is the enthusiast, so named from the nervous excitement said to make them twitch.
  • Digital culture: the live-streaming platform Twitch (2011) borrows the word for rapid, flickering, animated movement, making "twitch" a global technology brand.
  • Adjective spin-offs: twitchy (nervous, restless, jumpy; 20th c.) and twitch-grass (couch grass, so named because it must be twitched/plucked out of the soil).
  • Metaphoric use: in narrative prose the twitch is a stock device for exposing hidden feeling — a suppressed smile, a flare of temper, the itch of a lie — a single involuntary motion that gives away what stillness conceals.

Register and collocation. The register is firmly neutral and everyday — a core word of plain English used across literature, medicine, and conversation — yet because it is so physical and immediate, writers from Potter onward have prized it for its power to animate stillness, placing one live motion inside a frozen scene. Collocations: the tail twitched, his eye twitched, her lip twitched, twitch nervously, twitch with irritation, twitch involuntarily, a nervous twitch, muscle twitch.
