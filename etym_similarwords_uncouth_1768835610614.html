• Crude: Similar because it means lacking refinement; differs because crude names the raw state of a material — crude oil, a crude joke — without a social dimension, whereas uncouth is a judgement of a person's manners and knowledge.
  • Rough: Similar because it means unpolished and unrefined; differs because rough is a general word for unevenness of surface or manner, while uncouth keeps its epistemological core — the unpolished as the unknown.
  • Boorish: Similar because it means coarse bad manners; differs because boorish is active and intrusive — a coarseness that offends others — while uncouth is more a condition of being outside the circle of the known.
  • Loutish: Similar because it means clumsy, coarse, and ill-mannered; differs because loutish implies a physical clumsiness and brutishness, while uncouth implies a whole roughness of person, manners, and knowledge.
  • Churlish: Similar because it means rude and surly; differs because churlish (from Old English ceorl, a peasant) stresses a surly, stingy, ungracious temper, while uncouth stresses a lack of polish and cultivation.
  • Gauche: Similar because it means social clumsiness; differs because gauche is the specific wrong-footed gesture of the uninitiated, while uncouth is a whole roughness of person, manners, and knowledge.
  • Vulgar: Similar because it means lacking cultivated taste; differs because vulgar points at the common people and the taste imputed to them, while uncouth keeps its epistemological core — the unpolished as the unknown.
  • Unrefined: Similar because it means not purified or polished; differs because unrefined is a neutral process-word (unrefined sugar), while uncouth is a social judgement with a buried etymology of strangeness.
  • Unpolished: Similar because it means lacking finish or elegance; differs because unpolished is descriptive and often neutral, while uncouth is evaluative — the unpolished as the unknown, judged from inside the circle of the known.
  • Rude: Similar because it means lacking manners; differs because rude is the blunt, general word for discourtesy, while uncouth is more particular — an old-fashioned, slightly literary word for a roughness of person, manners, and knowledge.
  • Provincial: Similar because it means unsophisticated, of the countryside; differs because provincial is geographical — from the provinces — while uncouth is epistemological: outside the circle of the known, wherever one stands.
