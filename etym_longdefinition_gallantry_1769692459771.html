gallantry is an abstract noun with two chief senses: (1) courteous, attentive, and often flattering behavior shown by men toward women, and (2) conspicuous, spirited bravery in battle or in the face of danger. In the specific literary context of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Elizabeth Bennet remarks of Mr. Bingley's admiration of her sister Jane: "He could not help seeing that you were about five times as pretty as every other woman in the room. No thanks to his gallantry for that." Elizabeth's wit turns on the word's social sense: "gallantry" here is the polished, conventional attentiveness — the compliments and courting etiquette — expected of a gentleman in polite society. Her point is that Bingley's admiration was not a calculated performance of empty gallantry but an involuntary response to Jane's real beauty. In the comedy of manners the word therefore names both a social duty and a target of satire: gallantry can be genuine courtesy or a hollow, mechanical performance, and Elizabeth teasingly suggests she can tell the difference.

Primary senses. The word's two poles — courtliness and courage — both descend from the same root idea of a "gallant" man: one who is dashing, well-dressed, high-spirited, and pleasing, whether through his polished attention in the drawing-room or his conspicuous fearlessness on the battlefield. The military sense is today the formal one (medals "for gallantry"); the social sense, though slightly old-fashioned, remains the everyday one.

Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Courteous attentiveness of men toward women. Compliments, chivalrous deference, opening doors, gallant speeches — the ritualized social performance of respect and admiration. In Austen's age this was an etiquette expected of gentlemen; in modern English it is often used with irony or nostalgia ("his old-fashioned gallantry").
  • Conspicuous bravery; heroic dash in battle. "Gallantry under fire," "awarded for gallantry," "the gallantry of the troops." This sense stresses not mere courage but courage with style — boldness, flair, and noble spirit in danger.
  • Flirtatious or amorous conduct (gallantries, plural, archaic). "His gallantries" were his adventures in wooing and intrigue; the plural names particular acts of courtship or flirtation, sometimes with a hint of scandal.
  • Courtly love and the code of chivalry (historical). Gallantry is the secular, refined descendant of medieval chivalry: the knightly virtues of protecting and honoring women, reworked into the manners of the Renaissance courtier.
  • Obsolete / rare: gallant company; fine array. Older uses speak of "gallantry of dress" (finery, showy display) and "the gallantry of a procession" — a crowd of gallant people or splendid show.

Register, collocation, and grammar. Gallantry is usually an uncountable noun ("his gallantry," "an act of gallantry," "a display of gallantry") but has a countable plural ("his gallantries") for specific exploits. Common collocations: "military gallantry," "gallantry award," "medal for gallantry," "chivalrous gallantry," "empty gallantry," "false gallantry," "to pay a woman gallantries." The register spans military commendation, historical romance, and social satire; in Austen's novels it names the surface performance of gentlemanliness that her heroines must learn to see through — which is precisely what Elizabeth is doing when she says Bingley's admiration owed nothing to it.
