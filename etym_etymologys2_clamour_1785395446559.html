CHAIN for CLAMOUR: 1. PIE *kelh₁- "to call, shout, summon" — the root of calling, proclaiming, and crying aloud; rich in Italic and Hellenic, with no certain native Germanic reflex — reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE. 2. Latin clāmāre "to cry out, shout loudly, proclaim" — the -m- enlarged stem of the root (beside calāre "to call out, announce", source of calendar) — attested from Plautus, early 2nd c. BCE. 3. Latin clāmor "a loud shout, outcry, battle-cry" (accusative clāmōrem) — attested 1st c. BCE onward. 4. Old French clamour / clameur "a cry, outcry, lamentation; (in law) a public cry of distress, a formal complaint" — 9th–13th c. 5. Anglo-French clamur → Middle English clamour "loud shouting, uproar" — 14th c. 6. Modern English clamour "a loud and confused noise, especially of many voices; an urgent collective demand" — the -our spelling fixed by its French pedigree; American English clamor (20th c. standard). 7. CLAMOUR (the card word; British spelling). NOTE ON THE DEBATED STAGE: the split of the Latin family into calāre "call out" and clāmāre "cry out" is an accepted word-family division within the same root (the -m- enlargement is a well-attested Italic formation); no stage is unknown or invented. There is no native Germanic inheritance — every English member of this family (claim, acclaim, exclaim, proclaim, declaim, reclaim, calendar, council, and clamour) is a Latin or Greek loan, never an inherited cognate. Greek kaéō "I call" and its derivatives (ekklēsía "assembly", whence ecclesia) are the Hellenic cognates of the same root.
