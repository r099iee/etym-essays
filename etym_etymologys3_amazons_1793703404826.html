The word "amazons" traces its direct lineage back to the Ancient Greek term Ἀμαζών (Amazōn), which referred to a legendary race of female warriors. The ultimate etymology of this Greek word, however, is a subject of considerable scholarly debate and remains uncertain. The most widely known, yet now largely discredited, folk etymology suggests a derivation from Greek a- (privative prefix, meaning "without") and mazos (meaning "breast"), implying "breastless." This theory was popularized by ancient Greek historians and writers, who claimed the Amazons removed or cauterized one breast (typically the right) to facilitate archery and spear-throwing. However, there is no archaeological evidence to support this practice, nor do the earliest Greek artistic depictions of Amazons show them as breastless. Modern scholarship, including the Oxford English Dictionary and Beekes's Etymological Dictionary of Greek, generally rejects this as a fanciful folk etymology.

Alternative theories for the origin of Ἀμαζών often point towards an Iranian (Scythian) source, given the geographical proximity of the mythical Amazonian homeland to Scythian territories. One prominent theory suggests a derivation from an Old Iranian compound, possibly *ha-mazan-, meaning "fighting together" or "warrior." Another proposed Iranian root is *a-mazan-, meaning "unmated" or "not marrying," which would align with the mythological depiction of Amazons as independent women who shunned traditional marital relations. These Iranian theories are supported by the historical interactions between Greeks and Scythian tribes, where Greek writers might have adapted a foreign term to describe formidable female warriors they encountered or heard about. The Scythians themselves had a reputation for fierce female warriors, which may have contributed to the Amazonian myth.

From Ancient Greek, the word passed into Latin as Amazōn, retaining its meaning of a mythical female warrior. This Latin form then influenced Old French, where it became Amazone around the 12th century. English subsequently borrowed the term from Old French, appearing as Amazone in Middle English texts around the 14th century. By the 16th century, the modern English form "Amazon" (and its plural "amazons") was well established, referring both to the mythological figures and, by extension, to tall, strong, or formidable women.

The semantic evolution of "amazon" reflects a fascinating journey from a specific mythological entity to a broader descriptor of female power and independence. Initially, in ancient Greece, the Amazons represented an exotic, often terrifying, 'other' – a society that inverted Greek patriarchal norms. Their stories served as cautionary tales or as foils against which Greek heroes could prove their valor. Despite their 'otherness,' they were also admired for their courage and martial skill, often depicted with dignity in art.

During the Renaissance, the Amazon myth experienced a resurgence in European literature and art. Explorers like Francisco de Orellana, upon encountering fierce indigenous women warriors in South America in the 16th century, named the vast river and surrounding region the "Amazon" River and Amazon rainforest, directly linking the New World's perceived wildness and its inhabitants to the ancient Greek legends. This act solidified the word's geographical association and further embedded it in the global lexicon.

By the 17th and 18th centuries, the metaphorical use of "amazon" began to gain traction, describing any woman of exceptional physical strength, stature, or martial prowess. This period saw the rise of female figures in popular culture, such as circus strongwomen or female duelists, who might be colloquially referred to as amazons. The connotation was generally one of awe and admiration, though sometimes tinged with a sense of the 'unfeminine' or 'unnatural,' reflecting prevailing gender norms.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, with the advent of organized sports and the burgeoning women's rights movements, the term "amazon" took on new layers of meaning. It became a descriptor for female athletes who excelled in physically demanding sports, embodying strength, competitiveness, and resilience. Simultaneously, as women pushed for greater social and political emancipation, "amazon" began to symbolize female independence, assertiveness, and a challenge to patriarchal structures. The phrase "emancipated amazons," as seen in the source context, perfectly captures this modern semantic shift, where the word signifies women who have broken free from traditional constraints and assert their power and autonomy in society.

Today, "amazon" continues to evoke images of powerful, independent women, whether in the realm of physical strength, intellectual prowess, or social activism. Its enduring presence in language underscores the timeless appeal of the myth of the female warrior and its adaptability to describe evolving perceptions of female strength and agency across different historical and cultural contexts.
