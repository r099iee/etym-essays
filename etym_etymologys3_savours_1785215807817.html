Evolution of "savours." The history of SAVOURS begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *sap-, whose core meaning was "to taste, to perceive by the senses." This modest sensory root proved extraordinarily productive, and its most fascinating legacy lies in Latin, where the verb sapere split its sense along two paths that later came to seem almost contradictory. On the one hand sapere meant "to have taste or flavor," applied to food and drink; on the other it meant "to be wise, to have discernment." The Romans themselves felt the connection: the wise man was, at bottom, the man who had "tasted" things, who had sampled experience and knew its true savor. From the sensory sense came the noun sapor ("taste, flavor"); from the intellectual sense came sapiens ("wise," literally "tasting") and sapientia ("wisdom") — the roots of modern sapiens, sapient, and sapience — along with sapidus ("savory") and its negation insipidus ("tasteless"), the ancestor of English insipid. The intellectual branch also produced Old French savoir ("to know"), and hence modern French savoir and savoir-faire; the sensory branch produced the direct ancestor of the English word.

1. The sensory line into English. The line that leads directly to the English word passed through the noun sapor, which evolved in the Romance vernaculars and reached Old French as savour, "taste, flavor." Anglo-French carried savour into Middle English in the thirteenth century, where the noun meant literally the taste or smell of food and drink, and where the derived verb savouren meant to taste or to have a flavor. In this earliest English period the word is thoroughly sensory and material: one savours a dish by tasting it, and a thing savours of something when its physical taste or smell is perceptible. Even at this stage, however, the noun had begun to acquire a figurative shimmer, since reputation too could be "savored" — a man's "savour" could be his good or ill name, the flavor of his character as sampled by others.

2. The figurative turn (the moralized taste). The crucial semantic shift took place in the later Middle Ages and was complete by the fifteenth century: the verb acquired its figurative sense, "to have the flavor of, to smack of, to suggest a particular quality," a metaphorical extension by which an abstract trait was spoken of as something one could taste on the tongue. The syntax that crystallized was savour + of + the quality, and the construction became a standard tool of judgment. The negative tinge the word so often carries — savours of impertinence, savours of treason, savours of heresy, savours of hypocrisy — grew directly from this sensory metaphor. A flavor detected in a situation is rarely a neutral one; tasting is an act of evaluation, and the taste in question is usually the aftertaste of something discreditable. The polite verb thus became a delicate instrument of censure: it allowed a writer to say that a thing was tinged with a bad quality without the bluntness of a direct accusation. This is precisely its function in the sentence preserved in this card, where attempting to lift the veil on a private mystery "savours almost of impertinence" — the intrusion does not stand condemned outright but is detected, as by a delicate palate, to carry the flavor of an unwarranted trespass.

3. The positive branch. Meanwhile the same family sustained a positive, transitive sense that has grown stronger in modern English: "to savour" something means to taste or enjoy it fully, to dwell on it and extract its full pleasure or meaning. To savour the moment, savour the victory, savour every mouthful is to slow down and relish — the antithesis of the quick, suspicious taste of "savours of." This modern positive sense is in fact a re-extension of the ancient sensory meaning, a return to the direct enjoyment of taste from which the whole family began. American English, meanwhile, regularized the spelling to savor/savors in the nineteenth century, so that the British u-spelling of the present card marks the word as belonging to the Commonwealth tradition.

4. The modern balance. Today the verb carries both faces of its history. "Savour (of)" survives mainly as a literary, old-fashioned way of detecting a taint in a situation, prized by careful writers for its decorous precision; "savour (the moment)" is the living everyday sense, though still somewhat formal and deliberate. The adjective savoury (and the savoury served at dinner) keeps the sensory root alive in the kitchen, while the wisdom-branch of the same PIE root survives in sapiens, sapient, and French savoir — reminders that for the ancient speakers of Latin, to have tasted deeply was to know.

  • PIE *sap- "to taste, perceive by the senses" → Latin sapere "to taste; to be wise" (the "taste → know" doublet) and sapor "taste, flavor."
  • Old French savour "taste, flavor" → Anglo-French/Middle English savour (noun, 13th c.) and savouren (verb) "to taste."
  • 15th century: figurative savour of "to smack of, be tinged with a quality" — a moralized taste, usually of something discreditable.
  • Modern English: savours (of) "is tinged with" (literary censure, as in the present sentence); transitive savour "to enjoy, relish" (savour the moment); American spelling savor/savors.
  • Collateral: sapiens, sapient, sapience, sapid, insipid, French savoir/savoir-faire — all from the same PIE root, showing the deep link between taste and knowledge.
