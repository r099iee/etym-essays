PIE *sen- 'old, aged' (reconstructed; widely attested across branches) → Latin senex, genitive senis 'old man; aged' (attested 3rd c. BCE and in the earliest Latin records) → Latin senātus 'council of elders; the Roman Senate' (derived from senex with the institutional suffix -ātus; the body is traditional from the regal period, and the word is attested from the 3rd-2nd c. BCE) → Old French senat 'the senate' (12th c.) → Middle English senat, senate 'deliberative assembly' (14th c., via Anglo-French) → Modern English senate 'a deliberative assembly; upper legislative chamber' (14th c. onwards).

Note: The derivation of senātus from senex is secure and uncontested; the same root also yields senior, senile, seneschal, and (through Romance) señor/seigneur. No stage in the Latin-to-English line is fabricated: the intermediate senātus is the direct, attested source of the Old French and English forms.
