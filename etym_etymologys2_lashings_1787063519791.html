Root 1 — the strike sense:
Origin obscure (debated)
possibly imitative, echoic
of a whistling whip
or of violent motion
↓
Middle English (early 14th c.)
lasshen
"to move violently;
to strike, flog"
↓
English (15th-16th c.)
lash (verb)
"to strike with a
whip-like blow"
↓
English (16th c.)
lashing (noun)
"a beating; the
strokes of a whip"
↓
English (present)
LASHINGS
"repeated blows;
also (1835)
'an abundance, plenty'"

Root 2 — the binding sense:
Latin (1st c. BCE)
laqueus
"noose, snare"
↓
Old French (12th c.)
lacier
"to lace, tie,
bind"
↓
English (17th c.)
lash (verb)
"to bind or fasten
with cord or rope"
↓
English (nautical)
lashing (noun)
"a rope or cord
used for binding"

Note on debate: The origin of the strike-sense lash is uncertain — commonly held to be imitative, sometimes connected to Germanic verbs of violent motion, but with no proven ancestor. The idiom "lashings of" (plenty, 1835) is likewise of debated origin, possibly from the idea of quantities "lashed up" in bundles or of liquid "lashing" about in a vessel.
