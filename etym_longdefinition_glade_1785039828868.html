GLADE is a noun meaning an open space in a wood or forest — a clearing where the trees draw apart and light falls on open ground, usually grassy and often near water. The word's oldest and most distinctive sense is luminous: a glade is a bright place in the wood, a spot where the forest gloom breaks, and the English word is intimately linked to the idea of shining, openness and relief. It is more poetic and more specific than the plain, functional 'clearing': a glade is not just any empty patch of woodland but a place where light enters, where the eye and the traveller find room, where one can stand clear of the crowding trees. Because it combines openness with shelter (the trees enclose it, the light falls into it), the glade has become in literature and landscape a place of refuge, meeting, beauty and revelation — the sunlit stage of the forest. In The Hobbit the word appears as the travellers approach Rivendell: "The last green had almost faded out of the grass, when they came at length to an open glade not far above the banks of the stream." The details are precise and significant. The 'last green' fading out of the grass marks the dying of the light and the closing of the day; 'at length' marks a weary, earned arrival; and the glade is 'open' and 'not far above the banks of the stream' — a place of light and water, the first wide, habitable space after a long dark descent, and thus the threshold of the hidden valley. The glade is the place where the gloom breaks: it is the hinge between the darkening wild and the secret, sheltered home of Elrond, and its openness quietly promises the safety that the narrative is about to grant. The word's inherent luminosity ('a bright place in the wood') makes it the natural climax of a paragraph about fading light. Polysemy & secondary senses:
  • Forest and landscape: the standard sense — an open, grassy, sunlit space within woodland; also any wide open tract in a wood; in garden and estate design, a glade is a deliberate opening in a planting, cut or allowed so that light, views and walks open up.
  • American geography: in the southern United States, 'glade' (or 'glady') is applied to open, wet grassland among trees — treeless wet prairies, as in the Florida Everglades, a name that simply means 'river of grassy openings' — and to boggy, marshy openings in pinewoods; 'glades' in this sense are wet, not dry.
  • Poetry and myth: the glade is a stock place of encounter and transformation in pastoral, Romantic and fantasy literature — the bower, the trysting-place, the faerie ring, the green chapel, the sunlit stage where quests pause and truths are revealed; the word carries an almost sacred brightness.
  • Botanical and technical: in ecology a glade is a natural gap in the canopy where light reaches the forest floor; glade-communities of shade-loving plants and glade butterflies are studied in their own right.
  • Commercial and everyday: Glade is a well-known American brand of air fresheners (from 1956), which takes the word's fresh, bright, open-air associations; a glade is also a term in golf for an open sandy or grassy area, and glade skiing means skiing through open, tree-studded slopes.
  • Idiom and metaphor: to be 'in a glade' or to reach 'the glade' is figuratively to come out of difficulty into clarity; the word is used of any bright opening — a glade of clear sky among clouds, a glade of reason in confusion.
Set against its neighbours: clearing is the plain, functional term for any open space in woodland, natural or man-made, with no suggestion of light; dell is a small, secluded, tree-lined hollow or valley; grove is the opposite — a small stand of trees; meadow is open grassland, usually outside woodland, for pasture or hay; lawn is a mown cultivated area around a building; glen is a narrow valley, Scottish; copse and spinney are small woods. Glade uniquely joins 'open space' with 'light': it is the brightness in the wood, the gap that gladdens.
