spindly, rheumy, and flabby are three adjectives of physical frailty and decay, which Jack Vance arrays in a single devastating portrait: "It was a thing to arouse pity—a great head on a small spindly body, with weak rheumy eyes and a flabby button of a nose." Each word attacks a different axis of the creature's weakness — structure, moisture, and tone.

Part 1: SPINDLY — Definition

spindly (adjective): long, thin, disproportionately slender, and noticeably weak or fragile; shaped like the slender rod of a textile spindle. Etymologically rooted in Old English spinel ("spindle"), it describes physical frames, limbs, stems, or structures whose height or length far outstrips their mechanical strength or supportive breadth.

Polysemous Spectrum of SPINDLY

  • 1. Physical Limbs & Body Anatomy: Unnaturally long, thin, and weak legs, arms, or torsos. Contextual example: "The newborn calf wobbled uncertainly on its spindly legs."
  • 2. Botanical Etiolation & Plant Morphology: In horticulture, plants that grow weak, pale, and unnaturally tall due to light deprivation (etiolation); "spindly seedlings" are stretched stems too weak to stand.
  • 3. Mechanical & Furniture Architecture: Furniture legs, scaffolding, or structures that are thin to the point of fragility — "spindly chair legs" that look as though they might buckle.
  • 4. Figurative Fragility: By extension, any argument, institution, or scheme that is stretched too thin to support its own weight — "a spindly alibi."
  • 5. The Historical Spindle Metaphor: The whole word rests on the image of the drop-spindle — the tapering wooden rod on which thread was wound — the ancient standard of slenderness in Western technology and proverbial thinness ("spindle-shanks" for skinny legs).

Part 2: RHEUMY — Definition

rheumy (adjective): watery, damp, or discharging thin mucous moisture, particularly of the eyes or nose in cold weather, old age, or sickness. Rooted in Ancient Greek rheuma ("a flow, stream, or bodily fluxion"), it preserves in literary English the ancient medical theory of humoral fluxions — the body's fluids conceived as flowing streams.

Polysemous Spectrum of RHEUMY

  • 1. Watery, Weeping Eyes (Primary Literary Sense): The moist, filmy, moisture-laden eyes of age, sickness, or feebleness. Contextual example: "The old hound's rheumy eyes blinked slowly in the sun."
  • 2. Mucous Discharge (Medical-Historical Sense): In old medicine, "rheum" was any watery discharge of the eyes, nose, or lungs — a catarrh or fluxion; "rheumy" named the state of leaking such moisture.
  • 3. The Humoral Theory (Buried Historical Sense): Greek medicine held that the body's humours flowed like rivers; a rheuma was a "streaming" of fluid, and the word family (rheumatism, rheum) preserves the theory that joint pain and discharges were caused by a fluxion of humours.
  • 4. Atmospheric & Poetic Dampness (Rare): By extension, air or weather that is raw, damp, and moisture-laden — "rheumy dawns" in poetic diction.
  • 5. Figurative Feebleness: Anything watery, weak, and lacking substance or clarity — "rheumy sentiment" for mawkish, runny emotion.

Part 3: FLABBY — Definition

flabby (adjective): soft, loose, limp, and lacking firmness, resilience, or muscle tone; yielding immediately under pressure without springing back. Formed from the imitative English base fl- (mimicking loose, swinging movement, as in flap and flop), it highlights physical or metaphorical slackness and the absence of inner tension.

Polysemous Spectrum of FLABBY

  • 1. Flesh Lacking Muscle Tone (Primary): Soft, loose, pendulous flesh that yields under pressure without springing back. Contextual example: "He grew flabby from years of desk work and no exercise."
  • 2. Figurative Slackness of Character or Will: A "flabby" will, conscience, or resolve is weak, vacillating, lacking backbone — the moral transference of physical softness.
  • 3. Flabby Prose & Argument: Writing or reasoning that is loose, wordy, and structurally slack — lacking vigour, precision, and sinew.
  • 4. Flabby Commerce & Markets: Loose, sluggish, unresponsive markets or prices — the image of softness transferred to economic vigour.
  • 5. The Imitative Family: Flabby belongs to the sound-symbolic family of flap, flop, flaccid (a Latin borrowing), and flab — all built on the fl- base that imitates loose, swinging, flapping motion in English.

Interaction in Vance's Context

The three adjectives triangulate the creature's weakness. Spindly attacks structure — a body too thin to bear its own great head; rheumy attacks moisture — eyes leaking their contents; flabby attacks tone — a nose soft and formless as dough. Together they produce the "thing to arouse pity," each word supplying a distinct physiological register of frailty.

Historical Semantic Bridges

Spindly bridges from the pre-industrial spindle (the standard of slenderness) to a general adjective of structural fragility. Rheumy bridges from the ancient Greek medical theory of flowing humours to a purely descriptive word for watery, weeping eyes. Flabby bridges from imitative sound-symbolism for loose movement to a physical adjective of soft flesh, and then to a broad figurative word for slackness of every kind — character, prose, markets, and will.
