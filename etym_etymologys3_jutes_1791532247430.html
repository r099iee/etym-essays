The earliest evidence. The Jutes are essentially a textual people. They appear in Bede's Ecclesiastical History (731) under the Latin name Iutae, and in a handful of continental and insular sources that depend on Bede or on a common tradition. Archaeology can identify distinctive material culture in Kent and on the Isle of Wight, but it cannot read an ethnic label off a brooch. The word therefore reaches us through a single scholarly channel: the Latin of an early medieval monk.

The name's shape. Bede's Iutae implies a Germanic form with an initial vowel or semivowel, and the Old English Ēotas and Old Norse Jótar agree in outline. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic *eutiz is a convenience rather than an attested word. The initial sound developed differently in the different daughter languages: Old English kept a vowel, Old Norse developed j, and Latin rendered the name with I, which later English spelling turned into J.

  • The vowel of Old English Ēotas is long, hence the modern English "Jutes" with /uː/ rather than a short vowel.
  • The Latin Iutae was pronounced with consonantal i /j/ in classical Latin; the English pronunciation /dʒuːts/ reflects the later history of written J in English, not the Roman sound.
  • The final -s is the English plural, added after the word was borrowed; Latin Iutae is already plural.
  • No reliable Indo-European etymology has been established; the "giant" connection and the "jutting land" connection are both conjectural.

Bede's triad and its purpose. Bede's account of three peoples — Angles, Saxons, Jutes — has a theological and historiographical purpose: it explains the diversity of the English church and the political map of his own day. The Jutes are given Kent, the Isle of Wight and the Meon valley, and Bede notes that the Isle of Wight's people were still called Jutes in his time. The tidy allocation of one people to one kingdom is almost certainly a simplification of a mixed settlement, but it fixed the Jutes in the English historical imagination.

Kent: the Jutish kingdom. Kent is the showpiece of the Jutish tradition. Its fifth- and sixth-century cemeteries show a rich, Frankish-oriented culture; its kings claimed descent from Hengist, the legendary Jutish leader; and its law code, issued by Æthelberht around 600, is the earliest surviving English legislation. The kingdom's conversion by Augustine in 597 made it the cradle of English Christianity. None of this proves that every Kentish person was of Jutish descent, but it shows how a name can attach itself to a polity and a church.

Jutland and the continental background. The peninsula of Jutland, Danish Jylland, preserves the name in geography. Its archaeology shows close links with the North Sea coast and with the Danes, and the historical Jutes are usually placed in the northern part of the peninsula. Whether the Jutes of Bede were a distinct people or a political faction within a broader Danish-speaking population is unknown. The later Danish kingdom absorbed the region, and "Jute" ceased to be a living ethnic label on the continent.

Semantic and onomastic afterlife. In English the word survives almost exclusively as a historical term. It appears in chronicles, in antiquarian writing on Kentish customs, in nineteenth-century racial historiography, and in modern scholarship on the migration period. It has also produced a geographical adjective, "Jutish," and a regional identity in Kent and the Isle of Wight. The unrelated "jute" fibre, borrowed from Bengali in the eighteenth century, is a homograph that occasionally confuses readers but has no etymological connection.

  • Literary attestations: Bede's Historia; the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle; medieval Kentish histories; Victorian histories of the "English race"; modern archaeological syntheses.
  • Political uses: Kentish local pride and the traditional claim to a distinct Jutish heritage; the story of Hengist and Horsa as a foundation myth.
  • Scholarly revision: since the 1990s archaeologists have questioned the equation of grave goods with ethnic identity, and "Jutish" is now used cautiously, often in quotation marks.
  • Place-names: Jutland, Jylland, and the Kentish and Wightish landscape of the migration period.

Why a small name survives. The Jutes are a case study in how textual authority creates historical permanence. A small, poorly documented people became a permanent item of English origins because Bede named them alongside the Angles and Saxons. The word's survival is thus a fact about the transmission of books as much as about the movement of peoples — a reminder that lexical history is often the history of a single influential text.
