Mansions (the plural of mansion) are large, grand, or impressive houses — residences of wealth, rank, or power — and, in older and biblical English, simply dwelling-places and abodes. The modern default sense is the grand private house, often urban and imposing; the older sense is any place of staying, retained in elevated and scriptural register ("in my Father's house are many mansions," John 14:2). Etymologically the word descends from Latin mānsiō, "a staying, a halting-place, a night's lodging," from manēre "to remain, stay," so its deepest image is the place where one abides on a journey — a lodging — which over centuries rose in dignity to mean the great house of a great person. In the same dragon-inventory of The Hobbit that lists alleys and cellars — "halls, and lanes, and tunnels, alleys, cellars, mansions and passages" — the plural names the Mountain's great residential chambers: not humble holes but the splendid living-spaces of the dwarven kingdom, now scoured and emptied by Smaug. The word's scale contrasts in the same breath with alleys and cellars: high and low spaces alike fall, and the grandeur of the mansions makes the ruin the more complete. Tone: elevated noun inside a catalogue of devastation; grandeur listed only to be emptied. The phrase carries the biblical resonance of "many mansions" — many dwelling-places — darkened by the knowledge that here the dwellings have become tombs or emptiness. Core senses.
  • Grand private house (modern primary): a large, impressive, often imposing residence of a wealthy or prominent person or family — the "town mansion," the "country mansion."
  • Dwelling-place, abode (older, wider): any place of residence, in medieval and early modern English, and retained in biblical and elevated register.
  • Stopping-place, lodging, stage of a journey (earliest, from Latin mānsiō): the halting-place or night's lodging on a road, an ancient sense surviving in the word's root and in historical use.
  • Station or mansion of the moon (astronomical/astrological): one of the 28 divisions of the zodiac used in medieval astronomy and astrology — the "mansions of the moon" — where the word names the places the moon stays.
Polysemy: secondary and specialized senses.
  • "Many mansions": the fixed biblical phrase (John 14:2, King James) meaning many abodes or dwelling-places, which keeps the older wide sense alive in elevated English.
  • Real estate and architecture: in modern property language, "mansion" names a large house; the word also survives in "mansion block," a large apartment building in British English.
  • Historical administrative use: in the Roman Empire a mansio was an official halting-station on the road system; the word carried this sense of a station or post into early English use.
  • Figurative: the dwelling image is applied metaphorically — the "mansion of the soul," "mansions" of heaven — spaces of abiding in theological and poetic writing.
Historical and semantic bridges. The bridge from "lodging" to "great house" is a rise in social scale. Latin mānsiō named a place where one stays — a night's lodging, a stage on a journey, an official stopping-station. In Old French and Anglo-Norman the word mansion means a dwelling or abode without insisting on size; Middle English inherits this wide sense, and the biblical translation "many mansions" fixes the broad "abodes" meaning in the language. Over the following centuries the word climbed: the dwelling of a lord becomes a great residence, and by the 18th–19th centuries the modern default is the large, impressive house of wealth and rank. The "mansions of the moon" shows the astronomical branch, where the word names the fixed stations or resting-places of the moon in its course. The semantic constant through every sense is abiding — the place where someone remains. Differentiation. A house is the general dwelling; a palace is royal and ceremonial; a manor implies a landed estate; a hall may be a great room or a building of a certain type; a residence is formal but not necessarily vast; an abode is literary and general; a villa suggests a suburban or holiday house with land. Mansions keeps the sense of large, impressive dwellings, with the older "abodes" colour still haunting elevated English. The card preserves the plural: many grand chambers in the routed Mountain, not a single suburban house.
