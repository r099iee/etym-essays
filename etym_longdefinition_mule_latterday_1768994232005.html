mule — In the passage, "He takes as pay from a farmer an aged mule," the word works as a deflationary literary instrument. The Reverend Green's mount is not a chivalric steed but a commodity: a broken beast of burden accepted as wages in kind, ridden "aback" — mounted on its bare back — into the town of Nacogdoches. Every detail measures the fraudulence of the man's enterprise: the animal is "aged," spent, a ruined biological machine; the manner of mounting is inglorious; the payment is in kind rather than in coin. The mule's defining biological fact, its hybridity and consequent sterility, quietly mirrors the barrenness of a ministry that produces nothing — a creature that cannot reproduce stands in for a con that cannot deliver. The tone is unromantic, workaday, and faintly grotesque.

The primary, core sense is concrete and zoological. A mule is the hybrid offspring of a male donkey (a jack) and a female horse (a mare), bred for draft and pack work; the rarer reverse cross of a stallion and a female donkey (a jenny) is the hinny, smaller and slighter. The mule is valued for strength, endurance, sure-footedness, and patience, but it is almost always sterile — a trait that has made it at once a workhorse and a symbol. For centuries it was the engine of commerce: the pack animal of pilgrims, armies, and trade routes, and in the nineteenth-century American West the freight machinery of the gold rush and the wagon roads, where "mule trains," "pack mules," and "mule skinners" (drivers) carried supplies that horses could not haul so cheaply.

Polysemy and secondary modern senses.

  • The obstinate person (from the 16th century). From the beast's character the word and its adjective mulish (1570s) came to name a stubborn person: mulishness is the refusal to be moved, fused with laboring power — not the foolishness of a dupe.
  • The drug courier (20th century). In criminal slang a "mule" is a person who smuggles drugs or contraband by carrying it on or in the body — the human cargo-carrier, a metaphor that revives the animal's role as beast of burden.
  • The fashion mule (20th century). A "mule" is also a backless, usually closed-toe slipper or shoe. This is a separate word, from Latin mulleus, the red-dyed shoe worn by Roman magistrates — a false friend, not a descendant of the animal-word.
  • The spinning mule (18th century). In textiles the "mule" is Samuel Crompton's spinning machine of 1779, which hybridized the spinning jenny and the water frame — named for its mixed, cross-bred nature, exactly like the animal.
  • Technical and industrial senses. A "mule" can be a small narrow-gauge haulage locomotive (mining), a track-maintenance vehicle (a "motor mule"), a pallet jack (a "pallet mule"), or any small load-pulling device — each transferring the idea of a small, tough, load-pulling creature.
  • Genetic and proverbial extensions. In biology any sterile interspecific hybrid may be called a mule; proverbially, "stubborn as a mule," "mule-headed," and "to work like a mule" preserve the character and laboring senses.

The historical semantic bridge. The language repeatedly abstracts the animal's four attributes — sterility, strength, stubbornness, and hybridity — and reapplies them to machines, to people, and to metaphors of carrying. Where the horse lends prestige and the ass lends contempt, the mule supplies the plain, serviceable, stubborn power of toil, and that is the register McCarthy's sentence lives in.

latterday — In the passage, "the latterday republic of Fredonia" is a temporal verdict. Fredonia was a real, brief breakaway republic proclaimed at Nacogdoches by Haden Edwards in 1826–27, an already-failed polity by the time the Reverend rides through in 1849. To call it "latterday" is to say it belongs to the recent or closing phase of a historical sequence: a republic that came after the great events, an afterglow and an afterthought, its very newness a sign of its transience.

Polysemy and secondary senses of "latterday."

  • Modern or recent (19th century). The general sense, attested from the 1840s, is "belonging to a later period, present, modern": a current instance in a longer series — "a latter-day Robin Hood," "a latter-day Caesar."
  • Religious and eschatological. "The latter days" in biblical and apocalyptic speech names the final period of the world's history; the Latter-day Saints (the Mormon church, named from 1830) claim to be the church of that final dispensation, carrying full end-time gravity.
  • The terminal nuance. Because "latter" means the second of two and "latter days" means the end-time, the compound always carries a shade of the concluding and the exhausted — new and already ending, recent yet terminal.

The historical semantic bridge. "Latter" is the comparative of "late," whose Germanic history runs from "slow, slack" to "later in time" to "second of two." Attached to "day," it turns a plain unit of time into an eschatological marker, and from there into journalistic irony: "a latter-day X" is a diminished or comic reincarnation of something greater. McCarthy's single word therefore manages two directions at once — it dates Fredonia as recent and condemns it as terminal, applying the solemnity of Scripture to a squalid frontier episode, and the worn epithet carries the whole deflation.
