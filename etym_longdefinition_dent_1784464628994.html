DENT (noun and verb) — A slight hollow or depression made in a surface by a blow or by pressure; also, as a verb, to make such a depression in something. The word carries a rich secondary figurative life: a 'dent' can be a noticeable reduction in an amount ('make a dent in the workload'), a blow to someone's pride or confidence, or the first perceptible progress into a large task. Core sense and grammatical range. As a noun, dent names the physical scar left by impact: the hollow in a car fender, a copper kettle, a wooden door, or any softer surface struck by a harder one. As a verb it is most often transitive ('a branch dented the hood'), but it can be used intransitively ('this metal dents easily'). The word is a phonological and orthographic variant of older dint, from Old English dynt 'a blow, stroke,' so that the history of the word shows meaning sliding from the violent action (the blow itself) to its visible consequence (the hollow the blow leaves behind). Polysemy and secondary senses. Modern English has extended dent far beyond the body-shop:
  • Physical impact sense: a hollow or depression in a surface caused by a blow, collision, or sustained pressure; the everyday sense in which a fender or a kettle is dented.
  • Figurative reduction sense: 'to make a dent in' an amount or a task — to make a noticeable, if partial, reduction or progress ('we finally made a dent in the backlog'). Here the image is of eating into a large surface; the dent is visible proof of progress even though the surface is not destroyed.
  • Figurative damage sense: 'to dent someone's confidence, pride, or reputation' — to injure or diminish it, as if by a blow ('the rejection really dented his self-esteem'). The semantic bridge is direct: the blow that leaves a dent in metal leaves a corresponding wound in the spirit.
  • Technical and industrial senses: in engineering and materials science, a dent is a specific class of surface defect — a localized plastic deformation, as opposed to a scratch or a gouge; in the automotive industry 'ding and dent' shops repair just such impact damage.
  • Idiomatic and fossilized relations: the older form dint survives in the phrase 'by dint of,' meaning 'by force of, by means of,' preserving the original 'blow' or 'force' sense long after the physical word moved to the hollow.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The chain is: strike (the event) → force of striking (by dint of) → the visible mark left by striking (the dent). Old English dynt meant the stroke of a weapon, a fist, or of fate; Middle English dynt/dint still meant 'blow' and 'force,' while the variant spelling dent began to attach itself to the pushed-in place a blow leaves. By the early modern period the split was complete: dint grew archaic except in the idiom 'by dint of,' while dent became the ordinary word for an impact-hollow — and, later, for any figurative impression, reduction, or injury. The modern figurative 'make a dent in the budget' is thus a very old metaphor wearing a modern suit. Connotation and register. Dent is a plain, everyday, slightly comic word when applied to metal and wood, and a mildly dramatic one when applied to pride and confidence. It is central to the idiom 'make a dent in,' ubiquitous in informal and business English, and always carries a hint of incompleteness: a dent is not a hole, not a crack — just a noticeable, repairable mark of impact. Related words and cautionary note. The accidental resemblance of dent to dental/dentist is purely orthographic: those words descend from Latin dens, dentis 'tooth,' a completely different family. Scholarly hygiene requires keeping the Germanic 'blow' family (Old English dynt) distinct from the Latin 'tooth' family, even though modern spelling makes them look like relatives.
