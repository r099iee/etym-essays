sublime in the source passage — Our good and sublime emperor has the greatest part in the world to play, and he is so virtuous and noble that God will not desert him, and he will fulfil his mission—to strangle the hydra of revolution, which is more horrible than ever now in the person of this murderer and miscreant (Leo Tolstoy, War and Peace, Book 1, Chapter 1, spoken by the monarchist hostess Anna Pavlovna Scherer regarding Emperor Alexander I of Russia) — characterizes the Tsar with an aura of supreme moral majesty, sacred loftiness, and providential grandeur. In the heightened ideological rhetoric of the anti-Napoleonic European aristocracy, the Emperor is not merely an effective political ruler; he is an exalted, quasi-divine instrument of virtue destined to triumph over the demonic chaos of the French Revolution. The adjective blends Christian piety, imperial sovereignty, and transcendent moral beauty.

In broader literary, aesthetic, philosophical, and everyday English, sublime is an adjective and noun denoting that which is of such elevated majesty, transcendent grandeur, supreme beauty, or moral nobility as to inspire deep veneration, awe, wonder, or an overwhelming elevation of the human soul. Historically, it forms one of the central pillars of Western aesthetic philosophy, standing in sharp contrast to mere beauty: whereas the beautiful charms, pleases, and soothes with harmony and symmetry, the sublime overwhelms, astounds, and humbles the observer through vastness, untamed power, infinite depth, or heroic virtue.

  • Aesthetic and philosophical sense (awe, vastness, and terror in beauty): Describing natural phenomena, architecture, or cosmic scale that evoke awe mixed with a thrilling sense of human insignificance. For example: the sublime expanse of the alpine glaciers; the sublime fury of the hurricane at sea; Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony achieves moments of truly sublime terror and joy. In the philosophical traditions of Longinus, Edmund Burke, and Immanuel Kant, the sublime represents the mind confronting what exceeds its sensory comprehension.
  • Moral and spiritual sense (supreme virtue and heroic nobility): Characterizing thoughts, deeds, sacrifices, or spiritual states of the highest ethical purity. For example: a sublime act of self-sacrifice; sublime courage in the face of martyrdom; his life was dedicated to a sublime religious ideal. Here, it reflects the source passage's portrayal of moral sovereignty.
  • Chemical and physical sense (phase transition without melting): (Used also as a verb sublime / sublimate): The direct transition of a solid substance into a gas upon heating without passing through an intermediate liquid state (e.g. dry ice sublimes at room temperature; iodine sublimes into purple vapor). Historically rooted in medieval alchemy, where heating materials to vapor was seen as purifying and elevating coarse matter into spirit.
  • Colloquial and modern praise (perfection, delight, and consummate excellence): Informally applied in culinary, artistic, and athletic contexts to denote supreme quality or effortless perfection. For example: the pastry was absolutely sublime; she played a sublime backhand winner on match point; a sublime evening of chamber music.
  • Idiomatic and ironic usage (the sublime to the ridiculous): Immortalized in the famous proverb often attributed to Napoleon Bonaparte and Thomas Paine: "From the sublime to the ridiculous is but a step" (French: Du sublime au ridicule il n'y a qu'un pas), warning that grand, bombastic pretension easily collapses into absurd comedy when overplayed.
  • Collocations and grammatical behavior: Functions both as an adjective (sublime beauty, sublime confidence, sublime indifference) and as an abstract substantive with the definite article (the sublime, e.g. the theory of the sublime). Commonly collocates with words of vision, nature, and emotion: sublime majesty, sublime heights, sublime spectacle.

At its core, sublime is the vocabulary of elevation. From its ancient Latin architectural roots signifying reaching up to the lintel (the highest beam of a doorway), it has named humanity's encounter with the highest peaks of nature, spirit, art, and cosmos.
