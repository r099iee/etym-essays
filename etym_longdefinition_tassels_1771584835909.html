Tassels are tufts of loosely hanging threads or cords, gathered together, knotted, and left to fall free from a base, used as ornaments on clothing, curtains, cushions, and ceremonial dress. In the description of the court girdle in The Story of the Stone — “a court girdle of coloured silks braided at regular intervals into elaborate clusters of knotwork and terminating in long tassels” — the word supplies the terminal flourish of the whole costume. The girdle is built of braiding and knotwork — dense, laborious, structured decoration — and the tassels are where that structure ends and gives way to free, swaying threads. They are the “terminating” element: the costume's last word, and one that moves. As Bao-yu walks, the long tassels swing and toss, adding motion and life to an outfit of great formal richness, and mirroring the elaborate, knotty texture of the social world around him — a braid of relationships that ends, decorously, in a visible display of status. Primary senses.
  • The ornament (card's sense) — a hanging bunch of threads or cords, knotted or bound at one end and often finished with a smaller knot or button at the tip, attached to sashes, girdles, curtains, cushions, lampshades, and ceremonial dress.
  • The fastening (older sense) — in Middle English, tassel first meant “a clasp, a fastening, a mantle-fastener”, the little tie that secured a cloak; the ornament and the fastener are the same object doing two jobs.
  • Botany: the maize tassel — the tassel-like, pendent inflorescence at the top of the maize (corn) stalk, which bears the male flowers; the word was transferred to the plant in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
  • A tuft — a tassel can name any small hanging tuft of hair, fibre, or feathers, as of a beard or a brush.
  • Verb: to tassel — to furnish or ornament with tassels; the participle tasselled (British) or tasseled (American), “furnished with tassels”.
Construction and craft. A tassel is built in a few motions: the threads or cords are laid together in a bunch, bound tightly near the top to form a head, and allowed to hang loose in a skirt of strands below; the head is often covered with a woven or plaited cap or slipped into a metal or corded socket, and the tip is often finished with a knot or a small decorative button. The craft of tassel-making — passementerie — flourished in France, where the tassel, the fringe, and the cord (gimp) were made into elaborate trimmings for state robes, theatre curtains, and upholstery. A fine tassel is a small miracle of the rope-maker's art: the strands must hang evenly, the head must be firm, and the whole must move freely, since the tassel's beauty is partly kinetic — it is an ornament designed to sway. How tassels differ from their neighbours. A fringe is a band of hanging threads along an edge, continuous and attached along its whole length; a tassel is a gathered, knotted bunch hanging from a single point or from the end of a cord. A pompon (or pompom) is a round, fluffy ball of threads, the tassel's chubbier cousin, without the streaming strands. A tuft names a natural clump of hair, feathers, or grass, without the deliberate knot. An epaulette is a structured shoulder ornament that may end in fringed cords, but the tassel is the free-hanging element itself. A tass (an older word for the hanging end of a mantle) and a torsade (a twisted cord with tasselled ends, used as a tie-back) are close relatives of the same craft family. A frog, in dressmaking, is a decorative loop-and-button fastening, structurally different though related in spirit. Usage and cultural range. Tassels are among the oldest and most widespread of ornaments. They appear in the textile record of the ancient Near East and Egypt, on the fringes of priestly vestments (the biblical blue tassels — tzitzit — are a religious survival of the same ornament), on the silks and hangings of imperial China and the court girdle of the novel, on the braids and harnesses of the Ottoman and Mughal courts, and in the tasselled dress of European academe. In the modern world the graduation tassel, worn on the mortarboard and moved from one side to the other at the conferral of a degree, is the word's best-known ceremonial survival; tassels also trim curtains, cushions, lampshades, key chains, and bookmarks. In botany, the tassel of the maize plant names the male flower cluster at the top of the stalk, which sheds pollen onto the silk below — a striking transfer of the word from the weaving-house to the field. Historical bridge. The word entered English from Old French tassel in the thirteenth century, first in the fastening sense — a clasp or mantle-fastener — and by the late fourteenth century in the ornament sense of a pendant bunch of cords. The two senses are one idea: the little knotted bundle of threads was both the tie that fastened a cloak and the ornament that terminated its cord, and English simply kept both meanings until the ornament sense won. The origin of the French word is obscure — it may belong to a Mediterranean textile word of Semitic origin for a knotted fringe, or to a doubtful Latin derivation from taxillus, “a small die” — and the honest etymology must say so. What is certain is the word's career: from a modest clasp on a medieval cloak to the swaying finial of the academic cap and the field-top of the maize plant, tassel has always named the small, knotted, hanging thing that both secures and decorates.
