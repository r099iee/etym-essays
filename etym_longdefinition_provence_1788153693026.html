Provence (proper noun, place-name) — A historic region of southeastern France stretching from the Rhône to the Italian border and from the Alps to the Mediterranean, roughly the modern administrative region of Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur. In the passage on this card Walter Pater names the poetry of Provence as one of the rare happy conditions in which "the rude strength of the middle age turns to sweetness": the twelfth-century troubadour lyric, composed in Provençal (the language of Occitan spoken in the region), is the first great flowering of romantic love as a literary art and a school of feeling — the very "doctrines of romantic love" that shaped European poetry for centuries.

Primary sense — the region. Geographically, Provence is a land of limestone hills, olive groves, vineyards, and lavender plains, dominated historically by the cities of Arles, Aix-en-Provence, Marseille, and Avignon. Its coastline and hinterland have been inhabited since prehistory, and its Greek colony Massalia (Marseille, founded c. 600 BCE) made it the earliest Greek settlement in what is now France, the door through which Hellenic art, writing, and ideas first entered Gaul.

Secondary senses of the name in culture and usage. Provence is far more than a map reference; the name carries a dense cultural history that overlaps with several of its senses in literature and modern life:

  • The Roman province: the name descends directly from Latin prōvincia, because this was "the Province" par excellence — the first Roman province beyond the Alps, organised as Gallia Narbonensis after Rome's conquests of 121–118 BCE. The word Provence simply is the word province, kept as a proper noun while the common noun evolved separately.
  • The county of medieval Provence: after the Carolingian collapse the region was a county, later a marquisate and part of the kingdom of Burgundy, ruled by its own counts until 1481, when it passed to the French crown on the death of the last count, Charles of Maine.
  • The land of the troubadours: in literary and art-historical use Provence names the birthplace of the troubadour lyric (12th–13th centuries) — Guilhem de Peitieus, Marcabru, Raimbaut d'Aurenga, Arnaut Daniel — and with it the "doctrines of romantic love" (fin'amor) that Dante, Petrarch, and the whole tradition of European love poetry inherit. "The poetry of Provence" is a fixed phrase in criticism for this school.
  • The language: "Provençal" denotes the Occitan language as spoken in the region (and, loosely, Occitan generally in older scholarship); it was one of the great literary languages of medieval Europe and the vehicle of the troubadour corpus.
  • The modern region and lifestyle: since the 19th century — with the poetry of Frédéric Mistral, the paintings of Cézanne and Van Gogh, and the films and novels of Marcel Pagnol — Provence has become the emblem of Mediterranean France: light, colour, food, warmth, and leisure. "Provence" today is as much a marketable mood as a place.
  • Symbolic sense: in the passage's context the name stands for sweetness, refinement, and the classical revival — the point where the rude energy of the Middle Ages first turns toward the Hellenic ideal of perfect sweetness. Pater's Provence is a state of culture as much as a territory.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. One chain of events connects every sense: Rome's conquest made the region a province; the province's name outlived the empire and became the region's name; the region's medieval culture produced the troubadour poetry that made the name a byword for love and sweetness; and two thousand years of accumulated memory turned the byword into a modern ideal of the good life. No other French region so economically joins ancient, medieval, and modern senses in a single word.
