The word “culled” leads us back to a very early gesture: the hand reaching down to gather and pick out. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *leg-, “to gather, collect, pick out”, a base confined to the western branches of the family — Greek, Italic, Celtic, and Germanic — with no reflex in Indo-Iranian. In Greek the root took a spectacular turn: legein, “to pick out”, widened into “to count, to recount, to speak”, and from it flowed logos, “word, reason, account”, an entire philosophical universe — logic, dialogue, catalogue, anthology (literally a “gathering of flowers”), and lexicon all descend from it. The Germanic branch preserved the root more humbly: Gothic lisan meant “to collect”, Old English lesan “to gather, glean” (surviving in the obsolete English “lease”), and Old Norse lesa, “to gather”, developed independently the same fateful sense as Latin — “to read”, gathering letters into meaning; modern scholars, however, often assign these Germanic verbs to a separate base *les-, so the kinship, though traditional, is debated. But the branch that matters most for our word passed through Latin, where legere fused “to gather” with “to choose” and finally “to read”: for the Romans, to read was literally to pick out letters one by one.
  • Latin legere and its family: legio, “a legion”, literally “a chosen body” of soldiers; legenda, “things to be read”, whence legend; lectio, “a reading, a selection”, whence lecture, lesson, and lectionary; eligere, “to pick out”, whence elect and eligible and, through elegans, “choosing well”, the elegant; seligere, whence select; diligere, “to single out and value”, whence diligent; intellegere, “to choose between, to perceive”, whence intellect and intelligent; and neglegere, “not to pick up”, whence neglect.
  • The immediate parent: colligere, “to gather together” — com-, “with”, plus legere — produced collecta, “a gathering”, whence collect and college (collegium, “a body of colleagues gathered together”), and, through the Romance and Old French cuillir, “to gather, pluck, select”, the Middle English verb cullen/collen, recorded from about 1300 in the sense “to pick out, choose”.
  • Sound shifts: the Italic development kept the root *leg- remarkably stable (*g remained /g/ in Latin legere), and the English form shows the prefix collapsing: com- + legere gave Vulgar Latin *colligere, Old French cuillir, and Middle English cullen, where the double -ll- and the closed first syllable produced the modern monosyllable cull.
In English the semantic history is the interesting part. From the 14th to the 17th century “cull” meant chiefly “to gather, pluck, or collect” — flowers, fruit, and fine things — a positive, pastoral verb, the language of the garden and the harvest, with an undertone of choosing the best. The discriminating sense crystallized in the 16th century: to cull was to select the best specimens out of a larger body, as a connoisseur culls the finest passages, and this is the sense the passage above draws on, handbooks culling their metres from Swinburne and Bridges. The second, pruning sense is late: in the 19th and 20th centuries, herd- and flock-management practice extended “cull” to the removal of surplus or inferior animals, and modern conservation and epidemiology gave the word its clinical, sometimes controversial weight (deer culls, badger culls, disease-control culls), while the computer age added the technical jargon of backface culling and frustum culling in graphics. Both senses — gather the best, remove the surplus — coexist today, and the passage’s “culled” plays on the first while keeping the faintest shadow of the second, which is precisely what gives the sentence its amused, double-edged flavour. Literary attestations stretch from the Book of Common Prayer’s “culled” flowers through Milton and Dryden, who cull their poetic precedents, to the modern critical essay in which these handbooks cull their metres.
