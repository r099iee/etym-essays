[ROOT CORE (PIE): *dheu- (to die, pass away, come to an end; also to be obscured, vaporous)] — branch of DEADENED
├── Proto-Germanic: *daudaz (dead)
│ └── Old English: dēad (dead) ──> Middle English: ded / deed
│ └── English verb: deaden (dead + causative -en, 17th c.: to make dead, to deprive of force or sensation)
│ └── Modern English: deadened (past participle / adjective) ◄ TARGET
└── Cognate branches: Old Norse dauðr (dead), Gothic dauþs (dead), Old High German tōt (dead); the "vapour/dullness" branch of *dheu- gave Latin fūmus (smoke) and English fume, dull, deaf, dumb.
Note: The sense development from "to deprive of life" to "to muffle sound and dampen force" is transparent and is a specialization of the causative formation; there is no scholarly dispute over the chain itself.


[ROOT CORE (PIE): *reudh- (red, reddish)] — branch of RUSSET
├── Latin: russus (reddish, dark red) ──> Late Latin: russetus (reddish-brown)
│ └── Old French: rousset (reddish-brown; coarse homespun woollen cloth)
│ └── Middle English (14th c.): russet (the cloth and its colour)
│ └── Modern English: russet ◄ TARGET
└── Cognate branches: Sanskrit rudhirá- (red, bloody) and rohita- (reddish); Greek erythrós (red, in erythrocyte); Latin ruber (red, in ruby, rubric); Proto-Germanic *rauþaz ──> Old English rēad ──> English red.
Note: The Old French forms rousset and roussel coexisted; the English noun/adjective russet is securely derived from the French cloth-name, and the colour sense developed naturally from the undyed wool's hue. The chain is uncontested.
