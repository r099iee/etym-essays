- 15th c.: "banquet" enters English from French as a sumptuous feast, the grandest of meals, borrowed with courtly prestige. - 16th–17th c.: in Tudor and Stuart courts the banquet is a lavish state feast; banqueting houses are built for display (Whitehall Banqueting House, 1622). - 18th–19th c.: civic, political and charitable banquets flourish — mayors, guilds, societies; the word gains institutional, public, formal weight. - 20th c.: state banquets (royal, diplomatic) and wedding banquets; the term becomes global, applied to large celebratory dinners from China to the Gulf. - Modern: "banquets" suggests ceremony, formality, abundance — speeches, toasts, wine; the old "little bench" sense is long forgotten.
