Proto-Indo-European Origins and the Concept of Aiming
The agricultural verb till possesses a rich and fascinating semantic trajectory that originated far outside the domain of farming. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *del- (or *tel- / *dʰel-), which carried the fundamental conceptual meaning of "to aim at, count, reckon, calculate, reach toward, or strive for a goal."

  • This ancient root developed in two major semantic directions across the Indo-European dialects: one relating to counting, recounting, and speaking (which produced Proto-Germanic *talō, source of Old English talu → Modern English tale, and Old English tellan → Modern English tell).
  • The other branch focused on spatial aiming, purpose, striving, and physical attainment, laying the direct foundation for the Germanic ancestor of till.

Proto-Germanic Nominal and Verbal Developments
In Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE), this root developed into the substantive noun *tilą (meaning "aim, goal, destination, or mark") and its companion denominative verbs *tilōną and *tiljaną, meaning "to strive after, aim for, endeavor to reach, or labor to obtain."

  • Germanic Cognates: The noun and verbal forms spread widely across the ancient Germanic world:
    • In Old High German, it produced zil ("goal, mark, aim," surviving directly in Modern German Ziel, meaning "goal, objective, destination") and the verb zilēn ("to strive, hasten").
    • In Old Saxon, it appeared as tilian ("to obtain, acquire, strive").
    • In Old Frisian, it yielded tilia ("to cultivate, produce, generate").
    • In Dutch, it evolved into telen ("to breed, cultivate, grow, raise crops or livestock").
    • In Gothic, it survived in gatils ("fitting, convenient, suitable," literally "attained to the mark").

Old English: From General Striving to Agrarian Labor
In Anglo-Saxon England (c. 700–1100 CE), the verb was inherited in Old English as tillian (also spelled teolian and tiolian):

  • Original Broad Sense: In early Old English poetry and homilies, tillian retained its broad Proto-Germanic meaning of "to strive after, exert oneself for, procure, supply, or labor with diligence." One could tillian honor, salvation, or a livelihood.
  • The Semantic Shift to Husbandry: In an agrarian society where survival depended entirely on wresting food from the earth, the supreme form of "striving and laboring to obtain sustenance" was working the soil. Consequently, during the 9th and 10th centuries, tillian underwent a powerful semantic narrowing (specialization) to mean specifically "to labor upon the land, plow, cultivate, and sow crops."
  • Old English Derivatives: This period produced the agent noun tilia (a "tiller" or farmer) and the verbal noun tilþ (meaning "the labor of cultivating the soil" or "the crop harvested," which directly evolved into Modern English tilth, the agricultural term for cultivated soil condition).

Middle English and the Standardization of Agricultural Meaning
Following the Norman Conquest, the verb transitioned through Middle English as tillen (or tylien, tulien). By the 13th century, the generalized sense of "striving" had largely faded, leaving the agricultural meaning as the dominant primary definition:

  • In Middle English literature, including William Langland's Piers Plowman (c. 1370) and Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales, the tiller and the act of tilling were celebrated as the archetype of honest, humble Christian labor upon which all social estates depended.
  • Wycliffe's Bible translation (c. 1382) firmly established till as the standard English rendering for Latin colere and operari terram.

Early Modern English, the King James Bible, and Modern Agronomy
In the Early Modern period (16th–17th centuries), the verb assumed its modern spelling till:

  • The Authorized King James Bible of 1611 immortalized the word in foundational passages, such as Genesis 2:5 ("there was not a man to till the ground") and Genesis 3:23 ("to till the ground from whence he was taken"), securing its permanence in the English lexicon.
  • In the 18th-century British Agricultural Revolution, agronomists like Jethro Tull studied the mechanical dynamics of soil aeration, leading to modern horse-drawn seed drills and specialized tillage tools.
  • In the 20th and 21st centuries, the word transitioned into modern agronomic science, where it serves as the baseline for global soil conservation debates (e.g., intensive tillage vs. no-till farming), completing a 5,000-year evolution from an ancient Indo-European root of "aiming at a mark" to the scientific stewardship of Earth's soil.
