- [Old English ār] - From the very first records the word names the lever-like implement used to row a boat — a straight shaft with a flat blade; purely functional and neutral, the basic tool of coastal travel, fishing, and war.
- [Age of sail & sea-power (16th–19th c.)] - Becomes saturated with naval and maritime meaning: oars drove war galleys, longboats, and whalers; the word carries vigorous associations of disciplined labour, galley chains, and sailor endurance, while "to pull an oar" enters idiom as doing one's fair share.
- [Recreational & sporting shift (late 19th c. onward)] - With steam power ending the oar's working role, rowing turns into sport and leisure; the word softens toward athleticism and gentlemanly competition, while remaining the everyday term for any boat-propelling pole.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still the standard word for the rowing implement and its metaphors (a "new oar" for a fresh helper, having "an oar in" for meddling); functional, but tinted with vigour, teamwork, and the romantic pull of boats.
