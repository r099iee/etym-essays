- [Latin origin: mixed in common (c. 1600)] - From Latin promiscuus, "mixed in common, indiscriminate" (pro + miscere, "to mix"), promiscuously first entered English as a neutral adverb: things taken or mixed together without distinction, classes of people mingling freely, no sorting by kind.
- [The sexual narrowing (17th–18th c.)] - As promiscuous came to be applied to persons "not restricted to one sexual partner," the adverb's scope shrank; the neutral sense of heterogeneous mixing remained in print but began to feel old-fashioned.
- [Dominance of the sexual sense (19th–20th c.)] - By the twentieth century promiscuously was, in everyday use, nearly synonymous with sexual promiscuity, carrying strong moral disapproval; the unmarked, value-neutral sense retreated to the scholarly page.
- [Modern scholarly reclamation] - In academic prose today promiscuously is still used in its original colour, meaning "drawn freely and indiscriminately from many mixed sources" — as in drawing "extensively and promiscuously" on poets from Shakespeare to Yeats — a consciously eclectic, lightly self-deprecating mingling without fussy distinctions of taste.
