POSITS: The third-person singular present of posit, meaning to assume, put forward, or set down a statement, hypothesis, principle, or premise as a basis for argument, theory, or further reasoning. In the card’s sentence the philosopher “posits” that human consciousness cannot be fully reduced to mechanical biological processes, suggesting instead a fundamental dualism — the verb marks a deliberate act of intellectual architecture: the speaker is not merely suggesting or wondering, but installing a load-bearing assumption so that subsequent deductions can stand. The tone is formal, deliberate, and often slightly provisional; the posited claim may later be tested, refined, or abandoned, yet for the duration of the argument it functions as given ground. In scholarly prose the word signals epistemic self-awareness: the author openly acknowledges that a premise is being advanced rather than demonstrated. Senses and uses (full polysemy).
  • Sense 1 — to lay down a premise or foundational proposition (the primary sense): to set an idea, fact, or axiom in place as the starting point of an argument; the sense of the card’s sentence, where dualism is posited as the ground of the philosopher’s reasoning. The posited claim need not be proved at the moment of positing; it is the platform on which proof is built.
  • Sense 2 — to assume or take as given: to treat something as true for the sake of argument or inquiry, whether or not it is known to be true; close to “assume” but explicitly public and deliberate — one posits a working hypothesis in a paper, whereas one may silently assume it in one’s own head.
  • Sense 3 — to place, put, or set in position (the older, physical sense): from the Latin past participle positus “placed, set”, the verb could still mean literally “to place”; this sense is now rare and chiefly historical or technical, but it explains the word’s whole family — position, positive, deposit, expose, compose, impose, propose — all built on the same “placing” stem.
  • Sense 4 — philosophical and scientific jargon: in logic, metaphysics, and the sciences, to posit is to introduce an unproved but necessary element — a force, a field, an entity, a law — into a model; a physicist “posits” dark matter, a psychologist “posits” an internal mechanism, and the verb is the standard verb of theory construction across disciplines.
Near-synonyms and semantic boundaries.
  • Proposes: similar in putting an idea forward; differs because propose presents an idea for consideration or adoption, often inviting negotiation or a vote, and lacks the axiomatic weight of laying down a premise that the argument will stand on.
  • Asserts: similar in stating a proposition; differs because assert declares something as true with force and personal commitment, emphasizing the speaker’s certainty, while posit emphasizes the structural role of the claim as a premise rather than the speaker’s conviction.
  • Postulates: the nearest synonym; similar in assuming without proof, especially in mathematics and formal systems; differs because postulate is more technical and restricted to axiom-like propositions required by a deductive system, while posit is broader and serves in philosophy, social science, and general high-register prose.
  • Assumes: similar in taking something as true without demonstration; differs because assume can be tacit, psychological, or even careless (“you’re assuming too much”), while posit is explicit, deliberate, and publicly announced as part of an argument.
  • Hypothesizes: similar in advancing a claim pending further support; differs because hypothesize frames an empirically testable conjecture in the scientific method, while positing need not be testable — it may set a definitional, normative, or metaphysical ground rather than a prediction.
  • Theorizes: similar in offering an explanation; differs because theorize builds or offers a systematic theory, a large explanatory structure, while posit singles out the single premise or element placed at the foundation.
Collocations and register. The verb governs a that-clause (“posits that …”) and collocates with premise, hypothesis, principle, existence, dualism, and model; the participial forms posited and the noun positing are common in academic prose. Register is formal and intellectual; the word thrives in philosophy, science writing, and quality journalism, and is rare in casual speech, where it would strike the ear as learned. Its precision is exactly why the philosopher in the card’s sentence “posits” dualism: the verb announces that a premise is being deliberately installed, transparently and for the record.
