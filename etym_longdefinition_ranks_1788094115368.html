RANKS is the plural of rank and in the Tolkien context ("But he could not pierce their ___") denotes the ordered lines of soldiers in a battle formation — the compact rows of the enemy host that Thorin Oakenshield cannot break through. The core sense of the singular rank is "a row, line, or series", and in military usage a rank is specifically a line of soldiers standing side by side; the ranks collectively means the body of common soldiers, as opposed to officers. From the physical line of troops, the word developed its famous abstract sense of "a grade, degree, or position in a hierarchy" — military rank (private, corporal, general), social rank, and by extension any graded ordering: the rank of a university, a rank in a competition, the ranking of a website in a search engine.

Primary senses:

  • Military formation (noun): a row of soldiers standing abreast; in the plural, the entire body of troops drawn up in order. "To break ranks" is to leave the formation; "to close ranks" is to press together into a solid line (and figuratively, to unite in solidarity). This is the sense in the reading: the compact bodyguard of Bolg, which Thorin cannot pierce.
  • The ranks = common soldiers (plural only): "he rose from the ranks" — promotion from private soldier to officer; "the ranks" as a collective for enlisted men.
  • Grade or position in a hierarchy (noun): "the rank of captain", "a diplomat of high rank", "people of all ranks" — a level of official standing, dignity, or social class.
  • Ordered series or class (noun): "the ranks of the unemployed", "to join the ranks of the famous", "to swell the ranks" — a group or class of people considered as a body; also a row of things: "ranks of bookshelves".
  • To rank (verb): to place in a graded order, or to hold a position in such an order: "she ranks second in the world", "ranked by size". The verb is a back-formation from the noun (16th–17th century).
  • Ranking (derived noun): the position or standing achieved — in sport, "world ranking"; in computing, "search-engine ranking" and "page rank", the algorithmic ordering of results by relevance.

Related but distinct homonym — the adjective rank. English also has an adjective rank meaning (1) growing thickly and coarsely ("rank weeds"), (2) offensively strong-smelling or tasting ("rank cheese", "rank meat"), and (3) flagrant, utter ("rank dishonesty", "a rank outsider"). This adjective is a different word etymologically — from Old English ranc "proud, overbearing, showy" — and must not be confused with the noun. Its presence in the lexicon creates a subtle perceptual overlap: "rank growth" suggests excessive, coarse vigour, and "a rank smell" a pungent, corrupt odour; these connotations occasionally bleed into literary uses of the noun, but they are separate lexemes.

Semantic bridges. The abstract "grade" sense developed from the physical "line of soldiers" because in an army, standing in a particular rank implied a particular place in the order of battle, and standing in the front rank implied precedence. By the 16th century rank was used for any position of dignity or precedence in society ("men of rank"); by the 17th–18th centuries for official grades of office; and in the 20th–21st centuries the ordering sense became algorithmic and quantitative (statistical rankings, corporate ranking systems). The plural ranks also developed the collective sense of "the membership of an organization" — "to join the ranks" — through the image of soldiers entering the formation.

Register today. Ranks is formal-to-neutral; in military contexts it is technical and precise; in social and organizational contexts it is slightly elevated; in computing it is ubiquitous ("rankings"). In the Tolkien passage the word is used with full military force: the disciplined formation of the goblin host is a barrier that even a heroic charge cannot pierce.
