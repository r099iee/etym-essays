recesses — small hollowed-out spaces, niches, alcoves, or inner corners, especially those cut back from a surrounding surface; in the plural, also the innermost, most hidden, least accessible parts of a place, a building, or the mind. In the specific literary moment on this card the word appears in a phrase of unusual architectural precision: “on either hand there were high shelves of metallic stone and columnated recesses which the flashed rays of the torches could not always fathom.” The nuance is spatial and slightly awe-filled — the recesses are deep-cut, columnated alcoves in the cave walls, shadowy voids into which the torchlight cannot fully reach, niches where both eye and light lose their grip, and where, by implication, anything might be hidden. The plural multiplies the menace.

An exhaustive account must separate the word’s physical, institutional, and figurative senses, which have grown in different directions from the same Latin root.

  • Architectural sense — a niche or set-back in a wall: In building, a recess is a hollow or indentation cut back from a wall surface — a niche for a statue, a window bay, an alcove, a set-back section of a facade. “Recessed lighting” is lighting set into a ceiling recess; a “recessed” shelf or door is one set back from the surrounding plane. This is the sense at work in the cave’s “columnated recesses” — pillared hollows in the rock.
  • Plural spatial sense — the innermost hidden parts of a place: “The recesses of a cave / forest / building” are its deepest, most secret interiors, the parts that go back out of sight. The plural is the form that carries the weight of hiddenness: the phrase “the recesses of” transforms a plain hollow into a set of unknowable depths.
  • Figurative sense — the inner depths of the mind or heart: By metaphor from physical depth, “the recesses of the mind / memory / heart / consciousness” are the deepest, most private, often unconscious regions where thoughts and motives lodge. The metaphor is ancient and continuous from the seventeenth century to modern psychology, which speaks of the recesses of memory.
  • The break / adjournment senses — a related cousin: The noun recess also names a period of withdrawal from activity — the school recess (playtime), the parliamentary or judicial recess (the period when a court or legislature is not sitting), a recess in a trial. These descend from the same Latin recessus (“a going back, a retirement, a cessation”) but diverged from the physical-niche senses in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries; they are a separate branch of the family tree, related but distinct.
  • Verbal sense: To recess is to set a thing back into a hollow (recessed lighting), to make a recess in something, or — in American institutional usage — to take a recess, to adjourn briefly. The plural noun on this card, however, is a noun of place, not of time.

In synonym boundaries the word sits among words for voids and alcoves that differ chiefly in size, depth, and function. Niche is the precise architectural term for a shallow, usually decorative recess, often holding a statue, and implies human design; alcove is a slightly larger recess, often vaulted, again suggesting deliberate architecture; bay denotes a space set back between pillars; cavity and hollow are the generic physical voids without architectural implication; nook and cranny are small, cosy, informal gaps; cove is a sheltered, often rounded hollow. Recesses alone, in the plural, accumulates the sense of hidden depth and inaccessibility — a surface that keeps “going back” out of sight — which is exactly the effect the cave scene requires. Collocations confirm the register: in the recesses of, the dark recesses of, the innermost recesses of the mind, hidden recesses, columnated recesses, to fathom a recess. The word, here, is part of the machinery of dread: it gives the cave an architecture of concealment in which the torch rays fail and the imagination supplies what the light cannot reveal.
