- c. 1300 — imitative "rap" (verb) is recorded for striking a sharp, quick, audible blow, and the noun for the blow or its sound; from the start it is the report of a firm object meeting a hard surface.
- 15th–17th c. — the word develops the brisk uses that still run through English: to "rap" on a door, to "rap out" words or orders sharply, to "rap" a knuckle in punishment — always quick, crisp and percussive.
- 18th–19th c. — figurative senses harden: "a rap on the knuckles" is a rebuke; "not worth a rap" derives from the tiny Irish coin (1720s); "to take the rap" means to bear the blame — the sharpness of the blow becoming the sharpness of judgment.
- 20th–21st c. — "rap" extends from speech to music: American slang "to rap" (to talk, from the 1960s) and then "rap" as the music genre (late 1970s) keep the old percussive snap — the blow and the beat one and the same.
