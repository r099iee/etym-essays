The history of parchments begins not with a root but with a city. Pergamon, the steep-crowned capital of the Attalid kings in north-west Asia Minor, housed one of the Hellenistic world great libraries — two hundred thousand rolls, the ancient sources boast — and thus stood as Alexandria great rival in the prestige economy of books. Pliny the Elder tells the famous story: when the Ptolemies of Egypt banned the export of papyrus to starve the rival library, the scholars of Pergamum perfected the writing skin, and the product took the city name — pergamēnē (charta), the Pergamene sheet. Modern scholarship files a gentle demurrer: human beings had been writing on skins since the Assyrians, and the Pergamenes improved a craft rather than inventing it — but the brand stuck, as brands do, and the improved article conquered the book world under Pergamum name.

The Latin phrase shortened as it travelled: pergamēna first, then medieval pergamenum and variant parcamenum, the unstressed vowels drifting as Vulgar Latin reshaped them. The thing itself meanwhile transformed the book. Rolled papyrus tolerates only one written side and snaps when folded; skin folds into the codex — quires stitched at the spine, written both sides, bound between boards — and the codex so decisively beat the roll that Christians, lawyers, and eventually all Europe adopted it. Sound shifts on the road to French turned the Latin word into Old French parchemin: the first vowel lowering, the g softening before a into the sh-like affricate, the ending settling into the familiar noun shape. Middle English borrowed it by the thirteenth century, and then added its own quirk — the intrusive t that turns parchemin into parchment, probably by the pull of nouns like pavement, a piece of folk-phonetics frozen forever in the spelling.

The semantic journey from blank skin to laden deed is the quiet heart of the word. Because every solemn instrument — charter, conveyance, recovery, will — was engrossed on skin, the skins became the instruments: lawyers ordered parchments the way printers order paper, testators locked their parchments in chests, and novelists from Scott to Dickens made the dust of parchments the very perfume of inheritance plots. Palimpsests add a further chapter: skin was so costly that monks scraped old texts away and wrote over them, so that Archimedes and Cicero survive as ghosts beneath prayer books, recovered by modern imaging. Paper, arriving through Moorish Spain and triumphing with print, stole first the book and then the office, yet English law clung to skins for enrolled statutes and conveyances into living memory — Parliament only voted to end vellum enrolment in 2016.

  • The Pergamene brand (2nd c. BCE–100): rivalry of libraries, real or embroidered papyrus embargo, and the perfection of the prepared skin under Pergamum name.
  • Skin conquers the book (100–600): the codex of folded skins defeats the papyrus roll; Scripture, law, and literature migrate onto pergamena.
  • Scriptoria and charters (600–1300): monastic book production and feudal conveyancing make parchment simultaneously the medium of salvation and of property; Old French parchemin enters Middle English.
  • Paper presses in (1300–1800): rag paper and then print capture the book trade, but deeds, diplomas, and statutes stay on skin for solemnity — the intrusive t fixes the English spelling.
  • Radical scorn and romantic glamour (1800s): reformers denounce musty parchments as landlord armour, as in the source sentence, while antiquaries and novelists gild them with romance.
  • Heritage survival (1900 to now): Torah scrolls still demand klaf, calligraphers still stretch skins, diplomas still unroll parchments, and the kitchen baking parchment honours the name in silicone-coated paper.

Literary attestation is everywhere skins were unrolled: Domesday Book and Magna Carta as the supreme English parchments; Chaucer and Langland writing for a skin-reading audience; Shakespeare Shylock sealing bonds; Scott parchments driving half his plots; Dickens Jarndyce and Jarndyce drowning in them; and the source sentence own radical oratory turning the family muniments into exhibits for the prosecution. From a city name through a workshop craft to a byword for dusty legality, parchments have carried civilization memory on sheep backs for two thousand years — and the derision of reformers only proves how much power still sleeps in skins.
