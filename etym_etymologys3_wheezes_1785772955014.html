The history of wheeze is the history of a sound-word. English does not trace it to a polished classical root but to Germanic imitative vocabulary for air forced through a narrow gap — hissing, panting, whistling breath. Because the word is onomatopoeic, small phonetic variation across dialects is historically expected; the stable core is the acoustic image itself: the high, squeezed whistle of air through a constricted passage. No secure Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it, and scholars are appropriately cautious: sound-symbolic words notoriously resist the regular sound-laws, and attempts to attach wheeze to an Indo-European "wind/breath" root are speculative.

The most frequently cited external parallel is Old Norse hvæsa, "to hiss" — used of snakes, steam, and angry breath — and its Scandinavian relatives (modern Icelandic hvæsa "to hiss," Danish hvæse "to hiss"), which sit in the same imitative air-noise field. Within English the record begins in Middle English: wheesen (also whesen, weosen), attested from the 15th century, meaning "to breathe hard, to breathe with noise, to pant." The spelling and vowel quality gradually settled into modern wheeze as writers tried to capture the high, squeezed quality of constricted respiration; the doubling and the -ee- reflect the long, tense vowel of the strained sound.

From the late medieval and Early Modern periods the medical and everyday senses dominate: the asthmatic child, the cold-ridden adult, the exhausted climber all wheeze, and literature uses the word for pathetic or comic bodily weakness. Industrial modernity then extended the verb to machines — steam engines, organ bellows, aging radiators — that make analogous squeezed-air noises, reinforcing a metaphor of worn or obstructed passages: a wheezing machine is an organism straining through narrow, tired pipes. The radiator on this card, "clanking and wheezing" in the corner of a drafty apartment, is exactly this extension: a thing that breathes laboriously as it pushes warm air through old passages.

Nineteenth-century British slang and theatrical jargon added a second life. A wheeze became (i) a clever scheme or trick and (ii) a comic gag, actor's trick, or stock routine — and hence wheezes for old jokes and labored theatrical material. The semantic path is debated but the intuition is clear: something "got out with effort," or a tired joke that has to be dragged wheezingly across a stage, or simply the show-business fondness for sound-symbolic terms for stage business. The collocation "an old wheeze" for a tired joke is firmly recorded in British English.

In contemporary English the respiratory meaning is primary and international; the slang meaning is more British, informal, and slightly period-flavored, though still understood in theatrical and journalistic British English. The form wheezes thus lives a double morphology: as third-person singular verb for what lungs and machines do, and as plural noun for multiple sounds or multiple gags. Clinical language keeps the word precise (a wheeze as an auscultatory finding in asthma); fiction uses it for atmosphere of age, illness, cold, or comically failing machinery.

  • Root. Imitative Germanic sound-symbolic base; no secure PIE root; closest parallel Old Norse hvæsa "to hiss" (reconstructions of a Proto-Germanic *hwēs- type vary and are debated).
  • Sound. ME wheesen (15th c.) → Early Modern wheeze; spelling stabilized in the 16th–17th centuries; -ee- represents the long tense vowel of strained breath.
  • Semantic drift. "breathe with noise" → medical sign (asthma/obstruction) → mechanical "strained airflow" → British slang "scheme, gag, stock routine."
  • Socio-cultural factors. Medieval and Early Modern medicine; industrial-era machinery; 19th-century British theatre and slang; modern clinical auscultation training.
  • Literary and clinical attestations. Middle English texts on hard breath; 19th-century theatrical memoir and slang dictionaries; 20th-century medical textbooks; contemporary fiction (wheezing elders, wheezing radiators).
