- Late 15th-century English — "disorderly commotion": turbulence first names the agitation of crowds and factions, the restless disturbance of a people, and so carries a heavy moral and political weight.
- 16th–17th centuries — the commotion moves outdoors: turbulence extends to the upheaval of storms, seas, and winds — the rioting of the elements as much as of men, the mob of nature.
- 19th century — the scientific turn: in the work of Lord Kelvin and Osborne Reynolds, turbulence becomes the precise technical term for the chaotic eddying of fluids and gases, and the old human, moral sense begins to drain away.
- 20th–21st centuries — the physics of disorder: turbulence is today the neutral, impersonal word for violent, irregular motion — the rough air of a flight, the swirling storm, a mob made of wind, with no trace of the human crowd left in it.
