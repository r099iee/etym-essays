Revulsion is a bookish Latin loan, and its history falls into three clearly marked phases: a late-antique medical term, a seventeenth-century general term for reversal, and a nineteenth-century emotional term for disgust. Each phase left a deposit in the modern word.

Antiquity and the medical Latin noun

The base verb is Latin vellere, “to pluck, pull, tear,” attested from the earliest Latin (Plautus, Cato) and used of wool, hair, feathers, plants, and limbs. With the prefix re-, “back, away,” it formed revellere, “to pull away, tear off, wrench back,” a verb of physical force found in classical prose. The action noun revulsio, “a tearing away, a wrenching back,” belongs to later medical Latin; it is recorded in imperial and late antique medical writers, and it is in that technical register that the word passed into the modern languages. The same verb yielded the past participle revulsus and the rare English adjective revulsed, as well as the surgical noun revulsion.

The humoral doctrine behind the term

  • Galenic medicine explained disease as a disturbance in the balance and location of the four humours, so therapy aimed at moving them: evacuation, purgation, bloodletting, cupping.
  • Revulsion named the movement away from the seat of disease; derivation named the movement towards it. A physician who bled the arm to relieve inflammation of the liver was practising revulsion.
  • Because the theory required directional flow, medical vocabulary was saturated with verbs of pulling, drawing, and turning — attrahere, derivare, revellere — and these metaphors were ready to hand when writers later needed words for emotional movement.

French and English attestation

The noun appears in French as révulsion in the sixteenth century, in medical and anatomical writing, and English adopts it in the 1540s, at first with the strictly technical sense “the withdrawal or diversion of the humours from a diseased part.” For a century English revulsion is a physician’s word. The general sense, “a drawing or turning back; a withdrawal,” is recorded in the seventeenth century, and by the eighteenth it can be applied to opinion, affection, and fortune.

Orthography and pronunciation

  • Latin revulsio → French révulsion → English revulsion: Latin vowel quantities are lost, but the consonant skeleton r-v-l-s is preserved intact.
  • English keeps the Latin-stem spelling -vuls- (compare convulsion, revulsed) rather than a phonetic -vulsh-, a mark of learned borrowing.
  • Stress falls on the penult, /rɪˈvʌlʃən/, and the unstressed first syllable reduces to /rɪ-/ or /rə-/.

The nineteenth-century turn to disgust

The emotional sense emerges in the Romantic and Victorian period, when physiological vocabulary was increasingly used for moral and aesthetic experience. Writers speak of a “revulsion of feeling” and, by the early nineteenth century, of revulsion tout court as the feeling itself: a violent repugnance that seems to operate on the nerves. The change follows a familiar pattern in semantic history, in which a technical term for the movement of fluids is transferred to the movement of feeling and then hardens into a specific emotion. What makes revulsion distinctive is that the physical image survived inside the metaphor: even today the word suggests the stomach turning, not merely the mind disapproving.

Literary and public life

  • Nineteenth-century fiction uses the noun for the sudden recoil of a character from a person or proposal, often with a physiological shudder.
  • Twentieth-century journalism fixes the modern construction “revulsion at/against,” especially in reports of war crimes and political scandal.
  • In criticism, revulsion is a useful word for the swing of taste — as in the source passage’s revulsion against Cicero after centuries of admiration.

Modern status

Revulsion now belongs to the vocabulary of strong moral emotion. It is formal rather than colloquial, and it is almost always negative in a way that includes the body. Its medical sense is obsolete in practice but still legible to educated readers, which gives the word a faint archaic resonance; when a writer wants clinical detachment, repulsion or aversion is chosen instead. The modern word, in short, is a fossil of humoral medicine that has become one of English’s most physical words for moral disgust.
