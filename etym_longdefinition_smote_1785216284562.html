smote is the archaic, strong past tense of the verb to smite, meaning “to strike or hit, to deal a blow”, and — with great frequency in elevated, biblical, and literary usage — “to affect suddenly and overwhelmingly”, of an emotion, calamity, affliction, or divine judgment. In the literary context — “A pang of fear and loss, like an echo of Gollum's misery, smote Bilbo, and forgetting even to draw his sword he struck his hands into his pockets” — the word describes the emotion as a physical blow dealt by an impersonal force: the pang does not simply occur to Bilbo, it strikes him, with the sudden, decisive force of a sword-stroke or a judgment. The nuance is of passivity and totality: Bilbo is the object, smitten before he can react, so paralyzed by the blow that he forgets even to draw his sword. The tone is deliberately archaic and solemn — smote is the past tense of the Authorized Version of the Bible (“he smote the rock”, “the LORD smote the land”), and Tolkien's choice of it lifts the moment out of colloquial prose into a biblical, fateful register, dignifying Bilbo's sudden pity into something almost like an affliction visited upon him. Because the participle smitten survives in the modern idiom “smitten with love”, the word also carries a faint, unobtrusive warmth — though here the smiting emotion is fear and loss rather than love.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings — the smite family is rich:

  • Physical striking (Old English onwards) — to smite is to strike or hit with a weapon or the hand; smote is its strong past tense. “The knight smote the dragon with a mighty blow.” Though archaic today, the sense is the root of the whole family.
  • Divine affliction and judgment (biblical) — God or fate smites: “the LORD smote the land”, “smitten with blindness”, “smitten by plague”. The blow is impersonal, deserved, and overwhelming; this sense dominates the Authorized Version (1611).
  • Emotional overwhelming (17th c. onwards) — a feeling smites a person: grief, fear, awe, conscience, love. “A sudden realization smote his conscience.” The emotion is figured as a blow dealt from outside.
  • Smitten with love (17th–18th c. onwards) — the participle became a fixed idiom for being romantically captivated: “smitten with love/admiration”. The blow of love is sudden and irresistible; this is the family's only fully current survival in everyday speech.
  • The old 'smear, stain' sense (surviving in smut) — the same Germanic root that gave “to smite” also meant “to daub, smear, defile”, and that sense survives sideways in smut (“soot, filth”, and its modern senses of obscenity), a relic of the root's original meaning.


Among near-synonyms, each shades differently:

  • struck: similar in delivering a blow; differs because struck is the modern, neutral default past of strike, with none of smote's archaic, biblical, fateful colouring.
  • hit: similar in dealing a blow; differs because hit is colloquial and usually deliberate, whereas smote is elevated and often names a blow dealt to one by fate or emotion.
  • afflicted: similar in being overcome by a painful force; differs because afflicted names prolonged physical or mental suffering rather than a single sudden blow.
  • seized: similar in an impersonal force taking hold of a person; differs because seized implies a gripping, holding grasp rather than the one decisive stroke of smite.
  • overcame: similar in being defeated or dominated; differs because overcame suggests struggle and eventual defeat, while smote is a single instantaneous blow.


In register, smote is deliberately archaic: it no longer belongs to everyday speech and is used by writers who want a moment to carry scriptural weight. Its impersonal subjects — conscience, fear, awe, God, death — striking the passive human heart distinguish it from every modern synonym, and its strong, resonant form (a single syllable, a long vowel, a firm final /t/) makes it a favourite of poets and of prose stylists like Tolkien who command the full history of English.
