• barking: Similar because both describe the sharp vocal cries of dogs and dog-like creatures; differs because a bark is a single loud, guttural-to-sharp call used to warn, threaten, or greet, while a yelp is a shorter, higher, sharper cry of pain, fear, or surprise, the sound of sudden distress rather than of communication.
  • yapping: Similar because both describe small, sharp, repetitive dog-like cries; differs because yapping connotes fussy, insistent, irritating chattering of small dogs, whereas yelping connotes pain or alarm, the register of distress rather than of complaint.
  • whining: Similar because both describe high-pitched, plaintive animal sounds; differs because a whine is a long, drawn-out, nasal wail of complaint or misery, while a yelp is short, sharp, and sudden, staccato rather than sustained.
  • howling: Similar because both describe loud animal cries; differs because a howl is a long, deep, sustained, mournful ululation, of wolves, wind, grief, while a yelp is brief, high-pitched, and abrupt, the whole opposite of sustained sound.
  • squealing: Similar because both describe high-pitched cries; differs because a squeal is a shrill, sustained, often excited or fearful cry, of a pig, a child, a tyre, while a yelp is shorter and more percussive, a syllable rather than a drawn-out note.
  • shrieking: Similar because both describe sharp, high-pitched cries; differs because a shriek is a piercing, sustained scream of terror or excitement, usually human and often prolonged, while a yelp is brief, animal, and lower in intensity, the shriek of a person, the yelp of a dog.
  • squeaking: Similar because both describe short, high-pitched sounds; differs because a squeak is a very small, thin, mouse-like sound of protest or friction, a squeaking hinge, a squeaking mouse, while a yelp is louder and more bodily, an open-mouthed cry rather than a thin creak.
  • crying: Similar because both name the utterance of sound in distress; differs because crying is the broad, neutral word, to cry being to shout, weep, or call out, with no fixed pitch or duration, while yelping specifies a short, sharp, high-pitched, animal-like cry.
  • shouting: Similar because both involve raising the voice; differs because shouting is loud, deliberate human speech aimed at being heard, while yelping is an involuntary, brief, high-pitched cry that may not be speech-like at all.
  • yelling: Similar because both are sharp, loud, high-pitched cries; differs because yelling is human, prolonged, and deliberate, to yell at someone, to yell for help, while yelping is brief, involuntary, and animal, and its etymology, via the boastful gielpan, is quite separate from yell.
  • wailing: Similar because both describe cries of distress; differs because a wail is a long, mournful, sustained lament, of grief, of a siren, while a yelp is the momentary spasm, the opposite in duration and tone.
  • hollering: Similar because both involve loud cries; differs because hollering is regional, informal human shouting, to call out loudly, with no implication of pain or of an animal register.
  • screaming: Similar because both describe loud, sharp cries; differs because a scream is an intense, sustained, usually human cry of terror, anger, or excitement, whereas a yelp is brief, animal, and lower in intensity, a scream with the wind taken out of it.
  • squalling: Similar because both describe loud, unpleasant cries; differs because squalling refers to the harsh, prolonged crying of infants or the cries of birds and wind, noisy and sustained, rather than the short staccato spasm of a yelp.
