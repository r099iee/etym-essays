Brocade names a woven luxury textile whose pattern is not printed or embroidered onto the cloth but built into its very structure: extra weft threads float across the back and are worked into raised, embossed figures that catch the light like low relief. The pattern — flowers, scrolls, arabesques, figures — is part of the loom-work itself, made on a drawloom with supplementary wefts, typically of silk shot with gold, silver, or coloured thread. The word descends from the same family that gave English broach, brooch, and broccoli, and its deepest image is of something "pointed" or "studded": the cloth is, etymologically, a surface pricked out with raised work.

The word is polysemous across materials, crafts, and figurative use:

  • The fabric (core sense): a rich silk cloth, usually with raised patterns woven in gold or silver thread; by extension any fabric — velvet, cotton, linen — woven with a raised or figured pattern. It is a staple of ceremonial dress, church vestments, upholstery, and aristocratic interiors.
  • Raised or figured work (technique sense): the pattern itself, made by floating supplementary wefts across the back of the cloth; "brocaded" work may also appear on velvet, damask, or net. In sewing and fashion the term names both the fabric and the method.
  • The verb to brocade: to weave or adorn with a raised pattern; "brocaded" as a participle means "worked with a raised pattern," and by extension "embellished, ornate."
  • Figurative and literary use: anything elaborately patterned or ornamented can be called brocaded — a style of prose "brocaded" with ornament, a landscape "brocaded" with colour. The metaphor transfers the richness of the cloth to richness of texture elsewhere.
  • Technical jargon in natural history: several moths of the family Noctuidae are called "brocade" moths in English lepidopterology (e.g. the Silver Brocade, the Pale-shouldered Brocade), so named for the patterned, fabric-like markings of their wings — a purely descriptive transfer of the textile word to animal colouration.
  • Historical and mercantile sense: from the 14th to the 17th century the word marked the prestige silk trade of Lucca, Venice, and Genoa, and then of France; in period accounts "brocade" is the emblem of court magnificence, of the dowry, of the church's most sacred vestments.

In the scene from the Jia mansion the word appears in a detail of domestic pomp — every chair has a footstool in front of it, and chair-backs and seat-covers alike are dressed in "old rose brocade sprigged with flowers." The adjective "old rose" matters: this is not the dazzle of fresh gold tissue but a muted, rose-pink luxury that has mellowed with years, the colour of a house whose wealth is so settled it no longer needs to shout. The raised, textured surface makes the cloth feel substantial and authoritative under the hand, and the word quietly establishes the family's standing before a single line of dialogue is spoken.
