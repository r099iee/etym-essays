- 16th c. - damp enters English from Middle Dutch meaning not moisture but noxious vapor: miners dreaded the deadly 'damps' of pits, the poisonous exhalations that could kill without a flame.
- 16th–17th c. - the meaning drifts from 'poisonous vapor' toward 'moisture in the air'; the noun dampness is recorded for the state of being damp - a hanging, pervasive wetness distinct from being soaked.
- 17th–19th c. - miasma theory makes dampness a public enemy: physicians blame fevers and consumption on damp air; the word keeps a faint bodily threat, something the frame feels before the eye confirms it.
- 18th–19th c. - in Gothic and Victorian prose dampness becomes the atmosphere of cellars, tombs, and caves - cold, seeping, gone sour; it accumulates the dread of enclosure and stagnation.
- 20th–21st c. - now the mild, pervasive term for wetness suspended in the air: softer than 'wetness', more felt than 'humidity', the very breath of enclosed ancient places, saturating stone and lung with equal patience.
