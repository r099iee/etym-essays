ACCLAMATION, noun. Loud, enthusiastic, and often spontaneous public approval or welcome, typically voiced collectively — a shout, cheer, or cry of honour raised to greet or praise a person. The word is the noun of acclaim: where acclaim is the verb, acclamation is the act and the noise itself, the collective voice of a crowd, assembly, senate, or triumph raised in approval. Its defining features are (a) vocality — it is literally a shout or cry, not merely clapping; (b) collectivity — the voice of many raised together; and (c) a ceremonial, often political or religious dimension — the shout that hails a ruler, greets a victor, or approves a motion. In Ruskin’s Preface to Modern Painters: “I shall shrink from no labour... to the honour of those great living Masters whom we now neglect, only to exalt the dead with vain acclamation.” The phrase “vain acclamation” is deliberately oxymoronic. Acclamation is the natural instrument of honour — the shout of the crowd, the senate, the triumph — yet here it is poured out upon the dead, who neither hear it nor want it; it is sound signifying nothing, a reflex of fashion rather than of true esteem. Ruskin implies that the Victorian art-public has degraded public praise into noise, and the word’s ceremonial, almost political gravity makes the vanity of it all the more damning. The tone is scornful but measured: “vain” does the damage, emptying the loudest collective voice in the language of honour. The primary senses of ACCLAMATION:
  • A loud, enthusiastic, collective shout of approval or welcome. The core sense: the cry of a crowd greeting or honouring a person — ‘the hero was received with acclamation,’ ‘the announcement was met with acclamation.’ The emphasis is on the voice: a shout, cheer, or cry, not merely applause.
  • Approval expressed by a whole body without a formal vote. The procedural sense: a motion or election carried ‘by acclamation’ is approved by the unanimous voice of the assembly — the election of a pope by acclamation (adoration), the passing of a motion by the senate’s shout — with no ballot cast. The voice of the body is the vote.
  • Enthusiastic, often spontaneous praise generally. By extension, any hearty, unreserved approval or applause — ‘the proposal met with universal acclamation.’ In this sense it shades toward acclaim, applause, and approbation.
Secondary and historical senses:
  • The Roman acclamation. In ancient Rome, acclamatio was the institutional shout of approval — the ritual, chanted cries of the senate, soldiers, circus crowds, and the multitude at an emperor’s appearance or a bishop’s election. It was, in effect, the Roman ballot, the collective voice by which public opinion registered itself.
  • Ecclesiastical and liturgical uses. In church history, election by acclamation and the acclamations of the faithful (‘Amen,’ ‘Alleluia’) preserve the ancient pattern of a body voicing its assent together. The word retains this ceremonial gravity in religious use.
  • Figurative and ironic transfers. In modern and literary use, acclamation can describe any unanimous, enthusiastic reception — critical acclamation, popular acclamation — and, as in Ruskin, it can be emptied by irony into ‘vain acclamation,’ praise that is noise rather than esteem.
The semantic history of acclamation begins in the primal cry. Etymologically the word descends from Latin acclāmāre, “to shout at, to shout approval” — ad-, ‘toward,’ plus clāmāre, ‘to shout’ — built on the Indo-European root *kelh₁-, “to call, shout, summon.” From the beginning the word was public and collective: the shout toward a person, the voice of the many. In Roman political life acclamation hardened into a constitutional habit — the stylized cries that hailed a triumph or acclaimed a new ruler — and from Rome the word travelled, with the institution, into the church, the court, and the assembly, reaching English in the fifteenth century already freighted with ceremony. Among its near-synonyms, acclamation must be carefully set apart. Applause is the nearest rival and the most concrete: the clapping of hands in approval, and by extension any favourable reception. Acclamation differs in being specifically vocal — a shout, a cheer, a cry of ‘Hail!’ — and in carrying a ceremonial or political dimension: a motion passed ‘by acclamation’ is approved by unanimous voice, without a formal vote or ballot. Ovation, from the Roman ovatio, was the lesser triumph, the formal procession honouring a victorious general; in modern use it is prolonged, usually standing applause. Plaudits (from Latin plaudere, ‘to clap,’ the source of applause) is a literary plural meaning praise generally. Approbation is official, formal approval — cool and administrative beside acclamation’s heat. Acclaim, the verb and its noun, is acclamation stripped of ceremony — enthusiastic praise, but not necessarily collective or shouted. In Ruskin’s sentence the word’s ceremonial, vote-like gravity is the engine of the irony: only a word so solemn, so public, so nearly constitutional, can make the noise of the Victorian art-public seem so hollow.
