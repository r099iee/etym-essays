- Middle English (c. 13th-14th c.) - Emerged as an echoic verbal formation creken / criken, initially imitating the harsh, grating cries of crows, corncrakes, and other birds.
- Early Modern English (c. 16th-17th c.) - Specialized in maritime, domestic, and theatrical contexts to describe the groaning friction of ship timbers, water-gates, doors, and windlasses (frequently used by Shakespeare).
- 18th-19th c. - Cemented in Gothic fiction and romantic literature as an indispensable atmospheric device for suspense (creaking stairs, haunted castles) and applied to aging human joints.
- 20th-21st c. - Frequently utilized in sociopolitical and economic commentary to metaphorically describe overstrained, obsolete institutions ("a creaking bureaucracy").
