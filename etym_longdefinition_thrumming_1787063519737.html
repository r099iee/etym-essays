At its core, thrumming is the present participle and gerund of the verb to thrum, meaning the production of a low, continuous, humming or vibrating sound. The word names a sound that is felt as much as heard — a deep, resonant vibration with a steady rhythmic character, softer and rounder than a buzz, heavier and more tactile than a hum. In the source passage from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, a thrumming comes from the jaws and nostrils of the sleeping dragon Smaug; the word perfectly conveys the deep, near-subsurface drone of a great creature's breath, the kind of vibration that travels through ground and chest rather than through the air alone.

Beyond this central sense, the verb to thrum and its participle have developed several closely related meanings:

  • Playing a stringed instrument. To thrum (on) a guitar, banjo, lute, or similar instrument is to play it in a loose, continuous, rhythmic way, often monotonously, producing a soft humming or vibrating quality. This sense is close to strumming but emphasizes the resulting resonant sound rather than the sweeping hand-gesture that produces it.
  • Making a low vibrating sound. Engines, motors, machinery, insects, throats, drums, distant bass notes, and heavy air currents all thrum; the word describes a continuous low-frequency vibration that is registered physically as much as aurally.
  • Throbbing with feeling (figurative). A place, a crowd, a building, or a body can be said to be thrumming with energy, tension, life, or excitement — the physical vibration metaphorized into an atmosphere of intense, barely contained activity.

There is also a distinct but historically entangled noun, thrum, with its own verbal offshoots:

  • A tuft or loose thread. In weaving, the thrums are the short ends of warp threads left on the loom after the finished cloth has been cut away — waste or fringe threads, sometimes woven back in to give the fabric a napped texture.
  • Yarn waste, especially in nautical use. Loose rope-yarn and thread scraps were worked into canvas (as "thrummed matting") to deaden leaks and absorb water, and thrums were twisted into mops, fenders, and swabs on board ship.
  • To cover with thrums. From the noun came a verb meaning to trim or cover with loose threads, to make a fringe — a concrete craft sense quite separate from the sound word.

Semantic bridge. The two families — the vibrating-sound word and the thread-tuft word — are often confused in popular etymology, but lexicographers treat the sound verb as an imitative (onomatopoeic) formation, while the thread noun is an older Germanic word of uncertain ancestry. The Tolkienesque "thrumming" of a dragon belongs squarely to the imitative family: a sound word with physical, resonant, almost geological weight, perfect for a sleeping creature whose breath vibrates through stone.

Register and usage. Thrumming is primarily descriptive and literary, though it is perfectly at home in technical writing about engines, acoustics, and machinery. It carries a hint of warmth and organic liveliness — a thrumming engine seems alive, a thrumming string seems pliant — and fantasy and adventure prose value the word for its ability to convey deep, portentous, felt vibration in a single compact verb form.
