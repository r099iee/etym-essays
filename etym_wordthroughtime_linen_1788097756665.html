- [Old English — “made of flax”] līnen is an adjective: the cloth of the flax plant, in a wool-and-leather world a marker of cleanliness and cost.
- [Middle Ages — the holy and the royal] “Fine linen” becomes the byword of luxury and purity in Biblical translation and romance — altar cloths, shrouds, royal shirts.
- [16th–18th c. — body linen and the household metonymy] The word shifts to name the shirt and shift themselves — “linen” means underclothing — and housekeeping stretches it over sheets, napery, and towels: the linen press, the linen cupboard, the linen basket.
- [19th c. — industry, paper, empire] The Irish and Flemish linen industries boom; linen rags feed the paper mills, giving “linen paper” and the banknote feel; white linen connotes colonial gentility.
- [20th–21st c. — summer luxury] Linen becomes a seasonal high-fashion fibre — breathable, wrinkles accepted, eco-chic; “linen white” and the “linen look” are design terms; the word keeps its crisp, clean, costly aura.
