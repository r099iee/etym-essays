To have one's work cut out means to face a difficult, demanding, or time-consuming task that will require great effort to complete. If you have your work cut out to fix things up, sorting the mess will be hard labour, not a quick job. The phrase always looks forward to trouble ahead.

The image is tailoring: cloth has been cut out and piled up ready for sewing, so the labourer faces hours of stitching. Originally this meant preparation was done for you, but the meaning reversed to emphasise the burden: so much is laid out that you will struggle to finish it.

Grammar is fixed: have plus possessive plus work cut out plus for-phrase or to-infinitive. You can say I have my work cut out for me, she has her work cut out, they will have their work cut out to win. The task is usually stated after: to fix things, to persuade voters, to meet the deadline.

  • Core idiomatic sense — to face a tough assignment: You will have your work cut out getting them to agree. Means the goal is achievable but will demand skill, patience, and time. The bridge from tailoring is quantity of pre-prepared labour becoming difficulty.
  • Understatement and humour: Wodehouse and modern speakers use it wryly to downplay chaos. I shall have my work cut out to fix things up pretends the mess is merely a sewing pile. The humour lies in treating social disaster as manual labour.
  • Sport, politics, and business jargon: The manager has his work cut out this season, the candidate has her work cut out. Means experts judge success unlikely without extraordinary effort. Here the phrase functions as pundit shorthand for an uphill battle.
  • Distinction from literal cutting: Have work cut out never means to physically cut. Unlike cut out a coupon or cut out sugar, the participle cut out here is passive preparation, not removal. Learners must not confuse I cut out sugar with my work is cut out for me.
  • Related tailoring metaphors: to fit the bill, to suit, to be cut from the same cloth, to cut corners. All preserve cloth imagery. Have one's work cut out is the only one focused on daunting volume rather than fit or economy.

Register is standard idiomatic, suitable for conversation, journalism, and light exposition, slightly informal for statutes but fine for reports. It collocates with face, fix, persuade, win, survive, and with adverbs really, truly, certainly. The phrase implies sympathy for the worker and respect for the scale of the job, never laziness. To understand it is to hear a tailor's bench piled high with pieces waiting for the needle.

For learners, note the fixed possessive agreement and the forward-looking complement: my work cut out to fix, for the exam, before us. Do not split the idiom or literalise cut. Hear it as sympathetic warning — this will be hard — often softened with humour. Mastering it gives you a native way to acknowledge daunting jobs without sounding defeatist.

It remains warm and idiomatic.
