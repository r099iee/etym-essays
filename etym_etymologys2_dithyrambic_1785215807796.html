The word is a direct borrowing from Latin and Greek, with no secure Indo-European ancestry; the chain is short but well documented. Chain — from the cult term of Dionysus to DITHYRAMBIC: - Pre-Greek / Aegean substrate *dithyrambos (reconstructed cultic term, probably of Aegean or Anatolian origin; the element -ambos recurs in Greek cult words such as thriambos and iambos and is taken as a marker of a non-Indo-European substrate) → Ancient Greek Dithyrambos, the epithet of the god Dionysus, and dithyrambos, “the wild choral hymn sung and danced at his festivals” (attested from the 6th–5th c. BCE; Archilochus and Pindar) → Ancient Greek dithyrambikos “pertaining to a dithyramb” (adjectival derivative, attested) → Latin dithyrambicus (borrowed adjective) → Medieval and Renaissance Latin dithyrambicus → French dithyrambique → Early Modern English dithyrambick (17th c.) → Modern English DITHYRAMBIC “wildly enthusiastic, rapturous, unrestrained” (technical term of classical criticism; extended metaphorical sense from the 19th c.). Note on debate: There is no reliable Indo-European etymology for dithyrambos. The -nth- and -mb- consonant clusters mark the word as belonging to the Pre-Greek substrate, like labyrinthos and korinthos, and the traditional connection of dithyrambos with thríambos (“hymn of triumph”; source of Latin triumphus and English triumph) is often mentioned but remains unproven — the two may simply share the substrate element -ambos. No PIE root is forced onto the word.
