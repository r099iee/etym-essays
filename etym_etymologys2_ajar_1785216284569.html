PIE root *gers- / *ghers- "to turn, bend" (reconstructed; the ultimate ancestry of the Germanic turn-word is uncertain, so this PIE stage is debated) → Proto-Germanic *karzijaną "to turn" (reconstructed) → Old English cierran (also cerran, cyrran) "to turn, change, convert," attested from the 9th century, with the deverbal noun cierr (also cyrr) "a turn, a turning, an occasion" → Middle English char(e) "a turn, a single occasion, a piece of work" (by the 13th century), which survives in the phrase on char(e) "in the act of turning," attested early 14th century (c. 1325) → "on char(e)", reanalyzed and re-spelled as on jar / on a jar (attested 1670s), the phrase’s initial consonant shifting with the reanalysis, then fused as ajar "slightly open," first attested 1718, in the sense "on the turn", of a door → Modern English AJAR (postpositive adverb/adjective) "slightly open". NOTE ON THE DEBATE: the change of "ch" to "j" and the connection to the word "jar" (a vessel) or the verb "jar" (to shake) have long been discussed; the mainstream etymology connects "ajar" to "on char" (a turn), the "jar" of a lid is a folk-etymological red herring, and any influence of the verb "jar" "to shake or grate" on the form is possible but unproven.
