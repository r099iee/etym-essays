Chain for the verb and noun, from the earliest attested English form. The word is imitative (onomatopoeic), and it has no reconstructable Indo-European root; that must be stated rather than invented. 1. Old English pyffan 'to blow in puffs, to puff' (verb; the Old English form is attested, though sparsely, alongside the related noun). The word is imitative: its shape mimics a short expulsion of air through the lips. Related expressive forms occur across the Germanic languages (Middle Low German puffen, Dutch poffen, German puffen) and beyond (French pouf), showing it to be a widespread sound-symbolic formation, not a single inherited PIE chain. 2. Middle English puffen 'to blow, puff, pant; to swell with air' (verb, attested from the 13th–14th centuries), with the noun puf 'a short burst of air or smoke' (from the same period). 3. Early Modern English puff, verb and noun, extended senses: to breathe hard (16th century), to swell with air (16th century), and the figurative 'to puff' = to praise inflatedly (from the 17th century). 4. Modern English PUFF (verb: to breathe hard and fast, to pant — the sense used on the card — and noun: a short emission of air, smoke, or vapour). Notes on debate and nuance: Puff is classed by historical dictionaries as imitative or onomatopoeic, and no Proto-Indo-European root is securely reconstructable for it; attempts to trace it beyond Germanic rest on the superficial resemblance of similar expressive words in many languages (an 'echoic' family, not a genetic one). The Old English pyffan is the earliest attested form; its attestation is sparse, and the word may well be older than our records.
