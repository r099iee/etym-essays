Grocer is a Romance trade-name built on the idea of bulk, not on the idea of growing food. Its whole history is a story about commerce: how a Latin adjective for physical coarseness became, first, a way of talking about wholesale quantity, then a title for a class of medieval London traders, and finally the warm, unheroic shopkeeper of the Victorian high street and the hobbit's comfortable doorstep. 1. Latin grossus: thickness and bulk, and the missing deep root The chain begins with Latin grossus, "thick, coarse, bulky," attested in Late Latin alongside its Romance daughters. Its pre-Latin history is genuinely obscure: there is no securely reconstructable Indo-European etymon with a clear cognate set, and specialists often treat the adjective as expressive or of substrate (pre-Roman Mediterranean) origin. This matters methodologically: a careful etymology of grocer must begin honestly at Latin and resist the temptation to invent a deeper PIE root. What Latin contributes is the physical image of size and coarseness — the raw material from which the commercial sense of "wholesale quantity" will be carved. 2. Old French: from gros to en gros to grossier In Old French the adjective gros carries the senses "large, thick, big," and in commercial usage the adverbial phrase en gros comes to mean "wholesale, in bulk," opposed to en détail, "retail, by the piece." The semantic bridge is transparent: to sell en gros is to sell in large quantities, by the gross measure, to dealers rather than to single customers. From this phrase the noun grossier emerges in the 12th century for the wholesale dealer — the man who handles the big lots, the bulk merchandise, the heavy trade of provisions, spices, and dry goods. The modern French adjective grossier, "coarse, rude," preserves the other branch of the Latin adjective (physical coarseness applied to manners), and English will later leave that branch entirely behind. 3. Middle English: grosser becomes grocer, and the Grocers' Company English borrows the agent noun in the later Middle Ages, attested from around 1275 in the forms grosser, grossour, and grocer. The word enters through the everyday channels of Anglo-Norman commerce, and its early senses are strictly wholesale: a grocer deals in gross quantities of imported provisions — spices, sugar, dried fruit, oils, and other stored goods that arrived by ship and needed warehousing and bulk distribution. The institution that fixes the word in English commercial history is the Grocers' Company of London, in existence by 1345, which governed the trade of pepperers and spicers and their descendants. Company records and medieval account books give the word a solid, respectable, money-handling reality: grocers were men of inventory, scales, and credit, a step below the great mercers but a long way above the street huckster. 4. From wholesale to retail: the semantic slide The modern meaning — the retail shopkeeper who weighs your tea and sugar — is a later specialization. Over the 16th to 19th centuries, as urban retailing grew and the division between wholesale and retail sharpened, the grocer increasingly served the single customer across the counter rather than the dealer in bulk. The word keeps the wholesale memory in its shape and in its commercial associations, but its everyday centre of gravity moves decisively to the corner shop. The spelling was anglicized (the double -ss- of grosser simplified, the c pronounced /s/), and by the 19th century the grocer is a firmly domestic figure: the emporium of tea, sugar, spices, biscuits, and tinned goods, the emblem of comfortable, respectable, unadventurous middle-class life. The very words "grocery" and "groceries" descend alongside it to name the goods and the shop. 5. Cultural status and the Tolkien moment In Victorian and Edwardian England the grocer became a social symbol of safe, settled domesticity — precisely the opposite of the romantic or the heroic. That cultural sediment is what Tolkien activates when Gloin sneers that Bilbo "looks more like a grocer than a burglar." The word brings with it aprons, scales, and shopfronts; the insult classes the hobbit socially, filing him under respectable trade rather than dangerous adventure. For the reader, the joke is doubled by dramatic irony, because this particular grocer will prove the equal of any burglar in the story. Every layer of the word's history — bulk, guild, high street, domestic respectability — is packed into the dwarf's dismissive sentence, and the full weight of that history is what makes the insult so precise.
