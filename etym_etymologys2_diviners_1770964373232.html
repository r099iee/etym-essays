Single root word: diviners (English noun, plural; singular diviner), from the Latin line of the PIE root *dyew- "bright sky, heaven, god." Linear derivation chain (earliest reconstructed to present; exact forms with dates): 1. Proto-Indo-European root *dyew- "bright sky, daylight, heaven; sky-god" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE). The root denotes the shining daytime sky, and from it came the name of the supreme sky-god of the Indo-Europeans, *Dyēus (seen in Greek Zeus, Sanskrit Dyauṣ, Latin Iūpiter). It is one of the most culturally important roots in the family. 2. → Proto-Italic *djous / deiwos "divine, heavenly; god" (reconstructed Italic forms of the root). 3. → Latin deus "god" and divus "divine, godlike, of the gods" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE onward; divus is the adjectival form of the root, later used of deified emperors). 4. → Latin divinus "of or belonging to a god, divine, inspired, prophetic" (attested 2nd c. BCE onward; formed from divus + the adjectival suffix -inus, meaning literally "of the god"). The word is the direct Latin ancestor of English "divine." 5. → Late Latin dīvīnāre "to foresee, to predict, to interpret omens, to divine" (verb derived from divinus; attested in Christian and late-antique Latin, 4th c. CE onward). The semantic step is explicit: "to be god-like" becomes "to possess or exercise god-like knowledge of the future." 6. → Old French deviner "to divine, to guess, to prophesy" (attested 12th c.) and the agent nouns devin "soothsayer, diviner" (attested 12th c.) and devineor / devineur "one who divines" (attested 13th–14th c.). 7. → Anglo-Norman devinour "diviner, soothsayer" (attested 13th–14th c.), the immediate channel of the word into English. 8. → Middle English divinour / divynour / devinour "one who divines, a soothsayer" (attested 14th c., c. 1375–1400 in Chaucer and contemporary prose). 9. → Modern English diviner "one who practices divination; a seer, soothsayer, or dowser" (the modern form with the native -er agent suffix); plural form on this card, diviners (present day). Debate/nuance note: No stage in this chain is seriously contested; *dyew- "bright sky/god" is one of the most securely reconstructed roots in Indo-European, and the Latin-to-French-to-English transmission of divinus → dīvīnāre → deviner → diviner is well documented. One nuance: English has two distinct descendants of this line — the adjective/noun "divine" (from Latin divinus directly, and via Old French devin "divine, soothsayer") and the noun "diviner" (from the agent noun devineor/devinour), and the late Latin verb dīvīnāre is the semantic pivot that converts "godlike" into "predicting." The Middle English -our forms show French spelling influence; the modern -er spelling is an English regularization of the agent suffix.
