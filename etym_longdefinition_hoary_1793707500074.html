The adjective "hoary" primarily denotes a state of being grayish-white, particularly as a result of age, most commonly applied to human hair or to natural phenomena like frost. This is its most direct and frequently encountered usage, conjuring images of venerable age, profound wisdom, or the inexorable passage of time. When used to describe a person, it almost exclusively refers to their hair, specifically when it has turned white or gray due to advanced years. This transformation imparts an aura of dignity, ancientness, or accumulated experience. For instance, to speak of a "hoary old man" is not merely to identify an elderly individual, but rather to evoke an image of someone whose physical appearance, particularly their hair, visibly reflects a long life, possibly replete with significant events, deep insights, and a quiet authority born of time. The term possesses a distinct poetic or literary resonance, often employed to imbue a subject with a sense of antiquity or reverence that simpler descriptors like "gray" or "white" might not fully convey. It elevates the description beyond mere color to a statement about the subject's enduring nature.

Beyond its literal chromatic description, "hoary" extends metaphorically to characterize anything that is ancient, exceedingly old, or venerable. This semantic expansion is a natural and intuitive one, given that the physical manifestation of hoariness (gray or white hair) serves as a universally recognized signifier of advanced age. Consequently, a "hoary tradition" refers to a custom, practice, or belief system that has persisted for an exceptionally long duration, often implying that it is deeply ingrained within a culture, highly respected, and perhaps even considered sacred due to its profound antiquity. Similarly, "hoary tales," "hoary myths," or "hoary legends" are narratives that have been transmitted across countless generations, suggesting a deep historical lineage and frequently possessing a timeless, archetypal quality. In these contexts, "hoary" signifies not just age, but also a certain gravitas, an established presence, and an enduring quality that resists the ephemeral nature of newer phenomena. It can also be applied to objects or natural features that have acquired a grayish-white appearance, such as a "hoary frost" blanketing a winter landscape, where the intricate ice crystals create a pervasive white, aged look, or "hoary moss" clinging tenaciously to ancient, weathered stones. In these instances, the word underscores the visual characteristic of being covered in something pale, often textured, and reminiscent of the white hair of old age, thereby linking the natural world to human experience of time.

The word's semantic range also encompasses a more critical or even pejorative nuance in certain applications, implying something that is old-fashioned, stale, hackneyed, or utterly lacking in originality. This is particularly true when referring to ideas, jokes, clichés, or arguments. For example, a "hoary old joke" is one that has been recounted so many times over the years that it has irrevocably lost its freshness, its capacity to surprise or amuse, and its overall impact, becoming predictable, tiresome, and utterly devoid of novelty. This specific usage highlights the aspect of age leading to weariness, predictability, or a profound lack of innovation, rather than inspiring veneration. It suggests that the subject has outlived its utility or charm, becoming a relic rather than a revered artifact. This dual capacity to evoke both respect and disdain, depending on context, showcases the word's subtle power.

The versatility of "hoary" allows for its application across a diverse spectrum of subjects, ranging from living organisms to inanimate objects and abstract concepts. When employed to describe a geographical feature, for instance, "hoary peaks" might refer to mountain summits that are perpetually capped with snow or ice, lending them a pristine white, ancient appearance, or whose exposed rock faces are extensively covered in pale lichens. This usage evokes a powerful sense of timelessness, immense scale, and majestic grandeur. In the specialized field of botany, "hoary" serves as a technical descriptor for plants that possess a dense covering of fine, whitish hairs (known as pubescence), which gives them a distinctive grayish or silvery visual quality. This is a precise scientific application where the visual characteristic directly mirrors the primary meaning of "hoary" as "covered with white hairs." Notable examples include "hoary cress" (Lepidium draba) or "hoary plantain" (Plantago media), where the epithet accurately describes their characteristic foliage. This botanical usage vividly demonstrates how the core visual meaning of the word can be adapted with precision for scientific classification and description.

The metaphorical extension to "ancient" or "venerable" is particularly potent and frequently exploited in literary and poetic compositions. Authors often deploy "hoary" to evoke a profound sense of deep history, an air of mystery, or a tangible connection to a bygone era. An "old and hoary" tree, as exemplified in the provided source context, is not merely old; it possesses an appearance that eloquently speaks of countless seasons, perhaps its gnarled bark extensively covered in pale lichens, its branches appearing skeletal and white with age, thereby embodying the very essence of antiquity and resilience. This usage elevates the description beyond simple chronological age to a more profound statement about endurance, the relentless march of time, and the silent witness of nature. The word itself often carries an archaic or slightly formal tone, which significantly contributes to its ability to evoke a powerful sense of the past and a certain solemnity. It is considerably less common in everyday, informal conversational English than its more pedestrian counterparts "gray" or "old," reserving its deployment for situations where a more evocative, historically resonant, and aesthetically charged term is specifically desired.

Furthermore, "hoary" can also describe something that is covered with, or appears as if covered with, frost, dew, or rime, imparting a white or grayish appearance. This sense is intimately related to the "hoary frost" usage and places emphasis on the crystalline, pale covering. A landscape "hoary with dew" suggests a serene morning scene where every surface is adorned with a fine, delicate, white layer of moisture, sparkling exquisitely in the nascent light of dawn. This particular sense, once again, draws directly from the visual characteristic of a white or grayish covering, linking it intrinsically to natural phenomena that visually mimic the appearance of aged hair. The unifying thread that runs through all these diverse meanings and applications of "hoary" is the fundamental visual characteristic of a pale, often whitish or grayish, covering—whether it be human hair, crystalline frost, tenacious lichen, or fine plant hairs—which then extends metaphorically to encompass concepts of age, antiquity, veneration, and, occasionally, staleness. The enduring strength and evocative power of the word lie in its remarkable ability to condense these rich visual and temporal associations into a single, potent, and deeply resonant term.
