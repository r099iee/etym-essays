Old English Imitative Start - Grunt descends from the Old English grunian ("to grunt, murmur"), an imitative word whose "root" is the sound itself — a short, low vibration in the throat, first the noise of pigs.
Medieval Human Extension - Middle English grunten covers pigs, labouring humans, and low inarticulate complaint, so the word is established as the sound of effort, pain, and grudging assent, not just animal noise.
Early Modern Figurative Labour - Early modern and later English extend grunt figuratively: "grunt work" is dull, low-status labour, and the noun comes to name humble workers — the unglamorous, inarticulate bottom of a task.
Contemporary Sense - Today grunt is the verb for any short, deep, guttural sound of effort or acknowledgment, from pigs to reluctant dwarves, with its animal-adjacent, anti-eloquent comic edge intact.
