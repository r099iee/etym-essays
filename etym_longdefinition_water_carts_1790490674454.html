water-carts (singular water-cart) were in the 19th century horse-drawn or hand-drawn vehicles carrying a large tank, barrel, or butt of water for delivery and distribution. In the source passage the child Ruskin watches them with delight: the water-carts were filled through beautiful little trapdoors, by pipes like boa-constrictors. He is recalling Brunswick Square, London, in the 1820s, where municipal water-carts sprinkled dusty streets, watered gardens, or supplied houses before piped water was universal. The trapdoors in the pavement, the fat leather hoses coiling like snakes, the delicious dripping — all belong to early metropolitan street management: dust-laying in summer, mud-flushing, and the parish pump economy just before the great water companies and sewers transformed the city.

Functionally a water-cart is a tank on wheels. A wooden or iron cask holding 200–400 gallons sits on a two- or four-wheeled frame, fitted with a perforated sprinkler bar or rose at the rear, a tap, and a filling manhole on top. A carter drives a horse at walking pace while water fans out behind to lay dust without turning the road to mud. Variants served building sites (wetting mortar and lime), farms (carrying drinking water to stock), railways (filling locomotive tenders), the army (portable water supply), and fire services (hand-pumped reserves). The word is transparently compositional — a cart for water — and therefore needs both elements understood: water as the life-necessary liquid, cart as the humble two- or four-wheeled load-carrier.

  • Municipal street-watering sense (source sense): e.g. to send round the water-carts in hot weather, the parish water-cart. The purpose was public health and comfort: to keep down dust that choked lungs and dirtied goods, to cool macadam, and to flush gutters. Vestry minutes of St Pancras in the 1820s record payments for water-cart hire by the day. The bridge from general cart to civic machine is specialisation by load and fitting.
  • Delivery and supply sense: e.g. a farm water-cart, a railway water-cart, an army water-cart. Before standpipes and mains, brewers, builders, laundries, and cattle needed water fetched from wells, canals, or plugs. A water-cart was the tanker-lorry of its age. The semantic extension is from street use to any bulk water haulage.
  • Construction and agricultural sense: e.g. to follow the water-cart with the roller on a new road, to wet concrete with water-carts. Here the cart is site plant: damping hardcore before rolling, mixing mortar, or irrigating young plantations. Modern equivalents are called bowsers, tankers, or water-trucks, but the function is identical.
  • Water as substance (first element): the clear liquid H2O, rain, spring, and mains water; e.g. drinking water, hard water, holy water. In compounds it specifies purpose or content: water-butt, water-rate, water-plug, water-closet. The bridge to the cart is content-to-container: the cart is defined by what it carries.
  • Cart as vehicle (second element): a strong open vehicle with two or four wheels for heavy loads, drawn by horse, ox, donkey, or hand; e.g. dung-cart, dust-cart, night-soil cart. By extension any low, labouring carrier, including shopping carts and computer carts. The bridge is form-to-function: anything that trundles a load can be called a cart.
  • Figurative and idiomatic fringe: to be in the cart (in trouble), to put the cart before the horse (reverse order), and American water-wagon temperance senses touch water-carts only by analogy. A water-cart itself developed no strong metaphor beyond drudgery and municipal routine — as dull as a water-cart round — except in a child's eye, where dripping mystery makes it delicious.

Ruskin's memory is technically exact. London pavements had small iron trapdoors over mains-fed standpipes; the carter coupled a leather hose — ribbed, flexible, indeed faintly serpentine — to the plug and filled through the tank's top hatch. To a four-year-old the physics of pressure, the gurgle, the overflow, and the wet patch were a complete natural philosophy. The adult writer keeps the hyphen and the plural because each cart was a discrete, lumbering individual in the square's daily theatre, not an abstraction called watering.
