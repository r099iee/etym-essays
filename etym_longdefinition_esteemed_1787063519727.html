Esteemed is the past participle of the verb esteem ("to regard with respect, value, or high regard"), used both in perfect verb phrases ("she has always been esteemed by her students") and — far more commonly — as an attributive adjective meaning "held in high regard, respected, greatly valued." It describes a person, institution, or quality that is honoured and admired, typically because of merit, achievement, or standing: "an esteemed colleague," "our esteemed guest," "an esteemed tradition." The word carries a formal, ceremonious warmth: it does not merely state that someone is liked or even respected; it asserts that their worth has been weighed and found great.

  • Primary sense — highly respected: the dominant modern meaning. "Esteemed" characterises someone or something worthy of honour: an esteemed professor, an esteemed institution, an esteemed journal. It is warmer than "respected" and more personal than "reputable," implying sincere regard for worth or achievement rather than mere public good name.
  • Verb sense — to esteem: "to regard with respect; to value highly." Also used to express appraisal in the construction "to esteem X (as) Y": "I esteem it an honour to serve." The verb today is formal and somewhat elevated; it survives mainly in fixed phrases and in the noun "esteem."
  • Historical sense — to appraise, to estimate: the older meaning of "esteem" (inherited from its Latin ancestor) was to set a price or value on something, to estimate or judge the worth of — "to esteem the cost of the voyage," "the goods were esteemed at a thousand pounds." This financial sense survives mainly in the doublet "estimate" and in older legal and commercial texts.
  • Formulaic address: "my esteemed colleague," "esteemed members of the jury," "our esteemed leader," "esteemed sir/madam." In correspondence and ceremonial speech "esteemed" is routine courtesy; in modern use it frequently carries a flicker of irony, though it remains sincere in honours, obituaries, and respectful introductions.
  • Self-esteem: the noun phrase "self-esteem" (coined in the 17th century) names regard for one's own worth, and since the mid-20th century it has become a major psychological term ("low self-esteem," "building children's self-esteem").
  • Doublets: "esteem" and "estimate" are doublets — two English words descended from the same Latin verb aestimare by different routes (through French for "esteem," directly from Latin for "estimate"), one keeping the sense of appraisal, the other the sense of respect.
  • Register and nuance: "esteemed" is formal and somewhat ceremonial. In colloquial speech it is often gently ironic ("the esteemed panel of judges has spoken"), but the sincere sense is standard in academic, professional, and ceremonial writing, and in the respectful address of individuals of standing.

Semantic bridge: the meaning moved from "setting a monetary value on something" (Latin aestimare) to "valuing something highly" to "regarding with respect." The bridge is value itself: to esteem a person is to estimate them as worth a great deal. The adjective "esteemed" thus carries the residue of appraisal — an esteemed person is one whose worth has been assessed and found high — which is why, in the Tolkien sentence, Thorin's formal "our esteemed Mr. Baggins" so effectively crowns a small hobbit with genuine honour.
