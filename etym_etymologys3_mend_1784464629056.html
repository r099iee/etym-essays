Mend is the short, aphetic child of amend, and the whole history of the word is the history of a Latin verb of correction splitting into two English shapes with two registers. To understand modern mend we must begin on the Tiber, follow the word through monastic Latin and Old French into the mouths of medieval English speakers, watch the unstressed first syllable fall away, and then trace how a word of "freeing from fault" became the homely verb of sewing, fences, quarrels, and convalescence. 1. The Latin foundation: menda, mendum, and ēmendāre. The Latin noun menda (with its neuter variant mendum) means "fault, defect, blemish, error" — the kind of flaw that makes a thing imperfect. From it the verb ēmendāre was formed with the prefix ex- (before consonants ē-) "out of, away from": literally to take the fault out of something, to correct, to free from error. The deep pre-Latin stage is not fully secure — handbooks often reconstruct a PIE *mend- "blemish, physical defect" and point to Sanskrit mindā "blemish" and Greek míndos "dirt" as cognates — but the Italic root itself is the firm base of everything that follows, and the uncertainty at the very bottom of the chain should be stated honestly rather than papered over. 2. Through Romance to French: amender. Christian Latin and early Romance use emendāre for moral and religious correction, and in Old French the verb becomes amender, "to correct, improve, repair," now with the initial a- reshaped under the influence of the Latin prefix ad- or the general reanalysis of the prefix vowel. By the ninth to eleventh centuries the Old French verb is an everyday word for putting right what is wrong, both morally (amending one's life) and physically (repairing things). This is the register in which the word crossed the Channel with the Normans. 3. Middle English: amenden and the aphetic split. Anglo-Norman and Middle English inherit the verb as amenden. In Middle English the word stands in its full form through the thirteenth century and beyond. But a common phonetic process of the language, aphesis — the loss of an unstressed initial vowel — begins to shear off the opening a-: amenden becomes menden. Both forms live side by side in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. This is the crucial event in the word's biography: one verb is becoming two, and the two are beginning to specialize. 4. The doublet: mend and amend diverge. By early modern English the split is fixed. Amend, retaining the fuller form, drifts toward the abstract: amending a law, a constitution, a text, one's behaviour — the word of revision, improvement, and formal correction. Mend, the short form, keeps the concrete physical core: mending a coat, a net, a fence, a broken pot. The two are textbook doublets — the same Latin ancestor arriving twice in different shapes, here through the simple accident of a dropped syllable. Emend, a learned re-borrowing directly from Latin ēmendāre in the sixteenth century, adds a third, scholarly member: to emend a text is to correct its errors critically. 5. Semantic widening: from cloth to character to health. The concrete repair sense already carries the abstract potential of the Latin original. Because mend means to remove a defect, it applies naturally to a fault of character (mend one's ways, mend one's manners, attested from the sixteenth century), to a quarrel (mend a quarrel — to repair a rift between persons), to health (to mend after an illness; "on the mend," a fixed phrase from the nineteenth century), and, in the twentieth-century idiom mend fences, to restored diplomacy and friendship — the rural image of a neighbour repairing the boundary fence becomes a metaphor for repairing trust. 6. The idiomatic core of the modern word.
  • On the mend — convalescing, recovering.
  • Mend one's ways / mend one's manners — to reform one's conduct.
  • Mend fences — to repair damaged relations.
  • Mend a quarrel / mend a breach — to heal a rift between persons or parties.
7. On this card. In The Hobbit, the narrator observes that hobbits "can't make a thing for themselves, not even mend a little loose scale of their armour." The verb sits at the lowest rung of making: not forging, not smithing, merely refastening a single scale. The word's long domestic history — mending clothes, nets, fences — is exactly what Tolkien deploys: a race that knows the market value of work but cannot perform the humblest repair is a race of consumers, not makers. And the deeper moral history of mend ("freeing from fault") is quietly ironic: the hobbit who cannot mend armour will nevertheless be expected to mend the fortunes of a company of dwarves. The card form preserves the bare infinitive mend, the word at its plainest.
