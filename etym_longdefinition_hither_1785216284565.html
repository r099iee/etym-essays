"Hither" is an archaic and literary adverb meaning "to or toward this place" — that is, "here", but with the crucial difference that it encodes motion: "here" is where one is; "hither" is where one is going. In modern English the word survives almost only in the frozen binomial "hither and thither" ("this way and that"), in the flirtatious compound "come-hither", in the stock pair "hither and yon", and in jocular, biblical, or mock-archaic prose. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2 ("Roast Mutton"), the goblins "ran ________ and thither, falling over one another and getting very angry" — the phrase contributes the visual and kinetic chaos of a mob bolting first one way, then the other, tumbling over one another in panic; the register is deliberately old-fashioned, wearing medieval robes, which is why it fits the high, archaic narrative style so naturally. "Hither" belongs to the ancient deictic family that organized English direction-words. In the old system, fully alive until the early modern period:
  • here states location (rest); hither states destination (motion toward the speaker); thither states motion away ("to that place"); whither asks or states the goal ("to what place").
  • The ablative partners hence ("from here") and thence ("from there") complete the six-point system of directional adverbs inherited from Old English.
  • In the fixed phrase, "hither and thither" names movement now toward the speaker's viewpoint, now away from it — a scattering, darting, purposeless running in every direction.
  • "Hither and yon" is the rarer variant; "hitherward" is the longer, adverbial form.
  • The adjectival "hither" = "nearer, on the near side" (the hither bank, the hither side) is a frozen extension of the adverb, chiefly 17th–19th century.
  • "Come-hither" (a come-hither glance) turns the old adverb into the language of seduction — an invitation to "come here", the word's single most alive modern descendant.
In the passage, the word does not merely mean "here": it points to the speaker-relative geometry of the chase. The goblins run toward the narrative vantage point and then away, in frantic oscillation, "falling over one another" in their panic; "hither" is half of the paired phrase that gives the reader that to-and-fro, aimless scurry. Its near-synonyms, properly separated:
  • here — location, rest, no motion.
  • this way / over here — the ordinary modern colloquial equivalents, which lack the archaic gravity.
  • thither — the paired opposite, "to that place".
  • whither — "to what place", the interrogative and relative partner.
  • hence / thence — "from here / from there", the ablative partners.
  • toward(s) — a preposition governing a noun, not a self-contained deictic adverb.
Register & polysemy. The word's modern senses are few but sharply layered: (1) the adverb "to this place", archaic and literary; (2) the fossilized binomial "hither and thither" and its variant "hither and yon"; (3) the archaic adjective "hither" = nearer, on the near side; and (4) the compound "come-hither", whose meaning has drifted from pure direction to flirtatious invitation. The historical bridge is geographic deixis: every sense encodes orientation toward a point the speaker occupies or has in view. The word's power in modern prose is precisely its archaism — it is prized because modern English has no equally sharp single word for "toward this place", and because it carries the gravity of sermons, chronicles, and romance. Its tone in the Tolkien sentence is deliberately formal and old-fashioned, and the reader registers the archaism as part of the story's medieval costume.
