PANTHEISTIC is a word of the Enlightenment that has since conquered the emotions of the Romantics and the ethics of the environmental movement. It is a compound of two ancient Greek roots — pan- "all" and theos "god" — each with its own long pre-history, joined in a single philosophical coinage of 1705 and then allowed to drift from doctrine to mood to ethic. Its story is told here in two parts, one for each root, and then in the story of their union. Part one — the root pan- (all): The Greek word for "all" was pâs (stem pant-), attested already in the Mycenaean Linear B tablets of the 14th–13th centuries BCE as pa-si, "to all". In Homer it is everywhere: pantes theoi, "all the gods"; pâs aïōn, "one's whole life". The stem pant- came to be used as a combining form, pan-, before consonant-combining bases, from the classical period onward, and the Greeks built an enormous family on it: pandemonium (the realm of all demons), panacea (the cure for all ills), pantomime (an actor who imitates all), Pantheon (the temple of all the gods). The root's pre-history is debated — whether it goes back to a Proto-Indo-European *pant- or arose within Greek itself is uncertain — but its semantic shape is stable: pan- asserts totality, universality, the sweep of everything. When later words needed to express "everything, everywhere, the whole", they reached for this single Greek prefix; the name of the eighteenth-century Panopticon (Bentham's all-seeing prison) is of the same family. Part two — the root theos (god): Greek theós, "god", is one of the most important words of European civilisation and one of the most obscure etymologically. It is attested in Mycenaean Greek as te-o, already meaning "god", in the religious tablets of Pylos and Knossos. In classical Greek it is the standard word for a deity — Homer's theoi, the "gods" of Olympus; theos in the New Testament, the "God" of Christianity. But its derivation has never been settled. The traditional explanation connects it to a root *dhes- meaning "to do, put, place" — the god as "the one who places" or "the established one" — and would link it to Latin feriae and festus, "festival days". Modern scholarship has grown doubtful: Beekes and others treat theos as likely a pre-Greek substrate word, an inheritance from the languages spoken in the Aegean before the arrival of Greek speakers, and the old *dhes- connection is now regarded as unproven. Either way, the sound is striking: the initial the- is the aspirated dental of Greek, and the word's shape — the- as "god" — lies at the heart of theology, atheism, theism, and monotheism. Part three — the union (theos + pan): The Greeks themselves already joined the two roots: the adjective pantheos, "of all the gods", was used of temples that honoured every deity, and the Roman Pantheon — the temple of all the gods, built by Agrippa and dedicated to the whole pantheon — gave the compound a monumental name. But the modern word pantheist is not a Greek inheritance; it is a deliberate coinage of the English Enlightenment. In 1705 the Irish-born philosopher John Toland published Socinianism Truly Stated, in which he described the doctrine of Baruch Spinoza — that God and nature are one single substance, Deus sive Natura — and named its holder a "pantheist", from pan- + theos. The adjective pantheistic followed within a few decades (recorded from the 1730s), and the word took its place in the new vocabulary of philosophical taxonomy alongside theist, deist, and atheist. The drift from doctrine to mood to ethic:
  • 18th century — doctrine and polemic: pantheism was a fighting word. Toland was a radical freethinker; his opponents used "pantheism" as an accusation of atheism, since a God who is identical with the world seemed to be no God at all. The word circulated in the pamphlets and encyclopedias of the French and German Enlightenment.
  • 1800s — German Idealism and the rehabilitation of Spinoza: the German Romantics and Idealists (Goethe, Schelling, Hegel, the young Schleiermacher) rescued Spinoza from obloquy; for them pantheism became the highest form of piety, the sense that the finite world is the expression of an infinite divine life. Goethe called himself a firm non-deist and a "poly-pantheist" in jest.
  • English Romanticism — mood: in England the word slipped from metaphysics into poetry. Wordsworth's "sense sublime of something far more deeply interfused", Shelley's "Spirit of Nature", Coleridge's haunted engagement with Spinoza — all were called pantheistic by their critics and defenders alike. The word now named a feeling about nature rather than a proof about God.
  • 20th–21st centuries — ecology and immanent spirituality: in modern environmentalism and nature spirituality, a "pantheistic vision" means taking the living cosmos as sacred: nature possesses "an ambient, gentle awareness", as the source card puts it. The adjective has come full circle from Toland's pamphlet — where it was a technical, almost legal label — to the description of a reverent way of seeing the world, and it remains the sharpest single word for the claim that the natural world is not merely beautiful but holy.
Sound and shape: In English the word is pronounced /ˌpæn.θiˈɪs.tɪk/: the Greek aspirated the- kept its th spelling, the combining vowel of theos was lost before the -istic suffix, and the stress falls on the penultimate syllable as in theistic. Its derivatives — pantheism, pantheist, pantheistic, pantheistical (now obsolete) — form one of the cleanest and most useful of the "-theistic" families, each suffix marking a different grammatical function of the same two-thousand-year-old roots: everything, and god.
