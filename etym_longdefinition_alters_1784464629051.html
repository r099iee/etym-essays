Alters is the third-person singular present indicative of the verb alter: to change or make different in character, form, condition, or structure, typically without destroying or wholly replacing the thing changed. As the inflected -s form it asserts that a singular subject (he, she, it, or a singular noun) presently, habitually, or generally produces such a change. The verb sits on the Latinate, slightly formal layer of English vocabulary — more deliberate and bookish than the native changes — and its whole semantic core is captured by its etymology: Latin alter, 'the other (of two),' so to alter is literally to make something into its other state, to turn it into a different version of itself rather than into nothing at all. In the passage from The Hobbit — 'This news alters them much for the better' — the word does precise narrative work. The news (the map, the key, the new intelligence about the quest) does not destroy the dwarves' situation; it reframes it, changing their prospects, their plans, their degree of hope. The -s form makes the change a standing, present fact: the news now, generally, alters them. The adverb 'much' and the phrase 'for the better' attach a value judgement the verb itself is neutral about — alter merely marks difference, and the surrounding clause supplies the favourable colouring. This is exactly how alter behaves in ordinary modern English: the verb states the fact of modification, while context decides whether the modification is an improvement, a loss, or merely a variation. Primary senses
  • To make different in character, condition, position, or form; to change, modify, transform — the general and oldest core sense (alter a plan, alter the record, alter one's mind).
  • To change the fitting or appearance of clothing, furniture, etc., without remaking from scratch — the bespoke-tailoring sense (alter a jacket, have a suit altered), which is the image used in the card's mnemonic.
  • To change in a partial or adjusted way, distinguished from replace or destroy: what is altered continues to exist in a new form (altering a law, altering a route, altering the course of a river).
Polysemy, secondary senses, and modern technical uses
  • Psychology and psychiatry — an 'alter' (plural alters) is an alternate or distinct personality state within the dissociative identity disorder (DID) framework; the noun is a 20th-century development from the notion of an altered, separate self. The semantic bridge is the same Latin 'other' idea: an alter is literally 'an other' self present in one mind.
  • Religion and architecture — altar (not alter) is the homophone for the sacred table of worship; the two are distinct in spelling and etymology (Latin altāre, 'high place,' from altus 'high'), but the identical pronunciation makes the pair a classic spelling and proofreading trap. The card deliberately keeps alter, the verb.
  • Tailoring and dressmaking — the professional sense of reshaping a garment for a better fit; alteration (n.) is the trade term for this service, and it is this concrete, craft sense that the mnemonic presses into memory.
  • Computing — ALTER is a classic SQL command that modifies the structure of an existing database object (ALTER TABLE); a precise technical extension of 'change without replacement.'
  • Figurative and idiomatic — to 'alter one's tune' or 'alter course' applies the change idea to speech, behaviour, or direction; alteration names either the act or its result (the alteration of the river's course, the alteration of the agreement).
  • Register note — alter is formal-neutral: at home in official, legal, and academic prose and in elevated narrative (Tolkien's deliberate word choice), but plainer than change in everyday casual speech.
Near-synonyms and boundaries
  • Changes — the broad native synonym; alter is more deliberate, Latinate, and often implies a careful or formal modification.
  • Modifies — close, but strongly technical and usually partial (modify the settings, the clause); alter can be total or consequential.
  • Transforms — implies a radical, often dramatic change of form or nature; alter typically leaves the thing recognizably itself.
  • Converts — stresses a change of state, use, or belief; alter lacks the sense of turning from one category into another.
  • Amends — implies corrective improvement, especially of a text, law, or fault; alter is morally neutral about whether the change is for the better.
  • Varies — stresses difference between instances over time; alter stresses the act of making a thing different.
The inflected form ALTERS is third-person singular present (he alters, news alters, the tailor alters), not the infinitive; the card's sentence requires exactly this form because the subject 'this news' is singular and the tense is present.
