- Latin — neutral mixing: miscere means to mix, mingle, blend; no blame attaches to the stirring itself.
- Old French — mixing shades toward mingling: medler means to mix, to mingle in company, and already to embroil oneself in others' quarrels.
- 14th c. — English takes both senses: knights meddle in battle (mingle in combat, honourable) while gossips meddle in marriages (intrude, blameworthy).
- 15th–17th c. — the honourable sense dies: battlefield meddling fades with armour, amorous meddling fades with euphemism, leaving only the intruder.
- 18th–19th c. — the character type hardens: the meddling neighbour and the meddling relative become stock figures of novels and proverbs, always comic, always unwelcome.
- Today — the political sense dominates headlines: meddling now means covert electoral interference, while Saruman's complaint keeps the older private sense alive — pride calling counsel trespass.
