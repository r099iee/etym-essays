The recorded history of Flemish begins with the place, not the language. Latin and early vernacular documents of the seventh and eighth centuries name the coastal district as Flandris or Flandra, a marshy, tide-washed area behind the dunes of the southern North Sea. The name is usually derived from a Germanic word for flooding or flowing water (compare the reconstructed *flaumaz), which suits a landscape of creeks, salt marshes and peat bogs; but the etymology is not settled, and alternative proposals appeal to a substrate term or to a root meaning "flat". What is certain is that the name attached first to the waterlogged land, and only later to the county and its people.

From district to county. By the ninth century the pagus Flandrensis had become a Carolingian frontier march, and by the eleventh and twelfth centuries the Counts of Flanders controlled a dense network of towns. The county's wealth rested on the transformation of English wool into fine cloth at Ghent, Bruges, Ypres and Douai. Latin charters call its inhabitants Flandrenses; Middle Dutch calls a single one a Vlaminc, formed with the Germanic suffix -ing- that also appears in "king" and "shilling" and originally marked descent or belonging. From Vlaminc the adjective Vlaemsch was formed with the Germanic -isk- suffix, the same suffix that gives English -ish and German -isch. English did not borrow the Dutch adjective whole: it substituted its own cognate ending, giving Middle English Flemmysshe and eventually modern Flemish.

Sound correspondences and form. The English form preserves the medieval stem Flem- and adds -ish; the Dutch Vlaams shows the regular loss of medial -m- before the suffix and the vocalisation of -l- in the cluster, while the place-name Vlaanderen keeps the fuller stem. The initial F-/V- alternation reflects the West Germanic voiced/voiceless split: English borrowed the voiceless f- of the coastal dialects, whereas modern standard Dutch writes v-. The vowel of the first syllable, spelled ae in Middle Dutch and pronounced as a long front vowel, corresponds to the English short e of Flem-.

Semantic expansion. Three widening steps are visible:

  • Land to people: the district name yields the ethnonym Vlaminc, "a person of Flanders".
  • People to speech: the adjective Vlaemsch comes to name the language of those people, and English "Flemish" inherits both the adjective and the language-noun.
  • Speech to culture: from the sixteenth century the word also labels the products of the region, especially cloth, lace, horses and painting, and from the nineteenth century it becomes a political and administrative identity.

English attestations and stereotypes. Middle English texts of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries use Flemmysshe for the people, the language and imported goods; Flemish weavers were numerous enough in England to provoke both economic complaint and occasional violence, notably in the London riots of 1381. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries "Flemish" acquired a comic and sometimes hostile colouring: Flemish soldiers, sailors and exiles were associated in English satire with heavy drinking, and the phrase "Flemish drunkard" circulated as a stock type. At the same time the same adjective described the fine tapestries, lace and paintings that English collectors prized, so the word carried admiration and contempt simultaneously.

The modern identity. The nineteenth century transformed the word's centre of gravity. After Belgian independence in 1830, French dominated public life, and the Flemish Movement, associated with figures such as Jan Frans Willems, made "Flemish" a banner for language rights, literature and history. The campaign produced the 1898 equality of Dutch and French in law, the Dutch-language university of Ghent in 1930, and eventually the federal Belgium of the late twentieth century, in which the Flemish Community and Region are constitutional entities. In art history, meanwhile, "Flemish Primitives" and "Flemish Baroque" became fixed museum categories, and in the twentieth century "Flemish" could evoke either the trenches of the Yser and the Front Movement or the prosperous, outward-looking region of the postwar period.

Contemporary range. Today the word is used in at least four overlapping ways: a linguistic descriptor ("Flemish dialects", "Flemish Dutch"), a regional and institutional name ("Flemish Government"), a cultural and art-historical label ("Flemish Masters"), and a political marker ("Flemish nationalist"). Its polysemy is unusually visible because Belgium's language communities are also political constituencies; a single adjective can therefore describe a dialect, a government, a painting and a vote.
