The etymology of PROWLING begins with a Middle English verb of obscure, isolated origin; no Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed, and no cognates are known in any other language. Middle English prollen "to rove or wander in a stealthy manner; to move about in search of something" (first recorded c. 1386, in the late Chaucer era; of obscure origin — the OED records NO known cognates in any other language and NO reconstructible Proto-Indo-European root) → Middle English verbal noun prollinge "searching, seeking, roaming about" (mid-15th century, showing the base verb already productive in nominal use) → English prowl (spelling with an inorganic -w- inserted c. 1500, on the model of bowls and whole; pronounced "prôle" with a long /oː/ until well into the 18th century, so the spelling long outran the pronunciation) → English prowl, transitive sense "to go stealthily over (an area) in search of prey or plunder" (1580) → English prowler, agent noun, "one who prowls or roves, as seeking prey" (1510s) and the set phrase on the prowl (1803) → English prowling, present participle (formed with -ing, used intransitively "went prowling about" and transitively "prowling the streets") → English prowling (present participle in the Hobbit passage: "so he went prowling about"). NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: the word is a historical isolate. The OED and modern etymologists agree that prollen has no known cognates in any other language and no reconstructible Proto-Indo-European root. Older dictionaries sometimes guessed at an Old French source, but the evidence for any such loan is lacking, and the guess is now rejected. The near-identity of the word's sound with that of "prey" is a pure coincidence — the two words are unrelated — though it has coloured the feel of the verb ever since. Dialectal forms prole, prol, and provle (Scots and northern English) testify to the verb's long life in speech as well as print.
