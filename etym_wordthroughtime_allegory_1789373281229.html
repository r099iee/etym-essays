- 14th Century (Late Middle English, c. 1380s) - Enters the English language through Old French *allegorie* and Latin *allegoria*, rooted in biblical commentary and Chaucerian poetry; primarily understood as a sacred hermeneutic method for uncovering divine spiritual truths beneath historical scripture.
- 15th to 16th Century (Renaissance & Reformation) - Becomes the dominant artistic, poetic, and courtly mode; embraced by Tudor and Elizabethan writers (e.g., Edmund Spenser) to celebrate monarchical virtue, political statecraft, and Protestant moral theology through elaborate chivalric romances.
- 17th Century - Attains monumental popular resonance in religious and moral literature (John Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress*), while simultaneously coming under initial scrutiny from early Enlightenment empiricists and natural philosophers who championed plain, literal, and scientific speech over elaborate rhetorical veils.
- 18th to Early 19th Century (Romantic Reaction) - Relegated to a secondary artistic tier by Romantic theorists (Goethe, Coleridge), who disparaged allegory as a mechanical, intellectual, and rigid coding device, exalting instead the organic, mysterious, and emotionally transcendent "symbol"; however, poets like John Keats dynamically repurposed the term to express the existential, symbolic richness of human life.
- 20th Century to Present - Radically rehabilitated in modernist literature, political satire (George Orwell, Franz Kafka), and post-structuralist critical theory (Walter Benjamin, Paul de Man); celebrated as an essential postmodern vehicle for political resistance against censorship and as an honest, melancholy acknowledgment of the fragmentary, ruinous nature of human signification.
