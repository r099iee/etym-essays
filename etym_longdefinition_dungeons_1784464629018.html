Dungeons (the plural of dungeon) are dark, secure chambers — especially underground prison cells or vault-like strong-rooms — associated with confinement, depth, and fortress architecture. Historically the word first named the great tower or keep of a castle (the lord's stronghold and last refuge); English later specialized it toward the subterranean prison sense that most modern readers expect. In the dwarves' song — “To dungeons deep and caverns old” — the plural pairs with caverns as a formula of under-mountain space: deep built or fortress-like depths where gold and doom both sleep. The tone is high-archaic, ballad-epic, and slightly grim; this is not a modern jail inventory but the legendary underworld of stone and wealth. The plural inventories many such depths of the lost kingdom — the delvings, vaults, and strongholds of Erebor beneath the Lonely Mountain.

Precision about the word-family. A cavern is a large natural underground chamber; a dungeon (modern sense) implies built confinement or castle vault-space, even when metaphorically applied to mountain halls. A keep or donjon is the original architectural sense — the central, strongest tower of a castle, and still the French word's primary meaning; a cell is a single small prison room; a vault stresses arched storage or burial space; an oubliette is a bottle-shaped pit-prison entered only from above (a famous Gothic accessory); a prison or gaol is the general institution. Dungeon keeps the deep, secure, imprisoning or treasure-holding under-space of fortress legend.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings

  • Underground prison / vault (primary modern sense): a dark, damp, secure chamber beneath a castle or fortress used to confine prisoners or store valuables — the sense readers meet in Gothic fiction and fantasy.
  • Historical tower / keep sense: the original meaning of dungeon in Middle English — the donjon, the great central tower of a castle (the lord's residence and last defense). This is now usually spelled donjon in English to preserve the architectural sense.
  • Fantasy / role-playing-game jargon: in gaming, a “dungeon” is a subterranean game environment of chambers, traps, and monsters explored by heroes — as in “dungeon crawl,” “dungeon crawler,” and the famous “Dungeons & Dragons” (1974), which named the genre. The word's fantasy sense is now so dominant that it can be used of any labyrinthine underground adventure space, real or virtual, in computer games and tabletop RPGs.
  • “Dungeon master”: in role-playing games, the “Dungeon Master” (DM) is the game's referee and world-narrator who runs the adventure — the term derives from the dungeon-crawl environment and has become a widely known piece of gaming jargon.
  • Figurative: a “dungeon” of the mind or society: any dark, confining, oppressive place or situation (a “dungeon of despair,” a “dungeon of the soul”), extending the physical image of confinement to mental and social states.
  • Modern subcultural sense: in certain subcultures (notably BDSM practice), “dungeon” names a dedicated play space or venue designed for such activities — a neutral, established sense in that community's jargon, from the shared image of a secure, private room.
  • Tourism and heritage sense: historic sites advertise “dungeons” as underground chambers of castles and forts (e.g., the Tower of London), and the plural “dungeons” is the standard heritage-inventory form — many chambers, one complex.
  • Compound nouns: dungeon crawl, dungeon delve, dungeon master, dungeon entrance, dungeon keeper — a productive family, especially in gaming.

Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: The word's career is a classic downward-and-inward shift. Old French donjon named the lord's great tower — a place of power and defense, from Latin dominus “master, lord.” In English the same word, borrowed as dungeoun, began to name the keep's lower, darker chambers, which held stores and prisoners; by the later medieval and Tudor periods it had specialized to the underground prison sense. French kept the tower sense (donjon); English drifted to the pit. The fantasy-gaming sense then took the underground prison and made it an adventure space — a modern, cheerful domestication of the word's grim history, with the tower-meaning surviving only in the separate spelling “donjon.”
