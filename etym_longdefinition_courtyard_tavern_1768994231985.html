courtyard — "an open, unroofed space enclosed wholly or partly by walls, buildings, or galleries" — is in this passage the architectural threshold of the child's entire world. He "lives in a room above a tavern behind a courtyard": the courtyard is the hidden, airy interior that stands between his room and the street, the space of privacy and innocence into which the noise of the world cannot penetrate. Its literary nuance here is seclusion and sanctuary — the enclosed court shields the boy from the public violence of the tavern, an unroofed room that belongs only to him. The word's defining quality is enclosure: it creates an inside by drawing a line against an outside. Because the courtyard lies "behind" the tavern, the child's true dwelling is nested and defended; the sentence maps the moral geography of the story, with the guarded court above and behind, and the arena of vice below and before.

Primary sense of courtyard: any open area surrounded by walls or buildings and open to the sky. This is the core architectural meaning, applied to domestic houses, castles, monasteries, inns, and civic buildings alike.

  • Domestic courtyard: the inner yard of a house or tenement, often the site of wells, washing, children's play, and household work — in the Mediterranean and India the vital "outdoor room" of daily life.
  • Institutional courtyard: the quadrangle or court of a college, palace, barracks, or law building, where privacy is scaled up to institutional size.
  • Fortification sense: the courtyard inside a castle or citadel, valued for defence — attackers must pass through the gate before they reach the open court within.
  • Modern by-extension senses: any enclosed ground area before or behind a building, including the hotel forecourt and the landscaped inner garden; architects speak of the "courtyard house" as a type organised around a central open space, and retailers of the "courtyard mall."

Metaphoric and secondary shifts: because the courtyard is a protected interior bounded by architecture, the word has absorbed the connotation of protected privacy — a courtyard is where things are kept out of public view. It also connects, through its older relative court, to institutions of power: the medieval "court" was originally the enclosed yard of the ruler's residence where justice was administered and retainers assembled, so the concrete space generated the abstract institutions of law and monarchy. Modern technical and commercial usage (courtyard house, courtyard hotel, courtyard garden) trades on the same sheltered-open-hub idea, marketing calm and privacy as an amenity.

tavern — "an establishment where alcoholic drink is sold to be consumed on the premises; a public house" — is in this passage the arena of violence itself: the plank-built drinking-house below, where the child "comes down at night like some fairybook beast to fight with the sailors." Its literary nuance is the exact inverse of the courtyard's: where the courtyard is silent, safe, and unroofed, the tavern is noisy, dangerous, and closed — a public, smoky, lantern-lit space of men, drink, and brawls. "Tavern" here is deliberately older and more atmospheric than "pub" or "bar": it carries the candlelight, the rough seafaring clientele, the plank floor, the moral suspicion of the ages, and it gives the story a stage-like gravity.

  • Medieval legal sense: a licensed public house selling wine, distinct from the alehouse that sold beer; the tavern was the urban meeting place of merchants, travellers, and gamblers.
  • Early modern sense: a place of lodging, refreshment, and entertainment, ancestor of both the inn and the modern bar; in the 17th–18th centuries taverns were hubs of news, politics, and letters.
  • American colonial and frontier sense: the tavern doubled as courthouse, post office, and town hall in early America — the first public institution of the frontier town.
  • Modern nostalgic/literary sense: today "tavern" is largely archaic-literary, used for effect and by bars trading on historic charm; it has bleaker, more dangerous overtones than the cosy modern "pub."

Boundary words sharpen both entries. For courtyard: court is the older, more institutional word (palace, law); yard is workaday and open; quadrangle/quad is the college variant; atrium is covered or semi-covered, the inverse of the open court; patio is a small private paved terrace. For tavern: inn adds lodging and respectability; alehouse and beerhouse are humbler and sell beer; pub is the modern cosy word; bar is neutral and commercial; saloon is the rough American frontier house; dive is the disreputable extreme. Collocations: "inner courtyard", "the courtyard of the castle or inn", "a courtyard house"; "a tavern table", "tavern keeper", "the tavern sign", "a rowdy tavern." Both words carry the register of the story — architectural precision for the safe space above, and age-old suspicion for the dangerous space below.
