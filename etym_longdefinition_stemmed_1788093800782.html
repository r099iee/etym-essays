Stemmed, in the sense of the source context ("They had only stemmed the first onslaught of the black tide"), is the past tense and past participle of the verb stem: to stop, check, hold back, or dam the flow of something that presses forward — water, blood, an enemy's advance, a flood of imports, an outflow of capital. The core image is a dam or bulkhead pressing against a current until the current is checked; a "stemmed" onslaught has been halted at the point of contact, often barely and at great cost — the sentence's "only" gives the nuance of a hard-won, temporary hold.

Polysemy of "stemmed":

  • (1) To staunch or check a flow (the source sense): "stem the bleeding", "stem the tears", "stem the outflow"; this verb is a direct loan from Old Norse stemma, "to dam, stop, staunch", and the dam metaphor is total — pressure holds back water, blood, or any flood.
  • (2) To make headway against a current: in nautical idiom, "to stem the tide" originally meant to force the ship's bow-post (the ship's "stem", the prow) against the current and push forward; the phrase now blurs with (1), so "stemming the tide" of an epidemic or of public opinion means resisting it — the modern idiom fuses the dam and the prow.
  • (3) Botanical/culinary "stemmed": "stemmed" = "with the stems removed" ("stemmed strawberries", "stemmed cherries"), from the plant-stem noun — a different word family.
  • (4) Adjective "x-stemmed": "long-stemmed roses", "tall-stemmed glasses" — "having (such) a stem", again the plant/glass noun.
  • (5) "Stem from" (originate from): "the problem stems from poor planning" — the plant metaphor of a stem growing from a root or trunk; grammar guides warn that this is a different verb from "stem the tide", and the warning itself testifies to how easily the homonyms tangle.

Semantic bridges: the checking sense starts in the Old Norse dam: to stop the flow of a stream by pressure. That naturally extends, by the fourteenth century, to blood (staunching) and then to any flood-like advance — armies, tides, crowds, contagions. The nautical strand supplies a second image: the ship's stem (prow) cutting and pressing against the current, contributing the sense of forward resistance and making "stem the tide" mean "go forward against the current" as well as "hold it back". The botanical "stem" is etymologically unrelated — it belongs to the same ancient prop-and-post family but is a separate lexeme — and its participle "stemmed" (stems removed, or having stems) and phrasal "stem from" (originate) are modern English's busy distractions from the old dam-verb.

Grammar and register: a weak regular verb; "stemmed" doubles the final consonant before -ed in the standard CVC pattern, and it is transitive ("they stemmed the onslaught") or passive ("the tide was stemmed"). In register it is formal and reportorial — the verb of central banks stemming panics, of governments stemming outflows, of defenders stemming onslaughts; casual speech prefers stopped, halted, or staunched, but when the pressure is great and the check is hard-won, English still reaches for the old Norse dam.
