The chain for cathartic runs from Greek religious and medical vocabulary through Latin and French into English. The Indo-European stage is debated and possibly absent.

  • PIE *kad(h)- "to clean, to make pure" — a proposed root (accepted by some etymologists); alternative scholarship holds that the Greek stem is Pre-Greek, of unknown origin. The Greek evidence is secure from the earliest records regardless of which theory is right; the PIE attribution is the debated stage.
  • Proto-Hellenic / Ancient Greek katharós "clean, spotless, unmixed; morally pure; of metal, unalloyed" (attested from Homer onward).
  • Ancient Greek verb kathaírō "to cleanse, to purge, to purify, to wash away filth" (from katharós; the verbal base of the family).
  • Ancient Greek noun kátharsis "a cleansing, a purging; purgation" — both medical (evacuation, purging of the humors) and religious (ritual purification); taken up by Aristotle (c. 335 BCE, Poetics) for the purgation of pity and fear in tragedy.
  • Ancient Greek adjective kathartikós "able to cleanse, purgative" (from kátharsis; the adjective formed with the suffix -ikos).
  • Late Latin catharticus "purgative, cleansing" (a loan from Greek; the medical sense dominates).
  • French cathartique "purgative" (loan from Latin/Greek; the medical word carried into the modern vernaculars).
  • English CATHARTIC (adjective, recorded 17th century) "cleansing, purgative", from French cathartique / Late Latin catharticus / Greek kathartikós; the noun cathartic "a purgative drug" and, from the 19th–20th centuries, the extended psychological sense "providing emotional relief through the discharge of pent-up feeling".

Debate / nuance note: (1) The PIE root *kad(h)- is proposed by some authorities but is not securely attested; a large body of modern scholarship (notably Beekes) argues that katharós is a Pre-Greek substrate word, of unknown or non-Indo-European origin. The chain from Greek onward is secure; the Indo-European stage is genuinely uncertain and may not exist. (2) The English adjective enters as a medical word in the 17th century; the psychological/artistic sense is a later borrowing-back from Greek theory (through catharsis, recorded in English in the 19th century in the Aristotelian sense), so the "emotional relief" meaning is an extension of the older medical sense, not a direct transfer from Greek.
