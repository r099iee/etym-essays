CHAIN 1 — HORNY (built on HORN): Stage 1 — PIE root *ḱer- (also reconstructed *ḱr̥-, *ḱer-n-) "horn; head; top of the body" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the ancestor of the horn-word family across Indo-European: Sanskrit śṛṅga "horn", Greek kéras "horn", Latin cornū "horn", Proto-Germanic *hurną "horn".
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hurną "horn" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE), built on *ḱer- with an n-stem.
Stage 3 — Old English horn "horn (of an animal); also horn as musical instrument and drinking vessel" (attested from c. 8th c.).
Stage 4 — Middle English horn (12th–15th c.), form and sense unchanged.
Stage 5 — Modern English horn "horn" (16th c. onward).
Stage 6 — Old English hornig "made of horn, horn-like" (attested 9th–10th c.) → Middle English horny/horni (14th c.) → Modern English horny "made of or resembling horn" (with the 19th-c. slang extension "sexually aroused").
NOTE: The PIE reconstruction *ḱer- is standard, though its semantic range "head / top / horn" is debated in detail; de Vaan treats Latin cornū as from a separate *ḱer-h₂-n- base. The horn-branch itself is uncontroversial.
CHAIN 2 — PLATED (built on PLATE): Stage 1 — PIE root *plat- "to spread out; flat, broad" (c. 3000 BCE), extended base of Greek platýs.
Stage 2 — Ancient Greek platýs "flat, broad, wide" (attested from Homeric Greek, c. 8th c. BCE).
Stage 3 — Vulgar Latin *plattus "flat" (c. 3rd–6th c. CE), loanword from Greek platýs; source of the Romance flat-words.
Stage 4 — Old French plat "flat, level" (9th–14th c.), from *plattus; the feminine plate "a flat piece, a sheet of metal" (13th c.).
Stage 5 — Middle English plate "a flat sheet of metal; armor plate; a flat dish" (13th–15th c.), borrowed from Anglo-French plate.
Stage 6 — Modern English plate "flat sheet" → participle adjective plated "covered with plates or a layer" (16th c.).
COMPOUND NODE — Modern English horny-plated "covered with horn-like plates" (Burroughs, A Princess of Mars, 1912): the material-word horny + the sheathing-word plated, welded by a hyphen into a single adjective.
NOTE: The Greek→Latin→Old French→English path of plate is uncontroversial; the only minor debate concerns whether the Latin borrowing was mediated by Byzantine Greek trade usage, and the exact date of the French feminine plate.
