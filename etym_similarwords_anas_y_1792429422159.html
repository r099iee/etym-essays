• Arundhatī: Similar because both are celebrated as the supreme archetypes of *pativratā* (unshakeable marital devotion and spiritual fidelity) and wives of canonical Saptarṣis (Arundhatī to Vasiṣṭha, Anasūyā to Atri); differs because Arundhatī is immortalized in the heavens as the celestial companion star to Vasiṣṭha (Alcor and Mizar) viewed during Hindu wedding rituals, whereas Anasūyā is famed for mothering the Trimūrti incarnations and bestowing the divine ointment upon Sītā.
  • Sāvitrī: Similar because both are legendary women in Indian epic lore whose miraculous ascetic purity and devotion overcame the cosmic laws of nature and death; differs because Sāvitrī famously followed Yama (the god of death) into the underworld to reclaim the life of her husband Satyavān through philosophical brilliance, whereas Anasūyā performed austere penances that sustained the cosmos during prolonged droughts.
  • Lopāmudrā: Similar because both are brilliant Vedic female seers (*brahmavādinīs* / *ṛṣikās*) married to towering Saptarṣi sages (Lopāmudrā to Agastya, Anasūyā to Atri); differs because Lopāmudrā is renowned as a co-author of Ṛgvedic hymns on the harmonization of worldly desire and spiritual duty, whereas Anasūyā embodies serene ascetic detachment and motherly blessing.
  • Gārgī: Similar because both are revered ancient Indian women of supreme spiritual stature; differs because Gārgī Vācaknavī was an unmarried philosopher and dialectician who fearlessly debated Yājñavalkya in the court of King Janaka, whereas Anasūyā represents the perfection of domestic asceticism, maternity, and wifely loyalty.
  • Maitreyī: Similar because both were spiritually enlightened wives of Vedic sages; differs because Maitreyī sought philosophical knowledge of the immortal soul (*ātman*) from her husband Yājñavalkya rather than material wealth, whereas Anasūyā exercised monumental personal yogic powers (*siddhis*) of her own right.
  • Śaṇḍilī: Similar because both are Purāṇic figures whose unswerving devotion to their husbands gave them power over the sun and celestial luminaries; differs because Śaṇḍilī famously halted the sunrise to save her stricken husband Kauśika, prompting Anasūyā to intervene and restore the cosmic order.
  • Sītā: Similar because both are central heroines in the *Rāmāyaṇa* who embody spotless moral purity and withstand the harshest forest austerities; differs because Sītā is the young queen in exile who receives maternal counsel, celestial cosmetics, and protective blessings from Anasūyā as her venerable elder preceptor.
  • Ahalyā: Similar because both are sage-wives in the *Rāmāyaṇa* encountered by Rāma during his early wanderings; differs because Ahalyā endured a tragic curse of petrification after being deceived by Indra and was redeemed by the touch of Rāma's foot, whereas Anasūyā never suffered moral taint or domestic fracture.
  • Diti: Similar because both are ancient ancestral mothers of great lines in Vedic and Purāṇic myth; differs because Diti is plagued by bitter jealousy (*asūyā*) toward her sister Aditi and births the fierce Daityas (demons), whereas Anasūyā (literally "free from jealousy") births divine beings and avatāras.
  • Kardamī (Devahūti): Similar because they belong to the same maternal and spiritual lineage in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*; differs because Devahūti was the mother who received the philosophical Sāṃkhya teachings from her son Kapila, whereas Anasūyā was her virtuous daughter who became the mother of Dattātreya.
