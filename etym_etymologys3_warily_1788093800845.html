1. The Indo-European root. The family begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *wer- “to watch, guard, perceive”. Its reflexes are widespread: in Italic, Latin verērī “to fear, revere” — literally “to be wary of” — and its derivatives; the proposed Greek connection of horáō “to see” with *wer- is debated and probably secondary. In Germanic the root produced the rich cluster on which English draws: *waraz “aware, cautious” (Old English wær, Old Norse varr), *warōn “to guard, watch” (Old English warian), and *wardaz “guard, watcher” (Old English weard, “ward”). The semantic core is vigilant perception — watching, guarding, noticing.

2. Old English: awareness. Old English wær “aware, wary, cautious” (with a short vowel, distinct from the homograph wǣr “pledge, covenant”) is attested throughout the period; the prefixed gewær “aware” is the ancestor of modern aware. The derived adverb wærlīce “warily, prudently” is recorded in Old English prose, and the verb warian “to guard, beware” (the source of beware) shows the practical, protective cast of the concept: in Anglo-Saxon culture, wariness was the prudent vigilance of a people whose survival depended on watching borders, weather and strangers.

3. Middle English: “be war”. Middle English continued the adjective as war or ware “aware, cautious, on one's guard” (12th–15th centuries). The phrase “be war” — “be on your guard” — is a Middle English commonplace in didactic, homiletic and proverbial writing, and it is the direct ancestor of beware. The semantic centre was still awareness, but the guard-duty nuance was strengthening: to be war was to know what might harm you and to act accordingly.

4. The 16th-century re-formation. Around 1550 the adjective wary was formed afresh from war(e) with the adjectival suffix -y (“having wariness”), and about 1560 warily followed with the adverb suffix -ly. Two points deserve emphasis. First, the modern adverb is a fresh Early Modern derivation, not a direct survival of Old English wærlīce — the two are parallel formations half a millennium apart, a fact the spelling (wary, not ware-y) still records. Second, the new words crystallised the shift from “aware” to “cautious”: a 16th-century “wary” man is already one who moves carefully among dangers.

5. Early Modern to Victorian: suspicion grows. In the 16th–17th centuries wary and warily keep the sense of prudent caution; Shakespeare uses both, and “warily” appears in narrative and drama for guarded behaviour. By the 18th–19th centuries, in the novel, warily increasingly describes interpersonal caution — characters eye each other warily, answer warily, mistrust warily — and the modern distrustful nuance establishes itself. The collocation “tread warily” (to proceed with calculated prudence) rises to prominence in 19th–20th-century political and journalistic prose.

6. Modern English. Today warily is a precise, economical adverb of vigilance: narrative fiction (“she moved warily towards the door”), journalism (“markets reacted warily to the announcement”), diplomacy (“both sides are treading warily”). Its relatives — aware, beware, ward, guard (the last borrowed into English from Old French garde, itself from Frankish *warda, a Germanic word of the same root) — still cluster around watchfulness. In sound, Old English wær [wær] became Middle English [war], then Early Modern [wɛːr] and modern RP [wɛə], the same vowel history as care and mare. The word's career — from “awake, aware” in Anglo-Saxon England to “distrustful” in the modern novel — is the history of vigilance itself, gradually sharpened from perception into suspicion.
