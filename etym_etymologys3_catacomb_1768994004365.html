The biography of catacomb is a biography of a joining, and the joining is literally a descent. The word is a compound of two Greek elements, and its history falls into three acts: the hollow, the site, and the generalization.

Act one — the elements. The elder element is kymbē, "a hollow vessel, a cup, a bowl," attested in Greek from the archaic period (Hesiod, 8th–7th c. BCE). The word belongs to a small family of "hollow" words that scholars attempt to trace to a PIE root *kumb(h)-, "to bend, be hollow" — a reconstruction that is widely suggested but not universally accepted, since the "cup, pot, bowl" words of the Mediterranean are precisely the sort of culture-words that travel between substrate languages. What is certain is the family's shape across the Indo-European world: Sanskrit kumbha "pot, jar, water-vessel" (Vedic), Avestan xumba- "pot, jar," and, in India, the living descendant Hindi kumbh — the pot of nectar of the Kumbh Mela festival. The root's image is concavity: something cupped, hollowed, made to hold — a cup for wine, a pot for water, a cavity in the earth. The younger element is the Greek preposition katá, "down, beneath, along," attested from Mycenaean Linear B (ka-ta, c. 1400–1200 BCE) and endlessly productive in compounds: katabasis "a going down," cataclysm "a down-pouring," catalogue "a down-listing," catastrophe "a turning down." Its own deeper origin is debated — possibly PIE *kom, possibly Pre-Greek — but its meaning is fixed: descent, downward, below.

Act two — the site. The compound kata + kymbē, "the down-hollow," was applied to the low-lying hollows and quarry cavities of the Appian Way district south of Rome. The Latin form catacumbae / catacumbas is attested from the 4th–5th centuries CE as the name of the burial complex beneath the Basilica of San Sebastiano — a network of galleries hewn in soft volcanic tufa, its walls lined with tiers of burial niches, the loculi, in which the early Christian dead were laid, often sealed with inscribed slabs. Here the crucial semantic event occurs: a term for a geographical hollow becomes a place-name, and the place-name — the Catacomb district — becomes, by metonymy, the type of the thing. The Roman catacombs were, in the formative centuries of the Church, the place of burial for the martyrs and the faithful; they were the site of commemorative meals, of veneration of the saints, and, in periods of persecution, of worship and the eucharist itself. The word was thus loaded, at its very entry into the Romance languages, with a double charge: the engineering of mass burial and the devotion of the early Church.

Act three — the generalization. Through Italian catacomba and French catacombe the word entered English in the 17th century, almost always in the plural, catacombs, naming the Roman sites first and then any system of underground burial galleries — the catacombs of Naples, of Paris, of North Africa, of the ancient world generally. In the 18th century the Paris catacombs gave the word a second, terrible lease of life: the emptied quarries beneath the Left Bank were turned into an ossuary holding the disinterred remains of millions, and the catacomb became in the public imagination a byword for labyrinthine, bone-choked, mass-death undergrounds. The 19th century, with its Gothic revival and its travel literature, cemented that imagery, and the singular catacomb emerged as a back-formation for a single gallery or a single such cemetery — the form the source passage uses.

Bullet points on the trajectory:
  • c. 1400–1200 BCE — Mycenaean ka-ta, the first element already in use.
  • 8th–7th c. BCE — Greek kymbē "hollow vessel, cup" attested; Sanskrit kumbha "pot, jar" in the Vedas.
  • Classical period — kata + kymbē compounded as "the down-hollow" for subterranean hollows.
  • 4th–5th c. CE — Late Latin catacumbae names the Appian Way burial galleries.
  • 16th c. — Italian catacomba, French catacombe; the word generalizes beyond Rome.
  • 17th c. — English catacombs borrowed, plural and proper, inseparable from early Christian Rome.
  • 18th c. — the Paris ossuary re-employs the word; mass-death imagery hardens.
  • 19th–21st c. — archaeological precision on one side, gothic and figurative life on the other: "the catacombs of the city," "of the bureaucracy," "of memory."
Sound and form notes: the compound preserves the Greek sequence katā-kumbē intact, with only the accusative plural ending reshaping the Latin form (catacumbas); the English spelling catacomb keeps the etymological -mb (as in comb), silent in speech but voiced in the spelling, and the stress falls on the first syllable: /ˈkatəkəʊm/. Semantically the word never left its descent: from hollow, to hollow-that-holds-the-dead, to any buried labyrinth, the word's biography is itself a descent into burial — which is precisely the simile the source passage performs for the bare, long-deserted place.
