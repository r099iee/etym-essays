- Mid-17th c. — celestial navigation: borrowed directly from Renaissance astronomical Latin culmināre, strictly denoting when a star or planet reaches its highest altitude as it crosses the observer's meridian.
- Late 17th c. — metaphorical zenith: writers began borrowing the astronomical term to describe human lives, monarchies, and empires reaching their metaphorical "meridian" or highest zenith of fortune.
- 18th c. — dramatic and narrative climax: expanded into literary and theatrical criticism to denote the point where plot complications reach their highest pitch and produce a resolution.
- 19th c. — historiography and romantic prose: John Ruskin, Thomas Carlyle, and Victorian historians used culminates to describe historical movements and aesthetic legacies achieving supreme, transcendent realization.
- 20th c. — standard analytical usage: became universally adopted in journalism, political analysis, and musicology (frequently paired with in: ‘the dispute culminated in war’).
- Contemporary — ubiquitous formal verb: widely employed across literature, science, and public commentary to denote the crowning peak or conclusive outcome of any sustained effort.
