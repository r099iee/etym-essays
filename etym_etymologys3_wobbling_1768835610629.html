wobbling - the biography of a rhythm that has gone slack. The story begins with a root of two faces. The PIE root *webh- signified weaving - the interlacing of threads - but, in the grouping of Pokorny and his followers, also carried the allied sense of moving to and fro: the shuttle's rhythm on the loom was already both. To the Indo-European speaker the loom was the model of a certain kind of motion - the shuttle flying back and forth, the threads crossing, the whole apparatus in a steady oscillation of work - and the root recorded both what the loom made (the woven web) and how it moved (the to-and-fro). The two faces would eventually separate into two great word-families. The weaving face produced Sanskrit ubhnati, "he weaves, he binds", and Greek hyphaínō, "to weave" - the root that gave the Greek poets their deepest metaphor of cunning construction, the web of plot and craft (the Odyssey is a hyphos, a web). The Germanic branch developed the same face in *webaną: Old English wefan gave English weave; Old English webb, "the woven thing", gave web - first the spider's woven trap, then, in the late twentieth century, the most spectacular of all its metamorphoses: the World Wide Web, the woven network of all human information. The collateral forms weft (the crosswise threads) and the old feminine webster ("female weaver", surviving as a surname) complete this branch. None of this would be worth saying in an essay on wobbling if it did not explain the word's deepest logic: a wobble is a weave - motion that has kept the to-and-fro of the shuttle while losing the shuttle's purpose. The second face of the root - the to-and-fro motion - surfaces in the ablaut form *wab-, "to move unsteadily, to wave". Old English wafian, "to wave, to waver", underlies English wave and, with the frequentative suffix, waver: the hand's fluttering, the flame's hesitating. In the Low German dialects of the North Sea coast, the same formation produced wabbeln, "to move unsteadily to and fro" - the motion of a loose plank, a full barrel, a cart on a bad road, a thing rocking slackly in its fittings. This is the crucial stage: the word is attested in the maritime and agricultural German of the coast, the dialect world of the Hanseatic traders who carried Low German words into English seaports and workshops. English took the word in the seventeenth century as dialectal wabble, and the spelling settled as wobble in the nineteenth. The frequentative suffix -le - the same one in sparkle, crackle, wriggle, and wrestle - is the word's phonetic heart. A frequentative marks repeated, small, iterative action: a wobble is not a single motion but a repeated rocking, each little oscillation a tick of a loosened clock. The suffix gives the word its character: a wobble is a rhythm that has gone slack, a motion without purpose, an oscillation that has lost its regularity. The word's sound cooperates - the labial w, the doubled bb, the little tail -le - producing in the mouth the very looseness it names. English also kept the abstract kin: waver, from the same *wab-, for the faint hesitation of a flame or a resolve, while wobble took the heavier, more physical rocking. The honesty clause: some lexicographers treat wobble as a purely echoic (sound-symbolic) formation, an independent coinage that imitates the sound and feel of loose rocking, rather than a true descendant of *webh-. The *webh- link, supported by Pokorny's dual-sense root, is the best available account - it explains the Low German wabbeln and the Old English wafian elegantly - but the derivation is not absolutely certain. Etymology at its most honest: the family tree is probable, not proven. The word's modern career has run from the physical to the figurative. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries wobble is used of wheels out of true, jellies on plates, loose teeth, the unsteady gait of the drunk; the sense of slack fit dominates - a thing wobbles when its mounting is imperfect. In the twentieth century the word spreads into figurative use: wobbling opinions, a wobbling economy, a wobbling voice, a wobbling alliance - any position or quantity that trembles between states, neither standing nor falling, because its support is unreliable. The physics transfers perfectly: the figurative wobble borrows the looseness of fit, the oscillation without direction. And in the twenty-first century both senses thrive, with the comic-mechanical colour never far away - Peake's carving head "wobbling in a mechanical way from side to side when Mr. Rottcodd walked" is the perfect emblem: carved anatomy reduced to a loose gear, life narrated as mechanism, a rhythm that has gone slack in the machinery of the world. From the shuttle of the prehistoric loom to the slack head of a wooden carving, the root has kept its double face - weaving and wobbling - and both still mean the same old thing: motion, repeated, small, and free of purpose.
