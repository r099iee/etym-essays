Sorcerous is an adjective meaning of, like, or characteristic of a sorcerer: magical, wizardly, enchanting, or uncanny in a way that suggests power over fate and hidden arts. It is more literary and slightly archaic than plain magical; it carries the old association of the sorcerer as one who deals in lots, spells, and forbidden knowledge rather than stage trickery. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, Gandalf sits blowing smoke-rings in Bag End's dim light, already with a cloud of them about him, and the sight makes him look ‘strange and sorcerous’. Tolkien shifts the cosy party toward myth: the guest is no longer only a firework-hobbyist old man but a figure of genuine otherworldly craft. Tone: elevated, faintly ominous-wondering, atmospheric rather than technical.

Word family and formation. Sorcerous is built on sorcerer + the adjectival suffix -ous, and it sits inside a family that includes sorcery (the art), sorcerer/sorceress (the practitioner, masculine and feminine), and the related lot-and-fate vocabulary sort, consort, assort, and (more distantly) series—all from Latin sors, the lot cast to decide or divine fate. The adjective is a late arrival: the noun sorcerer entered Middle English in the late fourteenth century, and sorcerous is attested from the late sixteenth century. Its register has always been literary.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. Magical is the broad, neutral modern cover term for anything of magic. Wizardly stresses the wizard as a person-type and can be affectionate or whimsical. Enchanting has softened toward charm, delight, and allure. Occult leans toward the secret and esoteric as a body of doctrine. Necromantic specifies the arts of dealing with the dead. Arcane stresses obscure knowledge rather than power. Sorcerous keeps the sorcerer-stem and a literary, slightly dark glamour—fit for smoke, dimness, and fate. Context pairs it with strange: appearance transformed by smoke into something uncanny, foreshadowing power beyond hobbit domesticity.

Polysemy and modern usages. The word's semantic range today includes the following:

  • Characteristic of a sorcerer (primary sense): possessing the nature, arts, or manner of a sorcerer—magical, spell-working, fate-touched. The sense fixed by the context.
  • Fantasy-worldbuilding sense: in games, fiction, and fantasy vocabulary, sorcerous is the standard adjective for the sorcerer class or school of magic—as in sorcerous power, a sorcerous domain, sorcerous artefacts—often distinguished from clerical, druidic, or arcane magic in game systems.
  • Rhetorical and figurative sense: anything possessing an enchanting, unnatural, or inexplicable power or glamour—a sorcerous voice, sorcerous eloquence—where the adjective is hyperbolic for hypnotic effect without literal magic.
  • Literary-historical sense: in older English, the adjective carried a genuinely dark, uncanny weight, the sorcerer as one who deals in fate and spells, nearer to the occult than to the merely magical; modern usage retains a faint residue of that sinister glamour.

Historical bridge. The semantic reasoning runs: Latin sors ‘a lot cast to learn or assign fate’ → the person who works by lots, the fortune-teller or diviner (Vulgar Latin *sortiarius) → the spell-dealer, the sorcerer (Old French sorcier) → the art, sorcery → the adjective sorcerous, describing anything of the sorcerer's fate-working quality. The word never loses its old tie to lots, shares, and allotted destiny.
