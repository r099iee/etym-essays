The story of shrewdly is one of English's most startling ameliorations: from venomous mouse to wicked villain to admiring praise for business acumen, with the adverbial -ly riding along at the end. The starting point is Old English screawa, the shrew-mouse, a tiny insectivore popularly believed venomous, whose bite could lame cattle and whose passage blighted fields. Superstition made the animal malignant.

By c.1200 Middle English transferred the animal name to people: a shrewe was a wicked, malicious, scolding person — a villain, a scold, a shrew. The shrew as scolding wife preserves this. The adjective shrewed (with -ed meaning possessed of shrew-nature) thus first meant wicked, depraved, ill-natured (c.1300). A shrewed man was not clever but bad; a shrewed blow was a wicked, painful blow.

The pivot came through the idea that wicked persons are cunning. A villain who outwits you must be sharp. By the late 14th century (Chaucer, Gower) shrewde begins to mean cunning, artful, and by the 15th-16th centuries the wicked sense recedes while the clever sense advances. Shakespeare still straddles both: a shrewd fellow can be mischievous-sharp, a shrewd blow is severe, a shrewd observer is keen. The semantic bridge was sharp — sharp-tongued, sharp in pain, sharp in mind — one metaphor covering malice, intensity, and intellect.

By the 17th-18th centuries shrewd is fully complimentary in commercial and practical contexts: a shrewd merchant, shrewd investment, shrewd guess. Samuel Johnson defines it as cunning, artful, sly, but already notes sagacious use. Victorian self-help literature completes the rehabilitation: shrewdness is the Protestant virtue of practical wisdom without book-learning.

The adverbial suffix -ly has its own deep history. It comes from Old English -lice, from lic body, form, manner (cognate with like, German -lich), from Proto-Germanic *-lika- body, appearance, from PIE *leik- body, form. Old English snottorlice meant in a wise manner. Middle English reduced -liche to -ly. Added to shrewd probably in the 16th century, shrewdly thus etymologically means in the manner of a shrewd (once wicked, now astute) person.

In Dickens's sentence — the cold spoke out shrewdly in his grating voice — the older intensifying sense lingers: shrewdly here means piercingly, keenly, cuttingly, describing how the cold made itself felt and heard. This preserves Shakespearean shrewd as severe, piercing (a shrewd frost, shrewd pain). Modern readers hear only cleverly, but Dickens activates both keenly perceptible and astutely expressive: the cold speaks as if it were itself a shrewd, biting person.

Phonetics: OE scr- palatalized to ME shr- /ʃr/, vowel from ewe to ewd/eud to modern /uː/ in shrewd /ʃruːd/, adverbial shrewdly /ˈʃruːdli/ with syllabic ly. Spelling fixed shr- after Caxton, -ewd marking the long vowel.

  • Animal to insult: shrew-mouse superstition → human scold/villain.
  • Insult to intellect: wicked → cunning → sagacious via sharpness metaphor.
  • Intensity to manner: shrewd blow/frost (severe) → spoke shrewdly (piercingly, tellingly).

Debate note: the precise link between OE screawa animal and ME shrewe person is traditional but not phonologically airtight; some scholars posit independent pejorative formation. The standard OED account accepts the transfer via superstition about venom. The -ed in shrewed is also ambiguous between past-participle and possessive -ed (wicked = having wick). Neither uncertainty affects the later cunning → astute trajectory, which is richly attested.
