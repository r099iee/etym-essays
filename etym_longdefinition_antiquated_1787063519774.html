An antiquated thing or practice is one that is old-fashioned, out of date, or no longer in step with current standards — something that belongs to a previous era and has largely been superseded. The word carries an implicit comparison between the present and the past: an antiquated item may still exist and even still function, but it is perceived as belonging to an earlier age, a relic of its time rather than a live part of it.

Core sense — old-fashioned / out of date. The primary modern meaning is that something is aged relative to its context and therefore no longer appropriate, efficient, or stylish. Unlike ancient, which merely marks great age, antiquated nearly always implies that the thing has been left behind: superseded by newer forms, methods, or fashions. It is a comparative word — the thing is judged against what now exists.

Polysemy and extended senses:

  • Of things and institutions: antiquated laws, rules, procedures, machinery, and systems — implying they are obsolete in practice though perhaps still nominally in force; the law is still on the books, but no one would write it that way today.
  • Of ideas and beliefs: an antiquated worldview or an antiquated attitude suggests that a person's thinking reflects a bygone era; mildly disapproving, occasionally gently comic.
  • Of people: an antiquated person is presented as stuck in the past — usually condescending but sometimes affectionate (an elderly relative with antiquated manners).
  • Of language: words or styles described as antiquated are felt to be relics of earlier usage, though still recognisable; this shades toward the more technical term archaic.
  • Of technology and infrastructure: a very common modern application — antiquated equipment, antiquated software, antiquated data systems — implies inefficiency and incompatibility with modern standards rather than total uselessness.
  • Of financial and business practices: an antiquated pricing model, antiquated accounting, antiquated inventory methods — procedures outrun by competitors and by data science.

Degree and nuance. Antiquated sits on a spectrum of “old” words. It is milder than obsolete (which implies something has entirely fallen out of use or is no longer supported), but sharper than dated (which merely signals a recognisable past style). An antiquated system may still work; an obsolete one typically cannot. The register is formal-to-neutral and slightly judgmental; used of a person it can be humorous or dismissive depending on tone. It is rarely a compliment.

Historical bridge. The word descends from the Latin verb antiquare, “to make old,” and, in Roman legal language, “to reject (a proposed law) because it is already old.” The Latin participle antiquatus, “made old, grown out of use,” passed into English as the verb antiquate in the 16th century, and the participial adjective antiquated (first attested in English around 1600) fixed the meaning “grown old, no longer current.” The underlying Roman metaphor — something treated as belonging to the past and therefore rejected — survives in the modern sense of being left behind by progress. In the Roman assemblies, to antiquate a law was literally to vote it into the past; a modern reviewer who calls a policy antiquated is doing the same thing in miniature.

Common collocations. an antiquated law / system / notion, antiquated equipment, antiquated ideas, seem almost antiquated, antiquated and inefficient. It frequently appears in journalism, criticism, and historical discussion to criticise lagging institutions.

Related but distinct words. The near-synonyms differ in degree and register: outdated is the neutral default; old-fashioned is broader and can be positive; archaic is technical (chiefly of language); obsolete is stronger; antique (as adjective) values old age positively as an attribute of craftsmanship.
