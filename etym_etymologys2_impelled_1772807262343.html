Derivation chain for IMPELLED (the target root word is bold at the final stage): 1. PIE *pel- "to thrust, strike, drive, set in motion by force" (reconstructed; also the base of Latin pellere and pulsare, and of Greek pállein "to poise, sway, brandish"). 2. Latin pellere "to push, drive, strike, beat, banish, expel" (attested from c. 3rd c. BCE; e.g. Plautus). 3. Latin impellere "to strike against, drive into motion, urge on, force" (in- / im- "into, against, upon" + pellere; attested 1st c. BCE — Cicero, Caesar, Lucretius). 4. Latin impulsus, past participle of impellere "driven, urged, pushed" (also the source of English impulse and impulsive via the nominal stem). 5. English impel "to drive, force or urge forward" (a learned borrowing from Latin impellere, partly via French; first recorded in English c. 1520–1530s, OED earliest citation 1533). 6. English impelled (past tense and past participle) "driven, urged" — formed regularly with the English weak-past suffix -ed, in use from the 16th century. Debated / nuanced stages: none material. The chain from PIE *pel- through Latin is standard. The only detail worth noting is that English impel was a bookish loan rather than an inheritance, so the -ed past is a native English inflection grafted onto a Latin-derived verb, while the noun partner impulse preserves the Latin participle stem (impulsus).
