- c. 1300–1400: Borrowed from Anglo-Norman French (*rebuker*), originally carrying a predominantly physical sense of "beating back," "driving off," or checking an enemy's advance in combat.
- c. 1400–1550: Shifted rapidly from physical repulsion to severe verbal censuring and moral reproof across Middle and Early Modern English.
- c. 1550–1700: Heavily popularized in religious and canonical texts (such as the King James Bible and Tyndale's translations) as an authoritative, divine or patriarchal condemnation of sin, arrogance, and folly.
- c. 1700–1900: Solidified in formal literary, judicial, and social registers as the standard term for dignified, stern admonishment delivered by superiors, judges, or moral guardians.
- c. 1900–Present: Retained as an elevated, formal descriptor in political, legal, journalistic, and literary contexts for official censures, formal reprimands, or sharp disciplinary corrections.
