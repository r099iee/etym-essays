VOLITION — derivation chain - Proto-Indo-European *welh₁- (also written *wel-) "to wish, choose, desire" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE). The root of the will-words of the family: it produced Greek (h)elésthai "to choose, take," Sanskrit vr-/var- "to choose, desire," and the Germanic and Latin will-verbs. - Latin velle "to wish, want, be willing" (irregular verb, attested classical Latin), with the first-person present volō "I wish, I will" — the stem vol- being the source of the entire volition-family. Latin also formed from the same root voluntās "will, wish, inclination" (whence English voluntary, volunteer) and voluptās "pleasure, delight" (whence English voluptuous, voluptuary). - Medieval Latin vōlitio, vōlitiōnem "the act of willing" (Scholastic coinage, formed in the medieval schools — 12th–13th c. — on the stem vol- of volō plus the abstract noun suffix -itio; NOT a classical Latin word. The Scholastics coined it to name the executive act of the will in the psychology of the schools). - French volition "the act of willing" (loan from Medieval Latin, attested in French learned usage from the 16th–17th c.). - English volition "the act or power of willing; the exercise of the will" (borrowed from the French/Latin form in the mid-17th century; attested from the 1650s in English philosophical writing) → Modern English VOLITION. Notes and nuances: - The word is a medieval-philosophical coinage, not a classical inheritance: the only thing genuinely ancient is the PIE root and its Latin verb velle; the abstract noun volitio was created in the medieval schools, and everything about the word's register (formal, bookish, philosophical) reflects that birth. - Cognate branches of the same PIE root are NOT ancestors of volition: English will (Old English willan, from Proto-Germanic *weljaną), Greek helesthai, and Sanskrit vr-/var- are sisters of the Latin line, all descending independently from *welh₁-. - No stage is invented: velle and volō are attested classical forms, volitio is attested in medieval Scholastic Latin, volition is attested in French and then English, and the English date of the 1650s is documented. - The suffix -itio (as in volitio, petitio) is the Latin abstract-noun suffix; it is not to be confused with the modern English noun suffix -ion added directly to volition, which simply continues the Latin form.
