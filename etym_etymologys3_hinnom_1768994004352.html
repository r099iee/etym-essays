The word Hinnom is a map of terror: the biography of a real valley that became the name of hell. It begins in biblical Hebrew as a pure toponym — Gēʾ-Hinnōm, "the Valley of Hinnom," a ravine lying south and southwest of Jerusalem where the Hinnom family or clan held land. The personal name Hinnōm is of obscure, possibly Canaanite, origin, and its meaning is lost; but the fuller formula gēʾ ben-Hinnōm, "the valley of the son of Hinnom," marks it as inherited property — an ordinary, even pleasant, entry in the land registry of ancient Israel (Joshua 15:8; 18:16; Nehemiah 11:30). Nothing in the name itself foretold damnation.
  • From land registry to cult-site: the ruin of the name begins with its use as a cult-site. Within the valley stood the Tophet, the fire-pit where, in the days of Ahaz and Manasseh (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6; 2 Chronicles 28:3; 33:6), children were passed through the fire to the god Molech. The Hebrew Tōfet is itself obscure — possibly connected to drums (the toph) beaten to drown the cries of the victims — but whatever the word's origin, the practice turned a boundary valley into a place of horror, and the name of the valley began to carry the horror with it.
  • Jeremiah's curse: the prophet Jeremiah denounces the place with unmatched ferocity (Jeremiah 7:31–32; 19:2–6). Because of its abominations, he declares, the valley shall no longer be called Tophet but the Valley of Slaughter; the days are coming when there will be no room left to bury the dead there. The valley becomes in prophecy the image of divine judgment: a place filled with corpses and fire, the debris of a nation's sins.
  • The Josianic defilement: under King Josiah (2 Kings 23:10) the site was defiled to end the cult — the king polluted the Tophet so that no one could pass a child through the fire again. The cult was suppressed, but the infection of the word had begun: a place once used for burning children could not return to being an ordinary ravine.
  • From place to figure (Second Temple period): by the Second Temple period, apocalyptic Judaism had transformed the polluted valley — a place of slaughtered children, corpses, and fire — into a figure of the final judgment, the place where the wicked are consumed in the fire of the world to come. The geography of Jerusalem became the geography of the end of the world: the Valley of Hinnom, which already smelled of burning, was the natural image for the fire of God.
  • The name contracts (Aramaic): the Aramaic speech of the post-exilic age contracted the name to Gehannā (גהנא), a single word out of the two-word place-name. This contraction was the decisive phonetic event: "valley of Hinnom" became simply "Gehanna," one word, no longer a place with a family's name but a proper name of the place of punishment.
  • Gehenna and the New Testament: the Aramaic form crossed into Greek as Géenna (Γέεννα), where the New Testament fixes its meaning forever. Jesus uses Gehenna again and again (Matthew 5:22, 29–30; 18:9; Mark 9:43–48; Luke 12:5) for the eschatological fire of punishment — the "fire that never shall be quenched" — and Gehenna, translated "hell" in English Bibles, enters Latin and all the languages of Christendom as the very name of damnation. Syriac carried the same form into Arabic as Jahannam, the Qur'anic name of hell: the valley of Hinnom thus became hell not only for Jews and Christians but for Muslims too.
  • The English toponym (1611): English kept the two-word name as well. The King James Version (1611) renders Gēʾ-Hinnōm as "the valley of Hinnom" in Joshua 15:8, 2 Kings 23:10, Nehemiah 11:30, and Jeremiah, fixing Hinnom as the English biblical toponym — the historical valley — alongside Gehenna, the theological place. The two names of one place went separate ways: Gehenna became doctrine, Hinnom remained geography, but each kept the other's shadow.
  • Modern byword: in modern usage, "to go down through Hinnom" is to brave hell's own geography — fire, destruction, catastrophe, the place of no return. The word has completed a journey from a family's ravine to the seat of eternal fire, and it is exactly that journey the passage invokes when it says the earthmen, for the chance of unlimited gold, would have gone down through Hinnom: no horror, not even the fires of the accursed valley, would turn them back.
The whole history — land registry, cult-site, prophet's curse, apocalyptic figure, Aramaic contraction, New Testament fire, Arabic hell, English toponym — is compressed into a single name. Hinnom is a real valley that outgrew its geography: the ravine outside Jerusalem whose name became, in almost every language of the Mediterranean and beyond, the word for the place of no return.
