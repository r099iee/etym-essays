- c. 8th c.–13th c.: Old English tēman / tīeman (from tēam "brood, family, draft animals") meant to bring forth offspring, propagate, bear young, or be pregnant with a child.
- 1300s–1550s: Middle English temen expanded from animal reproduction to describe the fertile earth bearing crops and women bearing large families of children.
- 1580s–1700s: The participial adjective teeming flourished in Elizabethan and Jacobean literature (Shakespeare's "teeming earth", Milton's "teeming womb"), evolving from literal childbearing to metaphorical descriptions of nature and imagination.
- 1750s–1900s: Broadened in British English to denote torrential rainfall ("teeming rain"), while Victorian social writers applied it to describe rapidly growing industrial cities and crowded urban populations.
- 1950s–Present: Widely established across ecology (teeming biodiversity), cognitive science (teeming intellect), and cultural theory (Barthes's teeming textual flux).
