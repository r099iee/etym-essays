Procure is a formal transitive verb whose core modern sense is “to obtain or get possession of something, especially by effort, care or arrangement”. In the source, “his pathetic efforts to procure for himself a triumph” means Cicero’s manoeuvres to secure the honour of a triumph — the ceremonial procession through Rome voted by the Senate to a victorious commander. The verb is chosen for its note of contrivance: procurement is not mere receipt but a campaign of seeking, and “for himself” sharpens the self-interest.

The main senses

  • Obtain by effort. To procure funding, evidence, a visa, supplies, a copy, a passage. The word is formal and implies deliberate action, often through procedure or intermediaries; get is the neutral alternative and obtain the neutral formal one.
  • Business and government. Procurement is the process of sourcing and purchasing goods and services: public procurement, procurement officer, procurement contract, defence procurement. This is now the word’s largest domain, and here it is institutional and morally neutral.
  • Bring about, cause. An older and more literary sense, still alive in legal and formal phrases: to procure someone’s death, to procure a miscarriage, to procure an agreement or a settlement. The causative meaning is historically prior to the “obtain” meaning.
  • Obtain a person for sexual purposes. To procure a woman or girl for a man; “procuring” is the offence of recruiting or soliciting a person into prostitution, and a procurer or procuress is a pander. The construction treats a person as an object to be obtained, which is why the euphemism is also a moral indictment.
  • Archaic management. The Latin sense “to take care of, attend to, provide for” survives in the archaic “procure for” and in the legal office of procurator; it is the source of the whole family.

How the senses connect

Latin prōcūrāre is prō- “for, on behalf of” plus cūrāre “to take care of”, from cūra “care”. The original sense is therefore “to take care of something on someone’s behalf, to manage or administer”. From managing comes seeing to it that something happens — bringing it about — and from bringing it about comes obtaining it. The element of care explains the modern tone: procurement is careful, deliberate acquisition, not casual getting. It also explains the family of words: a procurator is one who manages another’s affairs, a proxy acts through procuration, and cure, curator, accurate, secure and sinecure all descend from the same Latin noun for care.

Register and connotation

  • Formal, slightly bureaucratic, and uncommon in casual speech; it belongs to written English, administration and law.
  • It implies effort, method and sometimes indirect means, so it can carry a faint suspicion of contrivance or string-pulling — exactly the colour it has in the source.
  • The sexual sense is euphemistic in form and condemnatory in effect; in general prose it is either avoided or made explicit, since “procure a woman” is archaic and potentially offensive.
  • Typical collocations: procure supplies, equipment, evidence, funding, a licence, a passage; procure a triumph (historical); procure the death of someone; procure a miscarriage; procure a woman for prostitution.

In this passage

Cicero’s “pathetic efforts to procure for himself a triumph” are doubly characterized: the verb suggests calculation and indirect means, and the reflexive pronoun suggests vanity. A triumph was not simply awarded for victory; it required senatorial approval and the commander’s presence with imperium inside the city, and Cicero’s hopes after his governorship of Cilicia were widely thought unrealistic. Hence “pathetic” — the efforts were both elaborate and doomed. The word also completes the triad of the sentence: kindness, inordinate love and political self-regard are offered as the three keys to Cicero’s character.
