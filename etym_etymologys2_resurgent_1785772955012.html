Proto-Indo-European *h₃reǵ- "to stretch out, make straight, direct, rule" (root of Latin regere "to rule" and Greek orégō "I stretch out") (c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Latin regere "to guide, direct, rule, keep straight" (attested from the 3rd century BCE) → Latin surgere "to rise, spring up, get up" (formed from *sub-regere, sub- "from below" + regere, with assimilation of the cluster; some authorities prefer an analysis through subrigere — see note) (attested in Republican Latin) → Latin resurgere "to rise again, spring up anew" (re- "again" + surgere) (attested from classical Latin) → Late Latin present participle resurgēns, gen. resurgentis "rising again" (participial/adjectival stem; also the source of resurrectiō "resurrection") → English resurgent "rising again, reviving after decline" (learned borrowing of the Latin participle, chiefly late 18th century onward) → RESURGENT Note on nuance: the internal morphology of surgere has long been debated in Latin historical grammar — the standard analysis is sub- + regere ("to rise from below") with assimilation of the cluster, but a derivation through subrigere has also been argued. The outcome, a verb of physical rising, is undisputed; the precise internal steps are the debated part.
