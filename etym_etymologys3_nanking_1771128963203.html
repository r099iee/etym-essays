Evolution of 南 (nán), "south" — the first root of Nanking.

The morpheme 南 (nán, "south") is one of the oldest continuously attested words of the Chinese language. Its earliest documentation belongs to the Shang dynasty (c. 1200–1046 BCE), when diviners inscribed the four cardinal directions on ox scapulae and turtle shells; in those oracle-bone texts 南 already functions as a fixed cardinal point, appearing alongside 東 (east), 西 (west), and 北 (north). The Old Chinese reconstruction *nˤəm (Baxter–Sagart) or *nəm (Schuessler) is itself a scholarly model built from later rhyme evidence, and the uncertainty of reconstruction is a standing caveat: no system of writing in the Shang period recorded pronunciation, so all Old Chinese forms are inferences from Middle Chinese and from rhyming patterns in the earliest poetry.

Phonologically the syllable has remained remarkably stable over three millennia: a nasal-initial (n-), a low vowel, and a final nasal (-m in Old/Middle Chinese, shifting to -n in Modern Mandarin). The Middle Chinese reading *nom recorded in the Qieyun (601 CE) is nearly identical to the modern nán; the main change is the transition of the final -m to -n, a merger that also affected the syllable in other morphemes. This stability testifies to the centrality of cardinal directions in Chinese cosmology — they are vocabulary of the oldest stratum, resistant to replacement because they were bound up with ritual orientation, the calendar, and astronomy.

Semantically, "south" carried heavy cultural value. In Chinese cosmology the south is the direction of warmth, growth, the sun at its height, and the element of fire; the emperor sat facing south ("south-facing" rule, 南面), so that the character 南 entered political metaphor as the orientation of legitimate sovereign power. This is why the "Southern Capital" is not a mere geographical descriptor but a claim to civilizational legitimacy. The same character radiates through modern vocabulary: 南 is found in 南方 (nánfāng, "the south/southern region"), in the surname 南, in Sino-Japanese 南 (nan) and Sino-Vietnamese 南 (nam) readings, and in the country name 越南 (Yuènán, "Vietnam", literally "Viet of the south"). Cognate syllables appear across the Sinitic family — Cantonese naam4, Hokkien lâm — demonstrating the morpheme's unity across dialects.

Culturally, the south was the region of rice, silk, canals, and the great riverine cities; poetry from the Shijing (Book of Odes, c. 1000–600 BCE) already uses 南 for southern lands and winds, and later "the south" becomes a literary byword for lush, romantic, prosperous territory. The morpheme's journey from oracle-bone cardinal point to the first syllable of a world-famous capital name is thus a history of how a direction became a place, and a place became a symbol of legitimacy, wealth, and memory.

Evolution of 京 (jīng), "capital" — the second root of Nanking.

The morpheme 京 (jīng, "capital") tells the story of how a concrete, physical word — a high place, an artificial mound — rose to the abstract political height of "capital city". Its earliest attested form is Old Chinese *kraŋ (Baxter–Sagart), appearing in Western Zhou bronze inscriptions (c. 1046–771 BCE). The character's pictographic origin is debated but most scholars read it as a picture of a raised platform or mound with a structure upon it — a watchtower, granary, or ancestral hall on a height. In the earliest texts 京 designates precisely such a high, fortified place: the residence of a lord raised above the surrounding plain, both for defence and for display. The English word "capital" has a parallel history, from Latin caput "head", and the Chinese case is the same semantic anatomy: the seat of rule is the "head" or "high point" of the realm.

The decisive semantic shift — from "mound/high place" to "capital city" — was complete by the time of the Western Zhou, when the royal seat was called 京. The phonetic path is equally clear: Middle Chinese *kjæŋ (Qieyun, 601 CE) evolves to Modern Mandarin jīng, with the expected change of the palatalized velar initial and the final -ŋ retained (the -k that appears in the English spelling "Nanking" reflects older dialects and early European romanizations, which preserved a final stop in the syllable's historical variety). In the compound 北京 (Běijīng) and 南京 (Nánjīng) the morpheme functions as a bound form meaning "capital", and the pair of capitals deliberately mirrors each other: when the Ming moved the seat north in 1421, the southern city kept its title as 南京, "Southern Capital", a name that then outlived its own status, preserved as a toponym for the city ever since.

The cultural charge of 京 is immense. It forms the Japanese kyō in Kyōto ("capital city") and Tōkyō (Tokyo, "Eastern Capital"), showing how the Chinese morpheme travelled through the Sinosphere as the very word for a seat of empire. In Chinese historiography the presence of 京 in a city's name is a fossilized claim to have once been the centre of the world. That claim — layered into Nanking from the Eastern Wu through the Ming and the Republic — is exactly what the card's context sentence exploits: the great and ancient mansions of the family stand in a place whose very name means "Capital". The evolution of 京, from earthen mound to imperial metropolis, is a compressed biography of Chinese political geography itself.

Compound and transmission to English.

Combining the two roots yields 南京 Nánjīng "Southern Capital", a toponym whose modern Chinese reading (Nánjīng) coexists with the older English form "Nanking". The English spelling descends from early modern European missionary and merchant transcription of the syllable 京, which preserved a final -k in the regional pronunciations they recorded; this was fixed in diplomatic history by the Treaty of Nanking (1842). The word thus carries, in a single spelling, both the ancient Chinese history of the two morphemes and the modern history of China's encounter with the West.
