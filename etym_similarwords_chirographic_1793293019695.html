• Handwritten: Similar because both mean produced by the hand with pen; differs because handwritten is the plain everyday word, while chirographic is the learned Greek compound used in scholarship.
  • Manuscript: Similar because both belong to the hand-copied book world; differs because manuscript names the object itself, while chirographic names the quality or culture of handwriting.
  • Holographic: Similar because a holographic will is entirely hand-written; differs because holographic is a legal term for wholly hand-authored documents, while chirographic covers all handwriting and its cultural order.
  • Calligraphic: Similar because both concern formed letters by hand; differs because calligraphic stresses beautiful deliberate penmanship, while chirographic is neutral about beauty and stresses the medium itself.
  • Inscriptional: Similar because both concern writing fixed on a surface; differs because inscriptional suggests carving in stone or metal for permanence, while chirographic suggests ink on parchment or paper by a moving hand.
  • Scribal: Similar because both evoke the copyist at the desk; differs because scribal centres the agent and his errors or habits, while chirographic centres the handwritten character of the product and culture.
  • Typographic: Similar because it is the great paired opposite in media theory, print against hand; differs because typographic names the press era that displaced handwriting, while chirographic names the manuscript order it displaced.
  • Literate: Similar because chirographic cultures are literate ones; differs because literate is general and person-centred, while chirographic specifies writing by hand as distinct from print or pixels.
