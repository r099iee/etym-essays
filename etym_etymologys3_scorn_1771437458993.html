The biography of scorn is a biography of a wound translated into words. It begins, on the standard reconstruction, with an Indo-European root of physical action — *sker-, "to cut, to shear, to clip" — and it ends in the drawing-rooms of a novel where a gesture of public laughter can destroy a reputation. The path from blade to sneer passes through Germanic, through French, through medieval England, and finally into the modern moral and emotional vocabulary, and at every stage the word kept a trace of its violent origin: to scorn a person is, etymologically, to cut at them.

1. The Indo-European cutting root. The PIE root *sker- is one of the most productive roots in the family, and its descendants map the whole idea of cutting: Greek κείρω (keírō) "to shear, to cut," Latin curtus "cut short," English shear, shard, sharp, score (a notch cut to tally), share (a cutting, a division), short. The root's core image is the blade or the cut, and its secondary image is separation: whatever is cut off, divided, or reduced.

2. Germanic: from cutting to mockery. In the Germanic branch the root produced a verb whose exact form is reconstructed as *skarnjan, "to mock, to deride," and the surviving witnesses make the meaning concrete. Old High German scern means "mockery, jest, derision" (9th century); Middle Dutch schernen means "to mock." The semantic bridge is transparent and, for Indo-Europeanists, entirely familiar: mockery is imagined as a cutting or shearing of the victim's dignity. Just as English still says "a cutting remark" or "a biting joke," the Germanic ancestors of the English word imagined derision as an act of cutting at a person. This is the moment the word is born as a word of contempt rather than of shears.

3. The French corridor and the Norman conquest of vocabulary. The word crossed into Romance through the contact zone of the Franks. Old French took up the Germanic stem as escarn "mockery, derision" and escharnir "to mock, to deride, to insult" (11th century). When the Norman conquerors of 1066 brought their French vocabulary into England, this family of mockery-words came with them, and by c. 1200 Middle English had scornen — first as a verb, "to treat with contempt, to mock," and shortly after as a noun. The borrowing is instructive about register: the word entered English as a Romance import in the same wave as derision, contempt, and mockery themselves, enriching the native Germanic vocabulary with a sharper, more public word for laughing at people.

4. Middle English and the idiom of ritual humiliation. In its first two centuries in English, scorn was overwhelmingly a word of public action. The formula to laugh to scorn — to make a person the object of jeering laughter — is attested from the 13th century and was so fixed that it survived into the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611), where it renders the Hebrew and Greek terms for mockery in passages of prophetic and apocalyptic derision. The idiom's grammar (laugh + to + scorn) is the fossil of an older construction meaning "laugh at/into derision," and its survival in literary English through the 19th and 20th centuries — including the sentence of this card — is a remarkable piece of linguistic archaeology: a medieval formulaic expression doing duty in a modern novel of social anxiety.

5. The Renaissance: internalisation and moral weight. By the 16th century the word had acquired its full modern range. Alongside the loud, public sense came the inward sense: scorn as the feeling of contempt itself, even when silent. Shakespeare uses the word with extraordinary range — as a verb of refusal ("scorned the offer"), as a noun of high disdain, and in the great moral contrast between the scorner and the honest man. The biblical scorner ("Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly... nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful") made the scorner a fixed character type of moral theology: the proud mocker of wisdom and virtue. From this point the word carries not merely emotion but moral architecture: the scorner is someone whose contempt marks a failure of humility.

6. The eighteenth century: refinement and the contempt/scorn distinction. The lexicographers and moralists of the 18th century began to carve out careful distinctions that survive in the modern dictionaries. Contempt, they said, is cold and considered — the intellectual judgment of worthlessness; scorn is contempt heated into mockery and derision, publicly performed. This division is exactly the one drawn in the synonym-field of the original card: the literary-critical tradition has kept scorn for the expressive, theatrical, emotionally charged form of contempt, and contempt for the sober verdict. The distinction is partly a product of the period's love of taxonomy, but it is also a genuine record of how the word was felt: scorn never lost the heat of its original "cutting" mockery.

7. The modern word. In contemporary English scorn remains vigorous in formal and literary prose. "To pour scorn on" an idea is the stock journalistic idiom for dismissing it with contempt; "scornful" and "scornfully" are workhorse words of narration for the curled lip and the cold stare. The word has also entered the language of social and political analysis — "class scorn," "intellectual scorn" — for the contempt that groups with claims to superiority direct at those they judge beneath them. And the oldest formula of all, to laugh to scorn, survives as a literary idiom whose very archaism is part of its power: it sounds like what it describes, a ceremony of humiliation fixed in the language for nearly a millennium. From a Bronze-Age blade to the drawing-rooms of the high society ladies of this card's sentence, the word has never quite stopped cutting.
