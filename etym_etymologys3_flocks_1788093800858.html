The word flock is a small triumph of Germanic philology precisely because its beginnings are obscure: every secure fact about it is attested, and the deeper past is honestly admitted to be beyond recovery. It unites a compact set of cognates — Old Norse flokkr, Middle Dutch vlocke, Middle Low German vlocke — pointing to a Proto-Germanic form *flukka- meaning “crowd, troop, band”. What immediately strikes the reader of the old records is that the oldest Germanic senses refer to people.

1. The Germanic record. An Old Norse flokkr is, first, a band of men — warriors, followers, a retinue — and, with a beautiful secondary application, a “batch” of verses: in skaldic poetry a flokkr is a sequence of stanzas without the refrain that marks the more elaborate drápa. The word thus moved in Old Norse between the muster of men and the muster of verses: a grouping, in both cases, of like items in a row. The single surviving Old English attestation of flocc likewise means “a company, band of men”, not a herd. After the Norman Conquest the word re-emerges in Middle English as flok (from the thirteenth century), and the standard historical explanation — given the near-absence of the word in Old English — is that the Middle English form was reinforced, perhaps wholly re-borrowed, from Old Norse flokkr in the Danelaw, where Scandinavian settlement kept the old Germanic word alive in speech while the written record of it faded.

2. The origin problem. Beyond Germanic, the etymology is unknown. The OED classes the word as of obscure origin. The status quo ante is often enlivened by comparison with Latin floccus “tuft, lock of wool”, and, deeper, with the PIE root *plek- “to plait, braid” (whence Greek plokos “lock, curl” and Latin plectere “to braid”), which would yield the attractive semantic bridge “a tuft or lock” → “a cluster” → “a gathering”. This remains speculation: the phonology is awkward, the semantic leap is wide, and English itself contains the two homonyms (the animal-collective flock and the wool-tuft flock from Latin floccus, borrowed in the thirteenth century) which may simply have converged by chance. The honest summary is: Germanic stock, opaque before that, possibly — but only possibly — connected with the wool-tuft word.

3. Semantic expansion in Middle and Early Modern English. The Middle English flok quickly develops the senses that define modern use. First, the agrarian sense: flocks of sheep and goats are the working units of the medieval wool economy — the “golden fleece” of England — and every manor record, shepherd's account, and rent roll uses the word. Second, from the fourteenth century, the bird sense: “flocks of fowls”, of geese and starlings and finches, enters natural description and, later, hawking manuals. Third, the religious sense: the psalmist's “the Lord is my shepherd” and the Gospels' “Fear not, little flock” (Luke 12:32, in the Authorized Version) fix “the flock” as the English word for the church congregation, with Christ or his ministers as shepherd — a metaphor that has powered pastoral rhetoric from Wyclif's sermons to the present day, and whose plural, flocks, extends it to many congregations. The verb to flock (“gather in a crowd”) follows in the sixteenth century, and the proverb “birds of a feather flock together” is recorded in English from the sixteenth century, securing the word in everyday idiom.

4. The modern career. From the eighteenth century onward, natural history and travel writing make flocks the plain, standard collective of bird life: every voyage narrative and every field observer counts flocks, and ornithology formalises the study of flocking behaviour — the coordination of thousands of starlings in a murmuration, the V-formation of migrating geese — as a branch of animal behaviour science. In the twentieth century the word jumps registers twice: the church keeps “the faithful flock”; meanwhile physics and computing adopt “flocking” as the technical term for decentralised swarming (the 1986 “boids” algorithm, in which simulated birds follow only three steering rules, is the canonical model), and marketing speaks of people flocking to products and places. The old collision with the wool-tuft homonym survives in “flocking” (applying fibre to fabric), a case of two etymologically distinct words sharing one spelling for seven hundred years.

Summary of the drift

  • Proto-Germanic *flukka- “crowd” → Old Norse flokkr (band of men; skaldic batch of verses) and Old English flocc (company of men).
  • Middle English flok: sheep and goats in the wool economy; birds from the 14th c.; congregations from the Gospels' shepherd.
  • Verb “to flock” and the proverb “birds of a feather flock together” (16th c.).
  • Plural flocks standard in farming, birdwatching, and travel narrative (18th–19th c.).
  • Modern: religious warmth retained; “flocking” now also a term of swarm science and computing; the wool-tuft homonym (Latin floccus) remains a separate word.
