Sleet is precipitation falling as a mixture of rain and snow, or as small pellets of ice; cold, wet, half-frozen fall that stings the face and rattles rather than drifting softly. In British use it most often means rain mixed with partially melted snow, falling as cold slushy drops. In American meteorological use it more narrowly means ice pellets — clear grains of ice that bounce when they hit — distinct from snow, hail, and freezing rain. In all varieties it signals raw, unpleasant winter weather, neither the clean beauty of snow nor the straightforward wetness of rain.

The defining qualities are mixture and chill. Snow is frozen crystals that float; rain is liquid that splashes; hail is hard layered ice that pounds in summer thunderstorms; sleet is the in-between, half-melted, half-frozen, driven sideways by wind. It wets like rain and chills like snow at once, soaking through clothing and numbing skin. That is why Dickens lists it last after rain, snow, and hail: sleet is the most miserable, most penetrating member of the winter quartet, yet even it can boast over Scrooge only that it comes down and yields to gravity and season, while he will not yield to humanity.

  • British sleet (rain-snow mix): the everyday UK meaning. Example: sleet was falling on the high street. Appears as cold rain with white flecks, melting on contact, making pavements treacherous. Forecast phrase: wintry showers, sleet and snow over hills.
  • American sleet (ice pellets): US National Weather Service definition. Example: sleet pinged off the windshield. Small translucent ice grains formed when snowflakes melt and refreeze falling through layered air. Bounces, accumulates like coarse salt, distinct from hail which is larger and convective.
  • Verb: to sleet / it is sleeting: impersonal weather verb. Example: it sleeted all afternoon. Means sleet is falling. No personal subject; parallel to it is raining, snowing. Participial sleeting rain, sleeting wind common in narrative.
  • How sleet forms (science): snow falling through a warm layer melts partially then passes through a sub-freezing surface layer and refreezes into pellets or slush. Requires a specific temperature sandwich. Hence sleet often precedes or follows snow in a warming or cooling event, a transition marker.
  • Hazards and practical sense: reduces visibility, stings eyes, coats roads in slush that later freezes to black ice. Pedestrians get soaked and chilled; drivers lose traction; flights de-ice. To say sleet rather than snow warns of wet cold rather than picturesque accumulation.
  • Figurative and literary use: sleet of bullets, sleet of abuse is possible but rarer than hail of or shower of; when used it borrows the sting and slant. More often sleet colours mood: sleety eyes, sleety light for grey-blue coldness. Dickensian overtone is moral: sleet is honest misery, openly unpleasant, unlike human coldness that pretends respectability.

Register is neutral and standard; children learn sleet with rain, snow, hail as basic weather words. It is not slang, not technical alone, but belongs to forecasts, novels, and conversation alike. To use it precisely, specify region if needed: in London sleet means wet snow-rain, in Chicago sleet means ice pellets. In both, keep the sting: if it does not chill and prick, it is not sleet.
