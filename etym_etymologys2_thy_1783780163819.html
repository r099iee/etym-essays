Chain for thy: 1. PIE *túh₂ ("you", second-person singular pronoun; reconstructed for c. 4500–3500 BCE) → 2. PIE possessive stem *tew- / *tey- ("of thee", the genitive/possessive base built on the *tu- pronoun; the source of Latin tuus and Greek sós in their respective branches) → 3. Proto-Germanic *þīnaz ("thine, thy", possessive adjective formed by adding the adjectival suffix *-no- to the possessive stem; attested by its descendants Gothic þeins, Old Norse þinn, Old Saxon thīn, Old High German dīn; reconstructed for c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → 4. Old English þīn ("thy/thine", serving both as possessive adjective and as possessive pronoun; attested from c. 700 CE) → 5. Middle English þīn / thin ("thy", the final -n being dropped before a following consonant in the weak/stressed-adjacent position, exactly as mīn gave my; the pre-vowel and absolute forms keep -n as thine; c. 1100–1500) → 6. Modern English thy (archaic, poetic, and liturgical possessive adjective; from c. 1500 onward). Debated / nuanced stages: (a) the PIE possessive stem is sometimes reconstructed *téynos rather than *tew-/*-tey- + *-no-; the two notations describe the same attested outcome — Proto-Germanic *þīnaz and Latin tuus — and the precise ablaut grade is a matter of reconstruction, not direct record; (b) the pre-consonantal loss of -n (thin → thy) is a well-attested phonetic rule of Middle English, but its exact dating across dialects is variable, with northern texts keeping -n longer; (c) the alternation thy/thine exactly parallels my/mine, a pattern long recognized in handbooks, and neither form ever changed its meaning — the split is purely phonological and positional.
