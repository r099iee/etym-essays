Linear derivation chain for INCRASSATE (a Latin loan into English): • Latin crassus — 'thick, dense, solid, fat' — the Classical Latin adjective. (Attested from the 2nd century BCE, e.g. in Plautus.) Origin uncertain: no generally accepted PIE etymology has been demonstrated (see note below). • Latin incrassāre — 'to make thick, to thicken, to fatten' — formed with the intensive prefix in- + crassus, 'to render thick.' (Attested in technical Latin, notably in Pliny the Elder, Natural History, 1st century CE, and in medical and agricultural writers.) • Latin incrassātus — past participle of incrassāre — 'made thick, thickened.' • English INCRASSATE — borrowed as both verb and adjective from Latin in the 17th century; first recorded in technical English dictionaries and medical writing, and still current as an adjective in botany. (17th century to present.) Note on the debate: the origin of Latin crassus is genuinely unknown. Older handbooks proposed a link to a PIE root *ker- meaning 'to grow' (a root that also underlies Latin creare 'to create' and crescere 'to grow'), on the idea that 'thick' is the result of growing or swelling. This connection is now regarded as unproven by historical linguists (it is marked as uncertain in de Vaan's etymological dictionary of Latin), and the safest statement is that crassus has no secure PIE ancestry. The English side of the chain, however — incrassare → incrassātus → incrassate — is fully attested and not in doubt. The related English adjective crass ('thick, coarse, lacking refinement') descends from the same Latin crassus by a different route.
