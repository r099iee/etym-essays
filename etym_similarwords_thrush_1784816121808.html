• Blackbird: Similar because it is the thrush's close relative (Turdus merula) and a famous songbird; differs because the male blackbird is wholly black with an orange bill and its mellow, fluted song differs in character from the song thrush's loud, repetitive phrases.
  • Mistle thrush: Similar because it is a thrush of the same family (Turdus viscivorus); differs because it is larger, paler, and wilder, sings even in stormy weather (the old name stormcock), and lacks the song thrush's neat, methodical snail-anvil habit.
  • Throstle: Similar because it is literally the same bird; differs only in being the native English doublet of thrush, now poetic and archaic.
  • Mavis: Similar because it is also the same bird (the song thrush); differs in being borrowed from French mauvis and used chiefly in poetic or dialectal contexts.
  • Nightingale: Similar because it is a famous songbird of the same family, long grouped with the thrushes in English lore; differs because it is a skulking, plain brown bird famed for song alone and neither knocks nor ventures so boldly into view.
  • Redwing: Similar because it is a true thrush (Turdus iliacus) of the same family; differs because it is smaller, streaked with red under the wing, and in Britain chiefly a winter visitor, without the song thrush's garden prominence.
  • Fieldfare: Similar because it is a thrush of the same family (Turdus pilaris); differs because it is a gregarious, largely northern and wintering species with a grey head and chestnut back, less a bird of the garden than of open country.
  • Robin: Similar because it is a small, familiar songbird of gardens and hedgerows; differs because it belongs to a different family (Muscicapidae / chat), has an orange breast, and is not a thrush at all.
  • Skylark: Similar because it is a celebrated songbird of the open country; differs because it sings in hovering flight high above the fields and belongs to a different family (Alaudidae), not the Turdidae.
  • Starling: Similar because it is a common, vocal garden bird; differs because it belongs to the Sturnidae and lacks the thrush's spotted breast and deliberate, melodious song.
