CHAIN 1: FROM PIE TO PINE (6 stages — derivation of the first element)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *peiH- "to be fat, swell" (traditional reconstruction, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE). The pine is conceived as the resinous, sap-swollen tree. DEBATED: de Vaan records no clear etymology and allows a pre-Indo-European Mediterranean substrate source; the *peiH- link is the traditional but not the certain one.
  2. Italic *pīnos "pine, fir" (reconstructed; *peiH-n-os "the swelling, resinous tree").
  3. Latin pīnus (feminine) "pine-tree" (attested from the 1st century BCE; Pliny uses pinus of the stone pine and similar conifers).
  4. Old English pīn "pine" (recorded in Old English, c. 8th–11th centuries, an early continental loan from Latin).
  5. Middle English pine "pine" (c. 13th–14th centuries, reinforced by Old French pin).
  6. English pine (form unchanged since Middle English) → PINE- (first element of this card).

CHAIN 2: FROM PIE TO ROOTS (5 stages — derivation of the second element)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *wréh₂d- / *wrād- "root, branch, twig" (reconstructed; Pokorny sets *u̯rād-, some set *u̯rōd-).
  2. Proto-Germanic *wrōt- "root" (reconstructed; initial *w- retained in the oldest Germanic records).
  3. Old Norse rót "root" (Viking Age, attested; in North Germanic the initial *w- was lost before *r, giving rót).
  4. Middle English rote "root" (12th-century Scandinavian loan, recorded from c. 1150–1200, gradually displacing the native Old English wyrt).
  5. English root (noun), plural roots (regular -s) → ROOTS (second element of this card).

COMPOUND: PINE + ROOTS → PINE-ROOTS (transparent endocentric compound; PINE specifies the species, ROOTS the organ; both elements retain their full stems and only the head inflects for number). The hyphen binds two separately inherited words into one image of the mountainside's exposed root-mat.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The PIE etymology of Latin pīnus is genuinely doubtful; de Vaan treats a pre-Indo-European Mediterranean substrate origin as quite possible, so Chain 1's first stage is traditional rather than assured.
  • For ROOTS, Latin rādīx (whence English radish, radical, eradicate) and Greek rhíza (whence rhizome) are cognates of *wréh₂d-; Old English wyrt (whence -wort) and Old High German wurz (German Wurzel) preserve the w- that Norse lost.
