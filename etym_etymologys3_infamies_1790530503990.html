1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Proto-Italic Foundations

The morphological ancestry of infamies originates in the primary Proto-Indo-European verbal root *bʰeh₂- (also reconstructed as *bhā-), signifying "to speak," "to utter," "to declare," or "to say." This root is one of the most prolific in Indo-European vocabulary, giving rise to Greek φωνή (phōnē, "voice/sound"), φημί (phēmi, "I say"), and Latin fārī ("to speak").

In Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE), this verbal base formed the noun *fāmā ("that which is spoken of," "talk," "rumor," "public report"), representing the collective chatter, news, and societal reputation circulating within a human community.

2. Classical Latin Morphology and Roman Jurisprudence

In Classical Latin, fāma embodied both neutral public report and positive renown. To construct its antithesis, Latin paired the negative prefix in- ("not," "un-") with the adjectival stem to form infāmis ("ill-spoken of," "notorious," "of bad repute").

From this adjective, Latin derived the abstract feminine noun infāmia ("ill fame," "disgrace," "ignominy"). In the Roman Republic and Empire, infamia became a rigorously codified legal instrument:

  • Infamia Censoria: A formal moral censure entered into the civic register by the Roman Censor (nota censoria), stripping a citizen of their tribe, voting rank, and equestrian or senatorial status.
  • Infamia Praetoria: A legal disability imposed by the Praetor’s edict, preventing convicts of theft, fraud, violence, or slander from appearing in court as advocates or representatives.

3. Anglo-Norman Absorption and Middle English Integration

Following the Christianization of the Roman legal framework, infamia entered Ecclesiastical Latin and subsequent medieval canon law. In the twelfth century, Old French adopted the term as infamie ("dishonor," "evil reputation," "outrageous deed").

The word crossed into Middle English during the late fourteenth century (c. 1380 CE), documented in the works of Geoffrey Chaucer and John Wycliffe as infamie or infamye. Early occurrences strictly maintained the singular abstract meaning of public disgrace, enduring shame, or scandalous reputation before God and the realm.

4. Pluralization, Reification, and Modern Political Rhetoric

  • 16th–17th Century (Early Modern Transition): During the English Renaissance and the Reformation, polemicists and dramatists (such as Shakespeare and Marlowe) began pluralizing the noun to describe discrete, abominable crimes. Acts of religious persecution, massacres, and regicides were cataloged as plural infamies.
  • 18th–19th Century (Enlightenment to Russian Realism): In the political writings of the French Revolution, the American Founders, and nineteenth-century novelists like Leo Tolstoy (e.g. in War and Peace, where Napoleonic warmongering is denounced), infamies served as a potent, resonant noun characterizing the horrifying excesses of despotic regimes and military campaigns.
  • 20th–21st Century (Enduring Legacy): While the singular infamy achieved modern immortalization through Franklin D. Roosevelt's Pearl Harbor address ("a date which will live in infamy"), the countable plural infamies remains an elevated, highly expressive literary and historical term reserved for compounding catalogues of monstrous wickedness, systemic corruption, and human rights violations.
