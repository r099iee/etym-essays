Eaves are the projecting lower edges of a roof that overhang the walls beneath them, carrying rainwater clear of the building's face. The word names the architectural margin where the roof stops and the wall begins — the strip of overhang that shields the wall, the doorway, and anyone standing beneath it from dripping water. In the passage from David Hawkes's translation of Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng (The Story of the Stone), the word does purely architectural work but acquires quiet narrative force: the characters walk along a verandah "which ran beneath the rear eaves of the hall" before passing through a corner gate into an alley-way running north and south. To be "beneath the eaves" is to be inside the protective compass of the Jia mansion yet not within its private rooms — a liminal zone of supervised passage. Primary senses and technical scope
  • Eaves (roof edge) — the overhanging lower edge of a roof; the standard architectural sense. Because the final -s is a fossil of an old singular, English treats eaves as a plural: "the eaves are leaking".
  • Eave (back-formed singular) — a modern singular ("the eave of the barn"), created because speakers misread the -s as a plural marker.
  • Eaves trough / eaves gutter — the gutter fixed at the eaves to carry rainwater away; also eavesdrip, the drip-line of the roof.
  • Eavesdrop (verb) and eavesdropper (noun) — to stand beneath the eaves, where the rainwater "drops", to overhear what is said indoors; by extension, to listen secretly anywhere.
  • Under one's own eaves — an idiom meaning "in one's own home".
  • Eaves swallows / martins — birds that build their nests in the shelter of the eaves.
Polysemy and the historical bridge The core noun is narrowly architectural, but it generated a rich figurative offshoot: eavesdrop. The bridge is concrete — the eave was the natural hiding-place of the curious servant, who stood under the roof's edge where rain "dropped" to hear what was said inside. From this physical image, the seventeenth-century word eavesdropper came to mean any secret listener, and the verb eavesdrop has outlived the architecture it names. A second, modern shift is morphological: the original singular eaves was reinterpreted as a plural, and a new singular eave was back-formed in the nineteenth century. The eaves in the novel function as a sheltering rim, part of the great house's overhanging protection: they keep the walkway dry and shaded, frame the visitors' movements, and mark the threshold between the gated domestic world and the alley outside — the roof's edge as the house's lowered eyelid, reinforcing the novel's abiding interest in the permeable boundaries between the intimate apartments and the world beyond. Register and use In register, eaves is a plain, concrete, architectural word, at home in builders' speech, yet capable of homely poetry whenever it appears with nests, icicles, or the boundary between home and wild. It collocates with beneath, under, overhanging, rear, low, dripping; with trough, gutter, and drip. It must be kept distinct from its neighbours: roof names the whole covering surface, of which the eave is only the protruding margin; cornice (from Italian cornice, Latin corōnis, Greek korōnis "curved") is an ornamental projecting moulding, decorative rather than functional; canopy denotes a covering stretched overhead, of cloth or a light frame, tent-like and removable; verandah or portico names the covered walkway beneath the eaves, not the roof edge above it — in the sentence the verandah runs beneath the eaves, so the two words occupy different strata of the scene; and gable is the triangular wall-end of a pitched roof, whereas the eave is its horizontal overhang. The word is nearly always plural in form and takes plural agreement, and the one-word derivatives eavesdrop and eavesdropper keep the noun alive in everyday English long after the architecture has been forgotten.
