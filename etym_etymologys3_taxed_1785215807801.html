The verb TAXED belongs to one of the oldest and most productive verbal families in the Indo-European lexicon, descending from the PIE root *tag-, "to touch, handle, arrange," the same root that yields tact, tactile, contact, contagion, and intact. From Proto-Italic *tag- Latin built the primary verb tangere, "to touch," and from tangere came the frequentative taxāre — literally "to touch repeatedly." The shift in meaning is instructive: the repeated touching of goods became the measuring of them, and the measuring became the fixing of their value. This is the concrete financial origin of the whole tax family: in Roman administration and law, taxāre meant to set the monetary worth of property, damages, or tribute. The same Latin verb, via Old French taster, is also behind English taste — for the Romans, as for us, to "touch" a thing with the tongue was to sample and assess it.

Latin taxāre passed into the Romance languages and, through Old French taxer and Anglo-Norman, entered Middle English as taxen around the fourteenth century, with the primary sense "to assess or impose a levy or tribute." For several centuries the word remained a fiscal and administrative verb, applied to tolls, customs, and the assessment of wealth, and the noun tax named the levy itself. The grammatical machinery of the English word was fixed in this period: the weak past and past participle taxed, and the family taxation, taxable, taxpayer.

The crucial figurative leap came in the sixteenth century. To assess a charge against a person's conduct was metaphorically to "tax" them, and so the verb developed its accusatory sense: "to charge someone with a fault, to confront or censure them openly." English usage from Shakespeare onward shows the pattern — "tax him with this abuse," "tax you with unkindness" — in which the legal vocabulary of assessment is redirected at a person's moral account, treating the fault as a debt owed and collectable. The construction is almost always with: to tax a person with a fault.

Both senses persist in modern English, though they have drifted apart in register:

  • The fiscal sense is universal and neutral — "to tax income," "heavily taxed," "taxation" — and it anchors the everyday noun and adjective family.
  • The accusatory sense survives as a literary or archaic construction, nearly always with "with": "he taxed me with hypocrisy," "taxed him with neglect." It is the sense used in the Mill biography, where the father openly taxes his son with being in love with another man's wife.
  • The straining sense ("the journey taxed his strength") extends the fiscal image of loading a demand onto someone or something, and it remains fully current in ordinary speech.

The biography of the Mills catches the word at the moment its two faces were still visibly one. James Mill's public confrontation of his son is described with a verb drawn from the assessment of debt: the father presses the charge home as if collecting what is owed, and the adverb "openly" emphasizes that no tact softened the accounting. For the modern reader, the word thus carries a double resonance — the familiar modern burden of taxation and the older, bookish moral sense in which a fault, like a debt, could be assessed and pressed home.
