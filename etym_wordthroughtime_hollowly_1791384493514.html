- Late 14th to 15th Century - [Physical sunkenness & hollow resonance] - Formed in Middle English from the adjective *holwe* ('concave, empty') + *-ly*; originally describing sounds reverberating from deep physical cavities or bodies marked by sunken hollows.
- 16th to 17th Century - [Insincerity, treachery & deceit] - Expanded in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (including Shakespeare) to characterize treacherous promises, false flatteries, and oaths lacking moral substance.
- 18th to 19th Century - [Gothic despair & haunted voices] - Solidified in Gothic and Victorian literature to describe the mournful, spectral vocal delivery of ghosts, dying men, or ruined heroes facing inevitable doom.
- 20th Century to Present - [Iconic dramatic cliché & comedic trope] - Celebrated in modern fiction and satire (especially P. G. Wodehouse) as the quintessential literary formula ('he laughed hollowly') depicting an unfortunate protagonist caught in overwhelming embarrassment or impending disaster.
