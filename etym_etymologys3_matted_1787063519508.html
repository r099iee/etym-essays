The etymological origin of the participle matted represents a fascinating fusion of ancient Mediterranean trade technology, monastic domestic material culture, and the organic metaphorical transference of textile manufacturing processes onto wild natural phenomena.

The ultimate root of the word does not trace to Proto-Indo-European verbal stock, but rather to an ancient Afroasiatic / Semitic substrate word, likely borrowed through Phoenician / Punic maritime trade into early Mediterranean Latin. The Semitic root *matta denoted a woven mat made of reeds, sedges, rushes, or coarse hemp, used for sleeping, flooring, and wrapping maritime cargo (cognate with Hebrew mittah, meaning "bed, couch, reclining frame").

In Late Latin, the word was codified as matta ("a mat of rushes or reeds"). It appears frequently in Christian Late Antiquity and Early Medieval monastic literature—notably in the Latin Vulgate Bible translated by Saint Jerome (c. 382–405 CE) and in the monastic Rule of Saint Benedict (c. 516 CE), where monk cell furnishings were strictly prescribed to include a simple matta (straw or rush sleeping mat). Through the Christianization of Anglo-Saxon Britain and Roman monastic administration, Late Latin matta was borrowed directly into Old English around the 9th century as matte or meatte.

Throughout Old English and Early Middle English, matte remained a concrete noun denoting floor coverings, door coverings, and bed pallets woven from intertwined reeds, rushes, or straw. However, during the 14th century in Middle English, the word underwent a major functional shift from noun to transitive and intransitive verb: matten ("to weave into a mat," and subsequently "to cause fibers to interlace and adhere like a mat").

By the 15th and 16th centuries (Early Modern English), speakers began applying the participial adjective matted metaphorically to any organic material that had naturally grown so entangled, clotted, and compressed that it mimicked the coarse, dense texture of a compressed rush mat. Writers began describing the "matted locks" of unwashed hair, the "matted fleeces" of neglected sheep, and the "matted briars" of impenetrable forest undergrowth. William Shakespeare employed this visceral descriptive power in Henry VI, Part 2 (Act III, Scene 2) when depicting the horrific physical state of the murdered Duke of Gloucester: "His hair up-stared, his eyeballs further out / Than when he lived, staring full ghastly, / Like lime-twigs set to catch my winged soul." John Milton in Paradise Lost (1667) similarly invoked "matted boughs" to conjure the primeval, tangled density of wild nature.

In the 19th century, the word received a secondary semantic tributary through phonetic convergence with the French adjective mat (feminine matte, meaning "dull, unpolished, deadened," historically derived from Old French mat, "checkmated/defeated," from Arabic māta, "he died"). This gave rise to the artistic terms for matted picture borders (the passe-partout border creating a flat decorative margin) and matted paint finishes (diffuse, non-glossy surfaces). In contemporary English, however, the primary participial sense remains steadfastly anchored in the physical entanglement of fibers, hair, and forest undergrowth.
