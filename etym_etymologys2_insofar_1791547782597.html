This compound word is an agglutination of three distinct Old English words: in + so + far.

1. in

PIE (c. 3500 BCE)
*en
"in, into"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*in
"in, into"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
in
"in, within, into"
↓
Middle English (12th c.)
in
"in"

2. so

PIE (c. 3500 BCE)
*so-
"this, that (demonstrative)"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*swa
"so, thus, in this manner"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
swā
"so, thus, to such a degree"
↓
Middle English (12th c.)
so
"so, in that degree"

3. far

PIE (c. 3500 BCE)
*per-
"forward, through, beyond"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*ferrō
"far, distant"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
feorr
"far, remote in space or degree"
↓
Middle English (12th c.)
fer / far
"far, distant"
↓
Early Modern English (15th–16th c.)
in so far
"to such an extent (phrasal)"
↓
Modern English
insofar
"to the extent that (compound)"

Note: In origin, insofar was an adverbial-prepositional phrase written as three separate words (in so far), nearly always followed by the correlative conjunction as. In 20th-century American printing, the three elements fused into a single solid word. While traditional British usage historically preferred the three-word form, the solid compound insofar is now universally recognized across all standard English varieties.
