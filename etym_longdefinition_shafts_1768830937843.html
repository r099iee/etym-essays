shafts: the plural of shaft — long, narrow, essentially columnar forms. The singular shaft names most generally the straight stem of a spear or arrow, and by extension any elongated, directed body: the handle of a tool, the pole of a cart, the passage of a mine, the rotating pole of an engine, or, as here, a beam of light.

Primary senses

  • The weapon's stem: the straight wooden body of a spear or arrow — the oldest sense, from Old English sceaft.
  • Any long straight body: the handle of a tool (axe-shaft), the pole of a cart, the stem of a column, the body of a feather or bone.
  • The mine-passage: a mine-shaft — the vertical passage sunk into the earth, straight as a spear.
  • The mechanical pole: a drive-shaft, crankshaft, propeller shaft — the rotating rod that transmits power.
  • The beam of light: a shaft of light, of sunlight, of lightning — a ray with the structure, rigidity, and direction of a weapon. This is the sense at work in the card's context.
  • Figurative weapon: a shaft of wit or ridicule — a verbal attack aimed like a spear.

The historical bridge

The word descends from a PIE root meaning "to cut, split" and originally named the stem cut from a tree — hence its association with the spear and with anything long, straight, and aimed. Through the Middle Ages the word spread from weapon-stem to any long straight object: cart-pole, column-stem, mine-passage. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the straightness and aim of the spear were transferred to light, giving the shafts of lightning and sunlight that remain a fixture of poetry and prose; in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the mechanical sense (drive-shaft, crankshaft) took over everyday speech, while "shafts of light" stayed frozen as a literary image. The plural, as used in the card, emphasizes multiplicity and direction: the sky's refracted rays are discrete, aimed columns.

Neighbours and boundaries

  • beam: the broadest term, originally the structural timber of a house, hence a massive, weighty column of light, often horizontal (a sunbeam). A shaft is narrower and more directed.
  • ray: a thin line radiating from a point, chiefly geometric or scientific; a shaft has thickness and body.
  • streak: a narrow mark on a surface, not a volume of light; a shaft occupies space.
  • pillar of light: vertical, architectural, massive; a shaft is narrower and aimed, not necessarily vertical.
  • lance: a shaft-bearing weapon used metaphorically of light's penetration (the sun lancing through clouds); shaft is the more neutral and structural word.
  • column: architectural, wide, supporting; a shaft is slender, straight, and directed.
  • needle: implies thinness and point; a shaft implies length and a straight body.
  • spear: the weapon itself, not its light; shaft names the straight stem that the spear-image lends to light.

Modern and technical usage

In everyday modern English the singular shaft is predominantly mechanical and mining vocabulary (drive-shaft, crankshaft, mine-shaft), while the plural shafts survives as a literary image for beams of light — "a thousand shafts of colored light." The card's context exploits the full semantic depth: the word gives each refracted ray the structure, rigidity, and direction of a weapon, yet the shafts are simultaneously threads that can be woven, so that the sky becomes a loom at work above the watcher. The tone is controlled wonder: light disciplined into shape, yet the shaped columns perform miracles of weaving — rainbow nets, jewel hues, refracted from a mesh of vast ripples and cross-ripples.
