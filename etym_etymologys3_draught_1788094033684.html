Introduction. Draught is one of the most perfectly transparent words in English: it means, in every one of its many senses, "something drawn." The noun was formed in Old English from the past participle of the verb draw, and everything it has ever named — air, drink, ships' depth, beer, sketches, fish, animals, a board game — is a different thing that gets drawn. Its history is thus less a story of changing meaning than of multiplying objects: each new century found one more thing to draw, and the word quietly collected them all. In the 19th century it acquired a second, political history — the American spelling reform that split draught from draft and made the word a marker of national identity.

Phase 1 — the Indo-European root (before 500 BCE).

  • The reconstructed PIE root *dʰregʰ- meant "to draw, drag, pull." Its best-known descendant outside Germanic is Latin trahere "to draw, drag" — the source of tractor, traction, trail, train, and portrait ("drawn" likenesses) — and many scholars derive both Latin trahere and Germanic *draganą from this single root, making draught a distant cousin of tractor and train. The link is generally accepted but not quite certain: a minority view treats the Germanic and Italic formations as independent expressive creations, and no single decisive proof exists either way.
  • In Proto-Germanic the root produced *draganą "to draw, pull, carry," the ancestor of English draw, drag, dray (a cart that draws loads), and draught itself.

Phase 2 — Old English: the verb and its noun (c. 700–1100).

  • Old English dragan was a strong verb of class 6 (dragan – drōg – dragen) meaning "to draw, pull, drag, carry, go": it covered drawing a sword, dragging a body, carrying a burden, and even "going" in some poetic uses.
  • From the past-participle stem came the noun dræht/dreaht "pulling, drawing, a draw" — the action-noun, first recorded around the 10th century. The -aht/-eaht spelling reflects the participle's -ht suffix, the same pattern that later produced the -aught/-ought spellings of taught, caught, sought, and brought.

Phase 3 — Middle English: from action to object (1200–1500).

  • Middle English draught began as the action ("the draught of a bow" — the drawing of it) and then made the crucial shift from action to object: "that which is drawn." This single metonymic step — naming the product after the pulling — unlocked every later sense.
  • By the 13th–14th centuries the object senses were multiplying: a draught of wine or water (what is drawn up into the mouth), a draught of fishes (a haul drawn in a net — the sense of the Bible's "a great draught of fishes," Luke 5:6), a ship's draught (the depth of water the hull draws down), a draught of the bow, and the drawing of lots.

Phase 4 — the 16th century: the great expansion.

  • The 1500s added the senses that dominate today. First, the current of air: air "drawn" through a chimney or opening became "a draught" — the sense Tolkien uses. Second, the sketch: a plan "drawn" with a pen became "a draught" (and its maker, in the 17th century, a draughtsman).
  • Third, the game: draughts (the board game) is named from drawing — moving — the pieces diagonally across the board; the word is attested from the mid-16th century, and the game itself became the British name for what Americans call checkers.
  • Fourth, medicine: "a draught" became a dose of liquid medicine drunk at once — "a sleeping draught," "a purging draught" — a sense still alive in pharmacy and fiction.

Phase 5 — the 17th and 18th centuries: beer, beasts, and boilers.

  • The tavern gave English on draught: ale or beer drawn directly from the cask rather than bottled — first recorded in the 17th century and still the standard phrase in British pubs. The sense spread to stout and cider; "draught Guinness" is today perhaps the single most famous use of the word in the world.
  • Agriculture and transport added draught animals: horses, oxen, and mules that draw ploughs and wagons — draught horse, draught ox, draught cattle. Engineering later borrowed the same idea for the draught of a chimney or furnace: the current of air drawn through a fire to feed it.

Phase 6 — the 19th century: Webster's spelling split.

  • Noah Webster's American dictionaries of the early 19th century standardised the spelling draft for the whole family, matching the pronunciation /dræft/ that had developed in American speech. The reform was part of his broader programme of simplifying spellings (color, honor, center).
  • British English refused the merger and split the word instead: draft for documents, bank drafts, and conscription ("the draft"), but draught for air, drink, ships, animals, and beer. The two nations thus divided the senses of one word between them — a rare case of a spelling reform creating a semantic border.
  • In the US today, draught survives mainly in the beer trade ("on draught," "draught Guinness"), in "draught horse," and in period flavour; Americans otherwise write draft for a current of air too ("a drafty room").

Phase 7 — the twentieth century and Tolkien.

  • In 1937 J.R.R. Tolkien wrote, at the moment Bilbo discovers the secret door, "Could there be a draught from that little hole?" The sentence uses the most everyday of the word's senses — a current of air — but the old spelling and the situation (cool air moving out of a mountain) give it an almost supernatural charge: the reader feels, with Bilbo, the first breath of the dragon's lair.
  • The word's double life continues: ordinary in Britain (a cold draught, a pint on draught), quaint-British in America, and fully alive in engineering, shipping, brewing, and the board-game shelf, where the British "draughts" and the American "checkers" still disagree about what to call the same drawn pieces.

Summary. From PIE *dʰregʰ- "to draw," through the Old English strong verb dragan and its participle-noun dræht, to the multiplying "drawings" of the Middle Ages and the 16th century, the tavern and the farm, the furnace and the board game, and the 19th-century spelling schism: draught is the noun of drawing itself — one word that still names everything, from air to ale, that gets drawn.
