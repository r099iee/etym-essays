octogenarian (noun, also used adjectivally): a person between eighty and eighty-nine years of age; one who is in the ninth decade of life. In the Gormenghast passage Nettel the octogenarian functions as a living archive: his age is itself the measure of the ceremony's antiquity, for he is the oldest memory still walking beneath the tower, the one witness whose lifetime spans the whole of the unchanging rite. The word's tone is formal, statistical, almost actuarial — it reduces a long human life to a numbered decade — and that very impersonality makes the aged witness more affecting, as eighty-odd years of personal history are absorbed into the impersonal eternity of the ceremony that outlives them. 'As far back as even Nettel could remember' is a measurement of time, and the word octogenarian is the unit of that measurement. Polysemy of 'octogenarian' — the senses and registers it carries today
  • Demographic / statistical sense (primary): an exact age-classification used by censuses, insurers, actuaries and demographers — 'the octogenarian population is growing'; the word converts a human life into a ledger entry of decades.
  • Honorific / eulogistic sense: in obituaries, tributes and news reports, 'the grand old octogenarian' names a survivor of remarkable endurance, lending the age dignity and monumentality.
  • Adjectival sense: 'an octogenarian musician' — functioning as an adjective meaning 'aged between eighty and eighty-nine'.
  • Medical / gerontological sense: in clinical and research writing, 'octogenarians' form a distinct cohort studied for health, cognition and frailty; the word is the natural companion of septuagenarian, nonagenarian and centenarian.
  • Everyday and humorous contrast: informal substitutes such as 'eightysomething' and 'in his eighties' carry no demographic gravity; the precision of octogenarian is what makes it formal, sometimes gently comic when applied to a vigorous, active person ('a sprightly octogenarian').
The historical bridge is morphological: Latin octō 'eight' + the distributive -gēnī 'eighty at a time' + the adjective suffix -ārius + English -an. The word belongs to the closed Latinate series of decade-classifications — sexagenarian (sixties), septuagenarian (seventies), octogenarian (eighties), nonagenarian (nineties), centenarian (one hundred and over) — of which it is the statistically neat middle term. Unlike 'elder', 'old man' or 'ancient', which name age without precision, octogenarian fixes the exact decade and belongs to the registers of demography, law, medicine and eulogy — precisely the tone Peake needs for a man who is less a character than a chronological marker.
armoury (noun; British spelling, American 'armory'): a storehouse or arsenal of weapons and military equipment; more broadly, any place where arms and armour are fitted, kept or supplied. The rusting armoury of Gormenghast is no longer a place of readiness but a museum of decay: the arms corrode because they are never drawn, and the word's whole martial history — the fitting of equipment, the keeping of arms, the preparation for war — is itself rusting into archaeology, exactly as Nettel rusts in his tower above it. The epithet 'rusting' is the hinge of the image: an armoury is by definition the heart of a fortress's readiness, so an armoury that rusts is a contradiction in terms — the castle's defensive memory has gone the way of Nettel's own. Polysemy of 'armoury' — the senses it carries today
  • Military sense (primary, BrE): a place where weapons and military equipment are stored — a storehouse, arsenal or weapons depot; in American English 'armory' also names a National Guard drill-and-assembly hall.
  • Heraldic sense (historical, 15th–18th c.): 'armoury' (and 'armory') names the science of coats of arms — the systematic description and recording of heraldic bearings; the College of Arms was the armoury of the nobility.
  • Figurative sense (modern): 'an armoury of...' — a stock or store of resources, skills, arguments or rhetorical weapons ('an armoury of legal precedents', 'the writer's armoury of devices'); the arsenal metaphor extends to any reserve held ready for use.
  • Sporting sense (chiefly BrE): the room or building where an athlete's equipment is kept; and, by extension in rowing clubs, the boathouse store.
  • Anatomical / technical extension: in medicine and biology, an 'armamentarium' (the learned Latin cognate) names the complete equipment — instruments, drugs, techniques — of a specialty; the plural imagination of a storehouse runs through all these uses.
The historical bridge is the root itself: Latin arma, 'tools, gear, weapons', is etymologically 'the things fitted' for a purpose, from the Indo-European root *h₂er-, 'to fit, join'. A store of arms was originally a store of 'fitted' equipment; the heraldic sense grew because coats of arms were the 'arms' — the distinguishing equipment — of a family; and the modern figurative sense extends the same idea of a fitted, ready store. In the passage the word's martial history is deliberately betrayed by 'rusting', and the decay of the armoury rhymes with the decay of the memory it should preserve.

Relation of the two words in the passage: Nettel's age (the octogenarian decade) and the armoury's rust are parallel measures of the ceremony's antiquity. The old man is a chronological marker; the armoury is a spatial one. Both are kept but neither is used — age that is only counted, arms that are only corroded — and together they make the rite's endurance a monument of neglect rather than of life.
