• fruit-trees: Similar as an overarching taxonomic and agricultural category describing woody plants cultivated for edible harvests; differs because fruit-trees is a broad generic umbrella covering apples, pears, plums, citrus, and figs, whereas peach-trees denotes specifically the stone-fruit species Prunus persica with its distinct pink blossoms and velvety drupes.

  • orchards: Similar because both refer to collections of fruit-bearing trees; differs because an orchard is a geographic tract of land or intentional agricultural plantation containing many trees, whereas peach-trees refers to the individual biological plants themselves.

  • nectarine-trees: Similar because nectarines are genetically identical varieties of Prunus persica sharing the same growth habits, wood structure, and floral biology; differs because nectarine-trees specifically yield glabrous (smooth-skinned) fruit lacking the soft fuzzy pubescence characteristic of standard peach-trees.

  • almond-trees: Similar as close botanical relatives belonging to the subgenus Amygdalus within the genus Prunus, sharing nearly identical spring floral structures and serrated leaves; differs because almond-trees (*Prunus dulcis*) are cultivated for their edible seed kernel encased in a leathery hull rather than for a fleshy, sweet pericarp.

  • apricot-trees: Similar as fellow members of the genus Prunus (*Prunus armeniaca*) that bloom early in spring with delicate white-to-pink blossoms and produce velvety stone fruit; differs because apricot-trees bear smaller, yellowish-orange fruit with smooth stones and distinct leaf morphology.

  • cherry-trees: Similar for their celebrated springtime flowering displays of white-and-pink blossoms in Asian and Western literature; differs because cherry-trees (*Prunus avium* / *Prunus serrulata*) bear clusters of small, long-stemmed, smooth-skinned fruits and have distinct horizontal bark lenticels.

  • saplings: Similar when referring to young immature trees planted in an orchard; differs because saplings denotes age and size rather than botanical genus or fruit-bearing capability.

  • espaliers: Similar when peach-trees are trained horizontally against sunny brick garden walls; differs because espalier refers to the horticultural architectural technique of two-dimensional lattice training rather than the tree species itself.
