- [Late 14th–15th c. (c. 1380s–1450s)] — Formed in Middle English from the adjective *actual* (borrowed from Old French *actuel* and Late Latin *actuālis*) + the adverbial suffix *-ly*, initially meaning "in deed, by active execution, through physical action" as opposed to contemplation, faith, or potentiality - [perception: understood as an active, pragmatic theological and legal term contrasting physical deeds with theoretical concepts]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English & Scientific Revolution] — Expanded in Renaissance philosophy, natural science, and legal jurisprudence to mean "in real existence, in objective reality," contrasting actual physical manifestations with theoretical hypotheses or fictitious notions - [perception: valued as an empirical, exacting term of scientific verification and philosophical rigour]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment to Victorian Era] — Developed into a widespread conversational intensifier and contrastive adverb, used by writers such as Jane Austen and Charles Dickens to register mild surprise, verify unexpected turns of events, or punctuate factual certainty - [perception: recognized as a refined stylistic device for affirming reality against appearances]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Discourse & Pragmatic Shift] — Proliferated exponentially in spoken and written English as a ubiquitous discourse marker, used to soften polite disagreements, initiate conversational pivots, or correct misconceptions (spawning popular internet culture tropes such as the pedantic "Well, actually...") - [perception: embraced as an indispensable conversational particle, though occasionally critiqued as an overused filler word; printed textual variants like *aetually* emerged through automated OCR scanning of classic print textbooks]
