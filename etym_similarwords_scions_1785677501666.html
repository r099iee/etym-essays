• HEIRS: Similar because both denote persons who succeed to a family's position or property; differs because heir is the legal word — one who inherits by law, will, or right, whether kin or not — while scion emphasizes blood, family, and the botanical image of the family tree; an heir need not be a scion, and a scion may not inherit at all.
  • DESCENDANTS: Similar because both mean persons who come after and are descended from an ancestor; differs because descendant is broad and neutral, covering every later member of a lineage without ranking or distinction, while scion specifically names a young, notable heir of a prominent house and carries admiration and the family-tree image.
  • OFFSPRING: Similar because both mean a person's children or a family's issue; differs because offspring is a neutral, biological word for children of any family, animal or human, while scion is restricted to human heirs of distinguished families and adds the lofty, dynastic tone.
  • PROGENY: Similar because both denote the descendants of a person or line; differs because progeny is a formal, collective term for all of a person's offspring, often used of animals and scientific contexts, while scion is singular-or-plural, specific to prominent human families, and individual rather than collective.
  • ISSUE: Similar because both can denote the children of a person or family; differs because issue is the formal, legal, almost cold term for all lineal descendants (a legal will speaks of "issue"), while scion is warm, admiring, and selective — the notable heir, not the whole line.
  • SUCCESSORS: Similar because both concern who comes after a person or line; differs because successor is about holding a position, office, or throne after another, by any means (election, appointment, inheritance), while scion is about descent and birth — the family heir rather than the office-holder.
  • BRANCHES: Similar because both use the family-tree metaphor for the members or offshoots of a lineage; differs because branch names a whole line of descent within a family (the elder branch, the cadet branch) rather than an individual person, while scion names the individual shoot or heir grafted onto the family stock.
  • SHOOTS: Similar because both are horticultural words for new plant growth; differs because a shoot is the generic word for any new growth of a plant, while a scion is specifically a shoot selected and cut for grafting onto another plant — the horticultural scion is defined by its role in grafting, which the plain shoot need not have.
  • GRAFTS: Similar because both belong to the grafting vocabulary of horticulture; differs because a graft is the operation of joining, or the result of a join, while a scion is the particular cutting used in the graft — the scion is the shoot that is grafted, the graft is the act or union itself.
  • HEIRS APPARENT: Similar because both denote the next in line to a title or position; differs because heir apparent is the precise legal-dynastic term for the person whose right to succeed cannot be defeated (the first-born son of a monarch), while scion is looser and more general — any notable descendant of a house, not necessarily first in line to inherit.
  • CHILDREN / SONS AND DAUGHTERS: Similar because both denote the young generation of a family; differs because children is the plain, neutral, everyday word for any family's offspring, while scion is selective and lofty — it names the heirs of distinguished houses and adds the botanical image of the family tree that children completely lacks.
  • POSTERITY: Similar because both concern the generations that come after; differs because posterity is the collective, impersonal word for all future generations (to be remembered by posterity), while scion names individual living heirs of a specific family and carries the warm, dynastic, horticultural image.
