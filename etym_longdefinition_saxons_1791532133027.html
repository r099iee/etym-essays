Who the Saxons were. The Saxons (Old English Seaxe, Latin Saxones) were a loose confederation of Germanic peoples whose homeland lay along the North Sea coast of what is now northern Germany, chiefly between the lower Elbe and the Weser and in the adjoining district of Holstein, with offshoots on the coastal islands and marshes. They were not a single tribe in the anthropological sense but a coalition of smaller groups — the later continental Saxons of Ostfalen, Westfalen and Engern — whose common name appears in Greco-Roman geography from the second century CE and whose seafaring violence made them feared throughout the late Roman world.

The name and the seax. The dominant etymology connects the ethnonym with the seax, the single-edged Germanic knife or short sword that archaeologists find in graves across northern Europe. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic noun *sahsą ("knife, dagger") descends from the Indo-European root *sek- ("to cut"), which also yields Latin secare ("to cut") and securis ("axe"), and English saw and section. On this reading the Saxons are literally "the sword-men" or "the knife-people," a warrior nickname that hardened into an ethnic name.

  • The derivation is a linguistic reconstruction; no ancient author explains the name, and the step from "knife" to "tribe" is inferred rather than documented.
  • A minority and now largely rejected suggestion connects the name with Latin saxum ("rock"), producing a folk etymology of "rock-dwellers" or "men of the stony country"; the sound correspondences do not support it.
  • Some scholars prefer a territorial origin in a district name, but no such place-name is securely attested, so the weapon etymology remains the standard account.

From raiders to settlers. Roman writers first mention the Saxons as pirates and raiders on the coasts of Gaul and Britain. The Litus Saxonicum ("Saxon Shore"), a chain of forts from the Wash to the Solent, was built or reorganised in the third century precisely to repel them. In Britain the traditional date of the Adventus Saxonum is 449, the year Bede assigns to the arrival of Hengist and Horsa, and Bede's famous tripartite scheme — Angles, Saxons and Jutes — became the founding narrative of the English. The kingdoms of Essex, Sussex and Wessex preserve the name in their very titles: the East, South and West Saxons.

The language label. Old English Seaxe also named a speech community. "Saxon" came to designate the West Saxon dialect, the literary standard of King Alfred's court, and hence the older stage of English itself; "Anglo-Saxon" is the nineteenth-century scholarly name for the language and civilisation of early England. On the continent the related Old Saxon of the Heliand represents a separate but cognate tradition. In English linguistic history "Saxon" therefore means both a people and a dialect, and "Saxon English" or "Saxonism" can denote a word of native Germanic stock as opposed to a Latin or French borrowing.

Modern and secondary senses.

  • Englishman, in Celtic usage: Irish Sassenach and Welsh Sais both descend from the Latin and Old English word for "Saxon" and generalise it to "Englishman." The bridge is historical: the Saxons were the Germanic people the Celtic-speaking British encountered most directly, so the tribal name became the generic ethnic label.
  • Anglo-Saxon as a geopolitical abstraction: in political science and journalism "the Anglo-Saxon world" means the English-speaking democracies, and "the Anglo-Saxon model" denotes their market-led economies and common-law traditions. This extends a nineteenth-century idea of a Teutonic "race" carrying free institutions.
  • A person from Saxony: the modern German federal state keeps the name, although its territory shifted eastward after the medieval duchy broke up; a "Saxon" today is simply an inhabitant of that state.
  • Transylvanian Saxons: German settlers invited into Hungary in the twelfth century were called Saxons although most came from the Rhineland and Flanders; the label was a legal and collective term rather than a precise origin.
  • Archaeology and history: "Saxon" labels pottery, sunken-featured buildings, brooches and cemeteries of the migration period, and "Saxon period" is shorthand for early medieval England before the Norman Conquest.

Technical and commercial uses. "Saxony" also names a fine soft woollen yarn and the carpet made from it, originally from the merino sheep of the Electorate of Saxony; the textile sense survives in the trade. In popular culture "Saxon" is the name of a British heavy metal band, an eponymous brand use with no semantic link beyond the ancestral name. Numismatists speak of Saxon coins and sceattas, and historians of law of Saxon law and the Saxon mirror (Sachsenspiegel).

Semantic bridges. The whole history of the word is a chain of widenings: a blade gives a nickname, the nickname gives a war-band, the war-band gives a coastal confederation, the confederation gives settlers, the settlers give a kingdom and a dialect, the dialect gives a language label, and the language label gives a modern geopolitical abstraction. Each step is a metonymy — weapon for warrior, people for place, people for speech, speech for culture, culture for political system — which is why one Iron Age knife-term can end up describing international capitalism.
