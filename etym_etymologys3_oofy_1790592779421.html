The story of oofy is a miniature mystery of slang etymology, where attestation is firm but origin remains debated. The base oof for money appears in Victorian sporting papers, music-hall patter, and thieves' cant dictionaries of the 1860s-1870s, glossed as cash, profit, or winnings. Hotten's Slang Dictionary and later Farmer and Henley record oof and oof-bird for a rich victim, without confident derivation. The adjective oofy, moneyed, emerges distinctly later, popularized enormously but not coined by P. G. Wodehouse in the 1910s-1920s; the Oxford English Dictionary finds early examples around 1917-1921 in Punch, sporting columns, and Wodehouse's Jeeves and Blandings stories, with RP pronunciation /ˈuːfi/ as a long /uː/ plus diminutive /fi/.

Phonetic and morphological development is transparent once oof exists: English productive -y forms adjectives of abundance as in juicy, lousy, filthy, beefy, so oof + -y naturally yields full of money. The sound oof itself is striking: a closed back vowel plus labiodental fricative, resembling grunts of exertion, wind knocked out, or Yiddish exclamations. This has fueled competing theories. One suggests onomatopoeia for the grunt of lifting heavy coin or of being punched by a loss. Another proposes abbreviation of German Uff? or auf? Another, most often repeated, proposes Yiddish or German-Jewish moneylenders' cant, perhaps from Hebrew of or German auf Geld?, or from Uf as exclamation, brought into London East End and racing slang via Jewish bookmakers and moneylenders. Partridge and Beale note possible Yiddish influence but mark it unproven; the OED labels origin unknown and explicitly warns against fanciful Hebrew derivations without evidence.

  • Victorian oof (1860s-1890s): Thieves, bookmakers, and music-hall comedians need a concealing word for profit; oof fills the niche alongside rhino, blunt, dibs, chink; oof-bird extends to the human source.
  • Edwardian diffusion (1900s-1910s): Sporting journalists and army officers carry oof from racecourse to club; adjectival oofy appears as playful elaboration, first in dialogue to signal knowing insider status.
  • Wodehouse apotheosis (1920s-1930s): Jeeves, Bertie Wooster, Mr. Mulliner, and Blandings narrators use oofy dozens of times for millionaires to be tapped; repetition fixes meaning as amiably rich and touchable, spreading to Punch and popular novels.
  • Decline and fossilization (1940s-present): Post-war tax, welfare state, and American slang like dough, bucks, loaded displace oof; oofy survives as deliberate archaism signaling Wodehousian nostalgia.

Socio-cultural context illuminates why oofy flourished when it did. Edwardian England combined conspicuous plutocracy—American heiresses, South African magnates, self-made soap kings—with a leisured class of younger sons chronically short of cash. A slang word was needed that was neither moralizing like mammon nor technical like capital, but comic and predatory, marking the rich as fair game for loans, subscriptions, and marriages. Oofy did exactly that, classifying men like Wodehouse's Tom as peculiar because wealth alters behavior: suspicion of parasites, ostentation, or oblivious generosity. Its masculine bias reflects patriarchal finance; its liquidity bias reflects a world of fivers, sovereigns, and cheques rather than portfolios.

Literary attestations cemented its charm. Wodehouse writes of oofy proselytes, oofy old birds, and the peculiarity he noticed in other very oofy men, using repetition to build a sociology of wealth. Saki,kc Wells, and Punch imitators echo it. Later writers like Evelyn Waugh avoid it as too light, preferring rich, which hastened its dating. Today etymologists treat oofy as a textbook case of slang with secure inner-English morphology—oof plus -y—but opaque outer etymology for oof itself, requiring explicit acknowledgment of debate rather than invented Yiddish pedigrees. Its endurance in vocabulary study owes less to economics than to sound symbolism: oof feels like money looks—round-mouthed, breathy, slightly absurd.
