The story of promiscuously begins in a Proto-Indo-European root of mixing, *meyk-, 'to mix, mingle', whose descendants are scattered across the family: Greek meignymi 'to mix', Sanskrit misra- 'mixed', Latin miscere 'to mix', and Old Church Slavonic mesiti. From miscere Latin built a rich vocabulary of mixture: mixtus, 'mixed', miscellaneus, 'of mixed sorts' (source of English miscellaneous and miscellany), and the compound that concerns us, promiscuus. The prefix pro-, 'before, forth, in public', in this compound contributed the idea of mixing out in the open, without concealment or order; the resulting adjective meant 'mixed in common, indiscriminate, common to all'. Classical Latin uses promiscuus of things that are common property, of indiscriminate mingling, and of behaviour that makes no distinctions. The adverb promiscue, 'indiscriminately, in common', was the everyday Latin adverb built on it.

The word entered English at the very beginning of the seventeenth century, in the learned register of the period's theological and rhetorical prose: promiscuous is first recorded in 1603 and the adverb promiscuously soon after, around 1604. The sense was entirely neutral and general — things taken or done together without distinction, classes of people mingling freely, material drawn without sorting by kind. This is the sense the modern preface uses, and it remained the ordinary sense for a century and a half. But a narrowing was already stirring inside the adjective.

Sound and form:
  • Latin promiscuus to English promiscuous: the English adjective is a straight learned loan, the Latin -uus ending simply dropped.
  • The adverb was built on English soil: promiscuous + the native adverbial suffix -ly to promiscuously (rather than the Latin promiscue being borrowed directly).
  • The stress pattern of the English adverb, /pre'miskjuasli/, follows the adjective's pattern, with the main accent on the second syllable.

The sexual narrowing:
  • 17th century: promiscuous means 'mixed, indiscriminate, common to all' — a neutral word for mingling without distinction.
  • Late 17th–18th centuries: as promiscuous came to be applied to persons 'not restricted to one sexual partner', the adjective and its adverb began to acquire sexual associations; the neutral sense of heterogeneous mixing remained in print but gradually felt old-fashioned.
  • 19th–20th centuries: by the twentieth century promiscuously was, in everyday use, nearly synonymous with sexual promiscuity, carrying strong moral disapproval; the value-neutral sense retreated to the scholarly page.
  • Modern reclamation: in academic prose promiscuously is still used in its original colour — drawing 'extensively and promiscuously' on poets from Shakespeare to Yeats — a consciously eclectic, lightly self-deprecating mingling; and in science the adjective promiscuous has been reborn technically, of enzymes and molecules that interact with many partners.

Scientific jargon: in modern biology and chemistry, promiscuous names interactions without the moral overtones: a promiscuous enzyme catalyses a wide range of substrates; promiscuous binding sites and promiscuous receptors accept many partners; in network science, promiscuous connectivity means links to many nodes. This technical reuse is a deliberate return to the root idea of mixing with many — a twentieth-century scientific revival of the word's oldest, most literal sense.

The final form, PROMISCUOUSLY, is therefore a study in how a neutral Latin compound travelled: born meaning 'mixed in common, without distinction', narrowed to the bedroom by Victorian morality, then reclaimed in the laboratory and the lecture-hall for its original, indiscriminate, widely-mixing sense — the very sense in which a scholar draws 'extensively and promiscuously' on the poets.
