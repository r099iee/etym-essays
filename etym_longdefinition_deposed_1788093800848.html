Core sense. Deposed is the past tense and past participle of the verb depose — most familiarly, "removed from high office, especially from a throne, by force or by legal process". A deposed king, emperor, dictator, or bishop has been pulled down from power against his will. In the source context the Master of Lake-town asks in outrage, "For what fault am I to be deposed?" — the crowd murmuring against his rule after the dragon's destruction of their town.

1. Primary sense: removal from power. The political sense dominates. Depose describes the act of unseating a ruler or leader — through coup, revolution, rebellion, vote, or judgment — and deposed is the state that results. It implies that the removal was forced and involuntary, and it usually carries the gravity of thrones: deposed monarch, deposed president, deposed tyrant. Unlike "dismissed" (of an employee) or "voted out" (of an elected official), "deposed" is reserved for rulers and supreme leaders, or is used with rhetorical inflation of lesser bosses. Historically it is the standard word for the fall of kings and, in church history, of popes and bishops removed by councils.

2. Secondary sense: legal testimony. In law, to depose means "to give sworn testimony", especially in a deposition — a formal, recorded, out-of-court questioning of a witness under oath. A witness who has given such testimony has been deposed. This sense is entirely standard in American legal practice ("the witness was deposed for three hours") and goes back to the Latin legal phrase deponere testimonium, "to lay down testimony" — the witness "puts down" his evidence, as one lays down a burden or a deposit. The two senses of the word share the same root image: putting something down — a ruler put down from his seat, evidence put down under oath.

3. Tertiary and archaic senses.

  • To lay down, put aside — the oldest English sense (14th c.), "to put down a burden or thing"; now archaic except in poetic use ("deposing his heavy pack").
  • To degrade, demote — used of clergy and officers in earlier English; survives mainly in "defrocked" territory and in historical writing about deposed bishops.
  • In Scots and historical law — the older spelling depone survives in Scottish legal usage: a deponent "depones" on oath.

4. Semantic bridge. Latin deponere = "to put down, lay aside" (de- "down, away" + ponere "to put, place"). Putting a ruler "down" from his position of power is the political metaphor; putting testimony "down" before a court is the legal one. Both are visible in the word's history from the start, and both survive today — one in headlines about coups, the other in American courtrooms. The bridge between them is simply the image of laying something down, applied to two different heavy things: a crown and an oath.

5. Register and collocations. Formal and weighty; standard collocations: deposed king/monarch/emperor, deposed leader, deposed in a coup, deposed by the army, the deposed president. In passive constructions ("was deposed") it is the headline word for overthrown rulers. The word-family: depose, deposition (both "removal from office" and "legal testimony"), deponent (one who testifies), and the twins deposit and depository (from the same Latin verb's past participle depositum, "laid down").
