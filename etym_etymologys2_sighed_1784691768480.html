Proto-Germanic *sīkaną "to sigh, to draw a long audible breath" (reconstructed; an imitative / onomatopoeic formation whose long sibilant onset mirrors the hissing outflow of breath; no Indo-European parent can be securely reconstructed — see DEBATE) → Old English sīcan "to sigh, groan, draw a deep breath" (attested in early poetry and prose, 8th–11th c.; a strong verb, past singular sāc / sīcte, past participle sicen) → Middle English sīken "to sigh" (12th–14th c.; the infinitive was reshaped, and a new infinitive sihen was back-formed from the old past stem in -sīhte) → Middle English sighen / sygh "to sigh" (14th–15th c.; the -gh spelling records a palatal fricative still pronounced) → Early Modern English sigh "to sigh" (16th c.; the fricative was lost in pronunciation, compare through, high, night, and the long vowel was raised and diphthongised by the Great Vowel Shift, so that sigh came to rhyme with buy) → Modern English sigh; weak preterite and past participle sighed "to have emitted a long, audible exhalation expressive of grief, relief, longing, or resignation" (the regularised -ed preterite of the modern verb; the form used in the text). DEBATE / NUANCE: The family is confined to the Germanic dialects and no Indo-European ancestor is securely reconstructable; scholars treat the Proto-Germanic base as imitative — the sibilant onset mirrors the hissing outflow of breath. Some have floated a link to an Indo-European root meaning "to long for, want" to account for the yearning sense of sighing, but the phonetic evidence is weak and this is not generally accepted. The Old English strong past (sāc) was replaced in Middle English by forms built on the -sīhte stem, and the modern weak preterite sighed completes that regularisation.
