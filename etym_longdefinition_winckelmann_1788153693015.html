Winckelmann names Johann Joachim Winckelmann (1717–1768), the German scholar generally credited as the founder of modern art history and scientific archaeology, and the prophet of neoclassicism. Born at Stendal in the Mark of Brandenburg, the son of a cobbler, he rose through poverty and a series of provincial posts to become the most celebrated antiquarian in Europe, Prefect of Antiquities in Rome, and the author of two books — Gedanken über die Nachahmung der griechischen Werke in der Malerei und Bildhauerkunst (Thoughts on the Imitation of Greek Works in Painting and Sculpture, 1755) and Geschichte der Kunst des Alterthums (History of the Art of Antiquity, 1764) — that reshaped how Europe looked at antiquity.

Primary sense — the revolutionary thesis. The core of Winckelmann’s teaching, crystallised in the phrase edle Einfalt und stille Größe, “noble simplicity and quiet grandeur”, was that Greek art of the classical period achieved a perfection in which the surface calm of the body expresses the depth of the soul; that this art was the product of Greek freedom and climate; and that modern artists could regenerate themselves only by imitating the Greeks. His history of ancient art was the first to organise the monuments chronologically and by styles — archaic, classical, Hellenistic — turning connoisseurship into a discipline with periods, causes and a narrative of rise and decline.

  • Secondary sense — the name as a movement. “Winckelmann” in criticism (Pater’s essay, included as the closing study of The Renaissance, is the canonical English instance) stands for the whole Greco-philhellenic aesthetic: the faith that beauty is the outward address of spirit, and that the study of antiquity is a spiritual discipline.
  • Tertiary sense — the martyr legend. The violent death in Trieste (8 June 1768), stabbed in his hotel by a chance acquaintance, Francesco Arcangeli, apparently for his medals and money, completed the biography into a Romantic myth: the scholar who loved Greece and died ungraciously like a wandering prince, mourned by Goethe as one who “came into the world to give a new sense of life”.
  • Modern academic usage. “Winckelmann” remains the eponym of method: Winckelmannian archaeology, the Winckelmann Society in Stendal, the Winckelmann-Medaille, and the adjective “Winckelmannian” for the idealising view of Greek art that modern scholarship has both inherited and corrected.
  • Linguistic bridge. The surname itself is transparently German — Winkel “corner, nook” + Mann “man” — originally indicating a man who came from a corner or small village, an etymology almost comically at odds with the world-historical fame the name now carries.

History of the person. The career: schoolmaster at Seehausen, rector at Stendal, librarian to Count von Bünau at Nöthnitz near Dresden, where he first read deeply in the antiquarian literature; conversion to Catholicism in 1754, the price of admission to Rome; the Roman years under the protection of Cardinals Passionei and Albani, keeper of the Albani collection; the trips to Naples, Herculaneum and Pompeii; the post of Prefect of Antiquities. Winckelmann’s influence ran through Mengs, Canova, David, and the whole French and German classicism of the following decades, and through Goethe and Schiller to the German Hellenism that Pater, Arnold and the Victorians turned into a European programme.

Register today. In art-historical writing the name is a dateline: pre-Winckelmann connoisseurship was anecdote and attribution; post-Winckelmann it is history. In general culture the name retains the glow of the man who taught Europe that “the only way for us to become great, and, if it is possible, inimitable, is the imitation of the ancients”.
