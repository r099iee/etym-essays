First element: Yule
Proto-Germanic (reconstructed; origin unknown)
*jehwlą
"midwinter festival season" — no secure PIE etymon;
a link to PIE *yek- "to speak" (cf. Latin
iocus "jest") is speculative
↓
Gothic (attested 4th–6th c. CE)
jiuleis — a month-name
"Yule-month (fruma/aftuma jiuleis)"
↓
Old Norse (attested from c. 10th c.)
jól (pl.)
"Yule, the great midwinter heathen feast; later Christmas"
↓
Old English (attested c. 8th–9th c.; Bede c. 725)
geōl / geōla
"Yule, Christmas; ǣrra geōla 'December', æftera geōla 'January'"
↓
Middle English (12th–15th c.)
yole / yule
"Christmas, the festival season"
↓
Modern English — compounded with tide:
Yule-tide
"the season of Yule, Christmastide"

Second element: tide
PIE (c. 4500–2500 BCE)
*dā- / *dī-
"to divide, cut up, allot" → *dīti- "allotted time"
↓
Proto-Germanic (reconstructed)
*tīdiz
"time, period, season" — cf. German Zeit,
Old Norse tíð
↓
Old English (attested c. 8th–9th c.)
tīd
"time, season, hour; festal day"
↓
Middle English (12th–15th c.)
tide
"time, season; (14th c.) the sea's ebb and flow"
↓
Modern English — combined with Yule:
Yule-tide
"the season of Yule (Christmastide)"

Note: modern "tide" chiefly means the sea's rise and fall; the older "time,
festival-season" sense survives in compounds: Yuletide, Christmastide, Eastertide,
eventide, Whitsuntide.
