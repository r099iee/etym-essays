- Old English 'glæm' - meant a beam or flash of light and also splendour and radiance generally; in Old English verse a gleam was often the flash of a weapon or the brightness of a jewel, with a dignity now lost.
- Middle English - 'gleme' remains chiefly a noun for a ray or glimmer of light, and the verb 'to gleam' is formed as its action; still largely positive, denoting a brightness that pierces darkness.
- 18th-19th century - the word specializes: a gleam becomes a small, soft, clear point of reflected or distant light against gloom — the gleam of water, of a far summit, of a faint hope — and a favourite of Romantic landscape poetry.
- Contemporary - 'gleamed' is standard for a steady but subdued shine, brighter and cleaner than a glimmer, softer than a glitter; still lightly literary, it retains a faint archaic lustre of splendour.
