LINEAR CHAIN FOR "gutter" (noun, "a shallow channel for carrying off water")

Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *gheu- (c. 4500–2500 BCE) — "to pour". A verbal root meaning "to pour out", attested in Sanskrit juhoti "pours (into the fire)", Greek khéō "I pour", and Latin fundō/fūt- "I pour". The root's semantics are solid, though the specific path to Latin gutta is debated (see note).

Stage 2 — Latin: gutta, -ae f. (attested 1st century BCE onward) — "a drop (of liquid)". From the pouring root (or from a debated alternative), Latin produces the noun for a single drop. Its medieval derivatives build the channel senses. (Note: some scholars derive gutta from a separate root related to "to drip"; the *gheu- connection is traditional but not certain.)

Stage 3 — Late Latin / Medieval Latin: gottaria / guttaria (early medieval) — "a water channel, a spout, a gutter". A derivative of gutta meaning literally "a thing that deals with drops" — a channel for catching falling rainwater. This stage is reconstructed from Romance descendants rather than directly attested.

Stage 4 — Old French: gotiere / goutiere (12th century) — "a water spout, a gutter". The direct descendant of guttaria; the same word gives modern French gouttière "gutter, waterspout".

Stage 5 — Anglo-Norman / Middle English: gotere, guttere (13th century) — "a gutter, water channel". Borrowed from Old French after the Norman Conquest; by the 14th century the spelling stabilizes toward gutter.

Stage 6 — English: gutter (14th century onward) — "a shallow trough or channel along the eaves of a roof or at the side of a street for carrying off water"; by the 19th–20th centuries extended to the gutter of a bowling lane, the gutter margin of a page, and the figurative "gutter" of society. NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: The principal debated point is the origin of Latin gutta. The traditional assignment to PIE *gheu- "to pour" (Pokorny, Etymonline) is plausible and widely repeated, but not universally accepted; de Vaan notes that gutta has no fully certain Indo-European etymology and could be an expressive or substrate formation. The Romance and English stages (guttaria → gotiere → gotere → gutter) are secure and well documented. A separate English word gout (the disease) descends independently from Latin gutta via Old French gote, under the medieval theory that the disease was caused by "drops" of humor flowing into the joints — an interesting parallel etymology, not part of this chain.
