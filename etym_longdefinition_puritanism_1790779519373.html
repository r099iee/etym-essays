Puritanism is a multifaceted noun denoting an extreme strictness, austerity, or scrupulousness in moral, religious, or behavioral matters. Historically rooted in the reformist movements of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century English Protestantism, the term has broadened across centuries to describe any social, psychological, philosophical, or institutional regime that enforces rigorous self-denial, views sensual pleasure and bodily indulgence with suspicion, and demands uncompromising adherence to an unyielding standard of purity and behavioral conformity.

The term encompasses several distinct yet interconnected primary, secondary, and figurative dimensions:

  • Historical and Theological Senses: Originally, Puritanism designated the religious principles and practices of the English Puritans—a faction of zealous sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Calvinist Protestants within the Church of England. They sought to "purify" the national church of all remaining Roman Catholic liturgical ceremonies, vestments, hierarchical episcopacy, and unscriptural traditions. In this historical context, Puritanism represented a comprehensive worldview founded on covenant theology, double predestination, intense personal piety, total depravity, the absolute supremacy of biblical scripture over ecclesiastical tradition, and the establishment of a godly commonwealth governed by strict moral surveillance.
  • Moral and Social Rigorism: By extension, the term refers to any moral code characterized by severe austerity, ascetic self-control, and the systematic suppression of recreational pleasures, theatrical arts, festive pastimes, and sensual indulgences. It denotes a cultural climate where recreation is viewed as frivolous or sinful, and where moral rectitude is measured by the degree of emotional restraint and bodily discipline exhibited by individuals.
  • Psychological and Psychoanalytic Dimensions: In modern psychology and psychoanalysis, puritanism describes a personality structure or neurosis marked by hyperactive guilt, sexual repression, and an obsessive need for moral perfection. In this sense, it denotes the internalized policing of natural instincts, where the ego and superego continuously censor spontaneous joy, physical intimacy, and creative playfulness out of a subconscious dread of contamination or transgression.
  • Aesthetic, Literary, and Critical Usage: In literary and cultural criticism, puritanism denotes an ideological or formal hostility toward aesthetic pleasure, ambiguity, sensuous ornament, and artistic play for its own sake. Critical puritanism demands that literature and art justify themselves solely through didactic moral instruction, political utility, or ideological correctness, viewing aesthetic pleasure without utilitarian justification as suspect or decadent.
  • Modern Socio-Political and Digital Jargon: In contemporary discourse, the term is frequently employed metaphorically to describe secular, algorithmic, or ideological policing across the political spectrum. "Technological puritanism" or "digital puritanism" refers to automated content moderation algorithms and corporate platform policies that sanitize online spaces by ruthlessly censoring erotic art, human anatomy, controversial speech, and complex artistic expression to maintain corporate brand safety and sanitised neutrality.

The semantic bridge connecting these diverse meanings is the foundational concept of purification through exclusion. Whether cleansing a church of popish ceremonies, purging an individual soul of carnal desires, stripping an artwork of ornamental decadence, or scrubbing an internet platform of risqué imagery, puritanism always operates by constructing an idealized boundary of immaculate purity and ruthlessly excising whatever is deemed corrupt, sensual, superfluous, or unruly.
