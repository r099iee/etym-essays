heap (noun and verb) — in its primary, literal sense, a heap is an untidy collection of objects placed haphazardly one on top of another; a disorderly pile or mound of things thrown or gathered together without arrangement. Unlike a stack (neatly aligned) or a tidy pile, a heap is by definition without form: gravity, not design, determines its shape. In the reading context of this card, from Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, Lennie "threw them in a heap on the old ash pile and went back for more and more": the dried leaves and twigs tumble into a crude mound beside the ash pile, exactly the word's elemental sense — matter gathered into a loose, unstructured mass, waiting for the spark that will turn it into the blaze of the neighbouring scene.

Primary senses
  • An untidy pile of objects — "the laundry was in a heap on the floor", "a heap of stones": the core concrete sense, always implying disorder and loose, unstable accumulation.
  • A mound or raised mass — "a heap of earth", "a compost heap", "a slag heap": a natural or artificial raised accumulation; in English the everyday word for a mound of material that has been dumped or gathered.
  • A large quantity (informal) — "a heap of work", "heaps of time", "heaps better": an abundant amount or degree, intensifying and colloquial; Australian and New Zealand English even use "heaps" as an adverb meaning "very" or "a lot" ("heaps good").

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings
  • Slang: a dilapidated motor vehicle — "that old heap", "a heap of rust": an old, worn-out, unreliable car or other machine, so called because it looks like a pile of scrap rather than a functioning vehicle; the "scrap heap" is the dump for such things.
  • Collapse idiom — "to fall in a heap", "to come down in a heap": to collapse or be knocked down into a shapeless mass, of a person or a structure ("she collapsed in a heap on the floor").
  • Social metaphor — "the bottom of the heap": the lowest position in a social, occupational, or competitive order, with "top of the heap" as its opposite. The metaphor pictures society as a pile in which people are stacked in layers, disorderly and competitive.
  • Computing: the memory heap — in computer science, the heap is the region of a program's memory in which objects and data are allocated and freed dynamically, without the strict last-in-first-out discipline of the stack; running out of memory in a language like Java is reported as a heap error. The name reflects the original unordered, "anything goes" character of the region.
  • Computing: the heap data structure — a binary heap is a specialised tree-based structure that satisfies the heap property (every parent is greater than, or less than, its children), used to implement priority queues and the heapsort algorithm. The informal, cluttered memory "heap" is thus quite distinct from this precisely ordered structure — a well-known and sometimes confusing polysemy within a single field.
  • Verb senses — "to heap up" (to pile or accumulate), "to heap scorn / insults / praise on someone" (to bestow abundantly), "to heap coals of fire on someone's head" (a biblical idiom from Proverbs 25:22, quoted in Romans 12:20, meaning to overwhelm an enemy with kindness so as to shame him).
  • Compound and fixed phrases — "scrap heap" and "the scrap-heap of history"; "refuse heap", "rubbish heap"; "heap" in archaeology for accumulations of domestic refuse (kitchen midden, shell heap); plural "heaps" as an indefinite large amount.

Historical semantic bridges
  • The bridge from a raised mound to a pile of objects is natural: any mass of material raised above its surroundings — earth, stones, or discarded goods — is visually a heap.
  • The bridge from concrete pile to "a large quantity" is quantitative metaphor: a big heap is simply a lot of stuff, so "a heap of trouble" means abundant trouble, and "heaps" became a general intensifier.
  • The bridge to the worn-out car runs through the scrap heap: a derelict vehicle looks as if it has already been dumped on a heap of salvage, and so the car itself is "a heap."
  • The bridge to the social metaphor runs through stacking: people "on top" and "at the bottom" of a heap reproduce the vertical disorder of a real pile, giving a vivid image of hierarchy without order.
  • The bridge to computing is technical naming: early programmers described the dynamic memory region as a "heap" because blocks of many sizes are dumped into it pell-mell, in contrast to the tidy, stack-like allocation; the mathematical data structure later borrowed the same casual name.

Modern technical and professional usage
  • Programming — "heap" is standard terminology in memory management, garbage collection, and data-structure textbooks (binary heap, min-heap, max-heap, heapsort).
  • Architecture and industry — "slag heap", "refuse heap", "compost heap": raised dumps of industrial, domestic, or agricultural material.
  • Geology and archaeology — heaps and middens are recognised layers of accumulated material valuable for stratigraphy.
  • Colloquial emphasis — "heaps" as a quantifier and intensifier is characteristic of Australian, New Zealand, and British informal English.

Boundaries against near-synonyms
  • Unlike pile, which is a general term that may be orderly, heap always implies disorder and haphazard accumulation.
  • Unlike stack, which is neat and systematic, a heap is formless.
  • Unlike mound, which suggests a rounded, regular elevation, a heap is loose and unstable.
  • Unlike hoard or cache, which imply stored valuables, a heap is open, casual, and often worthless.
