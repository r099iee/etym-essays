↓
Medieval / Law Latin (c. 14th–16th c.)
innuendo
‘parenthetical formula: "meaning thereby, to wit"’
↓
Early Modern English (c. late 17th c.)
innuendo
‘an oblique insinuation, veiled defamatory hint’
↓
Modern English (current)
innuendo
‘veiled derogatory or suggestive insinuation’

Note: The word is a direct transliteration of the Latin ablative gerund innuendō (‘by nodding toward’), from in- (‘toward’) + nuere (‘to nod’, from PIE *new-, the same root that produced Latin numen, divine nod or will). In medieval English common law pleadings, Latin was the official recording language; when a declaration in an action for slander had to explain an ambiguous English insult, the clerk inserted the parenthetical Latin formula (innuendo, ‘meaning thereby...’). Over time, the formulaic parenthetical phrase broke free and was re-analyzed by English speakers as a substantive noun.
