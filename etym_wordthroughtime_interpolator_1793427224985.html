- Classical Rome (1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE) — artisans and fullers: Latin interpolare originally described the practical work of fullers and wool-cleaners repairing, whitening, and furbishing old garments by sewing in fresh patches.
- Late Roman Law (2nd–6th c. CE) — legal tampering: transferred into Roman jurisprudence (in the Digest of Justinian) to condemn dishonest scribes who altered wills, deeds, or public registers by scratching out lines and inserting fraudulent clauses.
- Renaissance Humanism (15th–16th c.) — classical philology: revived by Renaissance scholars like Erasmus, Poliziano, and Scaliger to denounce medieval scribes and monkish copyists who inserted theological glosses into classical texts.
- 17th to 18th c. — English polemic and literary satire: anglicized as interpolator to label political censors, unauthorized printers, and meddling hacks, famously wielded by Jonathan Swift against his publisher Benjamin Motte.
- 19th c. — mathematical analysis: adopted in mathematics by Lagrange, Gauss, and Cauchy to denote an operator, formula, or calculator that interpolates missing values in tables of astronomical or numerical data.
- Late 20th c. to present — digital signal processing and computing: specialized into hardware and software components (DSP interpolators, pixel scaling engines, neural frame interpolators) that generate intermediate audio-video samples.
