The evolution of blotched runs through two obscure Middle English stain-and-sore words that collided in the 16th century to make a third. The first parent is blot, noun and verb, recorded around 1400 meaning a stain of ink and by extension any blemish. Its deeper origin is debated: the OED finds no French or Latin ancestor and notes a possible Scandinavian source, comparing Old Norse blettr meaning stain or blot, Danish plet spot, though the phonology is not airtight. Etymonline calls blot of obscure origin, perhaps from Old Norse. It belongs to the expressive bl- family of flat spreading things, compare blob, blur, bleed, but family resemblance is not etymology, and no Pokorny root covers it. What is secure is the Middle English usage: blottis of ink in scribal complaints, blots on honour in moral prose.

The second parent is botch, noun meaning ulcer, boil, or clumsily mended patch, and verb meaning to mend clumsily or spoil byPatching, recorded in the late 14th century as bocchen? Actually botch as noun c. 1400 for swelling and as verb c. 1530 for bungled repair. Its origin is equally obscure, possibly from Middle English bocche? swelling, or Anglo-French boche? lump, with no secure PIE pedigree. De Vaan, Beekes, and Pokorny offer nothing, because the word never belonged to the learned Latin-Greek stock they map. It lived among tailors, cobblers, and surgeons: a botch was a puffed seam and a botched job a visibly mended one.

  • Middle English duality (c. 1400–1500): blot as flat stain versus botch as raised sore or clumsy patch coexist without merging. Scribes fear blots, patients fear botches. Phonetics keep them apart by final -t versus -tch, though the vowels rhyme and the senses overlap on disfigured skin.
  • Mid-16th century fusion (c. 1560–1590): blotch appears as noun meaning both a discoloured patch and a pustule, fusing flat stain with raised swelling. The OED treats it as a blend or variant influenced both ways, Etymonline as perhaps blot plus botch. Early citations show medical and inky uses side by side: blotches on the face, blotches on paper. The blend succeeds because it names what neither parent quite does: a mark that is both coloured and textured.
  • 17th–18th century verb and adjective (c. 1600–1750): to blotch meaning to cover with blotches and blotched meaning so covered become standard. Herbals describe leaves blotched with white, physicians faces blotched with red, printers sheets blotched with ink. Regular -ed participial morphology needs no comment. Pronunciation settles with affricate -tch.
  • 19th–20th century specialization (c. 1800–present): dermatology, botany, entomology, and printing each adopt blotched as a controlled descriptor distinct from spotted, streaked, and mottled. Natural-history writers favour it for post-mortem and camouflage patterns, as in pink-and-grey porpoise skin, because it refuses beauty while staying precise.

Phonetically the path runs from monosyllabic blot and botch through affricated blotch to disyllabic blotched with devoiced ending, each step adding texture to sound as to sense. Semantically it runs from ink versus pus to fused blemish to diagnostic patch, a small triumph of expressive blending over learned derivation, honestly labelled as blend rather than inheritance.
