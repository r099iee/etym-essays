Uncertain deep stage: the Latin base menda / mendum "fault, defect, blemish" has no fully secure Indo-European derivation; handbooks often reconstruct a PIE root *mend- "physical defect, blemish" and cite Sanskrit mindā "blemish" and Greek míndos "dirt, filth" as cognates, but the exact root shape and its wider connections remain debated. [DEBATED — treat the pre-Latin stage as reconstructed with caution; some specialists view menda as an Italic innovation.] → Latin menda (fault, defect, blemish), attested from the 1st century BCE → Latin ēmendāre (to free from fault, correct, improve), formed with ex- "out of" + menda, attested from the 1st century BCE → Old French amender (to correct, improve, repair), attested from the 9th–11th centuries CE → Anglo-Norman / Middle English amenden (to correct, improve, repair), attested from the 13th century CE → Middle English menden (aphetic short form of amenden — loss of the unstressed initial a-), attested from the 13th–14th centuries CE → Modern English MEND — to repair, restore, heal, improve. NOTE: mend and amend are doublets — the same Latin ancestor in two English shapes after medieval aphesis; amend preserved the fuller form and specialized in abstract correction, while the short mend kept the concrete repair sense. The later learned borrowings emend (directly from ēmendāre) and mendacious (from Latin mendāx "lying," a different branch of the fault/falsehood complex) are separate words, not steps on the path of mend.
