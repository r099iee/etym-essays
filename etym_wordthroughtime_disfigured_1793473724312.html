- Late 14th c. — Theological and Chivalric Entry: Enters Middle English (via Old French desfigurer) in Chaucer, Gower, and Wycliffite texts, denoting both facial mutilation in battle and the spiritual disfiguring of God's image in man by sin.
- 15th–16th c. — Iconoclasm and Reformation Wars: Extensively applied to the defacement, beheading, and systematic disfiguring of Catholic statues, church altars, and royal tombs across England and Europe.
- 17th–18th c. — Historiographical and Epistemological Critique: Neoclassical historians (Hume, Robertson, Gibbon) adopt it to describe how unwritten historical traditions become grotesque, exaggerated, and disfigured when trusted to oral memory.
- 19th c. — Industrial Landscape and Clinical Pathology: Victorian social critics depict pastoral England as "disfigured" by coal mines, slag heaps, and factories, while medical texts document the ravages of smallpox and war wounds.
- 20th c. — Legal Codification and Reconstructive Surgery: Codified in criminal jurisprudence (defining permanent disfigurement in battery cases), while military conflicts spur the development of modern plastic and reconstructive surgery.
- Today — Architectural and Metaphorical Breadth: Widely used to condemn urban blight, environmental devastation, and corrupted digital or factual data, alongside its somber, dignified human bodily usage.
