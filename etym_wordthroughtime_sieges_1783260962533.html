- [Old French 'seat'] - In its current form since Middle English (from Old French sege, from Latin sedere 'to sit'), 'siege' first meant a seat or throne, then the sitting down of an army before a fortress - the investment of a town.
- [Epic military sense] - Through medieval and early-modern warfare it became the classic word for surrounding a fortified place, cutting its supplies and compelling surrender - the great set-piece of military history.
- [Metaphorical extension] - From the 17th century it extended figuratively: a 'siege' of illness, a city under 'siege' of troubles - pressure applied from all sides.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'sieges' are both historical operations and modern figurative or symbolic ones (a building under siege), the epic resonance of the old sieges intact.
