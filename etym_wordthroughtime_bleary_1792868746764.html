- c. 1300 — dim and rheumy: Middle English blere describes weak watery eyes, an infirmity of age and illness, pitied rather than laughed at.
- 14th–16th c. — blear-eyed beggars and crones: the adjective peoples sermons and ballads with the dim-sighted old, and blear also verbs, to dim or to cheat the eye.
- 17th–18th c. — bleary softens it: the new longer form spreads from eyes to looks, and drink joins age as a cause, the tavern's stare joining the crone's.
- 19th c. — the morning after: Dickens and the Victorians make bleary the adjective of late nights, long watches, and early trains, comic and urban.
- 20th c. — airports and night shifts: bleary-eyed travellers becomes a headline cliché, and the word migrates from faces to the dawns and photographs they see.
- Today — affectionate exhaustion: bleary forgives, whether the cause is babies, deadlines, revelry, or grief, and a rather bleary Sam is everybody's morning self.
