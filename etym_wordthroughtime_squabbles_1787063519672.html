- - c. 1600: both noun and verb appear in English at once, meaning a petty noisy quarrel and to bicker respectively; the origin is imitative or Scandinavian, and the word is from its first record comic and dismissive.
- - 1600s–1700s: settles into the language as the everyday word for trivial family and street disputes; the connotation is childishness and noise rather than harm.
- - 1700s–1800s: becomes a staple of humorous and satirical writing; essayists and novelists use "squabbles" for the ridiculous petty conflicts of neighbours, politicians, and clergymen.
- - 1800s–1900s: extends to birds and animals fighting over food; journalism adopts the word for factional political conflicts, marking them as unserious.
- - 1900s–2000s: retains its stable, mildly amused tone; in media and everyday speech "squabbles" are still small, noisy, and beneath real concern — never life-or-death.
- - Today: unchanged in essentials — the word has barely shifted in four centuries, because the thing it names (noisy, trivial contention) is itself changeless and faintly ridiculous.
