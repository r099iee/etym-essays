Chain — thrall 1. Proto-Indo-European *traǵʰ- (also written *tragʰ- or *tragh-) "to run, to drag, to trail" (Pokorny; the same root is often compared with Lithuanian trākšti "to crackle" and, loosely, with Greek tréchein "to run", though the Greek link is uncertain). 2. Proto-Germanic *þraχilaz "a runner, one who runs errands; a servant" — the traditional reconstruction of the source noun (OED notes the word probably represents a Germanic formation in -ila built on the "run" root; the form is not directly attested and the details are reconstructed). 3. Old Norse þræll "an unfree person, a slave, a bondsman" (plural þrælar; attested in saga literature and in runic inscriptions; Viking Age, 9th–13th c.). 4. Late Old English þræl / þrǣl "a slave, a bondsman" — a borrowing from Old Norse in the Danelaw (attested c. 1000, e.g. in Ælfric's works). 5. Middle English thral / thrall "a slave, a bondman, a serf" — also as an adjective "enslaved, captive" (12th–15th c.). 6. Modern English thrall "the state of being in bondage or under a dominating influence" (noun); the person-sense "slave" became archaic, while the state-sense in thrall (to) has dominated since the 16th century. THRALL. Debated / nuanced note: the etymology is not certain. The traditional link from *traǵʰ- "to run" through "one who runs errands" is accepted by many handbooks but has been questioned: the noun is attested only in North Germanic, and its derivational base is inferred. Some scholars suggest instead a connection with a Germanic stem meaning "to press, oppress" (cf. Old English þrēat "a throng, a menace"), while others leave the word as of uncertain origin. The Old High German drahsil "a turner (lathe-worker)" is often cited as a cognate showing the regular þ-/d- correspondence, but its sense is far removed, and the comparison rests on the root alone.
