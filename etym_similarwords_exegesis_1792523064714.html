• hermeneutics: Similar because both deal with the theory and practice of textual interpretation; differs because hermeneutics refers to the overarching philosophical methodology, epistemological principles, and theory of interpretation itself, whereas exegesis is the practical, specific act of close, verse-by-verse analytical commentary on a given text.
  • exposition: Similar because both involve setting forth and explaining the meaning of an idea or text; differs because exposition is a broad rhetorical term for clear descriptive explanation or presentation of information in general writing, whereas exegesis implies deep philological, historical, and textual interrogation.
  • explication: Similar because both involve detailed line-by-line textual analysis; differs because explication (as in French explication de texte) focuses primarily on unfolding literary style, poetic devices, and structural irony, whereas exegesis focuses on linguistic, historical, and doctrinal authority.
  • commentary: Similar because both provide explanatory notes on classical or sacred writings; differs because a commentary is a continuous book-length compilation of observational notes, reflections, or marginalia, whereas exegesis is the rigorous analytical discipline and interpretive process itself.
  • annotation: Similar because both provide supplementary explanatory information for difficult passages; differs because annotation consists of brief, localized footnotes or marginal glosses clarifying obscure names or terms, whereas exegesis provides a comprehensive, systematic interpretive synthesis.
  • interpretation: Similar because both involve explaining the significance or meaning of human expression; differs because interpretation is a general, everyday cognitive term that can be subjective or creative (as in an actor's interpretation of a role), whereas exegesis adheres to rigorous philological and historical standards.
  • textual criticism: Similar because both are foundational scholarly disciplines in manuscript studies; differs because textual criticism (lower criticism) seeks to reconstruct the original, uncorrupted wording of an ancient text from surviving variant manuscripts, whereas exegesis interprets what that established text actually means.
  • gloss: Similar because both clarify ancient or difficult phraseology; differs because a gloss is a short translation or brief synonym written between the lines or in the margin of a manuscript, whereas exegesis is an exhaustive critical analysis.
  • decipherment: Similar because both unlock hidden or difficult textual meaning; differs because decipherment solves cryptographic ciphers or translates unknown scripts and forgotten writing systems (e.g., Egyptian hieroglyphs), whereas exegesis analyzes known linguistic texts for their theological or philosophical depth.
  • eisegesis: Directly contrastive and antonymic; differs because eisegesis (from Greek eis-, 'into') is the intellectually flawed practice of projecting one's own subjective preconceptions, biases, and modern dogmas into a text, whereas exegesis (from ex-, 'out of') draws the author's original intended meaning out from the text itself.
