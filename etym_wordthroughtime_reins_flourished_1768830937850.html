reins:
- The rider's paired straps (14th-15th c.) - the leather straps held in the hands to guide, check, stop, and control a horse; the plural is idiomatic, the matched pair conceived as one instrument of command
- The earliest political metaphor (15th c.) - almost as soon as the word is English, ruling is conceived as holding 'the reins of government' and 'the reins of the empire'; power is imagined as something taut and held in the hands
- Idiomatic consolidation (17th-19th c.) - take the reins, hand over the reins, a free rein, to rein in: the word becomes the standard figure for command by direct, hand-held restraint, transferred from equestrian control to political, military, and managerial authority
- Contemporary - in daily speech the word now lives chiefly in its figurative uses; the literal sense belongs to riding, where it still names the precise, direct, handheld link between human intention and equine motion

flourished:
- Blossoming and thriving (13th-14th c.) - the verb enters English meaning to bloom, prosper, and thrive, of plants and fortunes alike, carrying the image of healthy growth
- Ornament and display (16th c.) - 'a flourish' becomes an ornamental stroke of penmanship or music, and the verb gains the sense of embellishing and showing off, parading one's wealth or skill
- The martial extension (16th-17th c.) - a sword is flourished: swept in a wide, showy, theatrical arc, a gesture of display, intimidation, and self-assertion before the blow is ever struck
- Contemporary - the word leads two parallel lives, 'to thrive' and 'to wave showily', fused by the shared image of something blooming outward from within - a sword 'flowering' in the hand, at once decorative and deadly
