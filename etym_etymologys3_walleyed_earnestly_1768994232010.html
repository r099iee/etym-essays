WALLEYED — the biography of a beam in the eye.
Old Norse vagl first meant a roost — a pole slung so that it could sway or rock, on which fowl perched — and by extension any projecting beam or ridge. The word belongs to the same family as Old Norse vega "to move, weigh" and vagga "to rock (a cradle)," whose ultimate ancestor is the PIE root *weǵʰ- "to move, carry," the same root that yields English weigh, way, and wagon, Latin vehere (whence vehicle and vector), Sanskrit váhati "he carries," and Greek ókhos "carriage." The sense "a speck, cloud, or opacity in the eye" is a metaphorical transfer: the little pale flaw in the iris was imagined as a "beam" lodged in the eye — an image that survives in the Biblical language of the mote and the beam (Matthew 7:3–5) and, etymologically, in walleyed itself.

The Old Norse compound vagl-eygr "having a beam in the eye" was built with eygr, the adjectival "-eyed" derived from auga "eye" (PIE *h₃ekʷ- "to see"). English borrowed the compound in the Middle English period (attested by c. 1300), at first chiefly as a veterinary term. In medieval and early-modern usage walleyed was applied most often to horses with a pale, bluish or whitish iris — the "wall-eye" that modern veterinary practice calls a blue or glass eye — and to the wandering, sidelong glance such an eye produces. From the animal the adjective moved to the person: a "walleyed" look became a glassy, startled, or askance stare, and the ophthalmological sense of an eye whose pupil and iris turn outward (exotropia) grew out of the older "whitish, staring, divergent" picture. By the nineteenth century the great North American game fish the walleye (Sander vitreus) was named for its large, luminous, sideways-glancing eyes, fixing the word in angling vocabulary and turning the adjective into a noun.

Across the twentieth century the word split into two registers: the clinical (exotropia has largely displaced it in medicine) and the literary-pictorial, where a "walleyed" glance still carries a strange, slightly comedic, slightly unsettling charge. In this passage the two senses — "opaque, cloudy, staring" and "turned outward" — still jostle in the dictionaries, and both inform the portrait: a man whose gaze is slightly skew, whose eye does not quite line up with his attention. What makes the detail memorable is its pairing with earnestly: the crooked eye and the straight intention are set against each other, so that walleyed becomes a note of strange physicality and earnestly the sign of the man's true, unwavering purpose.

  • The root family: *weǵʰ- "to move, carry" — Sanskrit váhati, Latin vehere, Greek ókhos, English weigh, way, wagon, vehicle, vector, wain.
  • The semantic bridge: a roost that sways (vagl) → any projecting beam → a speck in the eye.
  • The Biblical resonance: the mote and the beam as the ancient image of a "timber" in the eye.
  • The path into English: Old Norse vagl-eygr → Middle English walleiȝed (c. 1300) → walleyed, first of horses.
  • Modern technical use: exotropia in medicine; the walleye fish (Sander vitreus) in ichthyology and sport fishing.


EARNESTLY — the biography of a serious heart.
Earnest reaches English through a purely Germanic line. The Proto-Germanic adjective-noun *ernustuz "seriousness, zeal" appears in the earliest Germanic languages: Old High German ernust, German Ernst (still today a name and a common noun), Gothic *ernusts, and Old English eornost, eornuste "earnest, serious, zealous." Beyond Germanic the trail grows faint, and this is the one genuinely debated step in the word's history: many scholars connect the Germanic base to the PIE root *h₁er- "to set in motion, to stir, to rouse" (Latin orior "to arise," Greek órnumi "to rouse," Sanskrit ṛṇóti), imagining that "energy, effort, exertion" shaded into "seriousness of purpose"; others, including the editors of the OED, keep the origin open, recording the further etymology as uncertain. What is not in doubt is the semantic direction the word took once it existed: from zeal and determination to gravity, sincerity, and freedom from jest — "in earnest" meaning "in reality, not as a joke," the adverb earnestly meaning "with serious and sincere intention."

In Old English the adverb eornostlīce was already formed by the productive adverbial suffix -līċe, "in the body, manner, or form of," which descends from līċ "body, form" and, beyond it, from the PIE root *leyg- "to bind": the same root that gives Latin ligare "to bind" and English like, so that to do a thing "like" something is etymologically to do it "in the form of" it, and to do it earnestly is to do it in the very body of earnestness. By Middle English (attested by c. 1200 as ernestliche) the word was fully current, and the suffix contracted from -liche to -li to the modern -ly.

The cultural history of the word is as striking as its form. Earnest carried religious weight in the Middle Ages — God's "earnest" sincerity toward humanity, the "earnest" of a covenant — and then became a secular ideal of character. In the nineteenth century earnestness was the signature virtue of the Victorian middle classes: the quality of Carlyle's seriousness, of Ruskin's moral art, of Arnold's high seriousness, the conviction that duty, work, faith, and friendship must be undertaken with the whole soul. Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest (1895) set the whole ideal gently spinning by punning the virtue against the name Ernest, and the comedy's subtitle — "A Trivial Comedy for Serious People" — played on the same axis. In contemporary English the adverb is quieter but still weighty: one earnestly hopes, earnestly pleads, earnestly recommends, always with the old undertone of heartfelt gravity rather than mere politeness.

In this passage — "watching the kid earnestly as if he'd know his opinion about the rain" — the word carries that whole inheritance: serious, sincere, intent, the straight core of the man against whose slightly crooked eye the sentence sets it.

  • The Germanic line: *ernustuz → Old English eornost → Middle English ernest → earnest.
  • The debated root: the probable but uncertain link to PIE *h₁er- "to rouse"; the OED records the further origin as doubtful.
  • The -ly suffix: PIE *leyg- "to bind" → *līką "body, form" → Old English līċ → -līċe → -ly.
  • The cultural arc: medieval religious "earnest" → Victorian earnestness → Wilde's parody → the modern adverb of heartfelt seriousness.
  • The homonym trap: "earnest (money)" from Latin arrha is a different, unrelated word.
