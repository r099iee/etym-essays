Cannes is a toponym whose evolution condenses two thousand years of Mediterranean contact: Ligurian fishermen, Roman surveyors, Provençal monks and English aristocrats. The conventional etymology, repeated by ProvenceWeb, Cannes Estate, Wikipedia and the New World Encyclopedia, derives the name from Latin canna "reed, cane". In the 10th century the town was known as Canua; in 1030 a deed of donation by Rodoard's son to the Abbot of Lérins mentions De Portu Canue "of the port of Canue". The inference is topographic: the present Croisette and Rue d'Antibes were once swamps fringed with the tall canne de Provence (Arundo donax), so the settlement was the reed-place.

Latin canna itself has a deeper history. It is a loan from Greek kanna (kánna) "reed, cane, pipe", which in turn is a Semitic loan, cognate with Akkadian qanû "reed", Hebrew qaneh, ultimately from Sumerian gi-na. In Latin canna meant hollow reed, cane flute, water-pipe and by extension a small reed boat. Phonetics are simple: /ˈkanna/ with geminate -nn-, preserved in French spelling -nn- though modern French pronounces [kan] with silent -es. Occitan retains Canas [ˈkanɔs], showing the old plural / locative -as. The shift Canua → Canue → Canas → Cannes reflects medieval Latin case variation (-a nominative, -e ablative after portu) regularised into French.

Archaeology supports continuity: Canua was probably a small Ligurian port, then a Roman outpost on Le Suquet hill, where Roman tombs have been found. Le Suquet housed an 11th-century tower overlooking swamps; most early protection lay on the Lérins Islands, and Cannes history is tied to the abbey of Saint-Honorat. For centuries it remained a fishing village under Lerinese monks and Provençal counts.

The debated alternative must be stated. Theo Vennemann's Vasconic / Old European work notes a toponymical root kan- in Cannes, Le Cannet-du-Luc, Cannet, Chanousse and Italian Canossa, explained via Basque gain, gan, gañ "height, summit", referring to the hill on which Le Suquet stands with its watchtower. In this view Canua would contain Ligurian kan- "height" rather than canna "reed", and Canue would reflect the same. Local tradition sometimes combines both: reeds below, height above. Scholarship treats the reed derivation as conventional and the height derivation as interesting but unproven; both agree the root is pre-French Mediterranean, not Germanic.

Modern evolution is sociolinguistic. Until Lord Brougham wintered there in 1834, Cannes was obscure. Brougham built the Villa Eleonore, English aristocracy followed, and Cannes became a winter resort: promenades, palaces, the Croisette. The 20th century added the Cannes Film Festival (founded 1946, first held 1946-47), the Lions advertising festival, and MIPCOM. Pronunciation split: French [kan] (locally [ˈkanə]), English /kæn/ or /kɑːn/ (KA(H)N), Occitan [ˈkanɔs]. Eponymy inverted: the game baccarat crystal comes from the Lorraine town Baccarat, not this Cannes, but Wodehouse links them in one Riviera sentence — white mess jacket, shark (sponger), baccarat table — fixing Cannes in English comic imagination as the place where money, marriage and mischief meet.
