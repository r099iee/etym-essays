Primacy is the abstract noun for being first — first in rank, importance, time, or influence. It names not merely priority in a queue but structural dominance: the condition of coming before all others in a way that shapes what follows. The “primacy of practical purpose” in the source sentence is the doctrine that usefulness comes first and explains everything else — the view the essay then complicates with status and play.

Morphologically prim-acy joins Latin primus “first” with the abstract-noun suffix -acy (as in supremacy, legitimacy, privacy), denoting state, quality, or office. Stress falls on the first syllable: /ˈpraɪməsi/.

  • Rank / hierarchical sense (most common): supreme standing — “the primacy of the premier league,” “to challenge American primacy,” “the primacy of the eldest son.” Implies a contestable order in which one party sits atop; near-synonyms supremacy, hegemony, dominance differ in shading (see below).
  • Importance / explanatory sense: being treated as first in reasoning — “the primacy of profit,” “the primacy of the text,” “the primacy of practical purpose.” Here primacy justifies method: start here, subordinate the rest.
  • Temporal / causal sense: coming first in time or as a cause — “the primacy of early childhood,” “the primacy of the spring migration.” Less common, often recast as priority or precedence.
  • Ecclesiastical / institutional sense: the office and authority of a primate — the Primacy of Canterbury, the primacy of Rome — i.e. jurisdiction of an archbishop over a province. This is the oldest English use (14th century) and survives in church history and canon law.
  • Geopolitical / scholarly compounds: dollar primacy, English primacy, primacy effect (psychology: first items remembered best). Each transfers “firstness” into a domain: currency hierarchy, linguistic dominance, cognitive serial position.

Semantic bridges: all senses share the image of a primus — the first tablet, the first son, the first bishop — whose anteriority confers authority. From church office the word generalized in the 17th–19th centuries to states, ideas, and markets: what Rome was to bishops, reason is to passions, or manufacturing to culture. The suffix -acy matters: unlike priority (a slot in a queue) or precedence (a rule of ordering), primacy names a durable condition or doctrine — something defended, asserted, lost, or restored.

Connotation is weighty and contestable: to claim primacy is to invite challenge. Collocations — assert/claim/retain/lose primacy; primacy of place, of principle, of Parliament — stage political and intellectual struggle. Register is formal-academic; it belongs to essays, editorials, and treaties rather than chat. Antonyms include subordination, marginality, eclipse, irrelevance. In the source sentence the grandeur is deliberate and ironic: the auto industry as “great monument” to a doctrine of usefulness — a monument about to be shown to honor vanity and play as well. Mastering primacy thus means hearing both its trumpet (firstness proudly proclaimed) and its question-mark (first by whose measure, and for how long).
