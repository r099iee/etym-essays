In the phrase "They are meter writ large", the word writ is the archaic, poetic past participle of the verb to write, meaning "written" or "inscribed". It is not the modern word write in some new clothing; it is an old form of the word itself, preserved from Middle English by the accident of fixed phrases and by the deliberate archaism of poets and of the King James Bible. Within the idiom writ large the sense is "written out in large characters", hence "made plainly visible, magnified, manifested on a grand scale": the sentence says that the lines present metre in an enlarged, unmistakable form — the metre is not merely described but set down, inscribed, proclaimed big enough to be read at a glance. The tone is formal, solemn, and literary, with a distinctly biblical or legal resonance, since writ as a participle now survives almost exclusively in a handful of fixed expressions where it carries permanence, gravity, and elevation.

The word's double life is the key to its meaning. As a participle, writ means "written" and survives in the idioms writ large ("manifested on a grand scale"), writ in stone ("fixed, unalterable"), and the biblical writ on the wall (from the story of Belshazzar's feast). As a noun, writ is a standard legal term for a formal court order — a writ of habeas corpus, a writ of summons — a word that descends from the Old English noun (ge)writ, "a writing, a document". The two careers are the same fossil split in two directions: the old participle kept one half of the word's life in poetry and idiom, and the old noun kept the other half in the law courts. In the present sentence it is the participle sense that is in play — writ large — and it is that elevated, inscriptive sense that gives the phrase its solemn flourish.

  • Sense 1 — the archaic participle: "written", "inscribed", "composed"; used now only in poetic, biblical, or deliberately archaic diction and in fixed idioms. In writ large it means "written out in large characters", hence "manifested, magnified, epitomized" — the sentence's meaning.
  • Sense 2 — the legal noun: "a formal written command or order issued by a court or legal authority" — writ of habeas corpus, writ of mandamus, writ of certiorari, writ of execution. This is a fully living, technical, professional sense, the same word-form serving the law courts.
  • Sense 3 — historical-legal instruments: in medieval English law, writ specifically names a royal or chancery writ, the document that set a legal action in motion (the writs of entry, the writ system of the common law, "writ of right", "writ of assize"); legal historians speak of the "forms of action" as the great writ system.
  • Sense 4 — the idiom writ large: "written in large characters", hence "clearly visible, magnified, manifested on a grand or unmistakable scale" — a city as "a national crisis writ large", a character as "his flaws writ large". This is the dominant modern use of the participle.
  • Sense 5 — the idioms writ in stone and writ on the wall: writ in stone means "fixed, unalterable, permanent" (the rules were not writ in stone); writ on the wall, from Daniel 5 and Belshazzar's feast, means "an evident sign of coming disaster" (the writing on the wall).
  • Historical bridge for the secondary senses: all the senses share the root idea of something set down in writing. The participle preserved the simple meaning "written"; the noun narrowed from "a writing, a document" to "a formal written order"; the idioms extended "written out large" into "manifested on a grand scale". The bridge from "written" to "magnified" is the image of enlarged letters — what is written out big enough to be seen from afar is made manifest to all.

Why writ and not written. The choice of writ is a choice of register. Written is the neutral modern participle; writ is deliberately archaic, poetic, and ceremonial, lending the phrase elevation and gravity. In writ large the archaism is functional: it makes the metre sound inscribed and proclaimed rather than merely described, and it carries the biblical and legal weight that has made writ large one of English's most resonant fixed phrases.
