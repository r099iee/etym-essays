INLAY is both a noun and a verb. As a verb, "to inlay" means to set a piece of one material into a prepared cavity or bed cut in the surface of another, so that the inserted piece lies flush with the surrounding surface; as a noun, "inlay" denotes either the inserted material itself (gold inlay, mother-of-pearl inlay) or the ornamental work so produced. The word is transparently built from "in" + "lay" — to cause something to lie within something else. In the passage from David Hawkes's translation of Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng (The Story of the Stone), the noun does its quiet work in a connoisseur's inventory: "The ding was flanked on one side by a smaller antique bronze vessel with a pattern of gold inlay and on the other by a crystal bowl." The tone is precise, hushed, and technical — the narrator's eye moves from the great ritual tripod to its two companions, measuring each by its material and its workmanship. Inlay here is the craft term for gold set into bronze so that it lies flush with the surface, forming a pattern that cannot be removed without destroying the object. It signals the highest level of metalwork — the permanent marriage of two metals — and for Dai-yu, already reeling from the sumptuousness of the hall, it is one more quiet index of the family's wealth: decorative art at the level where the decoration and the object have become one thing. A gold inlay is wealth that has been fused into the very body of the bronze, and the sentence lets that immovable luxury register without comment, in the flat, accurate tone of a man who has seen such things all his life.

The senses of INLAY, primary and secondary, are these:
  • Verb, craft sense: to set pieces of a material into a surface so that they are flush with it — "to inlay gold into ivory," "to inlay a box with mother-of-pearl"; the past and participle form is "inlaid."
  • Noun, material sense: the inserted material itself — "a pattern of gold inlay," "a table with ivory inlay."
  • Noun, ornamental sense: the decorative work or technique as a whole — "the art of inlay," "fine inlay work."
  • Noun, dentistry: a tooth filling cast to fit a prepared cavity and then cemented in — "a gold inlay," "a porcelain inlay" — a modern technical extension of the verb's "set into a cavity" idea.
  • Noun, bookbinding and marquetry: inlay in wood veneers, leather bindings and paper, where pieces are set flush into a cut-out bed.
  • Figurative, literary: anything set deeply into something else so as to be part of it — "inlay of memory," "golden inlay of light" — a rare, poetic extension of the physical craft.

The semantic bridge from the craft to the modern senses is the idea of flush embedding. In every use — metalwork, woodwork, dentistry, bookbinding — the defining feature is the same: the inlaid piece is cut to fit a prepared bed and lies level with the surface, so that decoration and object become one thing, and removing the inlay damages the object. It is this permanence that makes the word so apt for the antique bronze in the Hong lou meng sentence: the gold is not applied to the vessel but fused into it, and the vessel's value lies partly in that marriage. The dentistry sense extends the same idea to the human tooth, where a prepared cavity receives a cast filling set flush with the enamel — an inlay in the mouth as well as in the metal.

Register boundaries against the craft near-synonyms sharpen the word:
  • "Marquetry" is the veneering of wood with thin pieces of wood forming patterns, used of furniture, and never of metal; an inlay may be of ivory, mother-of-pearl, gold or silver set into wood, metal or stone, while marquetry is restricted to wood on wood.
  • "Intarsia" is the Italian art of inlaying wood in a mosaic manner; it shares the inlay technique but stays within furniture and panel-work.
  • "Mosaic" covers a surface with many small cubes or pieces (tesserae) of glass, stone or tile; it builds a picture from a field of fragments rather than setting one piece into a prepared cavity, and it is a surface-covering art, not a flush embedding.
  • "Damascening" is the very close cousin — gold or silver hammered into steel to make the patterns of the famed Damascus blades — but it is a metal-on-metal specialisation, whereas inlay spans wood, stone, ivory, shell and metal alike.

In short, INLAY is the craft word for the permanent marriage of two materials: ornament embedded so deeply that object and decoration become one, and so prized that even a serving of it in a connoisseur's inventory registers the weight of a family's wealth.
