- Late 16th c. – Toponymic association: Connected strictly with Blarney Castle and the notoriously evasive, polite stalling tactics of Lord Cormac MacCarthy toward Queen Elizabeth I's emissaries.
- Late 18th c. – Folklore and local idiom: Spread across Ireland to denote silver-tongued conversational agility and flattering eloquence acquired by kissing the Blarney Stone.
- Early 19th c. – Romantic and stage-Irish popularization: Adopted into broader British literature and theater as the archetypal charm of the Irish raconteur, admired for its witty harmlessness.
- Mid-to-late 19th c. – Transatlantic immigrant idiom: Carried across the Atlantic by Irish immigrants, entering American urban vocabulary as a designation for persuasive salesmanship, political rhetoric, and amicable deception.
- 20th c. to Present – Universal colloquial metaphor: Broadened globally to describe any persuasive, flattering, or euphemistic banter, often positioned between harmless social lubricant and dismissible political hot air.
