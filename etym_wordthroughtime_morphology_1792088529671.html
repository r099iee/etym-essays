- 1790s (1796) — Coined in German as Morphologie by polymath Johann Wolfgang von Goethe to establish a new comparative biological science studying organic forms and metamorphosis in plants and anatomy.
- 1830s — Borrowed into English scientific prose, exclusively designating comparative biological anatomy and the physical structure of organisms.
- 1859 — Transferred into linguistics by German comparative philologist August Schleicher (Die Deutsche Sprache), who applied evolutionary biological concepts to the study of word structure and inflectional paradigms.
- Late 19th c. — Established across European and American universities as a standard core subdiscipline of linguistic science alongside phonology and syntax.
- Mid-20th c. — Formalized within American structural linguistics (Leonard Bloomfield, Charles Hockett) through morphemic analysis, item-and-arrangement, and item-and-process models.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Expanded across generative grammar (Distributed Morphology), natural language processing (morphological tokenizers, lemmatizers), and natural sciences (geomorphology, materials science, galaxy morphology).
