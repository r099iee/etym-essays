- Old English ballistic image — 'bolt' names the heavy, straight-flying crossbow arrow; from this the verb is born: to spring off suddenly and straight, like a released missile.
- 16th–17th century — 'to bolt' hardens as a verb of sudden, violent departure: a bolt of lightning, to bolt a door (bar it shut with a bolt), a horse that 'bolts' converting terror into one headlong flight.
- 18th–19th century — the panic sense settles: 'bolt' is used of animals, then of people ('he bolted for the door') and even of vehicles; it fuses suddenness, fear-triggered ignition, and uncontrollable speed.
- Contemporary English — 'bolted' still names an irrevocable breakaway at full speed, distinct from shying, galloping, or fleeing; alongside it the same verb lives on in 'to bolt one's food' — to swallow hurriedly, as if launched down the throat.
