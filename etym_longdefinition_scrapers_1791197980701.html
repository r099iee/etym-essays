Scrapers is the plural of scraper: any tool, implement, device, or person that scrapes - that is, drags a hard edge across a surface to remove adherent material, smooth it, shape it, or collect what is removed.

In its oldest and most concrete sense, relevant to the source passage, a scraper is a prehistoric stone tool: a flake of flint, chert, or obsidian retouched to a steep, sturdy edge for cleaning hides, scaling wood, scraping fat from skins, or shaping bone and antler. Archaeologists classify end-scrapers, side-scrapers, thumbnail scrapers, and hump-backed scrapers. These were among the first specialised tools after hand-axes, and the plural in "hammers, anvils, kettles, scrapers, arrow-heads" evokes a museum drawer of such lithics.

  • Hand-tool senses: Paint scraper (flat steel blade for stripping paint), cabinet scraper (thin rectangular steel plate for smoothing wood without sandpaper), dough or bench scraper (wide metal or plastic rectangle for bakers and chefs), ice scraper (plastic edge for car windscreens), razor scraper (holder for single-edge blades for glass and tiles), hide scraper, boiler scraper, road scraper. Each shares the perpendicular dragging action.
  • Heavy-equipment sense: An earth-moving motor scraper - a large machine with a central open bowl that lowers its cutting edge, scrapes up soil while moving, hauls it, and spreads it. Fresno scrapers, elevating scrapers, and towed scrapers built roads, canals, and airfields.
  • Human-agent senses: A person who scrapes (a ship's hull scraper, a tapestry scraper), a stingy person who scrapes by (a penny-scraper), a sycophant who scrapes and bows (a leg-scraper), or a poor performer who scrapes an instrument (a fiddle-scraper). These carry mildly contemptuous tone.
  • Digital / modern senses: A web scraper or screen scraper - software that automatically extracts data from websites or screens. Data-scraper startups, price scrapers, and content scrapers inhabit debates about copyright and robots.txt. The metaphor is exact: dragging a tool across a surface to gather residue.

Domain nuances matter. In woodworking a scraper produces gossamer shavings and a burnished surface prized above sanded finish. In cookery a bench scraper is gentle and indispensable, not aggressive. In construction a scraper is brutal and massive. In computing it is invisible and legally fraught. What unites them is removal by abrasion rather than cutting through: scrapers skim, not slice.

Emotionally the word feels utilitarian, humble, and slightly harsh. Unlike elegant planes or mighty hammers, scrapers suggest tedious, repetitive cleaning work - necessary, unglamorous, hands-on. In plural it evokes a toolkit, an excavation tray, or a server farm of bots, multiplying that sense of modest, persistent labour.

Finally, learning scrapers repays attention to edge geometry: a 70-90 degree steep retouch for hide work versus a lower angle for wood shaving determines whether the tool skims or bites. Modern bushcrafters who replicate flint scrapers report the same discovery Palaeolithic workers made - that a dull, steep, well-hafted edge removes fat without cutting hide, while a sharp thin flake slices through. This is why museums display whole trays of morphologically similar but functionally distinct scrapers, and why the plural matters: no single scraper does all jobs, but a kit of them clothes, feeds, and shelters a band.
