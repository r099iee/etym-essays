“Mock” entered English meaning to deride or imitate — to make fun of, to counterfeit — and attaching it to a noun marks that thing as performed, feigned, or imitative.
“Mock-politeness” is one of the transparent mock- compounds that English freely builds: politeness worn as a mask, the forms of courtesy without the goodwill — a show of deference that is really irony.
“Mock-” compounds flourished in the 18th–19th centuries in satiric prose — mock-heroic, mock-serious, mock-modesty — a whole vocabulary for performed manners that are really a joke or a slight.
“Mock-politeness” today still names that theatrical courtesy: the title, the tone, the bow of phrase put on insincerely, as when a speaker “turns with mock-politeness” to someone they are really teasing or skewering.
