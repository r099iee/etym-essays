- Late Middle English (15th c.) “riverbed fishing gear” - originated as a literal term for weighted fishing nets dragged along river bottoms or coasts, prominently used in early English Bible translations.
- 16th–17th c. “theological and moral metaphor” - sermonizers and moralists regularly invoked Christ’s parable of the dragnet to represent God’s net gathering righteous and wicked alike for judgment.
- Mid-to-late 19th c. “police and detective sweeps” - with the emergence of Scotland Yard and modern urban police departments, detectives began using “drag-net” to describe sweeping neighborhoods to round up pickpockets and fugitives.
- Early 20th c. “literary and satirical adoption” - popular fiction writers (including Arthur Conan Doyle and P. G. Wodehouse) popularized the term as exciting detective jargon or comic hyperbole for searching estates.
- 1950s–1960s “broadcasting icon” - Jack Webb’s American radio and TV franchise *Dragnet* cemented the word globally as the ultimate symbol of gritty, unglamorous, methodical police procedure (“Just the facts, ma’am”).
- 21st c. “digital surveillance and marine ecology” - contemporary usage splits between legal/civil-liberties debates over bulk “digital dragnet” data surveillance and marine conservation campaigns against destructive bottom-trawling.
