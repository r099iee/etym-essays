Chain 1 — the verb (to complain):

Deeper origin UNKNOWN — no reliable PIE, Germanic, or Old French chain is documented.
The form grouse appears suddenly in British Army slang in the 1880s;
it is possibly echoic (imitative of low grumbling) or a variant of grouch,
whose own origin is also uncertain. The link to Old French groucier (source
of English grudge) is sometimes suggested but is NOT accepted — grouse
cannot be traced to groucier by regular sound change.

↓

British Army slang grouse / grousing

"to grumble, complain habitually about army life, rations,
weather, and officers"
(first recorded c. 1886, in soldiers' speech in India)

↓

General informal English grouse (verb and noun)

"to complain, mutter discontentedly over petty matters";
"a petty complaint"
(spread from the 1890s; popularized by Kipling's soldier stories)

↓

GROUSE

"to complain, grumble, mutter discontentedly over petty matters";
also noun "a petty complaint or grievance"
(Modern English; still informal)


Chain 2 — the homonymous bird noun (Tetraonidae), separate word:

Origin UNCERTAIN — first attested 1530s; possibly from Old French
grue "crane" or from a Celtic word (e.g. Cornish/Welsh forms),
with the modern spelling settling by the 1600s. No PIE chain exists.

↓

Early Modern English grouse (also grows, growse, grouss)

"the red grouse, moor fowl, game bird of the uplands"
(1530s–1600s, British Isles)

↓

Modern English GROUSE (bird)

"any plump game bird of the family Tetraonidae"
(the bird word is entirely unrelated to the verb grouse;
they are homonyms only)
