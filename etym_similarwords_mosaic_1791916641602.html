• Mosaical: The older variant of the same adjective, common in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; similar in meaning exactly; differs only in form and flavour — it now sounds archaic and is largely confined to reprints of old divinity.
  • Pentateuchal: Similar because it describes the same five books and their legislation; differs in referring to the written corpus rather than to the person, and in belonging to source criticism rather than to theology (“the Pentateuchal sources”).
  • Torahic: Similar as a label for the law of the Pentateuch; differs in its Jewish frame of reference, where Torah means instruction rather than a code imposed by a legislator.
  • Levitical: Similar in describing the priestly and ritual material of the law; differs in scope — it refers specifically to Leviticus and the priesthood, not to Moses or the whole corpus.
  • biblical: Similar as a general adjective for scripture; differs in breadth, since it covers prophets, gospels and epistles, whereas Mosaic is restricted to Moses and the law.
  • Israelite: Similar as a description of the ancient religion and its institutions; differs in being ethnic and historical rather than textual, and in including periods and practices outside the Mosaic corpus.
  • mosaic (art): A homograph and homophone, identical in sound and nearly identical in spelling; differs completely in origin and meaning — it names a picture made of tesserae, from Latin musivum, “work of the Muses”, and has nothing to do with Moses.
  • tessellated: Similar to the art sense, describing a surface of small fitted pieces; differs in being purely technical and adjectival, with no noun for the picture itself and no figurative life.
  • inlaid: Similar as a description of decoration set into a surface; differs in technique — inlay inserts pieces into a recess, whereas mosaic lays them on the surface as the substance of the design.
  • patchwork: Similar to the figurative sense of mosaic, a thing made of heterogeneous parts; differs in its material origin (sewn cloth) and in its suggestion of randomness rather than deliberate composition.
