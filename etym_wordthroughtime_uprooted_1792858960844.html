- 1530s — a gardener’s verb: to pull plants up by the roots, a practical term of husbandry and land clearance, neutral and workmanlike.
- 17th–18th c. — moralised by preachers and poets: sin, heresy, and corruption are uprooted from the soul and the state, turning a field task into a figure of thorough reform.
- 19th c. — the human cost enters: industrialisation, emigration, and empire produce uprooted peasants and peoples, and the word takes on melancholy and pity.
- 20th c. — the century of refugees makes the figurative sense dominant: the uprooted become a recognised condition of war and decolonisation, studied by sociologists and mourned by novelists.
- Today — the inner life owns it most: anyone unmoored by grief, divorce, or a new city calls themselves uprooted, as in the source sentence, where the word names a small, desperate, dangling feeling rather than any literal plant.
