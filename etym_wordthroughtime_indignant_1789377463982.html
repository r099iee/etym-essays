- Late 16th Century (c. 1580s–1590s) - Borrowed into Late Elizabethan English directly from Latin *indignantem* or French *indignant*; initially used in scholarly and dramatic works to describe a noble person scorning that which is base, unworthy, or dishonorable.
- 17th Century (Jacobean & Civil War Eras) - Solidified in political, religious, and philosophical discourse (Milton, Hobbes); used to denote the justified fury of God, monarchs, or free citizens reacting against treason, blasphemy, and gross injustice.
- 18th Century (Augustan Age & Age of Sensibility) - Flourished in formal satire and epistolary literature (Jonathan Swift, Alexander Pope, Samuel Johnson); Jonathan Swift famously chose *saeva indignatio* ("savage indignation") for his epitaph, capturing the satirist's moral torment at human folly.
- 19th Century (Victorian Golden Age) - Ubiquitous in Victorian fiction and social critique (Charles Dickens, Oscar Wilde); described both noble champions fighting child labor and comical drawing-room figures whose ruffled pomposity was satirized (as in *The Remarkable Rocket*).
- 20th Century - Central to civil rights manifestos, wartime journalism, and democratic protest rhetoric; "indignant citizens" became the hallmark of grass-roots democratic accountability against bureaucratic or corporate overreach.
- 21st Century - Retains both its dignified moral-activist register in public discourse and a slightly ironic domestic register describing someone who is dramatically affronted by minor customer-service slights.
