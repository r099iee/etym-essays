FALTER is a verb meaning to hesitate, stumble, lose strength, or lose momentum, whether the subject is a person, a voice, a plan, a movement, a market, or an institution. At its physical core it denotes an unsteady, interrupted motion — a knee giving way, a foot catching, a speaker's words coming out broken — and by extension it describes any process whose smooth, continuous progress is suddenly interrupted, weakened, or made doubtful. The word is native English, built with the homely Germanic frequentative suffix -er, and its whole history is the story of a physical stumble becoming the standard metaphor for every kind of weakening and wavering.

1. Physical senses — losing one's footing or bodily strength. The oldest and most concrete sense of falter is "to move unsteadily, to totter, to stagger or stumble in walking or in gait." Someone who falters while climbing a stair, crossing a room, or carrying a heavy load momentarily loses balance or strength; the legs give way beneath them. Closely related is the sense "to lose bodily strength or vigour, to become weak and unsteady," so that a runner, an animal, or an aged person may be said to falter as their strength drains away. This is the sense at work when a racehorse "falters on the final straight" or when a climber's grip "falters" on a hold. The image is always of a body that should be steady suddenly folding, tripping, or giving way.

2. Speech senses — stammering, cracking, or breaking off. From the fifteenth century onward falter has also meant "to speak in a hesitating, trembling, or broken way — to stammer, to have the voice crack, to trail off." A voice that "falters with emotion" breaks because grief, fear, joy, or exhaustion disrupts the smooth production of sound; a speaker who "falters over their words" cannot produce a fluent sentence. This is the most emotive and literary sense, and it is heavily used in fiction and memoir when a character is overcome. The phrase "his voice faltered" is a stock device for a character on the verge of tears, and the card's own example ("Her voice began to FALTER as she spoke") uses it exactly so.

3. Mental and moral senses — hesitation, wavering resolve, loss of courage. Because stumbling legs and breaking voices both suggest a failure of steadiness, falter extended to the mind and the will: to falter in one's purpose, resolve, faith, or courage is to hesitate, waver, or shrink from continuing. A person "falters in his allegiance," "falters in his determination," or "does not falter in the face of danger." Here the word takes on strongly moral and heroic overtones: to "not falter" is a mark of steadfastness, loyalty, and courage. This is the dominant sense in the card's example ("The army did not FALTER despite the intense cold") and in the reading context ("We will not let the queen ..."). The negative construction is the most famous use of the word in the whole language: "we must not falter."

4. Momentum and process senses — economic, political, and athletic slowdown. In modern usage falter is routinely applied to impersonal forces and processes that lose momentum: an economy "falters" when growth slows or stalls; a company's share price "falters"; a political campaign "falters" when it loses support; a sports team "falters" when it lets its lead slip; a process or plan "falters" when it hits an unexpected obstacle. In all of these the core image is the same — something that had been moving steadily forward has suddenly stumbled, and its forward motion is now uncertain or interrupted. The twentieth-century expansion of falter into economic and financial journalism is one of the most striking developments in the modern life of the word.

5. Transitive and rarer uses. The word has a rare transitive sense, "to say something falteringly, to utter in a broken or hesitating manner" (e.g. "he faltered an apology"), and a rare noun use, "a faltering, a moment of hesitation or unsteadiness." In dialect and older usage one may also find it with the sense "to flap, flutter, or move with a wavering motion" (of wings, flags, or light), which preserves the original image of trembling, unsteady movement.

How the senses hang together (the semantic bridge). All the meanings of falter radiate from a single physical picture: a body or object that should be steady and continuous instead trembles, folds, trips, or breaks. That picture is then transferred by metaphor to the voice, to the will, to courage, and to whole institutions and markets. The chain of metaphor is so natural — a faltering knee, a faltering voice, a faltering resolve, a faltering economy — that modern speakers use the word for all of them without noticing the transfer. The word never lost its roots in the body, and that is what gives it its force in every abstract use.

Summary of senses.

  • To move unsteadily, totter, or stumble in gait (physical).
  • To lose bodily strength or vigour; to give way physically.
  • To speak hesitatingly; to stammer; of the voice, to crack or break with emotion.
  • To hesitate, waver, or weaken in purpose, courage, faith, or allegiance (moral).
  • To lose momentum, stall, or weaken (of economies, markets, campaigns, teams, plans).
  • Rare: to utter falteringly (transitive); to flap or waver in motion.
