Polynesians is the plural ethnonym denoting the Indigenous peoples native to Polynesia — the vast eastern triangle of the Pacific bounded by Hawaii in the north, Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in the east, and New Zealand (Aotearoa) in the south — including Hawaiians, Samoans, Tongans, Tahitians, Marquesans, Cook Islanders, Tuamotuans, Austral Islanders, Rapa Nui people, and Maori, together with their diasporas. Linguistically and ancestrally they are the eastern branch of the Austronesian family, speaking closely related Polynesian languages descended from Proto-Polynesian and sharing traditions of oceanic voyaging, chiefly organization, and monumental ritual architecture.

The core cultural sense emphasizes seafaring mastery and social complexity. Polynesians settled some of the most isolated islands on earth by intentional two-way voyaging in double-hulled canoes (waa, waka, vaka) navigated without instruments by stars, swells, winds, birds and clouds. They developed intensive horticulture (taro, yam, breadfruit, sweet potato), aquaculture (Hawaiian fishponds loko ia), pelagic fishing with thousand-ell nets requiring a hundred men as in the source sentence, featherwork, tapa cloth, tattoo (tatau/moko), and hierarchical chiefdoms (ali i, ariki, matai) capable of mobilizing labour for temples (heiau, marae), statues (moai) and warfare. This combination of economic provision and political organization, noted in the source, made Polynesian chiefdoms classic cases in anthropology of the origin of the state.

  • Peoples and nations sense: the Polynesians includes Hawaiians/Kanaka Maoli, Samoan, Tongan, Niuean, Tokelauan, Tuvaluan (partly Polynesian), Maori of New Zealand and Cook Islands Maori, Tahitian/Maohi, Marquesan (Enata), Mangarevan, Rapanui. Each is distinct; Polynesians is the umbrella.
  • Linguistic sense: Polynesian languages — a subgroup of Oceanic Austronesian with ~30 languages in Tongic (Tongan, Niuean) and Nuclear Polynesian (Samoan, Hawaiian, Maori, Tahitian, Rapa Nui). Mutual intelligibility is partial; Hawaiian and Maori share ~80% cognates.
  • Geographic sense: inhabitants of Polynesia as opposed to Melanesians (New Guinea, Vanuatu, Fiji, Solomons) and Micronesians (Kiribati, Marshalls, FSM, Palau). The tripartition is colonial but still institutional in censuses and linguistics.
  • Diasporic sense: Polynesian communities in Auckland (largest Polynesian city), Sydney, Brisbane, Los Angeles, Salt Lake City. Here Polynesian/Pasifika functions as pan-ethnic identity, e.g., Polynesian football players, Polynesian festivals.

Secondary usages and nuances reflect both admiration and stereotype. In popular Western imagination Polynesians have been cast as noble savages, carefree islanders, natural navigators, warriors (haka, tattooed Samoan taupou), dancers (hula, ori), surfers — images that celebrate but also exoticize. In sports journalism Polynesian often codes physical power and gentle Christian humility. In tourism Polynesian dance, Polynesian resort, Polynesian sauce trivialize into branding. Scholars warn against homogenizing: a Tongan monarchy, Hawaiian kingdom history, Samoan faamatai chief system and Maori iwi/hapu structures differ profoundly despite shared ancestry.

  • Distinction from neighbours: Polynesians are not Melanesians (darker-skinned western Pacific peoples speaking Papuan and Oceanic languages) nor Micronesians (small-atoll northwestern peoples), though intermarriage and Lapita ancestry connect all three. Fiji is transitional (Melanesian with Polynesian influence); Tuvalu and Tokelau are Polynesian outliers in Micronesia.
  • Self-naming preference: many prefer specific nation names; Polynesian is accepted as second-level umbrella, especially abroad, but inत्त्व homeland Maori in New Zealand rarely call themselves Polynesian first — they are Maori. Sensitivity parallels Native American vs Navajo/Hopi.
  • Adjectival uses: Polynesian navigation, Polynesian triangle, Polynesian tattoo, Polynesian voyaging society. These celebrate technical achievement and are uncontroversial.
  • Historical bridge for state-formation sense: large nets requiring a hundred men, irrigation complexes, temple building demanded coordination beyond kin, fostering ranked chiefs, tribute, craft specialization and warfare — hence the source claim that the united quest for food helped generate the state, a classic Wittfogel-Childe argument illustrated by Polynesia.

In short, Polynesians means the many-isled peoples of the eastern Pacific, master navigators whose cooperative subsistence built complex chiefdoms, understood today both as distinct island nations and as a shared voyaging heritage.
