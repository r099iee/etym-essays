The noun charlatan entered the English language in the early 17th century from the bustling theatrical street markets of Renaissance Italy. Its etymological lineage offers a rich cultural window into early modern medicine, itinerant commerce, and the linguistic fusion of onomatopoeic chatter with regional satire.

1. Renaissance Italian Origins & The Cerreto Connection:

  • The Onomatopoeic Base: In 14th- and 15th-century Italian, the expressive onomatopoeic verb ciarlare meant "to chatter, babble, prattle, or talk at rapid length without substance" (akin to ciarla "idle gossip").
  • The Town of Cerreto: Simultaneously, the inhabitants of the hill town of Cerreto di Spoleto in Umbria (the Cerretani) gained widespread notoriety across Italy during the 14th and 15th centuries. Licensed by the Pope to collect alms for the hospital of Santa Maria di Cerreto, many Cerretani abused their papal privileges, wandering from town to town aggressively peddling spurious papal indulgences, fake religious relics, and dubious herbal panaceas with theatrical eloquence. In Late Medieval Italian slang, *cerretano* became synonymous with a licensed rogue or fraudulent street vendor.
  • The Linguistic Blend: In the early 16th century, Italian speakers blended the verb ciarlare ("to chatter") with the noun cerretano ("itinerant rogue"), coining ciarlatano. The word described an itinerant mountebank who stood upon a portable bench (*ciarlatano da banco*) in piazzas, surrounded by clowns, jugglers, and trained monkeys, chattering endlessly to draw crowds and sell fraudulent medicines.

2. Transmission into French & English (16th–17th Century):

  • French Adoption: By the 1580s, the term was adopted into Middle French as charlatan (notably appearing in the essays of Michel de Montaigne to describe pretentious philosophers and medical impostors).
  • English Attestation: English playwrights and travelers encountered the phenomenon in Italy and France, introducing *charlatan* into Early Modern English around 1605–1618 (attested in the works of Ben Jonson, John Marston, and Thomas Coryat's Crudities). Early English authors used *charlatan* interchangeably with the native Germanic term *quacksalver* (later shortened to *quack*) and the Italianate *mountebank*.
  • Early Semantic Evolution: While initially restricted to fraudulent healers and itinerant drug sellers, English writers quickly recognized that the charlatan's core weapon was not medicine, but language—the art of dazzling the credulous with grandiloquent rhetoric.

3. Enlightenment Critique to Modern Cultural Jargon:

  • The Age of Reason: In the 18th and 19th centuries, Enlightenment philosophers (such as Voltaire and Denis Diderot) and Victorian essayists (like Thomas Carlyle in Heroes and Hero-Worship) expanded *charlatan* to designate intellectual frauds, corrupt politicians, pseudo-scientific mesmerists, and dogmatic theologians who claimed monopoly over truth without empirical proof.
  • 20th-Century Arts and Pedagogy: In 20th-century cultural debates—such as the heated factional disputes over dramatic training, psychoanalysis, and modern art—*charlatan* became a powerful weapon. Traditionalists and competing theorists used it to attack figures whose radical or mystical methods (such as Lee Strasberg's affective memory exercises or certain esoteric art movements) appeared to substitute psychological manipulation for rigorous craftsmanship.
  • Contemporary Digital Resonance: Today, the word remains fully alive, describing wellness influencers, financial snake-oil salesmen, and self-appointed tech visionaries who exploit mass media algorithms to construct profitable empires of deception.
