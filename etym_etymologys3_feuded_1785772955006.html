The story of feuded begins in the deep grammar of Germanic enmity. Proto-Germanic had an adjective *faihaz meaning "hostile, outlawed, foe-like," built on a root that many scholars connect with a PIE base *peik- "hostile, evil-minded," seen also in Sanskrit píśuna- "malicious," Greek pikrós "bitter, sharp," and Old English fāh "hostile." From that adjective Germanic formed an abstract noun *faihithō — "enmity, the state of being hostile" — which Old English recorded as fæhþ(u): not a mood but a legal-social condition, the state in which a kindred owed vengeance and might, by custom, lawfully answer blood with blood. Fæhþu figures in Anglo-Saxon law codes and in the vocabulary of exile and outlawry; the related adjective fāh even meant "outlawed, guilty, liable to feud." The word family is the same one that gave English foe.

The modern English word, however, did not come down only through this native channel. During the early Middle Ages, West Germanic tribal law codes — Frankish, Saxon, Burgundian, Lombard — institutionalized the feud as a regulated alternative to judicial punishment, and the Frankish form of the abstract noun, in the shape borrowed into Gallo-Romance, entered Old French as fede, faide, feide: "enmity, hatred, war, vengeance." It was this French word, brought to England after the Norman Conquest, that Middle English adopted in the thirteenth century as fede or feude, and it mingled in legal and chronicle texts with the native fæhþu heritage. The result is a word with a doubled ancestry: both streams are Germanic, but one arrived through the Low Countries and France.

Orthography was slow to settle. Middle English shows fede, feide, feude, fayde; the modern spelling feud, with the -u-, appears in the early sixteenth century and seems to have been reshaped by association with the entirely unrelated word feud "a fief," from Medieval Latin feudum/feodum — the ancestor of feudal. That chance similarity created a permanent false-friend: feud (enmity) and feudal (land tenure) look alike but share no ancestry, a trap that the mnemonic for this card (FEUDED sounding like FEUDAL) deliberately exploits.

The noun dominated the word's early career; the verb "to feud" — "to carry on a feud" — is a later verbal use of the noun, attested in the early modern period and common by the eighteenth century. The past tense and participle feuded appears wherever the verb appears, reporting participation in an ongoing state rather than a single event. In the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries, English writers deployed the verb heavily for the great private wars of the Anglo-Scottish border, the Highlands, and other clan societies, where raid and counter-raid were normal social fact; the word carried grim, honor-bound associations of duty and blood.

In the nineteenth century, print journalism and folklore fixed a new American prototype: the Appalachian mountain feuds — above all the Hatfield–McCoy vendetta — which made "feud" a vivid, regional, violent keyword in the national imagination. Anthropology in the twentieth century took up feud as a technical term for systems of reciprocal violence lying between simple crime and formal state war, restoring to the word its institutional seriousness. Meanwhile popular culture lightened the blood but kept the duration: by the late twentieth century, politicians, executives, athletes, and celebrities "feuded" in press cycles and social-media exchanges, and the noun became headline-stable ("a decades-long feud").

  • Sound changes: PGmc *faihithō → OE fæhþu (loss of the medial vowel and reduction of the -hþ- cluster); Frankish *faihida → Old French fede/faide (loss of medial -i-, early Romance consonant simplification); Middle English fede/feude → Modern English feud (vowel and spelling stabilization, with -u- probably reshaped under the influence of feudal).
  • Semantic drift: "state of enmity / vengeance liability" → "actual long war between families" → "any prolonged bitter mutual hostility" → "metaphorical public conflict (media, corporate, celebrity)."
  • Socio-cultural factors: Feud law in Germanic tribal codes; the Norman transmission of French legal vocabulary; border and clan warfare; nineteenth-century journalism and folklore; twentieth-century anthropology and celebrity culture.
  • Literary attestations: Anglo-Saxon law codes (fæhþu); medieval chronicles and legal texts (fede/feude); border ballads and Walter Scott's fiction; nineteenth-century American regional writing; modern political and entertainment headlines.
  • Debated points: The PIE root is given variously as *peik-, *peig-, or related sets in older handbooks; the exact PIE shape is contested. The influence of the feudal word family on the spelling of feud is probable but only approximately documented.
