The chain for the running verb is partially reconstructed and is explicitly uncertain at its deepest stage. Stage 1 (proposed donor, uncertain): Old Norse skjóta 'to shoot, dart, thrust, move swiftly' — a proposed but not securely proven ultimate source of the English scud/scuttle family; the connection is debated because no Old English reflex is attested and the Norse verb stresses 'shooting' rather than walking. Alternative hypothesis: the English verb is imitative/echoic of short hasty steps, with no recoverable ancestor at all. No stage before the 15th century is securely attested. Stage 2 (attested): Middle English skottelen / scuttelen 'to run with quick, hasty steps' — first recorded c. 1450; the form is a frequentative (suffix -le, adding iterative/repeated-action meaning) built on scud 'to move or run quickly'. Stage 3: Modern English scuttle 'to run with quick, short, hasty steps; to scamper' — established by the 16th century. Stage 4: scuttled, the regular weak past tense and past participle — attested from the 17th century. Final node: SCUTTLED. NOTE ON HOMONYMS (do not merge into this chain): the nautical scuttle 'to sink a ship by cutting holes' is a different word — chain: Middle French escoutille 'hatchway' → English scuttle 'a small opening/hatch' (15th c.) → the verb 'to cut a scuttle/hole in a ship, to sink' (17th c.); and the noun coal scuttle 'a bucket for coal' is yet a third word, from Latin scutella 'a dish, bowl' via Old French escuel(l)e. Debate note: because the running verb first appears in the 15th century with no recorded Old English ancestor, its Germanic genealogy is inferential; the OED classifies the origin as 'uncertain', and both the Norse (skjóta) and the imitative hypotheses remain open.
