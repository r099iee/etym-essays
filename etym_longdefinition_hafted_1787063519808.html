Core meaning. Hafted is the past tense and past participle of the verb haft, "to fit with a haft (handle)", and it also functions as a participial adjective meaning "provided with a haft; having a handle". A haft is the handle of a bladed or pointed tool or weapon — an axe, knife, dagger, sword, spear, halberd — the part of the implement by which it is gripped, swung, or thrust. Thus a hafted axe is an axe mounted on a handle; a silver-hafted axe is an axe whose handle is made of or ornamented with silver (exactly as in the source sentence).

Primary sense — fitted with a handle. The word belongs to the vocabulary of tool- and weapon-making. When an object is described as hafted, the emphasis is on the joining of two parts: the working head or blade (of stone, bronze, iron, or steel) and the handle or shaft (of wood, bone, horn, or metal). Key features:

  • Technical context: hafted is the standard participle in archaeology and museum description for prehistoric tools — e.g. a hafted stone axe, a hafted flint spearhead — where the presence or absence of a handle is a significant cultural and technological fact.
  • Craft context: in woodworking, hunting, and military crafts, to haft is the deliberate act of mounting a blade; a well-made tool is hafted securely, the handle wedged, bound, or cemented into or onto the head.
  • Adjectival and compound use: the participle forms compounds naming the handle's material or design: long-hafted, bone-hafted, oak-hafted, silver-hafted.

Secondary senses and extensions.

  • Completeness of construction: a hafted weapon is one that has been finished and made ready for use — the participle signals that blade and handle have been joined into a working implement.
  • Figurative (rare, literary): because the haft is the "grip", hafted occasionally suggests something firmly grasped, mounted, or set in place; this use is uncommon and poetic.
  • Archaic residue: the Old English noun hæft also meant "bond, captivity, seizure" (from the same root as have), and vestiges survive in archaic verse where a "haft" is a bond or grip; these senses are dead in modern use but explain the word's family.

Grammar and behaviour. Hafted is the regular past/past participle of the weak denominal verb haft (the verb is derived from the noun haft). It is used (1) passively: the axe was hafted in ash; (2) adjectivally: a hafted knife, a bronze-hafted dagger; and (3) in compound adjectives naming the handle material (oak-hafted, silver-hafted). The verb is of low frequency in everyday speech but is precise and normal in archaeology, craft, and literary description.

Register and connotation. Hafted is a precise, slightly formal or specialist word. In ordinary conversation one would more likely say "with a handle" or "handled"; hafted carries an antique, craftsmanly, or archaeological flavour and is valued in descriptive prose for its exactness and economy. It is the kind of word that gives a passage a period or heroic texture — fittingly, it appears in Tolkien's description of Thorin's silver-hafted axe.

Semantic history in brief. The noun haft descends from Old English hæft "handle; bond, captivity", which continues a Proto-Germanic formation on the root meaning "to grasp, hold, seize" (the same family as have and heave), ultimately from the PIE root *kap- "to grasp, take, hold". The verb "to haft" and the participle "hafted" developed in Middle English as the tool senses hardened into "the handle of a blade", and the word has remained essentially stable in meaning for six hundred years.
