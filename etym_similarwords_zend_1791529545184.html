• Avestan: Similar because it names the same language in modern usage; differs because Avestan is the accurate linguistic term, while Zend is an archaic misnomer for the language and properly names the commentary.
  • Zand: Similar as the correct Middle Persian form of the same word; differs because Zand denotes the commentary and translation, not the language.
  • Zend-Avesta: Similar as the compound title that combines the commentary and the scripture; differs because it names the corpus or a European book title, not the language alone.
  • Old Iranian: Similar as the broader language family to which Avestan belongs; differs because it includes Old Persian and other languages, not just Avestan.
  • Old Persian: Similar as a sister Old Iranian language; differs because it is attested in royal inscriptions and is not the language of the Avesta.
  • Pahlavi: Similar as the Middle Persian language of the Zand and later Zoroastrian literature; differs because it is a later stage of Iranian and the language of the commentary, not the scripture.
  • Middle Persian: Similar as the language in which the Zand is written; differs because it is a chronological and linguistic label, not the name of the commentary.
  • Gathic: Similar as the oldest variety of Avestan; differs because it names a dialect layer of the language, not the commentary or the whole language.
  • Younger Avestan: Similar as the later variety of the language; differs because it is a chronological dialect label, not a synonym for Zend.
  • Persian: Similar as a modern Iranian language descended from Old Persian; differs because it is not Avestan and has no direct role in the Zoroastrian scriptures.
  • Zoroastrian: Similar as the religious context of the Avesta and the Zand; differs because it is an adjective for the religion, not a name for a language or text.
  • zindiq: Similar as a derivative of the same Middle Persian root, meaning a heretic or freethinker; differs because it names a person or religious category, not a text or language.
