- 12th–13th c. — Old French verb compasser meant to measure by pacing, divide equally, draw with a divider compass, or devise/contrive an intricate plan.
- 14th c. — Borrowed into Middle English as compassen; intensive compound encompassen coined by prefixing en- to denote physically surrounding or encircling with walls, armies, or moats.
- 16th c. (Elizabethan era) — Shakespeare and contemporaries used encompass in rich dual senses: physically circumnavigating the globe or surrounding an enemy, and politically scheming to achieve a covert design.
- 17th–18th c. — Established in philosophical, constitutional, and legal treatises to denote the intellectual act of comprehending, including, or spanning diverse topics within a single systematic work.
- 19th c. — Broadly utilized in natural history and geography to describe mountain ranges, ocean currents, and biomes enclosing physical territories.
- 20th–21st c. — A staple of academic prose, engineering, and system architecture to designate holistic systems, models, diagrams, or policies that comprehensively cover and synthesize complex multi-step processes.
