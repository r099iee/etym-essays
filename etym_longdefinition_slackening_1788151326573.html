Definition.Slackening is the noun and gerund of the verb slacken: the action or state of becoming slack — looser, slower, weaker, or less intense — or of being made so. The word operates across physical, temporal, and abstract domains with remarkable uniformity: a rope slackens, a pace slackens, demand slackens, and a discipline slackens. The gerundial form slackening allows the process itself to be named, as in “the slackening of the current” or “a slackening of vigilance.”

  • Physical sense: loosening of tension. To slacken a rope, cable, line, or sail is to give out or reduce its strain; a slackening is the corresponding relaxation of tension. Nautical usage — “slacken sail,” “slack the sheets” — is among the oldest and most idiomatic, continuing the word’s origin in “slack” = lax, loose, not taut. The sailor’s world still furnishes the clearest image: a line made fast and then slackened sags visibly, and the sag itself is the slackening.
  • Motional sense: deceleration. A slackening pace, gait, or speed is a slowing down. The bridge from loosening is direct: a relaxed (“slack”) gait is a slower one, and by the seventeenth century “slacken one’s pace” was fully idiomatic. The word is standard in narratives of pursuit and retreat: the pursuer slackens, the fugitive gains.
  • Effort and intensity: diminution. “Slackening effort,” “slackening attention,” “slackening vigilance”: intensity that eases off. Here the word’s moral colouring appears, for to slacken is usually — though not always — to relax a discipline that ought to be kept taut. The ancient sense of slæc as “remiss, negligent” surfaces in this usage: a slackened watch is not merely slower but derelict, and the phrase casts a mild shadow of reproach.
  • Economic and statistical usage. In modern journalism and economics, “slackening demand,” “a slackening economy,” or “slackening growth” denotes deceleration of activity or loss of momentum. This abstract sense is a nineteenth–twentieth-century development, transferring the physical image of a slackening rope or sail to the plotted curves of trade, output, and employment.
  • Meteorological usage. Winds “slacken” or show a “slackening”; the noun is standard in nautical weather reports for a decrease in wind force, and in sailing accounts a “slackening breeze” is the recurring enemy of progress.
  • Bodily and sensory usage. Muscles slacken in relaxation; the slackening of the jaw marks surprise or fatigue; in physiology the term describes the relaxation of muscular or vascular tension. The word thus spans from ship rigging to neurology without changing its core figure: strain let go.

The semantic bridge. Every sense descends from Old English slæc, “slack, loose, remiss” — a word for what hangs limp or fails to hold strain. Relaxation of tension in a rope and relaxation of effort in the will were fused in the Germanic adjective, and every later sense — slow pace, weak demand, easing weather — is an application of one image: the taut line of work, speed, or growth letting go its strain. Slackening names that letting-go as a process, which is why it fits cables, gales, markets, and muscles alike: all of them, in the word’s own idiom, go slack.
