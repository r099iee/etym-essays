JEST is a noun and a verb whose modern core sense is "a joke, a witty remark, or playful banter" (noun) and "to speak or act in a playful, joking manner" (verb). The word occupies a distinct register in English: it is more literary and often slightly archaic than plain "joke", it appears in set phrases such as in jest, jesting apart, and a merry jest, and in elevated or older prose it retains a faint flavour of courtly wit and theatrical fooling. To jest is to make light of something — to say or do something that is not meant to be taken seriously. The adverb phrase in jest is the standard idiom for marking that a remark is not serious: "I said it only in jest." As a verb, jest is used intransitively ("he jested about the weather") and with at or with; it usually implies gentle, good-humoured teasing rather than malice. In the context passage of this card the word is treated as the target of a comic remark, exactly its everyday domain.

THE MODERN SENSES IN FULL

  • Noun, "a joke, a witty or humorous remark" — "he told a jest", "a merry jest". This is the core sense since the 16th century, and it still governs ordinary use.
  • Verb, "to joke, to speak or act playfully" — "he jested about his own clumsiness"; also with with, at, upon ("he jested upon the weather").
  • The fixed idiom "in jest" — "not seriously, without real intent", opposed to "in earnest": "she said it only in jest." Also "jesting apart/aside", "jesting aside", used to drop the playful mode.
  • The archaic noun sense "a deed, an exploit, an action" (obsolete), the oldest English value of the word, now surviving only in literary history.
  • The historical "geste" sense: "a tale of heroic deeds" — a narrative or romance of exploits; this is the medieval meaning (see Gesta Romanorum, the chanson de geste) and it lingers in phrases like "jest-book" (a printed collection of merry tales, a genre of the 16th–17th centuries).
  • "Jester" — the professional fool or court jester, a person employed to entertain a court with jokes and antics; the noun carries the modern playful sense and preserves the old performing context.
  • Figurative and phraseological uses — "to be jesting" ("kidding"), "a sorry jest" (a disappointing or poor joke), "jest and earnest" as an antithesis, "many a true word is spoken in jest" (proverb, the idea that jokes often carry real truth).
  • Derivatives — "jesting" (gerund and adjective), "jestingly" (adverb), "jester", and the rare "jestful", "jesting-stock" (an object of mockery).

THE HISTORICAL BRIDGE: FROM HEROIC DEEDS TO JOKES

The word's journey is one of the most striking semantic descents in English vocabulary — from the record of great deeds to the telling of a joke. The starting point is Latin gerere, "to carry, to do, to perform", whose past participle gestus, with the neuter plural gesta, meant literally "things done, deeds, exploits". The plural gesta became a title for books of deeds and lives, as in the famous medieval compilation Gesta Romanorum, "Deeds of the Romans". In Old French this became geste, used both for "deeds, exploits" and for the literary chanson de geste — the epic poem celebrating the heroic deeds of Charlemagne and his paladins. When Middle English borrowed geste (c. 1300) it meant "a tale, a story, a narrative, especially one recounting exploits". Then, as the old heroic narratives were displaced and minstrel tellers turned to lighter material — comic tales, funny interludes, humorous anecdotes — the word shifted from "a story of deeds" to "a story told to amuse", and by the 16th century "jest" firmly meant a joke or witty remark. The modern sense thus rests on a kind of medieval promotion of the joke-teller where the heroic narrator had stood.

REGISTER AND MODERN USE

In contemporary English "jest" is a decidedly literary, formal, or jokily archaic word. Speakers who wish to sound playful use it in stock phrases ("only jesting", "jesting aside"), while novelists use it to evoke period or courtly settings. Its derivatives — jesting, jestingly, jester, the rare jestful — keep the family alive, and the old plural "jests" appears in proverbs and set phrases. Because the comic sense is now dominant, the heroic "geste" sense survives only in literary history, in the names of medieval epic genres, and in fossilised phrases. The word therefore sits between the everyday "joke" (neutral, modern, frequent) and the consciously old-fashioned "quip"/"witticism", with its own hint of courtly, rehearsed, performative humour.
