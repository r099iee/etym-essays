Tropes is the plural of trope: in rhetoric, a figure of speech in which a word or phrase is turned aside from its literal, ordinary meaning to a figurative one — metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, and irony being the four master tropes of the classical catalogue. To call something a trope is to say that language has performed its signature trick: using one thing to mean another.

In the source sentence the word completes Emerson's geological image of language: speech is made of images or tropes which in their secondary use have long ceased to remind us of their origin. Every abstract word we utter — spirit (breath), consider (stargazing), right (straight) — was once a live metaphor, a trope coined by some forgotten speaker; centuries of use have worn it smooth, so that we spend these fossil metaphors daily without hearing the poetry in them, exactly as we walk on limestone without picturing the shells.

Core meaning in detail: classical rhetoric divided figurative language into tropes (turns of meaning in a single word — metaphor says he is a lion, turning the beast into courage) and schemes or figures (turns of arrangement — parallelism, antithesis, word order). Modern use has blurred the distinction, so that trope now covers any recurring figure, image, or device — but the strict sense, the turning of meaning, remains the engine of the word's history and the point of the source sentence.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Figures of speech turning meaning from the literal (primary rhetorical sense): metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, irony — each turns a word from its proper sense to another. The bridge is the Greek root itself: trepein means to turn, so a trope is literally a turning of language off its straight road.
  • Dead or fossil metaphors inside ordinary vocabulary (Emerson's sense): the images or tropes of which language is made — comprehend (to grasp), express (to squeeze out), supercilious (raising eyebrows). The bridge is time: live tropes used for generations die into literal senses, becoming the limestone strata of speech.
  • Recurring literary devices, motifs, and conventions (dominant modern popular sense): the reluctant-hero trope, the enemies-to-lovers trope, the chosen-one trope of fantasy and film. The bridge runs through criticism: reviewers borrowed the rhetoricians' word for any stock turn a story takes, and fandom made it the standard coin for plot patterns.
  • Musical interpolations in medieval chant (musicological sense): added words or melodies expanding the liturgy, flourishing from the ninth to the thirteenth centuries. The bridge is again turning: the chant turns aside from its set text into ornament — the same image of deviation, in song rather than speech.
  • Philosophical and theological turns (specialist sense): the sceptics' modes or tropes (arguments turning the dogmatist's claims back on themselves), and the turning of Scripture toward allegory. The bridge is argumentative reversal — turning a claim aside or upside down.

Register and connotation: in its strict sense tropes is the educated critic's word — lecture halls, review columns, rhetoric manuals. In its popular sense it is the friendliest coin of fan culture: to name a trope is to share delighted recognition (oh, the mentor always dies). Only pedants bristle when the popular sense stretches the classical one — but the stretch is itself a trope-like turn, language doing what the word describes.
