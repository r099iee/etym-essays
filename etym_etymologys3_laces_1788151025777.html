1. From snare to shoelace: the early career of the word. The history of laces begins not in the wardrobe but in the hunt. Latin laqueus was the noose that fowlers and hunters set to trap birds and animals, and by extension any cord or snare. Old French laz (also las) kept the whole range ‘snare, noose, cord’, and it is this cord-sense that crossed the Channel: Middle English lace is documented from the fourteenth century as a thong, a cord for fastening armor and clothing, a braid, or a net. In this first long phase the word was purely functional — the hempen lace held the hose up, laced the doublet, and laced the armor together — and the verb ‘to lace’, ‘to fasten with a cord drawn through eyelets’, was its daily work.

2. The ornamental turn (15th–16th centuries). Threads of precious metal changed everything. By the fifteenth century the same cord had become a braided or embroidered banding of silk, gold, or silver, sewn along edges, cuffs, and collars — military and court ‘gold lace’. Sumptuary legislation across Europe tried to reserve such laces to the nobility, which of course only increased their prestige. The word’s center of gravity moved from fastening to display: a lace was now something that adorned, and the verb acquired ‘to trim with lace’.

3. The apotheosis of openwork fabric (16th–17th centuries). The great transformation was the birth of lace as a fabric: a self-supporting, openwork web of threads created by needle or by bobbins over a pillow. Venice, Milan, and Genoa led needle lace; Flanders produced the celebrated bobbin laces (Mechlin, Valenciennes, point d’Angleterre); France under Colbert planted the crown-supported factories of Alençon and Argentan. Lace collars, cuffs, ruffs, and veils became the most expensive textiles in Europe by weight, a concentrated emblem of wealth, taste, and courtly hierarchy. The word ‘lace’ now named this precious openwork itself, and it is the sense that survives in fashion history.

  • Economy: lace-making supported armies of pillow-lace workers and nuns; the industry was industrial politics (Colbert’s prohibition of imported lace, 1660s).
  • Society: sumptuary acts, French court etiquette, and the late-medieval fashion for ‘great laces’ show the cord’s rise from humble fastener to diplomatic gift.

4. Mechanization and democratization (19th–20th centuries). John Heathcoat’s bobbin-net machine (1809) and the Leavers loom turned hand-lace into a mass product. Machine lace flooded the market; hand lace became a shrinking luxury. Almost in the same generation, the plural laces re-colonized the feet: the shoelace, with eyeleted leather footwear spreading to all classes, made ‘laces’ the daily domestic word. The ornamental senses did not vanish — they retired to ceremonial, bridal, and ecclesiastical registers — but the word now swung between two poles: the humble eyelet cord and the aristocratic web of threads.

5. Figurative and idiomatic drift (19th–21st centuries). The ornamental surplus turned metaphorical. ‘Laces and frills’, ‘frills and furbelows’, and similar pairings made lace the type of the non-essential extra — the trimmings by which civilization adorns the bare necessities of survival. In Will Durant’s source passage the Bedouins’ intelligence is ‘lavished on the perils of the hunt’ so that ‘nothing will remain for the laces and frills … the arts and comforts of civilization’: the word is a synecdoche for surplus culture itself. Meanwhile the web-image spun off ‘laced with’ (spiked drink, threaded prose), ‘lace curtain’ (Irish-American respectability), and technical uses like ‘lacewing’ for the net-veined insect.

6. Today. The plural survives in everyday speech chiefly as ‘shoelaces’; the ornamental senses live in fashion, bridal wear, altars, and the idiom of civilized refinement. One etymology — the hunter’s noose — thus spans the trap, the court collar, the loom, the boot, and the metaphor of culture as ornament.
