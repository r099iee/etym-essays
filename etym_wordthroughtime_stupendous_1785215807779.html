- 16th century - Enters English from Latin meaning “to be wondered at”; originally used of wonders that stun or astound the mind — a word of awe, not of size.
- 17th–18th century - Extends from the astonishing to the immense — stupendous mountains, stupendous efforts; magnitude grows but awe stays integral: the thing is large enough to stun the imagination.
- 19th century (Victorian rhetoric) - Reaches high currency in biography and travel writing; Mill's “His industry was stupendous” works precisely through laconic, understated awe.
- 20th–21st century - Dilutes into enthusiastic exaggeration — “stupendous success”, “a stupendous bargain” — losing much of its paralysing wonder, though the blend of magnitude plus awe survives in formal and dramatic prose.
