Introduction

SUPPING is the present participle of the verb sup, and the full history of the form is really the history of two distinct verbs that collided into a single word, welded together by medieval dining culture. One sup descends in an unbroken line from the Old English strong verb sūpan "to drink, sip, taste liquid"; the other sup is a much later creation meaning "to take the evening meal," built on the noun supper, which entered English from Old French souper and ultimately goes back to the very same ancient root that produced sūpan. The present participle supp-ing is the single visible form where both lines converge.

1. From the ancient root to Old English

The starting point is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *seup- (also written *sūp-), meaning roughly "to drink, sip, suck, taste." It belongs to a small family of roots describing the intake of liquid and has reflexes across the Indo-European family: Old Norse súpa "to sip," Old High German sūfan "to swill, drink (said especially of animals)," Middle Dutch supen, and — further afield — the root that underlies English sip, sop, soup, and suck. In the Germanic branch the root produced the Proto-Germanic strong verb *sūpaną "to drink, sip," a class II strong verb whose vowel alternation (sūp- : sup-) is still faintly visible in the fossilized past participle sopen behind the noun sop.

Old English inherited the verb as sūpan, recorded in glosses and texts from the early medieval period with the sense "to drink, swallow, sip, taste." Two phonetic points matter. First, the long vowel: Old English sūpan had a long ū, which in later Middle English underwent the ordinary shortening processes (shortening before certain clusters and in relaxed, everyday contexts) to give the short u of modern sup. Second, the conjugation class: as a strong verb it showed the pattern sūpan (infinitive), sēap (past), sopen (past participle), and it is from that participle that the noun sop "bread soaked in liquid" derives — literally "something that has been supped up."

2. Middle English: two streams flow together

In Middle English the native verb appears as suppen, soupen "to sip, take liquid," the direct descendant of sūpan. But at exactly the same period the Norman Conquest had brought a second, French-derived stream into the same shape. Old French possessed souper, a verb meaning "to have one's evening meal," alongside soupe "soup, sop" — and both of these descend, through Late Latin suppa, from the same Germanic liquid-food vocabulary that produced sūpan. The evening meal in medieval France was, literally, the "sop" meal: bread soaked in broth, dipped and supped from a common pot. When English speakers took over souper/supper as the noun for the evening meal (first attested in English in the 13th century), the verbal idea "to eat supper" naturally followed, and the English verb sup in its "dine" sense (attested from the 16th century) either came directly from the French verb or was re-created inside English from the noun supper.

The result was a perfect collision: two etymologically distinct verbs — one native Germanic "sip," one Romance-linked "take supper" — now spelled and sounded identically as sup. For the lexicographer this is a textbook case of convergence, or lexical merger. For ordinary speakers the two senses never felt far apart, because the supper itself was in origin a soup-and-sop meal that one sipped. The merger was semantic as much as phonetic.

3. The -ing form and the grammar of supping

The present participle supping is a straightforward formation: the verbal base sup plus the suffix -ing, with the spelling doubling the final p to keep the vowel short (compare cup → cupping, hip → hipping). The -ing suffix, historically from Old English -ung/-ing (on nouns) and -ende (on participles), is the standard Germanic progressive marker. In the source context "supping alone tonight," the progressive aspect frames the evening meal as an ongoing, unfolding experience rather than a punctual event — precisely the effect a writer wants when describing loneliness as a process rather than an instant.

4. Semantic drift and figurative extensions

From the 16th–17th centuries onward the word family expanded in figurative directions:

  • "To sup sorrow": to taste misfortune, to drink in an unpleasant experience — an extension from consuming liquid into consuming an emotional fate.
  • "To sup with the devil": the proverb about bargaining with evil companions ("sup with the devil with a long spoon"), exploiting the shared-meal sense of sup.
  • Register shift of supper: the noun moved from the standard word for the evening meal toward a homely, regional, or old-fashioned word in British English, while in much of American English supper remains the everyday term for the evening meal — a regional divergence that keeps the word family alive on both sides of the Atlantic.

By the 19th and 20th centuries, sup and supping in ordinary prose had become distinctly literary and nostalgic, beloved of historical novelists and poets precisely because they carry the warmth of the hearthside, the candlelit table, and the shared pot of broth. The fixed phrase "the Last Supper" keeps the noun culturally anchored as the archetypal evening meal shared at dusk, reinforcing the same warm, sacred, evening associations.

5. Literary and cultural attestations

  • William Shakespeare uses sup in its evening-meal sense across his plays (e.g., characters "sup with" companions, and the proverb about the devil appears in early modern collections).
  • The King James Bible and the Book of Common Prayer keep "the Last Supper" and the verb "to sup" in elevated religious register (e.g., Revelation 3:20, "I will sup with him, and he with me").
  • Victorian novelists (Dickens, the Brontës, Eliot) employ supper and supping as markers of domestic cosiness or of a frugal, homely evening routine.
  • Regional and dialectal writing preserves the older "sip" sense — "sup the soup," "sup it while it's hot" — especially in Northern English and Scots usage.

6. Modern survival and conclusion

Today supping is rare in spontaneous speech and thrives in deliberately literary or nostalgic contexts, which is exactly the environment of the source sentence. Its double heritage — the Old English sipping verb and the French-inspired evening-meal verb, both flowing from a single prehistoric root — makes it a compact illustration of how a language's food vocabulary records history: the Proto-Indo-European drinking culture, the Germanic migrations, the medieval French dinner table, and the English sound changes that fused two streams into one warm, glimmering word.
