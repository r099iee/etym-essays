PIE *dyeu- 'to shine, be bright; sky, heaven, day; the sky-god' (reconstructed; the root underlies the day, sky, and god names across Indo-European — Sanskrit dyāus, Greek Zeus, Latin Iuppiter) —>
Latin Iuppiter, stem Iov- (Jove/Jupiter, the sky-father god, king of the Roman gods, and later the name of the planet) (attested from the earliest Latin records; genitive Iovis and vocative Iūpiter are classical) —>
Latin Ioviālis 'of Jove, pertaining to Jupiter' (adjective formed on the stem Iov- with the suffix -ālis; attested in post-classical and Late Latin) —>
Italian gioviale / French jovial 'born under Jupiter; (astrologically) disposed to cheerfulness' (attested in the 16th century; the astrological sense of the planet Jupiter is the source of 'cheerful') —>
English jovial 'born under the influence of Jupiter; hence merry, full of good humor' (1580s) —>
JOVIAL 'cheerful, friendly, hearty, full of good humor' ◄ TARGET.

Debate note: the immediate route into English is debated — the OED and Etymonline allow either French jovial or Italian gioviale, both from Latin Ioviālis. The astrological bridge (Jupiter as the 'greater benefic' bestowing cheer) is securely the historical link from 'of Jupiter' to 'cheerful'; the purely astronomical sense has since been taken over by the separate derivative Jovian.
