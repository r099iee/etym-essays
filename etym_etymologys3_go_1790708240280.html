Indo-European Origins and Germanic Divergence
The verb go originates from the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰeh₁-, signifying 'to leave, depart, release, or let go', which is also cognate with Ancient Greek kikhanō ('to reach, find') and Sanskrit jahāti ('leaves, abandons'). In Proto-Germanic, this root formed the athematic verb *gāną (and variant stem *gēn-), meaning 'to go, walk, or step'. Uniquely in the Germanic languages, the paradigm of this verb became highly complex and suppletive, competing and merging with fragments of other verbal roots such as Proto-Germanic *ganganą (source of Old English gongan and Scottish gang, 'to walk/go').

Old English Conjugation and the Suppletive Past

  • Old English Paradigm: In Old English, the verb appeared as gān (infinitive), conjugated in the present as ic gā ('I go'), þū gǣst ('thou goest'), and hē gǣþ ('he goes'). However, Old English gān had no native regular past tense built from its own root. Instead, it relied on a completely distinct Proto-Indo-European root (*h₁ey- 'to go', source of Latin ire), producing the Old English preterite forms ēode ('I/he went') and ēodon ('they went').
  • The Middle English Shift to 'Went': During the Middle English period (c. 1100–1500), the phonological erosion of ēode caused it to be perceived as archaic and functionally weak. In the 15th century, speakers began substituting the past tense of the unrelated strong/weak verb wenden ('to turn, direct one's course', from Proto-Germanic *wandijaną, cognate with wind). The past tense went completely displaced ēode, cementing one of the most famous suppletive grammatical paradigms in global linguistics: present go, past went, past participle gone (from Old English gegān).

Semantic Widening and Idiomatic Proliferation
Throughout the Middle English and Early Modern English periods, go expanded from literal bipedal ambulation into an expansive semantic web. It began expressing spatial reach, competence, and boundaries—as illustrated in Daniel Defoe's 1719 text Robinson Crusoe, where 'as far as education... generally go' employs the verb to denote the upper ceiling of attainment and customary standard. It also developed into a linking verb of state-deterioration ('go bad', 'go mad') through the metaphor of straying from a sound path.

Grammaticalization into the Modern Future Tense
Between the 16th and 18th centuries, the construction to be going to [do something] transitioned through a textbook process of linguistic grammaticalization. Originally describing literal physical movement toward a destination with an intended goal (e.g., 'I am going to the market to buy bread'), it bleached of physical motion and reanalyzed as a pure temporal/modal marker of immediate futurity and intention ('I am going to speak'). In modern contemporary English, go remains one of the most dynamic verbs in existence, continually acquiring new pragmatic layers from colloquial quotatives to digital computing commands.
