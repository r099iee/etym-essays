Torque (also spelled torc) is a noun with two strikingly different lives, both descending from the same Latin verb. Its older and primary dictionary sense names a rigid ring of metal — often of gold, bronze, or silver, frequently twisted, and usually open at the throat — worn about the neck, the great ceremonial collar of the Iron-Age Celtic world, worn by the Gauls and Britons whom Roman writers described. Its newer life is technical: in physics and engineering, torque names the twisting or rotational force that causes a body to rotate about an axis — the "twist" a wrench applies to a bolt. In the passage, the word carries the first sense with full archaic-heroic weight: the boy wears "a golden torque in the likeness of a dragon," and the torque becomes at once an emblem of quasi-royal lineage and a fine, gilded confinement — a stiff ring of precious metal circling the neck of the pampered heir, jewellery turned insignia, a plaything turned mark of fate.

  • Primary sense — the Celtic neck-ring. A torque is a ring of metal worn round the neck, typically of twisted gold, silver, or bronze, with open or knobbed terminals, worn by Celtic warriors, chieftains, and deities in Iron-Age Europe. Archaeologically it is the most famous ornament of the Gauls, Britons, and other Celtic peoples; Roman writers describe the Gallic leaders and their "torques" as badges of rank and valour, and the classical world treated the torque as the distinctive mark of the barbarian nobleman.
  • Military and honorary sense in the ancient world. Roman soldiers who distinguished themselves in the wars against the Gauls and other peoples could be awarded a torques (the "torc of honour"), a military decoration in the form of a twisted collar; the award made the barbarian ornament a Roman decoration, a striking instance of cultural borrowing in military honours.
  • Physical-science sense — rotational force. In physics and engineering, torque (from the same Latin torquēre, "to twist") is the measure of the tendency of a force to rotate a body about an axis — the product of the force and its lever arm (τ = r × F). It is the "twisting" effort: the turning force applied by an engine's crankshaft, a wrench on a nut, a steering wheel's rim. The unit is the newton-metre. This sense, coined in the nineteenth century, is now the most common use of the word in everyday technical speech ("engine torque," "torque wrench"), and it coexists with the archaeological sense without confusion.
  • Obsolete and rare verbal sense. As a verb, "to torque" means to twist or turn, and "torqued" is used (mostly in technical or poetic contexts) to mean twisted or coiled; the noun "torque" in the sense of a twist or turn is rare outside mechanics.
  • Symbolic and literary sense. In literature the torque is a charged object: a ring of precious metal that both honours and confines, an emblem of rank worn about the throat. Because it is stiff and massive — unlike a necklace, which hangs and dangles — it reads as engineered, ceremonial, talismanic: jewellery as insignia. In the passage the dragon likeness, the imperial emblem, doubles the torque's claim of lineage.
  • Spelling variants. Both "torque" and "torc" are current. "Torc" is the more recent archaeological spelling, modelled on the Celtic form and preferred by many archaeologists; "torque" is the older English form, inherited through French from Latin torques, and is also the form used for the physics sense.

Word-boundary discriminations. Against "necklace," which is any ornamental band hung about the neck, the torque is a rigid, massive, often twisted ring — it does not hang but encircles. Against "chain," it is a solid band, not a linked, flexible sequence of loops. Against "collar," which in jewellery names a snug band worn at the throat and in dress names an item of clothing, the torque is specifically a metal neck-ring of the ancient Celtic type. Against "choker," a close-fitting necklace, the torque is typically heavier and historical. Against "carcanet," a jeweled necklace or collar of Renaissance fashion, the torque belongs to Iron-Age rather than Renaissance metalwork. Against "gorget," an armour piece protecting the throat, the torque is an ornament of rank rather than defensive equipment. The word torque uniquely names the twisted metal neck-ring of antiquity — and, by homonymy, the rotational force of modern mechanics.
