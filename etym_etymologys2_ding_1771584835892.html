Derivation chain for DING (Sinitic; no PIE ancestry):
  • Old Chinese ding *tVong (Baxter-Sagart reconstruction, c. 1300-1000 BCE) "sacrificial cauldron, ritual tripod/quadripod vessel" - earliest attested in the oracle-bone inscriptions of the Shang dynasty (c. 1300-1046 BCE), where the character is a pictogram of a two-legged cauldron with a rounded body, used in royal divinations about sacrifice.
  • Bronze-script forms (Shang-Western Zhou, c. 11th-8th c. BCE) - the same pictogram engraved on the bronzes themselves.
  • Seal script (standardized under the Qin chancery, c. 3rd c. BCE) - the legs of the pictogram simplified.
  • Clerical script and standard script - giving the modern character form ding.
  • Middle Chinese *tieng "ding" (rising tone; c. 6th-7th c. CE) - the medieval reading, from which the Sino-Xenic loans derive: Sino-Japanese tei (on-reading) with native kun-reading kanae, Sino-Korean jeong, Sino-Vietnamese dinh, Cantonese ding2, Hokkien tiann.
  • Mandarin ding "ritual vessel; tripod" - the modern standard reading.
  • English loanword ding (19th century; earlier romanized ting in Wade-Giles, now ding in pinyin) "an ancient Chinese ritual bronze vessel".
  • DING (the form on this card) - the standard modern English spelling.
Note: this word is a purely Sinitic formation with no genetic link to Proto-Indo-European; no Sanskrit, Hindi, Latin, French, or Italian branches exist. It is also not the imitative English ding "the sound of a bell", nor the German Ding "thing", nor a toponym - those are unrelated homophones.
