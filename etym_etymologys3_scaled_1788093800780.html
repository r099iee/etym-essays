One spelling, three histories. Before the climb can be described, the student must be warned: English scale is a set of three unrelated words that happen to share a spelling. The climbing word goes back through Latin to an Indo-European verb of springing and leaping. The fish-scale word comes from a Germanic root meaning 'shell, husk, split'. And the graduated-ruler and weighing-pan words come from the Latin 'ladder' via two different images — a ladder-like row of marks on a ruler, and a shell-shaped balance pan. The history of 'scaled' in its climbing sense is the history of the first family, but the homonymy is part of the word's modern life and structures how speakers parse the form today.

The Indo-European root and Latin. The reconstructed root is *skend- (also written *skand-), 'to climb, spring, leap', reflected in Sanskrit skándati 'he leaps, springs' and, in Latin, in the everyday verb scandere 'to climb, mount, ascend' — the verb for climbing into a chariot, up a mountainside, or over a wall (a possible connection with Greek skándalon, 'stumbling-block, snare', exists but is debated, and is best kept out of the chain proper). Roman military prose uses scandere for the assault of walls, and from scandere Latin derived the instrument noun scala, 'ladder, flight of steps' (from a form like *scand-sla, 'instrument for climbing', with the cluster simplified); the plural scalae means specifically a scaling-ladder or a flight of stairs.

Through the Romance languages. From scala the Romance world built a new verb, *scalāre (reconstructed; not directly attested), 'to mount by a ladder', which surfaces in Old French escaler 'to climb' (attested from the twelfth century), in Middle and modern French escalader 'to scale', and in the French noun escalade, whence English escalade in the seventeenth century and, via a humorous 1900 blend with 'elevator', the commercial word escalator — so every moving staircase quietly commemorates the ancient ladder. English borrowed the Old French verb in the fourteenth century as scalen, dropping the initial e- as English habitually did with French es- words (compare state from estat, special from especial). Strikingly, English possessed the verb for two centuries before it borrowed the noun 'scale' in the sense 'ladder' (16th–17th centuries, from Italian scala or directly from Latin) — so in English the verb actually preceded the noun it seems to rest on.

The military life of 'scaled'. For its first three centuries in English, 'scaled' was above all a soldier's word. 'Scaling-ladders' are the fixed collocation of siege warfare from the Elizabethan period onward, and the verb recurs through campaign narratives: walls, citadels, and castle faces are scaled under fire. This is the semantic atmosphere that modern adventure and fantasy writing still taps — to scale something is to take a defended height by direct, determined ascent, which is exactly what Tolkien's goblins do to the Lonely Mountain in the source passage of this card.

Mountains, the sublime, and the upward metaphor. From the seventeenth century the verb widens from fortifications to natural heights, and with the rise of mountaineering in the nineteenth century — the age of the first great Alpine and Himalayan ascents — 'scaling' becomes the journalistic and rhetorical verb of record for the conquest of peaks. In the same centuries the vertical metaphor runs upward into ambition: 'scaling the heights' of office, achievement, or fame, an image whose classical pedigree (the ladder of advancement) Romantic culture turns into a moral commonplace. To scale the mountain is, in the period's idiom, to scale oneself.

Modern life and the takeover of the homonym. In present-day English the climbing sense of 'scaled' has retreated in speech, where 'climbed' rules, keeping a formal and heroic niche in news prose, adventure writing, and fantasy fiction. Meanwhile the graduated-measure family has exploded through technology and business: 'scale up', 'scale down', 'scaled infrastructure', 'economies of scale' — the 'ladder of marks' image (English 'scale', 1550s; verb 'to scale (a drawing)', nineteenth century; 'scale model', twentieth century) now dominates the word's statistical life. A modern reader meeting 'scaled' in a sentence will often parse 'resized proportionally' before recalling the mountain: a genuine shift in the word's centre of gravity, driven by maps, models, spreadsheets, and servers rather than by climbing.

Sound and form. Phonetically the loan is transparent and stable: Old French escaler to English scale /skeɪl/, with the Middle English open-syllable lengthening giving the modern vowel; the past 'scaled' /skeɪld/ sits among its homonyms from the fish-scale and measure families as a model case of convergent spelling in the history of English vocabulary — three etymologies wearing one skin.
