Flanks (singular flank) are the sides of a body, formation or object. The word has three layered senses. (1) The fleshy side of a person's or animal's trunk between the ribs and the hip. (2) The left or right side of a military force, fortification or line of battle — the sides most exposed to attack. (3) By extension, the side of any elongated thing: a mountain, a building, a column of marchers, a glacier. Tolkien's "His flanks were unguarded" fuses (1) and (2): the great wolf-beast's weak sides are left open, exactly as a general who leaves his army's flanks uncovered invites disaster.

  • Anatomical sense. In a quadruped the flank is the soft hollow between the last ribs and the hip — the part that heaves after galloping, the spot a hunter aims at as the most vulnerable point of a running beast; in humans the flank is the side of the abdomen over the loins and kidneys, the seat of "flank pain" in renal colic and a standard descriptive location in clinical examination and in anatomical atlases of both species.
  • Military sense. Because pre-modern infantry fought in deep formations, the side of an army was its least defensible direction; the classic winning manoeuvre is to "turn" or "attack the flank" — a flank attack — while "to flank" (in English military vocabulary by the late 16th century) means either to guard a side or to pass around it in order to strike it. Fortifications were built with "flanking" bastions whose guns enfiladed the curtain walls; "flanking fire" is fire that rakes a line from the side; modern doctrine still speaks of flank security, flank guards and the exposed flank of an advance.
  • Extended and technical senses. The flank of a mountain, glacier or building is its side; in zoology and field guides the flank is a standard measurement and description point; "flank steak" (19th century) is the butcher's cut from the cow's belly side, prized for carpaccio and marinated grilling; rugby's "flanker" and "open-side flanker" (late 19th century) are the forwards who guard the sides of the scrum; the US Navy's "flank speed" (20th century) means maximum speed, from the mechanical "flank" — the last, extreme notch of the engine-order telegraph.
  • Figurative use. "The flanks" of an argument, a market, a political movement or a journey are their exposed, weaker sides; "covering one's flanks" means protecting oneself against unnoticed attack; "to be flanked by" is simply to have something on either side ("flanked by mountains", "flanked by bodyguards"). The permanent implication is vulnerability: a flank exists to be defended or turned, and the enemy of the word's military sense is never far from the metaphor.

Semantic bridge and form. The word travels from the anatomist's side of the body (Old French flanc) to the soldier's side (16th c.), then to any lateral surface, then to a verb of positioning ("flanked by...") and finally to an abstract metaphor of exposure; the constant across every stage is lateralness plus exposure. Formally "flank" doubles as noun and verb (flank / flanks / flanked / flanking): the verb means both "stand at the side of" and "attack from the side", a duality the military and the anatomical senses share, since both concern the side one must defend. The plural flanks, as in our context, is the default form when the sides of a body are viewed as a pair.
