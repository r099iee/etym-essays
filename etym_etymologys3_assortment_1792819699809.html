The assortment now piled in every holiday hall began in ancient Rome with the casting of lots — a journey from fate to chocolate boxes that passes through French wardrobes and English counting-houses.

Latin lots: The ultimate ancestor is Latin sors, sortis, meaning a lot, share, or portion decided by casting — the little tablet or pebble that assigned land, duty, or destiny. From the lot-cast came the idea of the allotted share, and from the share came the idea of the kind: things that fall together belong together. Late Latin already used sortire for obtaining by lot and, by natural extension, for choosing and arranging. The Indo-European root behind it, *ser-, carried senses of lining up, joining, and arranging in a row — order imposed on multiplicity — which fits the sorting story exactly.

French sorting: Old French built assorter from the prefix a- (to, toward) plus sorte (kind, manner, lot): literally to bring things to their proper kinds, to match and mate goods, clothes, and even marriage partners. A well-assorted wardrobe was one where everything matched; ill-assorted companions were a mismatch. The verb belonged to the practical world of drapers, stewards, and households — people whose business was making variety orderly.

  • From fate to kind: the lot that destiny casts becomes the category into which goods are sorted.
  • Matching as making: assorter means assembling the right companions, whether socks or spouses.
  • Order in variety: the root’s sense of lining up survives in every sorted shelf.

English commerce: Seventeenth-century English borrowed the verb as assort — to distribute into sorts — and formed assortment with the productive suffix -ment, which names both acts and their products. Early uses still meant the sorting itself, but the result sense won: the gathered variety on the counter. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century trade made it ubiquitous — assortments of cloth, of tea, of hardware — and the adjective assorted marched onto packets everywhere, promising the buyer one of each kind.

Modern festivity: The twentieth century put the word in fancy dress: assorted chocolates, assorted biscuits, and the great piled assortment of packages and parcels in the source sentence. What began as lots cast for Roman farmers now describes the happiest heaps of modern life — proof that sorting things into kinds was always, at bottom, a way of multiplying delight.

Choice and science: Modern commerce turned the assortment into a discipline: retailers plan assortments by season, region, and shelf-space, balancing breadth against the paradox that too much choice paralyses buyers. Psychologists study the assortment effect — shoppers buy more from a well-varied display and less from an overwhelming one — so the old shopkeeper’s instinct now wears statistical dress. Logisticians assort warehouses, streaming services assort catalogues, and the source sentence’s towering pile remains the purest image: abundance heaped high, daring everyone to choose.

  • Assortment planning: the retail science of which kinds, in which quantities, on which shelves.
  • Psychology of variety: perceived assortment shapes desire as much as the goods themselves.
