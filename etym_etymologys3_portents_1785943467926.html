The history of PORTENTS carries a single vivid image from the Indo-European world all the way to modern journalism: the image of something stretched forward across the path of the future. Every modern use of the word — the comet over a doomed court, the market crash "portending" recession — is the descendant of a Roman augur reading an unnatural sign that the gods were believed to have laid out, like a rope or a hand, in front of the human community.

The Indo-European foundation of "stretching":

  • The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch, extend, pull taut, spread out". This is one of the most productive roots of the Indo-European family: Greek teinein "to stretch" (and tonos "tension"), Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches" (and tantra, the stretched warp of a loom), Latin tenere "to hold" and tendere "to stretch", and ultimately English words as diverse as tendon, tenet, tense, tent, and tend. The root is a physical one — rope, hide, sail, and bowstring all "stretch" — and that physicality is the deep ancestor of the abstract idea of a sign "stretched forth" toward the future.
  • The Latin verb tendere "to stretch" descends directly from *ten-. In Roman daily life it was used of pitching a tent (tendere tabernaculum), stretching a net for hunting, setting a trap, aiming a weapon, and extending a hand. To "stretch toward" someone was already, in the Republic, to direct attention or intention outward.

Roman augury and the birth of the portent:

  • Latin combined tendere with the archaic prefix por-, the old form of pro-, "before, forward, forth" — itself from PIE *per-/*prō "before, forward, in front". The compound portendere meant literally "to stretch forth in front of oneself", and in the language of religion and law it acquired a specialised meaning: "to signify in advance, to foretell, to portend". A portended sign is one that has been physically extended across the horizon for observers to read.
  • Roman state religion institutionalised the reading of such signs. The noun portentum, the neuter past participle of portendere, named the sign itself: an eclipse, a lightning strike on the temple, the birth of a two-headed calf, a rain of stones. These were prodigia — "prodigies" — which broke the expected order of nature and were treated as communications from the gods of impending disaster or dynastic change. The Senate ordered rituals of expiation (supplicationes, lustration) to avert the ill omen. The surviving record — Livy, Cicero's De Divinatione, Julius Obsequens' book of prodigies — is a running chronicle of the portents by which Roman society interpreted the collapse of the Republic and the rise of the emperors.
  • It is important that portentum was a neutral-sounding participle turned noun: the "thing stretched forth". Its range included both the general "omen, sign, token" and the specific "monstrous or portentous birth, prodigy". Even in Latin, the word already carried the two futures of the English term — the natural sign and the unnatural marvel.

The journey to English: medieval and early modern channels:

  • Latin portentum entered the learned vocabulary of medieval Europe directly, as a term of classical rhetoric, history, and religion. In medieval chronicles, celestial phenomena continued to be read as portents — comets over Hastings in 1066, eclipses before the death of kings — and the Latin word was the standard instrument of that interpretation.
  • Unlike many English words of classical origin, portent was borrowed in the 16th century directly from Latin rather than through French (compare the parallel French portente, which did not take root). The first recorded English uses date to c. 1570, in the poetry of Edmund Spenser, who used the word exactly in its Latin sense: a warning sign of coming catastrophe, often celestial. The plural portents, used on this card, was from the start the natural form for chroniclers gathering a sequence of signs.
  • The 16th and 17th centuries were the great age of the English portent. Shakespeare makes portents surround the assassination of Julius Caesar — lions in the streets, the dead walking, comets, blood on the Capitol — and surrounds the murder of Duncan in Macbeth with the darkness, the storm, and the unnatural horses that "ate each other". The word sat at the meeting point of ancient Roman religion, medieval chronicle, and Tudor political anxiety, where regicide and civil war were expected to announce themselves in the heavens.

Socio-cultural factors and semantic drift:

  • As astronomical and biological knowledge grew after the Scientific Revolution, the literal reading of portents receded from elite culture. Comets were shown to be physical objects; monstrous births were increasingly medical rather than theological events. The word did not die; it migrated inward. Portent became the literary and rhetorical name for any grave, atmosphere-laden sign of change, whether supernatural or merely figurative.
  • In the 19th and 20th centuries the semantic centre shifted from the supernatural to the figurative. Political commentators speak of "portents of war", economists of "portents of recession", journalists of the "dark portents" of a crisis. What the portent has lost in cosmic authority it has gained in range: the word now covers any dramatic, publicly legible indicator that a momentous transformation is at hand.
  • The adjective portentous split in an interesting way. Originally "full of portent, ominously significant", it developed in the 17th century the secondary, derogatory sense "self-importantly solemn, pompous, pretentiously grave" — a semantic slide that shows how the language treats a word once freighted with divine warning. The verb portend, likewise, moved from "to signify as an omen" to the everyday "to point toward, to foreshadow".
  • Sound and form have changed little since the borrowing: Latin portentum became English portent with the final syllable truncated in the 16th century, and the plural portents is simply the regular -s plural. The word remains pronounced /ˈpɔː.tənt/ (singular) and /ˈpɔː.tənts/ (plural), keeping the stressed Latin first syllable. It is this stability of form, combined with the constant renewal of sense, that lets a Roman augural term sit naturally in a sentence about stock markets or storm warnings.
