Pleasantries are good-humoured, polite, inconsequential remarks exchanged to smooth social transitions. The word is almost always used in the plural; the singular pleasantry names one such remark. In the literary context of this card, the word functions as a small but crucial piece of ritual machinery. Lady Wang has been sitting with the matriarch and the assembled women; now she "obediently rose to her feet and, after exchanging a few __________, went out, taking Li Wan and Wang Xi-feng with her." The word marks the departure as graceful rather than abrupt: instead of simply leaving, the senior lady trades a few courteous remarks first, settling the social account before withdrawing with the younger women. The narrator records these pleasantries as one would record a recognized genre of speech — scripted, unmemorable, and socially indispensable. The literary tone is gently observant and faintly ironic: the pleasantries are noted as an object of interest in themselves, a ritual whose very forgettability is its point. Because the word carries its Latin root of "pleasing" inside it, it quietly underscores the hierarchy of the scene: the pleasantries are performed before obedience is enacted, so that the departure seems voluntary and gracious rather than commanded. In the rigid social world of a great household, the exchange of pleasantries is the oil that lets the gears of rank and obligation turn without friction.

The senses of pleasantries are few but worth distinguishing precisely:

  • Primary modern sense: polite, trivial remarks — the courteous small-talk of greeting and parting: remarks about the weather, health, the family, exchanged to acknowledge another's presence and smooth the transition into or out of a conversation. "They exchanged pleasantries about the weather before getting down to business."
  • Older sense: jests, witty remarks — in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a pleasantry could be a joke or witty sally, a piece of humour designed to please. The word's history runs from "jest" toward "polite remark," and the older sense survives in phraseological fossils and in literary irony.
  • As a count noun, singular — one polite or witty remark: "He made some pleasantry about the rain." The singular is now rarer than the plural but still current in literary English.
  • In social ritual and etiquette — "to exchange pleasantries" is a fixed formula for the scripted courtesies that open and close social encounters; the phrase is nearly always plural and carries a faint note of obligation — the pleasantries are what one is expected to exchange before business, before departure, before obedience.
  • Figurative and ironic uses — the word is often used ironically of conversation that is deliberately empty: pleasantries as the currency of insincerity, the talk that fills time without risk. The card's sentence trades on this irony: the pleasantries are exchanged, then obedience is enacted, and the two are felt to belong to the same ritual of hierarchy.

The semantic history is a study in the softening of words. The word comes, through French plaisanterie, from the Latin verb plācēre, "to please" — itself descended from an Indo-European root meaning "to be flat, level, smooth, calm," so that "pleasing" is etymologically "smoothing over." A pleasantry is literally "a pleasing remark," and the word's drift from "jest" (a witty remark that pleases) to "polite small talk" (a courteous remark that pleases) records the history of politeness itself: society moves from wit to smoothness, from the sally that amuses to the courtesy that oils. The word arrived in English in the mid-seventeenth century, was common in the eighteenth as a term for light jests, and by the nineteenth had settled into its modern sense of courteous triviality. The plural pleasantries, fixed in the formula "to exchange pleasantries," is the dominant modern form.

In contemporary English the word is mildly formal and slightly ironic, at home in literary prose, journalism, and descriptions of formal social life. It is used of the courtesies that frame diplomacy, business, and ceremony — the remarks exchanged "before getting down to business" — and it carries a built-in acknowledgment that such remarks are both indispensable and forgettable. The card's sentence shows the word at its most characteristic: the pleasantries are the prelude to obedience, and their very emptiness is what makes the hierarchy run smoothly.
