halibuts — In Prince Caspian, Trufflehunter’s exclamation “Horns and halibuts!” is a mild oath, a comic substitute for a stronger, potentially blasphemous phrase. The plural “halibuts” is the key comic stroke: the speaker calls on a pair of great flatfish exactly as a human oath-monger might call on sacred or monstrous things. The word is deliberately mundane, earthy, and enormous — the halibut is the largest of all flatfishes, a cold-water giant that can weigh hundreds of pounds — so the oath is both absurd and warm-hearted, and it quietly celebrates the natural abundance of the Narnian world, where the very fish of the sea can be invoked in surprise. The tone is robust, jocular, and folk-like: Trufflehunter is a Talking Beast of the woods, and his “holy fish” sits naturally beside his “horns.”

Primary sense. A halibut is a large, edible, right-eyed flatfish of the family Pleuronectidae, most notably the Atlantic halibut (Hippoglossus hippoglossus) and the Pacific halibut (Hippoglossus stenolepis). A flatfish lives its adult life lying on one side on the sea bed, with both eyes on the upper side of its head; the halibut is the largest of them, growing to several metres in length and hundreds of kilograms in weight in exceptional cases. The plural here is the regular English plural halibuts (the fish is also treated as an invariant mass noun, “two halibut”). The word is used of the fish in both zoological and culinary contexts: firm, white-fleshed, mildly sweet, and highly prized as food.

Polysemy & secondary senses.

  • Culinary sense: halibut is a premium food fish, sold as steaks, fillets, and cheeks; “grilled halibut,” “pan-seared halibut,” and “halid” are standard in restaurants; the fish is prized for its dense, white, low-oil flesh.
  • The exclamation “Horns and halibuts!”: a Narnian folk-oath; the word is drawn into the class of comically mundane expletives used to express surprise, shock, or strong feeling, substituting a harmless natural object for a profane name. It is exactly parallel to “Good heavens!” or “By gum!”
  • Commercial and fisheries jargon: “halibut” names a major commercial species with its own management regime, quota, and stock; “halibut fishery,” “Icelandic halibut,” “Greenland halibut” (a related species, Reinhardtius hippoglossoides, also called turbot in some markets) are everyday trade terms.
  • Historical sense — the holy flatfish: the name literally means “holy flatfish”: the fish was so abundant and sustaining that it was permitted on holy days and fast days when meat was forbidden, and the very name encodes that religious licence.
  • Metaphoric / colloquial use: because of its size, “halibut” can stand loosely for anything huge and flat, and the plural “halibuts” in the oath suggests a comic multiplicity of giant fish.

Register and usage. “Halibut” is a plain, workaday word — neither literary nor slang — but it is regionally weighted toward cold northern seas and their fishing communities (Atlantic Canada, Iceland, Norway, the North Pacific). Its register has shifted over centuries from “common food of the poor” to “premium restaurant fish,” tracking the decline of the stocks. The plural form halibuts is worth noting because many fish-names are invariant in plural (salmon, trout, fish), while halibut freely takes the regular -s plural, especially when individual fish are counted; this regularity is what makes the comic plural in Lewis’s oath land so neatly.

Historical bridge. The semantic bridge is religious: the Middle English name halybutte meant “holy + flatfish,” a fish so useful for feeding people on holy days and fast days that its very name celebrated its licence. From that origin the word has moved through ordinary fishing and eating to become, in the Narnian world, a token of the abundance and rightness of the natural creation — something solid and good to swear by.
