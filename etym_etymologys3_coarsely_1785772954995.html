COARSELY is a word whose history is a story of semantic decline disguised as a spelling change: it began as the word for "a running" or "a course" — an image of orderly motion — and ended as the adverb for rough texture and vulgar manners. Its biography shows how "ordinary" quality becomes "inferior" quality, how cloth and grain become metaphors for human refinement, and how one word split into two spellings. 1. The Indo-European root: running. The chain begins with the Indo-European root *kers- "to run," which produced Latin currere "to run" and, from its supine stem, the noun cursus "a running, a course." In Latin, cursus already carried the extended senses of "a way, a journey, a career, an order, a sequence" — the "course" of events, of a river, of a life. This is the meaning at the root: things that proceed along their ordinary path. 2. French and the ordinary course. Latin cursus developed into Old French cors/cours, and Middle English borrowed it around 1300 as course: a path, a way, a customary order. Crucially, course was used for the "ordinary course" of things — the customary, everyday quality of goods. Sixteenth-century writers could speak of "course cloth" or goods "of the common course," meaning cloth and goods of ordinary, everyday quality, as opposed to the fine and costly kind. At this stage the word was neutral: "common" meant "usual," not yet "bad." 3. The slide from "ordinary" to "rough." The pejorative turn is a textbook case of semantic degradation (pejoration). Because ordinary, everyday cloth was made of rougher wool, and ordinary fare was plainer and coarser than luxury goods, the word for "of the ordinary course" came to imply "of rough, inferior quality." By the sixteenth century coarse described texture: coarse cloth, coarse meal, coarse sand — things with large grains or rough fibers. The social application followed naturally: rough goods implied rough manners, and coarse came to mean "vulgar, unrefined, rude" when applied to people, speech, and behavior. The card's word coarsely still carries both: the recipe's "coarsely ground grain" (physical) and the moralist's "coarsely spoken" (social). 4. The spelling split of the sixteenth century. As the senses diverged, English speakers began to distinguish them in spelling. The path/sequence noun kept the spelling course (a course of study, a racecourse, "of course"); the quality adjective settled on coarse (coarse grain, coarse manners). This spelling split — completed in the sixteenth century — is the visible signature of a semantic split that had already happened in the language. The two forms, once one word, became near-homophones that still confuse spellers today: course and coarse, coarsely and courses. 5. The adverb and its two lives. The adverb coarsely, formed with -ly in the late sixteenth century, inherits both halves of the adjective's career:
  • Physical and technical life: coarsely ground, milled, chopped, woven, textured — a precise, value-neutral technical adverb used in cooking, milling, textiles, and materials science, where it is the standard opposite of finely. The card's watermill sentence ("coarsely milled ... leaving noticeable husk fragments") is squarely in this tradition: the physical fact of large fragments is the whole meaning.
  • Social and moral life: coarsely spoken, laughed, jested, behaved — rude, vulgar, lacking refinement; the pejorative sense in literature, satire, and moral description. "He laughed coarsely at the joke" means the laugh betrayed bad breeding or brutishness.
6. Cultural logic of the mapping. The mapping of physical coarseness onto moral coarseness is ancient and cross-cultural: fine/coarse is a universal scale for both matter and manners, so that "coarse" language is to "fine" language what coarse sand is to fine sand. The word thus participates in the deep metaphor that refinement is a kind of fineness — a metaphor that gives coarsely its power in moral description and its precise neutrality in cooking. 7. Sound and form in English. Coarsely (RP /ˈkɔːs.li/) rhymes loosely with "hoarse-ly," and its vowel is the open mid back vowel of "course." The spelling retains the -ea- that now distinguishes it from course; the -ly suffix marks it as the adverb. In modern usage the physical sense is far more common in everyday speech (recipe instructions), while the social sense is more common in literary and formal prose; both remain fully alive, two branches of a single word that once simply meant "a running.
