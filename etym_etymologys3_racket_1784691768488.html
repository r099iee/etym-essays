The story of racket is the story of a word that began as a noise and ended, in one of its branches, as a crime — and of a homograph that has nothing whatever to do with either. The noun in the sense of loud, disorderly disturbance first appears in English in the 1560s, in the middle of the Elizabethan period, where it designated riotous frolic and noisy merrymaking. Its origin lies in an expressive, imitative formation — a word built to sound like what it describes — from a cluster of similar terms for clattering and rattling found across the Germanic languages, such as Middle Dutch racken, 'to make a noise', and English rattle, itself an imitative word that entered the language from Middle Dutch ratelen. Scholars have also proposed influence from Gaelic racaid, 'riot, noise, disorderly play', which would have reached English through Anglo-Irish contact in the sixteenth century; whether or not it is the ultimate source, it plausibly reinforced both the sound and the sense of the English word. The key point, philologically, is that the word is expressive: it is a sound-word, and its meaning is written in its phonetics, which is why no Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it and why none should be invented. From this starting point the word's meaning developed by steady extension. In the seventeenth century racket came to refer to the confused, clashing din made by many things breaking or striking together, and then, by a further extension, to any boisterous disturbance of the peace. Throughout it retained a tone of complaint: a racket is never simply loud — it is loud in a way that annoys someone, that disturbs the peace or the concentration of a hearer. The verb to racket, 'to make a racket', 'to live a noisy, gay, dissipated social life', is recorded from the same period and long served as a genteel word for youthful dissipation; the phrase 'to kick up a racket' belongs to the eighteenth century. This is the register in which Bilbo uses the word in The Hobbit, chapter 2: he 'had sniffed more than once at what he called "all this dwarvish racket"' — the dwarves' singing, jingling gear, and heavy boots strike his sensitive hobbit ears as a breach of the hush he loves. The narrator's aside immediately deflates the complaint with a joke about relative perception: 'though I don't suppose you or I would have noticed anything at all on a windy night, not if the whole cavalcade had passed two feet off.' The 'racket' exists largely in the ear of the beholder, and the word performs both a character sketch and a quiet comedy of scale. The twentieth century brought the famous semantic leap from sound to crime. In early twentieth-century American English, racket came to mean a dishonest scheme or fraudulent business, most notoriously in the 'protection racket', the systematic extortion of money in exchange for 'protection'. The bridge is the older colloquial sense of racket as 'a line of work, a way of life', which American slang developed from the noise-word via the idea of a busy, bustling, money-making affair; by the 1920s a racket was specifically an illegitimate one. From it, American English coined the agent noun racketeer (1920s), formed with the suffix -eer on the pattern of buccaneer and pioneer — a word that travelled the world and made the process respectable enough to generate such derivatives as to racketeer and racketeering, adopted into police, journalistic, and eventually international legal usage. Meanwhile the phrase 'to stand the racket' — to pay the cost, to take the consequences — kept the older, honourable sense of the word alive into the twentieth century. The homograph. The sporting implement — the racket or racquet of tennis, squash, badminton, and racquetball — is a completely separate word that merely happens to be spelled and pronounced the same. It descends from Arabic rāḥat (or rāḥah), 'palm of the hand', so called because the earliest form of the implement was literally the gloved hand, and then a board strapped to the hand, used to strike the ball; the word passed into Old French as rachete or rechette in the fourteenth century (the period of the game's first codification at French courts), into Italian as racchetta, and into Middle English as raket in the fifteenth century. Modern English preserves both spellings, the French-influenced racquet (favoured in sports like squash and racquetball) and the assimilated racket. The two words thus share a phonetic identity but no etymology at all: the noise-word is imitative and native, the implement-word is a loan from Arabic by way of Old French and Italian. The wider cultural history is worth a paragraph. Racket the noise-word is a witness to the sixteenth-century English taste for expressive, onomatopoeic vocabulary and to the noise-life of the early modern town, where public merrymaking was both ubiquitous and regulated — a racket was what your rowdy neighbours made, and the reproachful colouring the word has carried ever since is the fossil of those complaints. Racket the crime-word is a witness to a very different era, the urban America of Prohibition and the rise of organised crime, and it came into English at the exact moment when the word's older sense of 'a busy, lively, money-making line' could be re-read as 'an illegitimate one'. Between these poles, the word has served as a neutral colloquial name for an occupation ('what's your racket?') and, in the phrase 'the whole racket', for the whole business or affair of anything. The coexistence of the imitative noise-word, the criminal sense, the occupational sense, and the Arabic-derived implement — four meanings in one phonetic shell — makes racket one of the most instructive homograph collisions in the English dictionary. Milestones in brief: - 1560s: racket 'noisy play, riotous merrymaking' first recorded (expressive/onomatopoeic; possible Gaelic racaid reinforcement). - 17th century: 'loud, confused din'; 'boisterous disturbance'; verb to racket. - 18th century: 'to kick up a racket'; 'to stand the racket' (pay the cost). - 14th–15th century (separate chain): Arabic rāḥat → Old French rachete → Middle English raket → racket/racquet, the sports implement. - Early 20th century: American racket 'dishonest scheme'; protection racket; racketeer (1920s). - 1937: Bilbo's 'all this dwarvish racket' in The Hobbit.
