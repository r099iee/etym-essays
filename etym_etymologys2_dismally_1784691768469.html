PIE (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE): *dyēw- / *dyeu- "bright sky, daylight; the sky-god" — the root of Latin diēs "day"; collateral reflexes include Greek Zeus, Sanskrit dyāúṣ "sky, heaven", and Hittite šiwatt- "day". Latin (2nd–1st c. BCE): diēs "day" — a fifth-declension noun from PIE *dyēw-; related Latin forms from the same root include deus "god" and Iuppiter "sky-father". Latin (origin of the adjective debated): malus "bad, evil, wicked" — the second element of the phrase; its etymology is disputed: some scholars derive it from a PIE *mel- "to deceive, do wrong", but no secure agreement exists, and an origin outside the core Indo-European vocabulary cannot be excluded. Late Latin / medieval calendar Latin: diēs malī "evil, unlucky days" — the two inauspicious days of each month, the so-called "Egyptian days", marked as calamitous in the almanacs of the late antique and medieval periods. Anglo-Norman (13th c.): dis mal "the unlucky days" — the phrase carried into insular French and then into English. Middle English (c. 1300): dismal — first a noun, "the unpropitious days of the month" (whence the later "dismal days" of folklore); then an adjective, "unlucky, calamitous, gloomy". Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): dismal — the adjective "dreary, cheerless, gloomy", the calendar sense fading from living use. Early Modern English (16th c.): dismally — dismal + -ly, the native Germanic suffix that forms manner-adverbs from adjectives; recorded from the sixteenth century. Modern English (17th c. to present): dismally "in a gloomy, depressing, or wretchedly disheartening manner". NOTE ON DEBATE: The Latin adjective malus has no universally accepted etymology; the link to PIE *mel- "to deceive, do wrong" is plausible but not established, so the ancestry of the -mally element is less secure than that of the dies element. The English adverb itself is a native formation (dismal + the Germanic suffix -ly), not a borrowing.
