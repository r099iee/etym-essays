- Classical Antiquity to Late Latin (1st–4th c. CE) - Latin *ineffabilis* (*in-*, "not" + *effabilis*, "speakable", from *effari*, "to speak out") was coined to translate Greek *arrhētos* and *anektālētos* in philosophical (Neoplatonic) and theological texts to denote the unspeakable nature of God.
- Middle English (c. 1380s–1400) - Introduced via Old French into theological and mystical writings (Wycliffite Bible, Chaucer's *Boece*, Walter Hilton) specifically for the "ineffable name" of God (the Tetragrammaton) and divine celestial joy.
- Renaissance & Early Modern (16th–17th c.) - Maintained its central role in apophatic (negative) theology (Pseudo-Dionysian tradition), while poets (Milton, Donne) applied it to transcendent spiritual and romantic mysteries.
- 19th Century (Romanticism & Transcendentalism) - Broadened beyond theology into aesthetic theory; Wordsworth, Shelley, and Emerson used *ineffable* to describe the sublime majesty of nature, deep emotional communion, and musical power.
- 20th Century - Embraced by philosophy of language (Wittgenstein's famous boundary: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent") and literary theory (Roland Barthes on reading ecstasy) to denote experiential reality exceeding semiotic codification.
- 21st Century - Continues in high-register literary prose, aesthetic philosophy, and contemplative spirituality to denote profound experiences that transcend linguistic formulation.
