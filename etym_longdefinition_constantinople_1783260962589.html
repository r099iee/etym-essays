Constantinople (proper noun) — the imperial capital of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire for over a millennium, founded by the Emperor Constantine the Great in 330 CE on the site of the ancient Greek colony of Byzantium, officially styled "New Rome," and captured by the Ottoman Turks under Mehmed II in 1453, after which it became the Ottoman capital (modern Istanbul). For roughly a thousand years it was the wealthiest and most populous city of Christendom, the seat of the Orthodox patriarchate, and the crossroads of European and Asian trade, diplomacy, and learning. CORE SENSES
  • The Byzantine capital. The primary sense: Constantinople was the capital from 330 to 1453, home of the Byzantine emperors, the imperial bureaucracy, the Ecumenical Patriarch, the great palace and hippodrome, and the churches of Hagia Sophia, the Holy Apostles, and the Blachernae.
  • The imperial foundation. Named "the City of Constantine" in Greek (Konstantinoupolis), it was Constantine's deliberate new capital — a Christian, Greek-speaking New Rome on the Bosporus, refounded on the site of Byzantium and dedicated, according to tradition, in 330.
  • The Queen of Cities. The city's own epithets became its meaning: "the Queen of Cities" (Basilissa ton poleon), "the New Rome," "the City" (he Polis) par excellence — so dominant in the Greek world that "the City" alone meant Constantinople.
  • Symbol of Christendom's fortress. Protected by the Theodosian walls and the sea, Constantinople stood for centuries as the unbreachable bastion of Orthodox Christendom, besieged again and again (Avars, Arabs, Bulgars, Rus, Crusaders) and falling only twice — to the Fourth Crusade in 1204 and finally to the Ottomans in 1453.
SECONDARY AND MODERN MEANINGS
  • Metonymic sense. Constantinople stands metonymically for the Byzantine Empire itself, for the Orthodox church, and for Greek imperial learning — "Constantinople fell" means the whole Eastern Roman world ended.
  • The Ecumenical Patriarchate. Even today "Constantinople" in ecclesiastical usage names the Ecumenical Patriarchate and its see (the Phanar), so the word survives as an institutional title long after the political fall.
  • Scholarly-historical sense. In modern academic writing Constantinople refers specifically to the Byzantine millennium (330-1453), while Istanbul is the name of the living modern city; historians use the two names to separate periods of the same site.
  • Popular-culture sense. The 1953 song "Istanbul (Not Constantinople)" by The Four Lovers (later the Four Seasons) and its many covers made the old name a playful cultural reference; "Constantinople" now carries nostalgic, ironic, or historical romance in music and fiction.
  • The Grand Strategy sense. In strategic and historical commentary Constantinople is the archetype of the "city of the world's desire" — the choke-point where continents, seas, and civilizations meet — a byword for geopolitical location.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE The name means, etymologically, "the City of Constantine," from Greek Konstantinos and polis "city," and its whole semantic history grows from that single founding act. A Greek colony named Byzantium became the Roman world's new capital; the personal name of its founder became a place name; the place became the Byzantine Empire in miniature; and the toponym then accumulated a millennium of associations — wealth, holiness, siege, art, fall — until "Constantinople" names not just a city but an era, an ideal, and a memory, distinct now from the Turkish Istanbul that occupies the same ground.
