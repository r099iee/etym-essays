Latvian is the adjective and demonym of Latvia, a Baltic state on the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea, and the name of its official language. The word refers to a modern nation, a people, and an East Baltic language that is closely related to Lithuanian but not mutually intelligible with it.

Primary sense: the language

Latvian (latviešu valoda) is spoken by roughly 1.5 to 2 million people, chiefly in Latvia, with diaspora communities in the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States, Canada, Australia and Brazil. It is an East Baltic language, written in a Latin alphabet with diacritics (ā, ē, ī, ū, ņ, ķ, ļ, ģ, š, ž, č, ğ), and it preserves a pitch accent and a system of long and short vowels that are important in historical Baltic phonology.

Secondary sense: the people and the state

As a demonym, Latvian denotes a citizen of the Republic of Latvia or a member of the Latvian ethnic group. The modern state declared independence on 18 November 1918, was annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940, occupied by Nazi Germany in 1941–1944, re-annexed in 1944, and restored independence on 21 August 1991. It joined the European Union and NATO in 2004.

Historical senses

  • The Baltic provinces — before 1918 Latvians lived in Courland, Livonia (Vidzeme) and Latgale, administered successively by the Livonian Order, Poland-Lithuania, Sweden and Russia.
  • Lettish — the older English name, from German Lettisch; it is now archaic but survives in older books, maps and library catalogues.
  • Latgalian — the eastern variety, sometimes treated as a separate language; “Latvian” in the narrow sense excludes it, while the state recognises it as a historical variety.

Linguistic and technical senses

In linguistics, Latvian is classified as an East Baltic language alongside Lithuanian, with which it shares a common ancestor but from which it diverged early. Its dialects are usually divided into Livonian (Tāmnieku), Middle and High Latvian (Latgalian), and the standard language is based on the Middle dialect. Livonian, a Finnic language once spoken along the coast, left a substrate influence on vocabulary and phonology and is now effectively extinct as a native language.

Cultural and figurative senses

In cultural writing “Latvian” evokes the song festivals (Dziesmu svētki), the collected folk songs of Krišjānis Barons, the plays of Rainis and Aspazija, the Art Nouveau architecture of Riga, and the amber of the Courland coast. In the diaspora the word carries a strong émigré connotation, especially for the generation that fled in 1944.

Semantic bridges

The word moved from a regional and tribal designation, through the nineteenth-century national revival, to the name of a sovereign state in 1918. The replacement of Lettish by Latvian in English is itself a semantic event: it signalled that the country was naming itself rather than being named by its German-speaking neighbours.
