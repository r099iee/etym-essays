Whit denotes the smallest imaginable amount or particle of something — a jot, a scrap, a mote. In modern English it survives almost exclusively inside negative idioms: not a whit ("not at all, to not the slightest degree"), no whit, and the rarer never a whit. Etymologically it is the weakened form of Old English wiht, "a thing, a creature", so that not a whit means literally "not a thing" — a quantity of nothing. In the poem from The Story of the Stone describing Bao-yu, "Yet not a whit cared he how folk might jeer!" the couplet has already piled up a list of the boy's delinquencies — "harum-scarum", "doltish", "acts outlandish", "nature queer" — precisely so that the final line can measure his indifference to them: the world may jeer, and he will not care by even one particle. The word's nuance is one of absolute, quantified negation: not "he cared little", but "the amount of his caring was zero — not even a whit". Primary senses and usage
  • Not a whit — not at all, to not the smallest degree: "I don't care a whit what they say." This is the living sense, always negated.
  • No whit / never a whit — the older and more literary variants of the same idiom, common in verse and formal prose.
  • Not a whit the worse / better — the idiomatic comparative construction: "not a whit the worse for wear", meaning "not the least bit worse".
  • A whit (rare, affirmative) — the bare noun "a very small amount" appears only in deliberately archaic or negated contexts; one does not normally say "a whit of bread".
Polysemy and the historical bridge Whit is a case of radical narrowing. The Old English noun wiht meant "creature, being, thing, person" — one of the most common words in the language, used of warriors and monsters alike. In Middle English the full form survived as wight ("a person, a creature", kept alive by Chaucer and later revived in fantasy literature), while a weakened reduced form whit came to mean "a tiny thing, the least particle". The noun drifted "thing" → "little thing" → "least particle" → "a quantity of nothing", and as the full noun was replaced by thing (from Old Norse), whit survived only where it had fused with negation — hence not a whit. The same root, combined with nā "no", produced Old English nāwiht "nothing", the ancestor of modern English not, so that the ordinary negator of English and the literary whit share one ancestry. Register and use In register, whit is bookish and faintly archaic, lending a line of verse or formal prose an intensified emphasis that plain "not at all" has lost. It belongs to a small family of "very small amount" nouns: jot (from Greek iōta, the smallest letter of the Greek alphabet) and iota are its closest partners — "not a jot", "not one iota" — and, like whit, they live almost exclusively in negative idioms; bit and scrap are broader, can be used positively ("a bit of bread"), and lack the intensive "absolutely nothing" force of whit; shred implies a torn fragment of something that could be torn ("not a shred of evidence"), while whit is more abstract, a pure measure of degree; tittle (from Latin titulus, a scribal dot) is the rhetorical twin — "not a tittle" — but is even rarer and more bookish. The precise boundary: whit measures amount or degree rather than matter; you can say "not a whit the worse", but you would not say "a whit of bread". In the verse Hawkes translates here, the word retains all of its ancient force: Bao-yu's care for public opinion is reduced to nothing at all — not even the smallest thing.
