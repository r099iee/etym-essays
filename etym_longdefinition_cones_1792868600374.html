Cones, the plural of cone, are in the source sentence the dry seed-cases of coniferous trees: the woody, scale-armoured fruits of pine, fir, spruce, and larch, gathered with dead sticks to feed a camp fire in the resin-scented dark.

The scene is woodcraft-exact: fallen cones are rich in resin and shaped to burn, their scales opening as they dry so that air fingers every crevice, and a handful of them will catch from a spark and nurse a flame until heavier sticks take light. Every child of pine country knows the autumn harvest of crackling brown cones, the sticky fingers, the sharp clean smoke, and the small triumph of a fire built from nothing but forest litter.

Core meaning in detail: Botanically a conifer cone, or strobilus, is a compact cluster of woody scales arranged about a central axis, each scale sheltering two winged seeds at its base. Female cones, the familiar pinecones, ripen over one to three seasons, opening their scales in dry weather to release spinning seeds and closing them against rain; male cones are small, soft, pollen-shedding catkins that wither within weeks. The scales move by hygroscopy, dead tissue drinking and spilling water, so that a fallen cone visibly breathes with the weather long after leaving the tree.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Conifer seed-cases (primary botanical sense, the source sense): pinecones, fir cones, spruce and larch cones, the reproductive architecture of gymnosperms. The bridge is the shape itself: the ovoid tapering body that Greeks named kônos, a spinning top of wood.
  • The geometric solid (mathematics, from the 16th c.): the cone, a solid tapering evenly from a circular base to an apex, with its volume, surface, and conic sections. The bridge is pure form: mathematicians borrowed the pinecone's silhouette for the abstraction, and Euclid's kônos still rules the textbook.
  • Retinal cones (anatomy, 19th c.): the cone cells of the eye, photoreceptors for colour and daylight vision, packed dense in the fovea. The bridge is microscopic shape: under early lenses the cells looked like tiny tapering cones beside their cylindrical rod neighbours, and the name stuck through electron microscopy.
  • Traffic cones and marker cones (20th c.): the hollow orange pyramids of roadworks and drill fields. The bridge is utility of form: the tapering shell stacks neatly, rights itself in wind, and catches the eye, so the geometric cone became street furniture worldwide.
  • The ice-cream cone (food, from 1904): the crisp edible wafer, rolled or moulded to hold a scoop. The bridge is domestic geometry meeting appetite: the St Louis World's Fair made the pastry cone famous, and summer has been licked from tapering wafers ever since.
  • Volcanic and shell cones (geology and zoology): cinder cones, the symmetrical heaps of scoria about a vent, and the elegant cone shells of tropical seas. Both bridges are silhouette: nature builds tapering heaps and spirals wherever material accretes about a point, and each borrows the old Greek nickname.

Register and connotation: In the botanical plural cones is a humble outdoor word — crackling, resinous, autumnal — smelling of fires and school nature-tables piled with pinecones, conkers, and acorns. The singular cone climbs higher, into lecture halls and laboratories, but even there it keeps a tactile memory of the pinewood. Gathered cones in a dark wood mean resourcefulness and comfort: travellers who stoop for dead sticks and cones will be warm within the hour, and the resin-scented dark of the source sentence is already half a promise of flame.
