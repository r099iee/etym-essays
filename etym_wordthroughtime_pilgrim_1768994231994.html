- 12th–13th c. - pilgrim (Old French pelerin, Latin peregrinus, 'foreigner, one from abroad') enters Middle English first meaning a foreign wayfarer - someone passing through - and quickly narrows to one who journeys to a holy place in devotion.
- 13th–15th c. - the sacred sense rules the age: the pilgrimages to Canterbury, Compostela, and the Holy Land make the word heavy with vow, penitence, and the dignity of holy seeking.
- 16th–17th c. - the Reformation suspects the pilgrim as papist, yet the word survives and is reborn in 1620 on the Mayflower - the 'Pilgrim Fathers' inherit the sacred-journey idiom for a New World crossing.
- 17th–19th c. - Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress (1678) turns 'pilgrim' into the universal metaphor of the whole life as a journey of faith, and by the 19th century it generalizes to any earnest seeker - a pilgrim of knowledge, a pilgrim of the ideal.
- 20th–21st c. - still the literal word for the Mecca, Compostela, or Lourdes journeyer, it remains the writer's choice for dignifying any solitary wanderer whose motion is itself a seeking - exactly what the narrator does here for the boy sleeping on the deck.
