Santeuil refers primarily to Jean Santeuil, the eponymous protagonist and title of an extensive, unfinished autobiographical novel composed by Marcel Proust between 1895 and 1899. Abandoned by the author in loose handwritten manuscripts and discovered only posthumously in 1951 before its publication in 1952, the work represents the foundational stylistic, emotional, and intellectual laboratory from which Proust subsequently constructed his monumental masterpiece, À la recherche du temps perdu (In Search of Lost Time).

In literary history and genetic criticism, Jean Santeuil occupies a critical transitional position. Composed when Proust was in his mid-to-late twenties, the manuscript spans over a thousand pages detailing the emotional education, social ascent, and artistic awakening of Jean Santeuil, a delicate and impressionable young bourgeois man. The novel directly fictionalizes key episodes of Proust's own life: his childhood anxieties at Illiers and Paris, his deep attachment to his mother, his stormy infatuations, his entry into the aristocratic salons of the Faubourg Saint-Germain, and his ardent defense of Alfred Dreyfus during the political turbulence of the Dreyfus Affair (including vivid courtroom depictions of Émile Zola's and Colonel Picquart's trials).

Polysemy, Literary Significance, and Historical Context:

  • The Proto-Proustian Aesthetic Workshop: In literary theory and Proustian scholarship, Santeuil signifies the preliminary, unintegrated stage of Proust’s artistic vision. In Jean Santeuil, Proust wrote in the third person ("il"), creating an awkward, episodic distance between narrator and protagonist. It was only by abandoning this conventional third-person realist framework and discovering the transformative, subjective first-person voice ("je") in Contre Sainte-Beuve (1908–1909) and Du côté de chez Swann (1913) that Proust resolved his aesthetic dilemma: realizing that true art arises not from superficial social observation, but from the resurrected sensations of the "inmost self" (le moi profond).
  • The Historical and Ecclesiastical Eponym: Beyond Proust's novel, Santeul or Santeuil is celebrated in French literary history through Jean-Baptiste Santeul (1630–1698), a famous Neo-Latin poet, hymnographer, and canon regular of Saint-Victor in Paris. Renowned for his brilliant Latin hymns composed for the Paris Breviary, his sharp satirical epigrams, and his eccentric, mercurial behavior, Santeul was immortalized by the Duc de Saint-Simon in his memoirs and by Jean de La Bruyère under the portrait of "Théodate." Proust, steeped in seventeenth-century French classical literature and aristocratic memoirists, was intimately familiar with this historical figure when selecting the aristocratic-sounding name for his hero.
  • Toponymic Senses in French Geography: Santeuil is also the name of historic rural communes in northern France: Santeuil in the Eure-et-Loir department (near Chartres, in the Beauce region close to Proust's childhood Illiers/Combray) and Santeuil in the Val-d'Oise department (in the Vexin français). Etymologically, these toponyms originate from ancient Gallo-Roman settlements situated along Celtic road clearings.
  • Critical Metaphor ("The Santeuil Phase"): In modern narrative theory, critics frequently refer to an author's "Santeuil phase" as the raw, autobiographical, and structurally unassimilated draft stage of a writer's development—the essential crucible where raw experience has been recorded but has not yet undergone the transcendent alchemy of mature literary form.
