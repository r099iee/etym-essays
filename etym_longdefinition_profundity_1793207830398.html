Profundity is depth raised to its highest power. In its central modern sense it names intellectual, spiritual, or emotional depth of a rare order: thought that goes to the bottom of its subject, feeling that runs far beneath the surface, or meaning that cannot be exhausted at a single glance.

In the source passage Cobbett wields the word as a weapon of irony. To ascribe the ruin of the English landholders to deliberate contrivance, he says, would credit Pitt and his followers with too much ______ — too much deep scheming wisdom. The sting is that their policies produced exactly the effect a deep conspiracy would have produced, yet without requiring any depth of mind at all; profundity here means farsighted cunning, the capacity to plan far ahead in secret, and Cobbett denies his enemies even that dark compliment.

Core meaning in detail: The noun is built on the adjective profound plus the abstract ending -ity, literally the quality or state of being profound. A profound thinker sees what others miss; a profound book keeps yielding fresh meaning on every rereading; profound grief or profound silence is felt in the whole body rather than merely noticed. Profundity gathers all such uses into one abstract noun, and it always carries weight: no one speaks of the profundity of a shopping list or a weather report except as a joke.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Depth of intellect or insight (primary modern sense): the quality of thinking that penetrates to first principles, as in the profundity of a philosopher or a scientific theory. The bridge from physical depth is direct metaphor: just as deep water hides what shallow water shows, so deep thought reaches what shallow thought merely skims.
  • Depth of feeling or seriousness: profound sorrow, profound respect, profound apology. The bridge runs through intensity imagined as depth: a feeling that touches the bottom of the heart rather than rippling across its surface.
  • Depth of meaning, abstruseness (often with an edge): the profundity of a poem, a scripture, or a legal text. The bridge is that meaningful things, like wells, must be sounded: what is profound repays the effort of plumbing it, though writers also use the word sarcastically for obscurity that merely poses as wisdom.
  • Farsighted cunning or scheming depth (Cobbett's ironic sense): the capacity to contrive far-reaching plans in secret. The bridge narrows intellectual depth to a single craft, plotting: a conspirator's mind is called deep because its workings, like deep water, cannot be seen into.
  • Physical depth (earliest sense, now rare and literary): the profundity of the ocean or of a cavern. This was the word's starting point in the fourteenth century, and though everyday English now says depth for measured distance downward, poets still reach for profundity when depth awes them.
  • Religious and mystical depth (theological register): the profundity of divine mystery, of contemplative prayer, of a sacred text. The bridge joins the physical abyss to the spiritual one: mystics from the Psalms to the present have called God a depth without bottom, and the word carries that vertigo still.

Register and connotation: profundity belongs to high and middle-high style — essays, sermons, reviews, prize citations. It praises far more often than it blames, conferring gravity and respect; to grant a work profundity is to declare it worth a lifetime of study. Its shadow is pretension: readers have learned to ask whether an author's profundity is genuine depth or merely deep-sounding fog, a suspicion Cobbett already exploits when he ironically withholds the compliment from Pitt. Used sincerely it ennobles; used ironically it quietly accuses the target of being shallow.
