A WORD WITH NO CERTAIN PARENT

Sauntering is a rare case in this vocabulary: a common, vivid English word whose ancestry is genuinely unknown. The OED marks the verb saunter as of obscure origin, and no Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it, because it has no established Indo-European pedigree at all — it simply appears in fifteenth-century English without a documented family tree. The earliest recorded form is the Middle English verb santren (also sawtren), first attested around the middle of the fifteenth century, and its earliest meaning is surprising: it meant 'to muse, to daydream, to be in a reverie', or, in the words of the old glosses, to act idly. A santrer was a dreamer, a moper, a person lost in thought — not, yet, a person going for a pleasant walk.

FROM DREAMING TO LOITERING

The crucial shift happened in the sixteenth century, when the sense slid from mental idleness to physical idleness. To saunter came to mean to wander about without a purpose, to loiter, to drift — and the word carried a contemptuous overtone for a long time. The saunterer was the idler, the wastrel, the man who could not be trusted with a task, the drifter at the edge of respectable society; moralists and proverbs scolded the saunterer for wasting God's time. This is the reverse of the modern meaning: far from a graceful, confident stroll, the early saunter was an aimless, slack, disreputable wandering. The transition from 'musing' to 'loitering' is easy to feel — the person lost in thought is also the person wandering without attention — and it is that accidental, careless quality that gave the early word its bad reputation.

THE GENTLEMAN'S WALK: 17TH–18TH CENTURIES

The brightening of the word belongs to the culture of fashionable leisure. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, as English towns grew and polite society took its ease in parks, promenades, and spa towns, the saunter was polished from a vice into a pastime: the gentleman or lady who walked slowly, hands behind back, stopping to admire and converse, was sauntering — and the word shed its moral stain to take on the elegance of unhurried ease. The OED records the modern sense, 'to walk in a leisurely, careless, or idle manner', as established by the seventeenth century and standard from the eighteenth. The famous folk etymology attached itself precisely in this period: the story — repeated by Samuel Johnson — that saunter comes from Sainte-Terre ('Holy Land'), because medieval crusaders and pilgrims wandered aimlessly toward the holy places, going a-sauntering to the Holy Land. The tale is false, and modern dictionaries reject it, but its popularity is a testimony to the word's new glamour: the saunterer had been reborn as a sort of pilgrim, a leisurely wanderer on a sacred errand of idleness.

THOREAU AND THE ART OF WALKING

The word reached its philosophical peak in nineteenth-century American transcendentalism. Henry David Thoreau, in his essay 'Walking' (delivered as a lecture in 1851, published 1862), embraced the folk etymology and built an entire ethic on it: the saunterer, he insisted, was properly a crusader bound for the Holy Land — 'a holy-lander' — and true walking was a pilgrimage of attention into nature, the saunter being the free, unhurried, devout movement of the soul through the landscape. For Thoreau, sauntering was the opposite of walking for exercise or business: it was walking for its own sake, receptive, meditative, and free. In Paris the same idea took its urban form in the tradition of the flâneur — the stroller who reads the city slowly and indifferently, the urban saunterer whose unhurried observation became a model of modern consciousness.

THE MODERN WORD

  • Register: 'saunter' is a literary-to-informal verb of unhurried motion; it is warmer than 'loiter' and more stylish than 'amble', and it always implies leisure and often confidence.
  • Attitude: the modern word carries the body language of ease — hands in pockets, loose gait, no urgency; sauntering in late is a deliberate performance of unconcern, which is why it can be either charming or infuriating.
  • Form: the modern pronunciation /ˈsɔːn.tə.rɪŋ/ shows the vowel lengthened from the early forms; the spelling saunter settled in the 17th century.
  • Today: the word remains alive in everyday speech for any slow, confident, casual walk, from a holiday amble to an ironic late arrival, and the old folk-tale of the Holy Land is still retold wherever the word is discussed.
