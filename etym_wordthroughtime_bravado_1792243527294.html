- Late 16th c. (1580s–1590s) — Borrowed into Elizabethan English via French bravade and Spanish/Italian bravata, capturing the swaggering bravado of continental mercenaries and Italian dueling manuals.
- 17th c. (Jacobean & Restoration) — Naturalized into English drama (Shakespeare, Jonson, Dryden) to characterize comic braggarts and false courage, contrasting theatrical bluster with true stoic honor.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment) — Adopted in political satire, historiography, and military memoirs to describe empty diplomatic threats, naval saber-rattling, and uncalculated military recklessness.
- 19th c. (Romantic & Victorian) — Analyzed psychologically as a defensive facade; portrayed in Victorian novels (Thackeray, Dickens) as a transparent mask worn by bankrupt rakes and nervous adventurers.
- 20th–21st c. — Expanded into corporate, political, and academic discourse to characterize triumphalist rhetoric, speculative market hype, and technoscientific overpromising (as seen in humanist critiques of scientific arrogance).
