Proto-Indo-European *plek- “to plait, fold, braid” (reconstructed; root of interlacing fibres and folded cloth) │ └── Latin plicāre “to fold” (Italic; frequentative-class verb; cf. plica “a fold”; attested 1st c. BCE) └── Vulgar Latin *displicāre “to unfold, spread apart” (prefix dis- “apart” + plicāre “to fold”; reconstructed, the source of the Romance verbs) └── Old French despleier “to unfurl, unfold, spread out” (11th c.; of banners, sails, extended wings) └── Anglo-French desplaier / Middle English displayen “to unfold, spread out; to show, exhibit” (14th c.) — display, the doublet that kept the prefix └── (aphesis: unstressed initial de- dropped) Middle English spleien “to unfold, spread, extend” (c. 1400) └── English splay (verb) “to spread out, extend apart, flatten” (15th c. onward; intransitive and transitive) └── English splayed = splay + regular past/participial suffix -ed (attested from the 15th c.; adjective of spread-flat limbs and structures) └── Modern English: splayed Note: splay and display are historical doublets — both descend from Old French despleier, one via aphesis (loss of de-), the other retaining the prefix. The Greek reflex of the same PIE root is πλέκω (plékō) “to weave, braid,” and the Germanic branch gives *flehtaną “to plait” (Old Norse flétta, German flechten); plait and pleat reach English through Old French pleit and Latin plicātum. These are collateral branches, not stages in the English splay chain.
