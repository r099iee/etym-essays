Evolution of “cynicism.” The word is a rare case of a philosophical school becoming a universal mood. It begins with a dog in the streets of ancient Athens and ends with a shrug in the corridors of modern politics; the journey passes through a lamp, a barrel, and a famous joke at the expense of Alexander the Great.

1. The dog. The chain starts with the PIE word for dog, *ḱuṓn, one of the most securely reconstructed roots in Indo-European — it survives in Greek kýōn, Latin canis (our canine, kennel), Sanskrit śván, and Old English hund (our hound). Greek kýōn gave the adjective kynikós, “doglike,” and from that adjective the school took its name: the followers of Antisthenes and Diogenes of Sinope were called Kynikoí, “the dogs.” The nickname was deliberate and defiant. The ancient sources give two explanations: that the Cynics lived shamelessly, like dogs — eating, talking, and relieving themselves in public, indifferent to the conventions of decency — and that they were the pupils at the gymnasium of Kynósarges, the “White Dog,” where Antisthenes taught. Either way, they embraced the name: the Cynic was the watchdog who barked at pretence and bit hypocrisy.

2. The method. Cynicism as a philosophy was an asceticism of the will. Its goal was virtue, achieved not by argument but by practice — askēsis, training. The Cynic sought self-sufficiency (autarkeia), freedom from desire and fear, and shameless plainness: Diogenes lived in a large earthenware jar (the famous barrel), carried a lamp by daylight “seeking a man” (a real human being, he said, as opposed to the counterfeit ones around him), and when Alexander the Great offered to grant him anything, he answered, “Stand out of my light.” To the Cynic, all the apparatus of society — wealth, rank, fashion, ceremony — was a sham, and the philosopher's first duty was to tear the sham away. This unmasking is the seed of the modern word: the Cynic's method was precisely to read beneath the respectable surface and find the animal, the appetite, the interest underneath.

3. The school enters Rome and English. The Cynic school passed into Roman culture (where Seneca and Epictetus both engaged with it) and survived as a recognised philosophical label. Latin cynicus brought it into English in the 16th century: a cynic (first recorded c. 1540) was, neutrally, “a follower of the ancient Cynic school.” The abstract noun cynicism follows in the 17th century (first recorded 1674), meaning simply “the doctrine of the Cynics.” At this stage the word is a term of doxography — the history of philosophy — with no general meaning beyond the school.

4. The shift to a mood. The crucial transformation happens in the 18th–19th centuries. As the word passed from the handbooks of philosophy into general writing, the Cynic's characteristic method — the barking unmasking of hypocrisy — detached itself from the ascetic doctrine and hardened into an attitude: the disposition to believe that every lofty profession hides a base motive. The 19th century made it a name for the worldly knowingness that greets idealism with a sneer. This is exactly the sense in the present context: Mill's biographer writes that “it is easy for the ordinary worldly cynicism to curl a sceptical lip over sentences like these” — the sentence is a snapshot of the semantic change, using cynicism for the jaded distrust that dismisses the panegyric to Harriet Taylor as sentimentality.

5. The modern hardening. In the 20th century cynicism became a fully recognised modern disposition — the knowing, jaded laughter of the marketplace aimed at ideals — and central to political and cultural commentary. Political cynicism, institutional cynicism, voter cynicism: the word now names a generalised, reflexive distrust of public rhetoric and of sincerity itself. It is distinct from skepticism (doubt about evidence), from pessimism (expectation of bad outcomes), and from nihilism (denial of value); it is specifically the assumption of self-interest, the doctrine that the dog was right about the world.

  • PIE *ḱuṓn “dog” → Gk kýōn “dog” → kynikós “doglike” → Kynikoí “the Cynics” (Antisthenes, Diogenes; c. 4th c. BCE) → Lat cynicus → English cynic (c. 1540) → cynicism (1674) “Cynic doctrine” → 18th–19th c. “distrust of motives” → modern “cynicism.”
  • Semantic arc: philosophical school of shameless ascetics → method of unmasking hypocrisy → general attitude of jaded distrust of sincerity → political and institutional cynicism in modern commentary.
  • Register: pejorative in modern use, neutral-historical in philosophical use; adjective cynical, person cynic.
