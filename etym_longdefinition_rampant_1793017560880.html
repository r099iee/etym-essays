Rampant is an adjective of unchecked aggressive growth or spread: vegetation running wild over ground, or, by extension, any evil, fashion, or force multiplying beyond control.

In the source sentence it qualifies the last rank of the weed catalogue: rampant nettles and thistles. Here it is the countryman's verdict on plants that sting and stick, spreading by root and seed until they own the waste. The nettles do not merely grow; they invade, and rampant makes their victory sound deliberate.

Core meaning in detail: Applied to plants, rampant means spreading vigorously and invasively, covering ground, smothering neighbours, indifferent to walls and paths: rampant ivy, rampant brambles, rampant nettles. The force of the word is moral as well as physical; it accuses the plant of ambition. From the garden the word marches into public life, where inflation, corruption, disease, and rumour all go rampant, each borrowing the weed's energy for human disorder.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Spreading unchecked, of plants and vegetation (primary modern sense): the source sense, rampant weeds owning waste ground. The bridge from the beast is posture: the heraldic lion rears upright and strikes outward, and vegetation that rears and strikes outward earns the same word.
  • Rife and uncontrolled, of social evils and abstractions (dominant figurative sense, from the 19th c.): rampant inflation, rampant corruption, rampant speculation. The bridge is the weed writ large: just as nettles overrun a field, so costs or crimes overrun an economy, and the gardener's complaint becomes the editorial's.
  • Heraldic, rearing on the hind legs (oldest English sense, c. 1300): a lion rampant stands erect with forepaws raised, the default attitude of lions in coats of arms. The bridge is direct inheritance: this is the word's birth in English, borrowed whole from Old French rampant, climbing or rearing.
  • Violent, fierce, and extravagant in behaviour (historical literary sense): rampant soldiers, rampant passions in chronicles and romances. The bridge runs through the rearing beast: men who rear and strike like the lion are rampant too.
  • Architecture, sloping or springing upward: a rampant arch or rampant vault rises with one side higher than the other. The bridge is again posture: masonry that climbs like the beast or the briar.
  • Standing upright, luxuriant to excess, of hair and ornament (rare, poetic): rampant locks, rampant foliage in carving. The bridge is visual exuberance: anything that rears and flourishes beyond neatness.

Register and connotation: rampant is the editorial's favourite weed-word, vigorous, disapproving, and faintly gleeful: to call inflation rampant is to enjoy the chase even while condemning it. In heraldry it is neutral and technical, the correct term for the commonest lion. In the garden it is the plain accusation of the source sentence. Across all uses runs the same upright energy: the lion on its hind legs, the nettle breast-high, the graph climbing off the page. Few adjectives carry their posture so consistently from battlefield shield to newspaper leader.
