1. A name bound to a place. Everyone who has ever been called Laurence traces the line to Laurentum, an ancient Latin town on the Tyrrhenian coast near the mouth of the Tiber, in the marshy country between Ostia and Lavinium. Virgil’s Aeneid makes the Laurentine kingdom the stage of the landing of Aeneas and of his war with Turnus, and the legendary geography of the site was bound together with a prophetic sow that brought forth thirty young — the sign that led Aeneas to the place — in a grove that the Romans, eager for explanation, associated with the laurel. Whether laurus “laurel” really stands behind the toponym, or whether the city name is Etruscan and the laurel merely a folk etymology, is now undecidable; scholars note that a genuine Etruscan presence on the Latin coast makes a substrate origin perfectly possible. What matters for the name’s destiny is that the Romans themselves believed in the laurel: the adjective laurens, laurentis and the ethnonymic suffix -ius produced Laurentius — “the man from Laurentum” — and the laurel’s semantics of victory, poetry and civic honour trailed the name from the start.

2. The saint who made the name European. The real explosion of the name was Christian. Laurentius, a deacon of the Roman church, was martyred in the persecution of the emperor Valerian in 258 CE, and the passion legend — roasted alive on a gridiron, joking that he was “done on this side, turn me over” — made him one of the most beloved saints of the West. His fame produced the basilica of San Lorenzo fuori le Mura, one of the seven pilgrim churches of Rome; his feast, 10 August, was marked by bonfires and by the falling star of the Perseids, still called the “tears of St Lawrence”. From the fourth century onward the personal name Laurentius spread through Italy, Gaul, Spain and Germany with the saint’s cult, and it was this Christian veneration, not the memory of the Latin town, that carried the name into the medieval vernaculars — Laurent in French, Lawrence in English, Lorenzo in Italian.

3. The Medici adoption. In Florence the family of the Medici, wool-brokers turned bankers, claimed San Lorenzo as their parish saint. Their quarter, the quarter of San Lorenzo, gave the family its base; the basilica of San Lorenzo became the Medici church; and generation after generation of the house was baptised with the family’s favourite names — Cosimo, Giovanni, Piero — and, above all, Lorenzo. It is worth stressing how ordinary the name still was: in 1369, when the branch of the family that would become famous was establishing itself, the name Lorenzo was a standard Florentine Christian name, respectable but unremarkable.

4. The Magnificent inflation. When Lorenzo de’ Medici (1449–1492) took over the Republic on the death of his father Piero in 1469, “Lorenzo” began its second life. The magnifici were simply the city’s leading citizens — the formal address of a Florentine great — but the handsome, athletic, learned young man who outlived the Pazzi conspiracy of 1478, who patronized Botticelli’s Primavera and the young Michelangelo, who collected the ancient gems and medals that later formed the nucleus of museums, who rhymed carnival songs and serious canzoni, and who kept Italy in a famous balance of power, turned a formula of address into a biographical fact. Posterity translated il Magnifico as “the Magnificent”, and “the age of Lorenzo” entered the historiographical canon as the type of the many-sided, centralised, complete culture — the passage in which Pater himself places the phrase.

5. The name after the man. After 1492 the name could no longer be innocent. The Lorenzos who succeed him in history — Lorenzo the Younger, Duke of Urbino, to whom Machiavelli dedicated The Prince — are read as falling shadows of the first. In art history the name is institutionalised: Lorenzo Ghiberti cast the Gates of Paradise, Lorenzo Lotto painted the melancholic portraits of the Veneto, Lorenzo Valla opened the philology of Latin, Lorenzo da Ponte wrote the libretti of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Così fan tutte. And because the sanctity of the fourth century and the magnificence of the fifteenth never cancelled each other, the name still carries both registers: the Roman martyr on his gridiron, and the Florentine prince in Botticelli’s glow. Modern parents choosing “Lorenzo” are choosing, mostly without knowing it, fourteen hundred years of layered European history.
