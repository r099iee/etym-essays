Besiegers is the plural of the agent noun besieger: the people — in practice almost always an armed force — who besiege a place, i.e. who surround and blockade a town, fortress, castle or other defended position and press it by bombardment, assault, mining or starvation until it falls. Morphologically it is transparent: the verb besiege + the agentive suffix -er (“one who does X”) + the plural -s. The plural far outweighs the singular in actual usage, because besieging is an army’s collective work: the besiegers means “the besieging army or forces” taken as a body — the besiegers of Constantinople, of Sebastopol, of Leningrad.

1. The primary, military sense. In narrative history and journalism the word names the attacking party in a siege: the encircling, blockading, pressing host. It is more precise and less merely violent than attackers: besiegers do not simply fight — they surround, cut off supplies, dig in, build siege-works, endure and wait — and their opponents are the besieged, the garrison or the defenders. The antithesis besiegers/besieged is one of the great structural pairs of war-narrative, as old as the genre: Thucydides’ Athenians before Syracuse, Titus’ legions before Jerusalem (70 CE), the Crusaders before Acre (1189–91), the Ottomans before Constantinople (1453), the besiegers of Vienna (1683), the German and Soviet armies at Leningrad (1941–44). In the source sentence, from Tolkien’s The Hobbit, the besiegers are the host of men and elves encircling the Lonely Mountain — and the text itself explains the military logic of the word: to besiege a mountain you must be able to encircle it wholly and close every gate, and “for that they had not sufficient numbers”.

2. Extended and transferred senses.

  • Crowding and importuning: because the verb besiege also means “to surround with attention or requests”, the plural noun can describe those who mob a person or place — journalists besieging a celebrity, suitors besieging an heiress, reporters besieging a courthouse. The word carries the mild sensory violence of being hemmed in and shouted at.
  • Pressure and threat: in figurative journalism “besiegers” are those who assail an institution, ideal or network from every side — critics besieging a government, hackers besieging a bank. The military image is preserved: they come from all directions and they do not go away.
  • Rarely, of non-human agents: floods, plagues or pests can be described as the besiegers of a region or a crop — a poetic extension found in 19th-century natural-history prose.

3. Register, grammar and form. Besiegers belongs to literary, historical and journalistic narrative; formal military prose prefers the phrase the besieging force or the besieging army. The singular besieger is rare and belongs chiefly to figurative or poetic contexts (“the besieger of her heart”). The abstract noun is besiegement (rare); the defenders are the besieged — a word that has itself become a metaphor for anyone beleaguered by pressure (a besieged government, a besieged reputation). Spelling and sound: b-e-s-i-e-g-e-r-s, stressed on the second syllable, /bɪˈsiːdʒəz/, the -ge- giving /dʒ/ as in siege; the initial be- is the old English intensive/transitive prefix, long since emptied of independent meaning for most speakers.

4. Note on perspective. Unlike aggressor or enemy, the word is morally neutral: chroniclers use it of heroes and villains alike, of besiegers who starve cities into surrender and of besiegers who liberate them. Its flavour is grave, patient and geometrical rather than savage — the word pictures armies settling in for months before ramparts — and it is this patient, encircling quality that the word contributes to every sentence it enters, including Tolkien’s, where the besiegers must “encircle the whole mountain” and find their numbers wanting.
