The chain for METICULOUSNESS rests on a word of uncertain — probably non-Indo-European — origin; the earliest secure stage is Latin, not PIE. This is stated explicitly rather than disguised with an invented root. 1. Latin metus "fear, dread, anxiety" (attested from c. 200 BCE, e.g. in Plautus and Terence) — of uncertain, possibly pre-Italic or non-Indo-European origin; no securely established cognates elsewhere in the family. 2. Latin metuō, metuere "to fear, to dread, to shrink in fear" (attested from c. 100 BCE) — the verb built on metus. 3. Latin meticulōsus "full of fear, timid, faint-hearted" (attested in the Classical period) — metus + the medial element -cul- + the possessive suffix -ōsus, "full of". 4. English meticulous "fearful, timid, faint-hearted" (1530s; borrowed from Latin meticulōsus, in part via French méticuleux, which long preserved the timid sense). 5. English meticulous "scrupulously, painfully careful" (the famous semantic inversion, c. 1850s) — fear of error reinterpreted as watchful care. 6. English meticulousness "extreme, exacting, detail-loving carefulness" (1860s) — the abstract noun formed on the new sense → English METICULOUSNESS (this card). Note on debate: Latin metus has no generally accepted Indo-European etymology; modern authorities (de Vaan; Beekes) regard it as of unclear, possibly non-IE, origin, and no Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia cognate exists. A minority proposal connects it to a PIE root *mei-, "to avoid, to shrink back", but the link is conjectural and generally rejected, so it is not inserted as a stage.
