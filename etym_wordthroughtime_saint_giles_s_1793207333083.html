- c. 710 — the hermit: Giles of Provence, crippled by an arrow meant for his hind, becomes patron of cripples and beggars — the saint of outcasts.
- 12th c. — the hospital: a lazar-house for lepers rises outside London’s walls under his dedication, in the fields that give in-the-Fields.
- 17th c. — the city swallows it: speculators carve the parish into courts and tenements; the respectable flee west and the poor flood in.
- 18th c. — the legend of horror: the great rookery of Seven Dials, Hogarth’s Gin Lane (1751), Gay’s warnings — St Giles’s becomes the national word for slum.
- Cobbett’s 1820s — the moral yardstick: half Saint Giles’s measures how far honest country labour has sunk toward capital-city degradation.
- Victorian clearance — New Oxford Street (1847) and model dwellings drive through the rookery; Dickens and Mayhew elegise what the pickaxe removes.
- Today — the address without the horror: St Giles Circus hums with offices and traffic, and the name survives as history, not warning.
