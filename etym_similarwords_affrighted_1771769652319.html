• Frightened: Similar because both describe being afraid; differs because frightened is the plain, everyday word for a mild, easily recovered scare, while affrighted is stronger and more complete — a fear laid on the whole person by an intensive prefix, and archaic-literary in register.
  • Terrified: Similar because both describe strong, overwhelming fear; differs because terrified suggests near-paralyzing, overwhelming panic — the modern, colloquial intensifier — while affrighted suggests a settled state of dread fully entered into rather than a surge of panic, and it carries a formal, archaic dignity that terrified lacks.
  • Alarmed: Similar because both describe being stirred by fear; differs because alarmed implies a sudden warning that mobilizes vigilance — an alert rather than a dread — whereas affrighted is a pervasive, filling terror that clouds the mind rather than sharpening it.
  • Startled: Similar because both describe a sudden fear reaction; differs because startled is a brief, involuntary jump that fades at once, whereas affrighted is a persistent, settled state of complete terror.
  • Aghast: Similar because both describe being struck with horror; differs because aghast pictures a person frozen, open-mouthed, horrified at something seen — a reaction to a spectacle — whereas affrighted is a fear that fills the whole mind and imagination, not merely a response to the visible.
  • Panicked: Similar because both describe loss of composure through fear; differs because panicked suggests loss of self-control and herd-like, disordered flight — an active, disorganized response — whereas affrighted is a passive state of being filled with dread, without the frenzy of panic.
  • Scared: Similar because both describe fear; differs because scared is the plainest, most casual modern word for being afraid, entirely colloquial, whereas affrighted is elevated, archaic, and literary — the two stand at opposite ends of the register scale.
  • Horrified: Similar because both describe a strong negative emotional reaction; differs because horrified emphasizes revulsion and shock at something dreadful or repulsive, a judgment on the object, whereas affrighted is pure fear — terror without the component of disgust or moral recoil.
  • Daunted: Similar because both describe being subdued by something fearful; differs because daunted implies a feeling of being discouraged or overawed that checks one's efforts, a lesser and more rational fear, whereas affrighted is total, involuntary, and overwhelming.
  • Appalled: Similar because both describe being struck by something shocking; differs because appalled implies dismay and moral horror that leaves one unable to act — closer to aghast — while affrighted is primal physical fear, the terror that seizes the whole being.
