Linear chain for found (this card):
1. PIE *pent- "to go, tread, walk, find one's way" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the path-finding root; its children include Greek pátos "a trodden way, path," Latin pons, pontis "bridge, gangway," and Vedic Sanskrit pánthāḥ "path, way" (whence Hindi panth, Odia pantha). It is even possible, though debated, that English path (Old English pæþ) is a distant loan from an Iranian member of this same root.
2. Proto-Germanic *finþaną "to find, come upon" (c. 500 BCE–CE 500) — a rebuilt present-stem with a nasal infix, literally "to come upon by going," to find by walking the path. Cognates: Old High German findan (German finden), Old Norse finna, Gothic finþan.
3. Old English findan "to find, discover" (attested from c. 8th–11th c.).
4. Middle English finden "to find" (12th–15th c.).
5. English find (v.) — a strong verb whose paradigm is find–found–found; the past participle found also gains the sense "provided, supplied, furnished" (the master "finds" his servants in meat and lodging).
6. English found (n., 19th c.) "food and lodging supplied as part of wages" (TARGET) — the maintenance "found" for a worker; collocations "board and found," "wages and found," "room and found."
Anti-merging note (debated/nuanced): this "found" must NOT be confused with the homograph "found" = "to establish," which descends from Latin fundāre and its root fundus "bottom, ground" (cf. foundation, profound, fund). Two English homographs, two unrelated etymologies. The participle-to-noun shift for THIS word is a 19th-c. hardening of the old participle sense "provided, supplied," and that date and mechanism are the attested record — no intermediate stage is invented.
