CLAMOUR is a noun (and a verb) meaning a loud and confused noise, especially the shouting of many voices at once — and, by extension, an urgent collective demand. In the passage from The Hobbit, the word falls at the climactic moment when the ring of Wargs answer their grey chief's signal, and their dreadful clamour shakes the pine-tree almost enough to dislodge Bilbo. Its literary force rests on volume and coordination rather than on mere disorder: a clamour is not random noise but a collective vocal explosion, many throats crying out at once in a single, overwhelming roar, so the noun conveys both sheer sound and the pack's terrifying cohesion. Tolkien stresses that all the Wargs answer "all together", and the word therefore carries its Latin sense of a crowd's battle-cry, a sound deliberately produced to intimidate; its effect here is psychological as much as acoustic, collapsing the hobbit's fragile security into a few splintering seconds of pure noise while the great pine sways above the snapping jaws. Because the etymology preserves a demand, an urgent call for something to happen, modern usage retains an undertone of protest and collective wanting — a clamour for reform, for bread, for justice — that suits the hungry, circling pack perfectly: the Wargs are not merely loud, they are demanding.

The polysemy of clamour is a small but rich stack:
  • Sense 1 — loud confused noise of voices (the core sense): the shouting of a crowd, the outcry of many people or beasts together; a sustained, overwhelming vocal noise, louder and more purposeful than random din. This is the sense in the card.
  • Sense 2 — an urgent collective demand (the modern political sense): the cry of a crowd for something — the clamour for rights, for reform, for bread, for justice; the noun and the phrase "clamour for" harden into a statement of grievance and demand.
  • Sense 3 — the legal shade (historical, from Old French law): in Old French and Anglo-French law a clameur was a public cry of distress or a formal complaint; the legal sense survives as an undertone in modern "clamour" and in the judicial idiom of raising an outcry.
  • Sense 4 — the verb, to clamour: to cry aloud, to shout; to demand or complain noisily, often with "for" or "against" — "they clamoured for his release"; the verb is as old in English as the noun (14th c.).
  • Sense 5 — extended to non-human sound (metaphoric transfer): the clamour of bells, of the sea, of battle, of birds and beasts — any loud, urgent, collective sound that mimics the outcry of a crowd; Tolkien's howling Wargs are a prime example.
  • Sense 6 — the spelling pair clamour / clamor: clamour (British, from the French -our spelling) and clamor (American, the Latinized form); both are the same word, and the British spelling is the one preserved in the card.

The historical bridge runs from the PIE root of calling to the Roman battlefield. The root *kelh₁- meant "to call, shout, summon", and in Latin it produced calāre "to call out, announce" (whence calendar, from the Calends) and, in an -m- strengthened form, clāmāre "to cry out, shout loudly", whose noun clāmor named exactly the roar of a crowd or army that Livy and Tacitus describe. Through Old French clameur the word entered English in the 14th century with both its vocal and its legal colouring, and from "a loud outcry" it drifted to "a sustained, confused uproar of many voices" and then to "an urgent collective demand". The word has always meant noise with a purpose: not the random clatter of things but the raised voices of beings who want something.

In register, clamour is slightly elevated or literary. It is not the broad, neutral noise, nor the informal racket, nor the disordered uproar; it is the word for sound that is loud, collective, and freighted with demand. In grammar it is a regular noun (clamour, clamours) and a regular verb (clamour, clamoured, clamouring), and its French-derived -our spelling marks its passage through Old French into Middle English — a permanent stamp of its ancestry.
