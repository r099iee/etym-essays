Introduction. Slew is an archaic-looking fossil that is actually the regular descendant of one of the oldest verbs in the language. Its present tense slay goes back to an Indo-European root meaning “to strike,” and the past-tense form slew carries the full phonological history of Germanic strong verbs: a root vowel change (ablaut) marks past time. Its career — from the everyday “strike and kill” of Old English warriors, through the Middle English vernacular, the King James Bible, and into modern epic and fantasy fiction — shows how a fully ordinary verb can harden into a marker of high and archaic style.

Phase 1 — Indo-European and Germanic (before 700 CE).

  • The PIE root reconstructed as *(s)leh₂k- (Pokorny slak-, “to strike, hit”) underlies the Germanic verb: the same root is often cited for Latin lacerāre “to tear, mangle” and Greek lákos “torn rag/garment” — the core image is a blow that tears or cuts. The reconstruction is standard but, like all PIE roots, probabilistic.
  • Proto-Germanic reformed the verb as *slahaną “to strike, smite, kill,” a strong verb of class VI: its past singular had the lengthened grade (*slōh), which is the ancestral source of the long vowel in the modern past tense.
  • The Germanic family is everywhere: Old English slēan, Old Saxon slahan, Old High German slahan (whence German schlagen “to strike, beat”), Old Norse slá, Gothic slahan. English slay, German schlagen, and Norse slá are all the same verb, diverged but recognisable.

Phase 2 — Old English (c. 700–1100).

  • Old English slēan is the contracted spelling of *slahan: the medial velar fricative h was lost, lengthening the vowel. It meant “to strike, strike with a weapon, smite; to kill; to sacrifice.” Sense order matters: the oldest core is striking, and killing is initially the striking that results in death.
  • The past tense was slōg (with variants slōh), the class VI lengthened grade — the direct ancestor of modern slew. “He slōg hine” — “he smote/killed him” — is the ordinary heroic formula of the chronicles and of the world of Beowulf.
  • The participle slægen yields modern slain, keeping the third member of the strong triad slay-slew-slain.

Phase 3 — Middle English (1100–1500).

  • Middle English sleen/slēn meant “to kill, slay” — now with killing as the primary sense; “strike” faded behind it. The past appeared as slou, slow, slewe: the vowel underwent the Middle English changes that raised and rounded the old long ō.
  • Medieval usage is transparently everyday: chronicles record that kings “slow” their enemies; romances say knights “slow” dragons; the Paston letters use it bluntly. Slay/slew was not yet literary — it was the plainest word for killing.
  • The weak variant “slayed” appears sporadically in medieval texts but never ousts the strong form in the standard language.

Phase 4 — early modern English and the Bible (1500–1700).

  • In the 1500s the spelling settled as slew, aligned with the parallel strong pasts crew, drew, flew (the -ew spelling is analogical, chosen once the vowel had moved to the sound of those words).
  • Shakespeare uses slew freely in his history plays; Holinshed’s Chronicles and the courts used slay for homicide. The 1611 King James Bible made slew canonical: “David slew the Philistine” (1 Samuel 17:50), among hundreds of occurrences, giving the word a scriptural gravity that ordinary killing verbs never acquired.
  • Because every English speaker for three centuries heard slew in church, the word accumulated a hushed, solemn charge: killing by the hand, sanctioned or damned, narrated with biblical plainness.

Phase 5 — the modern retreat (1700–1900).

  • In the 1700s–1800s the spoken language moved to kill, and slay/slew/slain became the property of elevated prose: epic translations, ballads, adventure novels, big-game hunting accounts (“the lion was slain”), and sermons.
  • The strong triad became a hallmark of heroic register — the vocabulary of sagas, ordeals, and dragons. Poetic diction found the one-syllable past slew rhythmically convenient, with its heavy weighty vowel for line-end closure.
  • American English simultaneously created a homograph: slew (a large number), from Irish sluagh “host, multitude,” first recorded in the 19th century — a new word wearing the old past tense’s coat, and permanently complicating the form’s identity.

Phase 6 — the twentieth century and today.

  • Fantasy and historical fiction kept slew vigorous: Tolkien’s Middle-earth prose (“whose father you slew in Moria”), sword-and-sorcery, and biblical retellings all reach for it as the default epic past tense.
  • The slang present slay took a parallel modern path — 20th-century US “you slay me” (amuse me irresistibly) and 21st-century fashion slang “slay” (to look stunning, to dominate) — but these slang senses use the weak past slayed, leaving slew unambiguously the epic past of killing.
  • Today slew is immediately understood but never casual: it is the past tense that kings and dragons receive in prose, and that comedians quote for mock-heroic effect. Its eight hundred years of vowel change are invisible; what survives is a single heavy syllable freighted with Beowulf, the Bible, and the battlefield.

Summary. Slew descends unbroken from an Indo-European word for striking, via the Germanic strong verb *slahaną, the Old English past slōg, and the Middle English slow, gathering along the way its spellings, its sacrificial and biblical senses, its epic register, and finally a homograph from Ireland. It is at once the most archaic-looking and the most continuously attested past tense in this batch — a true survivor.
