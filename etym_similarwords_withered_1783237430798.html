• Shrivelled: Similar because both describe contraction and loss of moisture; differs because shrivelled stresses surface wrinkling and carries far less moral or figurative weight.
  • Wizened: Similar because both denote a dried, wasted appearance; differs because wizened is reserved for persons, especially the aged, and is grotesquely affectionate rather than accusatory.
  • Sere: Similar because it means dry and withered; differs because it is the archaic, purely literary word (Shakespeare's "the sear, the yellow leaf") with no everyday or figurative life.
  • Parched: Similar because both describe dryness; differs because parched comes from heat or thirst, is often temporary, and lacks the sense of life slowly draining from within.
  • Dried-up: Similar because both mean desiccated; differs because it is the flat colloquial equivalent, literal and without the gravity or moral overtones of withered.
  • Blighted: Similar because both describe damaged, deadened vegetation or life; differs because blighted implies attack from without (frost, disease, curse), while withered is slow wasting from within.
  • Atrophied: Similar because both describe a wasted limb or organ; differs because atrophied is the clinical, scientific term for disuse-wasting, lacking the dried-vegetation image of withered.
  • Decayed: Similar because both mean decomposition of vigour; differs because decayed implies rotting or putrefaction, while withered is desiccation without rotting.
  • Decrepit: Similar because both describe worn-out weakness; differs because it refers to general aged frailty of persons or things, not to the specific drying and shrivelling of moisture.
  • Wasted: Similar because both mean drained of strength; differs because it can also mean squandered or emaciated, a broader word that loses the desiccation image.
