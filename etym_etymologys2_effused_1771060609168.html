Derivation of effused PIE *ǵʰew- "to pour, to pour a libation, to invoke (by pouring)" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *fūðō "to pour" (the regular Italic reflex of the *ǵʰ → f sound change, c. 1500–800 BCE) → Latin fundo, fundere, fūdī, fūsum "to pour, to pour out, to shed, to melt, to cast (metal)" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; e.g. Plautus) → Latin effundō, effundere, effūdī, effūsum "to pour out, to shed, to spread abroad, to lavish, to utter" — prefix ex- "out of, from" + fundere (attested 1st c. BCE, Cicero) → Latin effūsus "poured out, spread abroad, lavish, boundless" (past participle, 1st c. BCE; also the adjective "effūsus" used of generosity and speed) → English effuse, effused — borrowed from the Latin past participle effūsus (and partly via learned European usage) (verb "effuse" recorded from the 17th c.; participle "effused" with it; the noun "effusion" arrived earlier, 14th c., via Old French "effusion" from Latin "effūsiō") → effused (Modern English) Debated / nuanced points. The reconstruction of the PIE root is secure as *ǵʰew- 'to pour, to make a libation'; the Italic treatment of *ǵʰ as f- (so *ǵʰew- → Latin fū- in fundere) is a regular, well-established sound law. There is a debated detail concerning the Greek branch (Greek khéō 'I pour' is the same root, but the Greek forms come from a variant *ǵʰew- with the laryngeal treated differently in the aorist), which does not affect the Latin-to-English chain. The English verb "effuse" is a learned borrowing modelled on the Latin participle, not a continuous spoken development from Latin; the older loan in the family is the noun "effusion" (14th c.). These two facts are sometimes conflated in popular etymology and should be kept distinct.
