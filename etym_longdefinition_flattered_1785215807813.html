The verb flattered (base form flatter) means to praise someone excessively, insincerely, or insidiously in order to gratify their vanity — to stroke a person's self-regard with pleasing words, often to win favour, advantage, or compliance. In the sentence that anchors this card — "Mrs. Mill may have flattered her husband's vanity by echoing his opinions, or she may have indeed been an Egeria, full of inspiration and intellectual helpfulness" — the word presents one hypothesis about Harriet Taylor Mill's influence on John Stuart Mill: that she may have gained power by the insidious method of echoing his opinions back to him, pleasing him with agreement and stroking his vanity, rather than by any independent inspiration. The alternative offered is flattering by contrast: the legendary nymph Egeria, who was said to have inspired the Roman king Numa with genuine wisdom. The word thus carries the full weight of its suspicion: to flatter is to praise with an ulterior purpose, and flattery is the currency of the courtier, the dependent, and the manipulator. Core senses and shades of meaning:
  • Insincere praise: to compliment lavishly or untruthfully, with the aim of pleasing the object of the praise.
  • Gratifying vanity: to please someone by dwelling on their merits, achievements, or opinions — "to flatter someone's vanity, pride, or ego."
  • Ingratiating motive: to praise in order to win favour, advantage, advancement, or indulgence — the sense of the courtier and the sycophant.
  • In the passive, positive sense: "I'm flattered" — genuinely pleased and gratified by praise or attention; the passive often drops the suspicion and expresses delight (as in "I was flattered to be invited").
  • Reflexive, self-deception: "to flatter oneself" means to believe something pleasing but questionable about oneself — "he flattered himself that he was indispensable"; also to have an unrealistically high opinion of one's own merits.
  • Enhancing appearance: of clothes, light, or angles, "to flatter" means to make someone look better than they really are — "that dress flatters you," "flattering lighting." Here the word is wholly positive and aesthetic.
  • To gratify or soothe: an older, broader sense of giving pleasure or satisfaction to a person or their pride — "the thought flattered him," i.e., it pleased him.
Register, grammar, and collocations:
  • Register: common, everyday, and flexible, from formal moral essay to casual compliment. Flattered is standard in all registers.
  • Grammar: transitive verb; patterns include "flatter someone," "flatter someone's vanity/pride/ego," "flatter oneself that...", "be flattered by/to/that." The past-tense/past-participle form on this card is flattered.
  • Common collocations: "flatter someone's vanity," "flattery will get you nowhere," "I'm flattered," "flattered to be invited," "flattering light/dress/angle," "empty flattery," "blatant flattery."
  • Related words: flattery (the practice or the words), flatterer (the person), flattering (adjective). The derived noun flattery and agent noun flatterer entered English alongside the verb.
Historical and etymological bridge:
  • The English verb flatter is not native: it entered through Old French flater, which meant first "to smooth, stroke, caress, pat" — a physical gesture — and only secondarily "to coax with words."
  • The French verb is a Germanic loanword (from Frankish, related to "flat"): the original image is of smoothing or stroking a surface, then of soothing a person, and finally of stroking a person's vanity with pleasing words.
  • The deep root is the Indo-European *plat-/*plet-, "flat, broad, spread out," which also gives English flat, plateau, platypus, and Sanskrit prathati ("spreads out").
  • The moral suspicion attached to flattery is ancient and goes back at least to classical antiquity (the Greek κόλαξ, "flatterer," is a stock figure in comedy and moral philosophy), so the word entered English already freighted with the disapproval that the Mill context exploits.
Synonyms in brief: compliment (which may be sincere), praise (general approval, no implication of falsehood), adulate and toady (servile, excessive worship), cajole (coaxing persuasion), blandish (coax with pleasant words), butter up (informal). Flatter uniquely combines pleasing with self-interest, so that its sincerity is always in question.
