ROOT WORD: gait — “a person’s or animal’s manner of walking.” Single root. The word is Germanic-internal; its origin beyond Germanic is unknown, so the honest chain begins at Proto-Germanic.
Stage 1 — Proto-Germanic *gatwō “way, road, street” (reconstructed; reflected by Gothic gatwō “street,” 4th c. Wulfila Bible, and Old Norse gata “path, way”): the earliest reconstructible form. Its ultimate origin is unknown; some scholars have proposed a connection to a PIE root meaning “to go, to release” but this is not generally accepted, and the OED records the word as of unknown origin outside Germanic.
Stage 2 — Old Norse gata “path, way, road” (attested Viking Age, 9th–11th c.): the everyday Norse word for a way or street, carried to England by the Viking settlements; its modern Scandinavian descendants include Swedish gata and Danish gade “street.”
Stage 3 — Middle English gate / gait “a way, path, road; a going, a journey” (borrowed from Old Norse, attested 12th–14th c., chiefly northern and Scottish dialects): survives in Scots “to go one’s gait” and in northern place-names; not to be confused with the southern gate “a hinged doorway,” which is a different word from Old English geat.
Stage 4 — Early Modern English gait “a manner of walking or stepping” (attested 16th c.): a specialised spelling of gate adopted to separate the new “manner of going” sense from the “pathway” and “doorway” homographs.
Stage 5 — Modern English GAIT (the form on this card): the technical term of horsemanship, medicine, and biomechanics for a manner of walking, and the neutral observational word of prose.
Notes on debate and nuance: The ultimate origin of Proto-Germanic *gatwō is unknown; a PIE “going” root has been floated but is not accepted, so the chain honestly begins at the Germanic stage rather than with a PIE reconstruction. The spelling gait (16th c.) is a deliberate orthographic differentiation of the shared gate, not a sound change. English gate “a doorway” (Old English geat) is an unrelated homograph and must not be included in this chain.”
