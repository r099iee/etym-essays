The biography of humour is one of the most astonishing in the English lexicon, for it begins in the soil and ends in laughter. No other common English word travels so far: from the ancient Indo-European word for the earth, through the medical theory of the four bodily fluids, to the modern name for the quality that makes people laugh. Every stage of the journey is attested, and the chain of sense-changes is a classic of semantic drift.

I. The earth-root: PIE *dʰǵʰem-

The story begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰǵʰem-, "earth, ground". It is a root of remarkable reach, attested in nearly every branch of the family. Greek has khthṓn "earth", which survives in English as chthonic, the adjective for the dark, underground powers. Sanskrit has kṣam "earth". Hittite, the oldest recorded Indo-European language, has tēkan. Lithuanian has žemė, as in the regional name Žemaitija. And — most surprisingly — the same root lies hidden in the Germanic word for "earthling", *guman- "human being", the ancestor of the archaic English guma "man" that survives today only inside the word bridegroom. The root's core meaning, "the ground beneath one's feet", was one of the most stable possessions of the ancestral language.

II. Latin humus and the humbling family

In Latin the root produced humus, "ground, soil, earth", and from humus Latin built a whole family whose moral colouring is unmistakable. Humilis meant "low, near the ground", and from it came humble and humility — the virtue of being low to the earth, without pride. Humāre meant "to bury", giving English inhume (to bury) and exhume (to dig up). But the decisive member of the family for our word is the verb humēre, "to be moist, damp, wet". Why should "earth" produce "moist"? Because the defining physical quality of cultivated ground is its dampness — the soil that grows crops is wet soil. The semantic step from "to be of the earth" to "to be damp" is short and natural, and it is the pivot on which the whole later history of the word turns.

III. From dampness to fluid: hūmor

From humēre Latin derived the noun hūmor (also written umor), "moisture, liquid, fluid". In classical Latin it was the plain word for any juice or fluid — of the earth, of plants, of the body. Note the honest correction that the standard handbooks insist on: an older popular etymology connected hūmor to a reconstructed PIE root meaning "to be wet" (linking it to Sanskrit ukṣati, "to wet, sprinkle"), but this is rejected, because the Latin word is transparently derived from humus "ground" through humēre "to be damp". The chain runs earth → ground → dampness → fluid, and no reconstructed "wet" root is needed.

IV. The four humours: medicine takes the word over

In the medical theory inherited from Hippocrates and systematised by Galen, the body was held to contain four humours — four fluids — whose balance determined health, character, and temperament: blood (the sanguine), phlegm (the phlegmatic), yellow bile or choler (the choleric), and black bile or melancholy (the melancholic). The theory dominated Western medicine for over fifteen hundred years, and it gave humour its decisive expansion. From "a bodily fluid" the word naturally came to mean the temperament that fluid was held to produce — a person's "humour" was their balance of humours, their constitutional frame of mind. Middle English imported the word in the fourteenth century with exactly this medical meaning, and from "constitutional frame of mind" the modern senses began to unfold: a "good humour" is a well-balanced temper, an "ill humour" a disordered one.

V. From temper to laughter: the comic turn

The great semantic adventure happened in English between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. From the mood sense ("in good humour", "out of humour") the word widened in two directions. First, a person's "humour" came to mean a temperamental bent or fancy — even a whim — and the verb "to humour" meant to indulge that mood or whim to keep the person happy. Second, and more momentously, "a humour" came to name a peculiar cast of character, and Ben Jonson's "comedies of humours" — plays in which each character is ruled by a single ruling passion — made the word a theatrical term. From "the comic portrayal of humours" the step to "the comic quality itself" was short: by the eighteenth century humour named the disposition of a mind that finds the world amusing, and by the nineteenth century the phrase "a sense of humour" was fixed as the name of the capacity to laugh. The word's journey from fluid to laughter was complete.

VI. Austen's usage and the double career

  • In Pride and Prejudice Austen uses the mood-and-comic senses with perfect precision: Mr. Bennet's mind is "a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humour, and caprice" — a temperament that takes a sharp, playful delight in the absurd, poised exactly between mood and mirth.
  • The word kept its double career: in ordinary modern speech "a sense of humour" names the comic capacity, while "in a good humour" and "out of humour" preserve the older mood sense.
  • British English keeps the spelling humour; American English, following Noah Webster's spelling reforms, drops the "u" to humor. The pronunciation is identical in both.
  • The literal "fluid" sense survives in scientific terminology: the aqueous humour and vitreous humour are the fluids filling the chambers of the eye — a fossil of the word's medical age.

VII. The modern field of humour

  • Modern English has built a rich compound system on the comic sense: black humour, dark humour, gallows humour, dry humour, deadpan humour, self-deprecating humour, and slapstick humour all live in the field of "humour".
  • The humoral adjectives survive as psychological vocabulary: sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, and melancholic still describe temperaments, and the theory's ghost presides over the whole vocabulary of mood.
  • No other English word of such humble, earthy origin has risen so high into the air of laughter; humour remains the great example of how semantic drift can carry a word across the whole map of human experience.
