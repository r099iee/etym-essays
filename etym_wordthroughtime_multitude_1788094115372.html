- Borrowed in the 14th century from Latin/French as a plain quantitative word: 'a great number' of people or things, neutral in tone (Chaucer's 'multitude of peple')
- 16th–17th centuries: the definite phrase 'the multitude' hardens into a social label — the common people, the uneducated masses, often with elite suspicion attached (Shakespeare's 'the rude multitude'; Hobbes on 'the multitude' as unruly)
- 17th–18th centuries: biblical translation keeps the word solemn and vast (Authorised Version's 'multitudes'), while political writers (Hobbes, Spinoza) give it a technical meaning as 'the many' set against the sovereign or the corporate people
- 19th century: the word becomes the stock term of oratory and journalism for the crowd and the electorate ('the great multitude'), and 'a multitude of sins' crystallises as a proverb
- 20th–21st centuries: everyday speech retreats to 'crowd'; 'multitude' grows bookish though still used for impressive scale ('a multitude of problems'); radical philosophy (Negri & Hardt, 2000) revives Spinoza's 'multitude' as the name of the global networked working class — a technical resurrection of a four-centuries-old social sense
