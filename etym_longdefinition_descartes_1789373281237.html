Descartes (proper noun; fully René Descartes, Latinized as Renatus Cartesius; 1596–1650) designates the seminal French philosopher, mathematician, and natural scientist widely celebrated as the father of modern Western philosophy, the architect of Continental rationalism, and the inventor of analytic geometry.

Operating during the tumultuous intellectual crisis of the 17th-century Scientific Revolution, Descartes sought to rebuild human knowledge upon an indubitable, rock-solid foundation. Rejecting the decaying scholastic Aristotelianism of medieval universities, he pioneered the method of radical hyperbolic doubt, demonstrating that while sensory perceptions and even mathematical axioms could theoretically be manipulated by an omnipotent deceiver (the malin génie), the very act of doubting proves beyond contradiction the existence of the thinking subject—crystallized in his immortal proposition, Cogito, ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"). From this solitary foundational certainty, Descartes reconstructed a rationalist universe governed by mathematical law and strict metaphysical division.

The name and legacy of Descartes display profound polysemy, functioning across philosophy, mathematics, literary genre theory, physiology, and cultural idiom:

  • The Historical Philosopher and the Rationalist Tradition: As an intellectual figure, Descartes represents the genesis of modern epistemology and rationalism. His masterpiece, Meditationes de Prima Philosophia (Meditations on First Philosophy, 1641), established the epistemic standard of "clear and distinct ideas" (*idées claires et distinctes*), arguing that human reason possesses innate truths placed in the mind by God, independent of deceptive sensory experience. His philosophy fundamentally redefined Western humanity's relationship to nature, transforming the cosmos from an organic, teleological organism into a vast, clockwork mathematical machine.
  • The Discourse as a Philosophical and Literary Genre: In literary history and genre theory—as directly invoked in the Proustian passage contrasting the novel with "the discourse (as Descartes would have it)"—Descartes represents the philosophical *discourse* (*Discours de la méthode*, 1637). Written not in scholastic Latin but in lucid, accessible vernacular French, the *Discourse on the Method* established a distinct literary genre: an intellectual autobiography and philosophical treatise narrating the author's personal journey through doubt, error, solitary contemplation beside a Bavarian stove (*poêle*), and systematic rational discovery. In French intellectual history, the "Cartesian discourse" established the paradigm of transparent, linear, logical argumentation where every step proceeds inexorably from first principles.
  • Cartesian Dualism (The Mind-Body Problem): In metaphysics and philosophy of mind, Descartes formulated substance dualism, dividing all reality into two radically distinct, incompatible substances:
    • Res cogitans (thinking substance): immaterial, unextended, indivisible, conscious mind and soul, endowed with free will and immune to physical decay.
    • Res extensa (extended substance): material, spatial, divisible, unthinking physical bodies and nature, governed exclusively by mechanistic, deterministic laws of motion and contact geometry.
    Descartes famously localized the mysterious interaction between these two distinct realms within the brain's pineal gland (*conarium*), bequeathing to modern philosophy, psychology, and neuroscience the perennial "mind-body problem."
  • Analytic Geometry and the Cartesian Coordinate System: In mathematics, Descartes revolutionized science by bridging classical Euclidean geometry with symbolic algebra in his 1637 treatise La Géométrie. By introducing the coordinate plane (the Cartesian grid of perpendicular $x$ and $y$ axes), he demonstrated that geometric shapes (lines, curves, circles, parabolas) can be represented as algebraic equations, and conversely that equations can be visualized geometrically. This invention laid the indispensable mathematical foundation for Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz to invent calculus.
  • Mechanistic Physics and Animal Automata: In natural philosophy and biology, Descartes championed an uncompromising mechanistic materialism. He rejected Aristotelian "occult qualities" and substantial forms, explaining optics, physiology, planetary motion (the vortex theory), and circulation through kinetic collisions of material particles. He notoriously posited the *bête-machine* (beast-machine) hypothesis, asserting that non-human animals lack conscious souls (*res cogitans*) and are merely complex, unfeeling biological automatons or clockwork mechanisms.
  • Cultural Eponym ("L'Esprit Cartésien"): In French cultural and linguistic idiom, the adjectival derivative Cartesian (French: cartésien; l'esprit cartésien) characterizes an intellectual temperament distinguished by rigorous logic, methodical clarity, skepticism toward unverified dogma, orderly structural arrangement, and elegant deduction from clear first principles.

In literary criticism and Marcel Proust's aesthetic philosophy, Descartes represents the sovereign ideal of philosophical discourse—a totalizing, rationalist prose architecture that Proust's modernist masterpiece sought simultaneously to absorb, emulate, and transcend through the alchemy of poetic memory.
