Derivation chain for TREACHERY: 1. Proto-Indo-European *terkʷ- (to twist, turn, wind) — reconstructed; the traditional, but debated, ancestor of Latin tricae. 2. Latin tricae (plural: trivial entanglements, tricks, trifles, perplexities) — attested from the 2nd c. BCE (Plautus). 3. Latin tricārī / tricāre (to make difficulties, quibble, shuffle, play tricks, dissemble) — attested 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE. 4. Vulgar Latin *triccāre (to cheat, trick, deceive) — reconstructed from Romance reflexes, late antiquity (4th–6th c. CE). 5. Old French trichier / trechier (to cheat, deceive, trick) — 10th–12th c. CE. 6. Old French tricherie / trecherie (cheating, deceit, treachery) — 12th c. CE. 7. Anglo-Norman trecherie → Middle English trecherie / triacherie (treachery, deceit) — 13th c. CE (attested c. 1250). 8. English TREACHERY (modern spelling with -ach- and -ery) — 14th–16th c. CE onward. Note on debate: the derivation of Latin tricae from PIE *terkʷ- "to twist, turn" is plausible — the image of twisting or entangling facts fits the sense "tricks, perplexities" — but it is not certain; some scholars prefer a separate or reduplicated source for tricae. The Vulgar Latin stage *triccāre is not directly attested and is reconstructed on the evidence of the Romance descendants (Old French trichier, Italian treccare, Old Occitan trichejar). The modern English spelling with -ach- was stabilized under the influence of the adjective treacherous (from Old French trecheros), while Modern French retained tricherie with the -ch- spelling.
