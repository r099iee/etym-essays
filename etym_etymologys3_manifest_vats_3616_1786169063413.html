THE EVOLUTION OF 'MANIFEST'

The story of manifest begins in the law courts of ancient Rome, with a thief caught red-handed. Latin manifestus is a compound of manus, 'hand', and the participial stem -festus, 'struck, seized', and its earliest attested colour is forensic: the Twelve Tables and the jurists speak of furtum manifestum, 'a manifest theft', one in which the criminal was seized with the goods literally in his grasp. A manifest fact was, for the Romans, a fact you could reach out and take hold of — palpable proof. This tactile, judicial origin gives the word its permanent temperament. Manifest things are not merely apparent; they are proven, exposed, seized, held up before the world. The image at the root is not the passive visibility of a thing, but the active, gripping demonstration of a truth.

  • Roman stage: manifestus means 'plain, clear, evident' of proofs, guilt and omens; the phrase manifestum furtum 'open, flagrant theft' is a fixed legal formula, and the word carries the weight of a verdict already delivered.
  • French bridge: Old French manifeste (12th century) brings the word into the vernacular with its judicial gravity intact; it remains an adjective of evidence and exposure.
  • Middle English: English borrows the word around 1400 meaning 'clearly revealed, plain to see', used of dreams, miracles, truths and events that stand openly before the mind's eye; the King James Bible (1611) makes 'manifest' one of its great theological words — 'the works of God should be made manifest', 'God manifest in the flesh' — giving the adjective a sacramental undertone of revelation.
  • Renaissance broadening: the 16th–17th centuries generate the noun and the verb. The noun manifest becomes the shipmaster's sworn list of cargo — the public 'showing' of what the hold conceals — while the verb to manifest means to show forth: symptoms manifest a disease, experiments manifest the properties of bodies, saints manifest grace. Locke and the empiricists use the word of ideas that are self-evident to the understanding, and Dryden and the neoclassicists of the doctrine of 'manifest' evidence of the external world.
  • The 18th–19th centuries: the adjective consolidates its formal dignity — 'manifest injustice', 'manifest error', 'manifest danger' become the fixed vocabulary of law and politics — and the word reaches its most famous hour in 1845, when the journalist John L. O'Sullivan coined Manifest Destiny. The plainest adjective of evidence was pressed into the service of the most ideological of doctrines: the westward expansion of the American republic was declared so obvious, so openly written in the course of events, that it required no argument. The phrase carried the word from the courtroom into prophecy.
  • The 20th–21st centuries: the word bifurcates. In formal and legal registers it remains a dignified intensifier — 'manifest failure', 'manifest error'. In self-help and New Age culture the verb is reborn and inverted: to 'manifest' your desires is to summon them into reality by intention and visualisation, a secularised miracle in which what is manifested was never hidden but only wished for. And the noun has gone digital: a software 'manifest' is the metadata file listing an application's components, permissions and dependencies — the cargo list of a digital vessel. In seven centuries the word has travelled from the Roman courtroom to the smartphone, and at every stop it has meant the same thing: making the concealed visible and certifiable, seizing the truth by the hand.


THE EVOLUTION OF 'VATS'

The history of vat is the history of a workman's word, and it is older than English itself. Its ancestor is the Proto-Germanic noun *fatą, 'vessel, container', a word so basic that it belonged to every Germanic household and appears in every branch of the family: Old Norse fat, Old Saxon fat, Old High German faz, German Faß (the beer and wine cask of the modern tavern), Dutch vat, and Gothic fat 'vessel'. The PIE ancestry is genuinely uncertain — Watkins proposed *ped- / *pod-, 'vessel', with Greek pithos 'wine jar' as a distant cognate, while other scholars favour a connection to *pet-, 'to spread', the image of a hollowed, spread-open container — but the Germanic stage is solidly attested, and the word's meaning has never strayed far from 'container'.

  • Old English: fæt means any vessel, pot or cup. It is the water vessel of the household, the wæterfæt; the poet's cup of ceremony; the humble pot of daily cooking. Anglo-Saxon poets and law-codes alike use it of containers of every sort, from the precious cup to the common tub — a broad, unglamorous word for holding things.
  • The Middle English sound shift: around the 12th–13th centuries, the southern dialects of England began to voice initial /f/ to /v/, a change that produced dozens of modern doublets — fox / vixen among them — and turned fæt into vat in the south while the north kept 'fat'. The southern form won the standard, which is why the modern word is vat and not 'fat'.
  • Industrial narrowing: from the late Middle Ages onward the word tightens from 'any vessel' to its modern specialist sense: the large open container used in brewing, dyeing, tanning, pickling and wine-making. The brewer's vat, the dyer's vat of indigo, the tanner's vat of lime are fixtures of every pre-modern process industry. It is a word of the workshop and the wharf, never of the drawing room — the plainest possible word for the biggest possible container.
  • The industrial and scientific revolution: the 19th century scales the vat up: iron and steel fermentation vats, chemical vats, the great containers of the new industrial chemistry. The vat becomes the emblem of mass production — anonymous, capacious, ceaselessly working.
  • The 20th-century leap into the artificial: the vat enters science fiction and synthetic biology. It is the pulp and golden-age writers — and Vance pre-eminently — who make the vat the womb of manufactured life: 'vat-grown', 'vat-born', the creature raised in a nutrient tank, artificial humanity in a steel cauldron. In Turjan of Miir the alchemist's vats are exactly this: the tanks in which homunculi are cultured, the false wombs of the story.
  • Metaphor and accident: since the 20th century 'a vat of' has meant an undifferentiated abundance — a vat of coffee, a vat of money, a vat of rage — and the coincidence with the tax acronym VAT (value added tax) has made the word a gift to headline writers. Yet through every extension the core has never moved: a vat is a large open container in which things are processed. Where manifest climbed into abstraction and ideology, vat stayed anchored in the brewhouse, the tannery and the laboratory — and in this card the two words together enact the oldest epistemological drama in the language, the open word against the closed container, the visible against the concealed.
