Inept is an adjective meaning lacking skill, competence, or aptitude; awkwardly unsuitable, clumsy, and ineffective in action or expression. To call a person inept is to say they bungle the job — the inept manager loses papers, the inept driver stalls at every junction, the inept teacher cannot explain what good writing is. To call an action, remark, or attempt inept is to say it is out of place and ineffectual — an inept apology, an inept metaphor, an inept intervention. The core is a mismatch between demand and capacity, producing foolishness, blundering, or embarrassing failure.

Modern dictionaries distinguish two closely linked branches. The dominant branch is unskilful, incompetent: lacking the knowledge, dexterity, or judgement a task requires. Here inept is stronger than inexperienced and more judgemental than unskilled; it implies a painful, almost comic inadequacy (wonderfully inept, utterly inept). The second branch is unsuitable, out of place, inappropriate: an inept remark at a funeral, inept praise, inept timing. This older, more Latinate sense survives in formal prose — inept as infelicitous rather than merely clumsy. Both senses meet in the source sentence: English teachers are wonderfully inept at describing good writing, simultaneously unskilled and producing infelicitous critical language.

Polysemy extends into domain-specific uses. In law, an inept charge historically meant a badly framed pleading, void for informality. In medicine and psychology, socially inept denotes inability to read cues, sustaining conversation, or behaving tactfully — the inept suitor, the inept guest. In politics and journalism, inept leadership signals administrative bungling, dithering, and loss of authority. The adverb ineptly and nouns ineptness, ineptitude carry the same charge: ineptitude is not mere ignorance but displayed, consequential clumsiness.

  • Unskilful / incompetent: lacking ability; an inept cook burns soup, an inept handyman strips screws; the central contemporary sense.
  • Foolish / bungling: absurdly clumsy in a way that invites ridicule; inept attempts to flirt, inept cover-ups.
  • Unsuitable / inappropriate: out of place in context; an inept comparison, inept condolences; the classical Latinate sense.
  • Socially inept: unable to navigate interaction gracefully; awkward, tone-deaf, tactless in company.
  • Legally inept (archaic): of writs or allegations, defectively stated and therefore void; preserved in older law reports.
  • Grammatical profile: predicative (be inept at, inept in) and attributive (inept handling); collocates with utterly, hopelessly, wonderfully, socially, politically; antonyms adept, apt, competent, dexterous, felicitous.

Tone is formal-critical rather than vulgar. Inept belongs to educated evaluation — book reviews, reports, appraisals — and wounds precisely because it sounds measured. To be called inept is worse than to be called inexperienced: it suggests a settled incapacity, a constitutional unfitness for the role claimed, whether describing prose or praising it.
