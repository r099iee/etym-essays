- Old English Period (c. 9th–11th Century) - Individual roots *pīn* (borrowed from Latin *pīnus*) and *wudu* flourished separately in Old English, occasionally appearing in descriptive phrases like *pīntrēowes wudu* ("wood of the pine tree").
- 14th to 15th Century (Late Middle English) - The distinct compound noun emerges in trade, building accounts, and silvicultural records to specify pine timber as distinct from native oak, ash, or elm.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - Solidified across maritime shipbuilding logs, carpentry manuals, and domestic inventories as England imported vast quantities of Baltic pine wood ("deal") for flooring, masts, and house joinery.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Acquired strong pastoral and literary resonance in romantic poetry and fairy tales (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*), symbolizing both wild northern forests ("the pinewood") and fragrant domestic hearth fires ("pinewood fire").
- 20th to 21st Century (Contemporary Usage) - Widely employed in interior design, sustainable construction, paper chemistry, and ecological conservation, denoting Scandinavian-style pine furniture, engineered timber, and protected pine habitats.
