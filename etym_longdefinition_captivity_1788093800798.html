Captivity — the state or condition of being captured, imprisoned, confined, or held against one's will; the condition of being a captive. In the Tolkien context, the Elvish sword Orcrist had been taken from Thorin when the dwarves were captured and imprisoned by the Elves of Mirkwood: Thorin languished in their halls, and the sword passed from him "in captivity".

Primary senses:

  • Imprisonment of persons: being held in gaol, camp, or dungeon — by a state (prisoners of war, hostages, political prisoners) or by private captors (kidnappers, pirates).
  • Confinement of animals: "in captivity" is the standard conservation phrase for animals kept in zoos, reserves, or breeding programmes, contrasted with "in the wild"; captive breeding has saved species such as the California condor and the Arabian oryx.
  • Slavery and bondage: the historical core of the word — the state of the enslaved, from Roman captives of war to the transatlantic slave trade.

Historical and scriptural extensions:

  • The Captivity (capitalized): the Babylonian exile of the Jews in the 6th century BCE — the "Babylonish Captivity" of the Old Testament; the King James Bible uses "captivity" throughout for this exile.
  • The Babylonian Captivity of the Church: the 14th-century Avignon papacy (1309–1377), when the popes resided in France; the name was applied by contemporaries and fixed by Luther's treatise "On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church" (1520), which used the Old Testament image to attack papal bondage.
  • Captivity narratives: a literary genre born in the 17th–18th centuries, first among European captives of indigenous peoples in North America (Mary Rowlandson's 1682 account is the archetype) and later in the Barbary captivity books; the genre fed both sensationalism and antislavery writing.
  • Spiritual captivity: Christian discourse of the soul held "captive" by sin, the Devil, or the law (Paul: "leading captivity captive", Ephesians 4:8, itself quoting Psalm 68) — the ancient Latin captivitas carried this sense from Late Antiquity.

Metaphorical and technical senses (modern):

  • Captive audience: people unable to leave while being addressed — passengers on a flight, students in a lecture, viewers during a compulsory ad-break; the idiom means exactly "people held, unable to escape".
  • Captive market: consumers with no alternative supplier — customers of a railway refreshment room, a prison commissary, or a proprietary software ecosystem; economics borrowed the word's core: no freedom to choose.
  • Captive insurance / captive company: an insurer owned by its own parent to insure the parent's risks — "captive" in the sense of dedicated, not free-floating in the market.
  • Captive portal: in networking, the login page that seizes your connection until you accept terms (hotel Wi-Fi, airport Wi-Fi) — a technical metaphor that has revived the word in everyday digital life.
  • Captive breeding / animals in captivity: standard conservation terminology (captive assurance populations, reintroduction programmes).
  • Metaphors of the mind: "held captive by an idea", "captive to fear" — the passive, helpless register of the word is its strongest modern colour.

The semantic bridge: Latin capere "to seize, take". A captive is one who has been taken; captivity is the state of having been taken and held. Every sense — war prisoner, zoo animal, enslaved person, Wi-Fi login page — is "taken and held", and the word's history is the history of that seizing: military, legal, economic, spiritual, and finally digital.

Summary: CAPTIVITY = the state of being seized and held against one's will — by armies, gaolers, enslavers, zoos, markets, or login pages.
