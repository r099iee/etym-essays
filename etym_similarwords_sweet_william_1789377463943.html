• Carnation: Similar because both belong to the genus Dianthus and possess sweet, clove-scented fringed flowers; differs because a carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus) produces larger, solitary or few-flowered blooms on taller stems, whereas Sweet-william (Dianthus barbatus) bears dense, flat-topped terminal clusters of numerous smaller florets.
  • Pink: Similar because both are members of the Caryophyllaceae family sharing fringed petals and fragrant nectar; differs because "pink" is a broad generic common name for dwarf, creeping, or rock-garden Dianthus species (such as Dianthus plumarius), whereas Sweet-william is an upright biennial with distinctive "bearded" flower clusters.
  • Wallflower: Similar because both are traditional biennial cottage-garden plants celebrated for rich colors and spicy, sweet fragrance; differs because the wallflower (Erysimum cheiri) belongs to the cabbage/mustard family (Brassicaceae) with four-petaled cruciform blooms, whereas Sweet-william belongs to the pink family (Caryophyllaceae) with five fringed petals.
  • Sweet pea: Similar because both are fragrant, heirloom cottage-garden favorites bearing "sweet" in their common names; differs because the sweet pea (Lathyrus odoratus) is a climbing leguminous vine of the Fabaceae family with papilionaceous (butterfly-like) blossoms, whereas Sweet-william is an erect, self-supporting herbaceous pink.
  • Campion: Similar because both are herbaceous plants in the Caryophyllaceae family; differs because campion (genus Silene) typically has deeply notched, five-lobed petals with swollen calyxes and lacks the dense, flat, multicolored cluster morphology of Sweet-william.
  • Stock: Similar because both are old-fashioned, highly fragrant biennial border flowers; differs because stock (Matthiola incana) produces dense vertical spires or spikes of cruciform flowers, whereas Sweet-william forms flattened, rounded corymb-like terminal heads.
