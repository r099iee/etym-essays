- 14th–15th c. [Middle English banefyre / bonefyre] - Literally a fire of animal bones burned on Midsummer Eve (St. John's Eve) and pestilence days to disinfect miasmas and clear slaughterhouse waste.
- 16th c. [Tudor England] - Folk etymology began interpreting the word as 'good fire' (from French bon 'good'), as clean timber replaced bones and the blazes became civic celebrations for royal births, coronations, and military victories.
- 17th c. [Post-1605 Jacobean Era] - Deeply intertwined with Guy Fawkes Night (November 5th) and the burning of papal and political effigies, cementing its status as an annual national festival.
- 19th–20th c. [Modern Era] - Expanded to denote autumnal agricultural garden refuse fires and powerful cultural idioms like Tom Wolfe's 'Bonfire of the Vanities' and political 'bonfires of regulations.'
