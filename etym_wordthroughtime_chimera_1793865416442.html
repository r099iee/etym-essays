- Archaic & Classical Greece (c. 8th–4th c. BCE) - A literal, terrifying female fire-breathing beast of Lycian myth slain by Bellerophon; represented chaotic natural destruction, volcanic earth vents, and terrifying composite monstrosity.
- Roman Antiquity & Middle Ages (c. 1st c. BCE–14th c. CE) - Allegorized in Christian bestiaries, patristic homilies, and Dante's Inferno as an emblem of deceptive temptation, carnal lust, or composite worldly fraud and vanity.
- Late 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Early Modernity) - Figuratively secularized into French (chimère) and English to designate an absurd, wild, or monstrous figment of an overheated imagination, ridiculous project, or impossible pipe dream.
- 18th & 19th Century (Enlightenment & Victorian Era) - Standard rhetorical term in political philosophy, economics, and literature for utopian schemes, unattainable social panaceas, or illusory political manifestos.
- 20th–21st Century (Genetics, Biology, & Computing) - Codified as a precise scientific term ('genetic chimera') for an organism composed of cell populations derived from distinct zygotes; also in paleontology for fraudulent composite fossils and digital asset synthesis.
