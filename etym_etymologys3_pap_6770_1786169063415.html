THE EVOLUTION OF 'PAP'

Pap is not a word that evolved by the usual machinery of sound law and borrowing; it is a word that was born in the mouth. Its history begins in the universal baby-talk of Indo-European children, in the syllable pa-pa, among the first sounds any infant can make — a labial stop repeated, the sound of lips opening for food. Latin pappa was the child's word for eating, recorded by Varro; Greek had pappa; and the same syllable, endlessly reduplicated, produces the world's 'papa', 'dad', 'food' and 'breast' words. Pap is thus one of the few English words whose etymology is genuinely prehistorical — not because we can reconstruct a proto-form, but because babies have always made this sound.

  • Latin and Romance: Latin pappa 'food' passed into Old French as pape / pappe 'mush, porridge', and the nursery word crossed the Channel with the Anglo-French speech of the court and the clergy. Middle English pap appears by the 15th century meaning soft food for infants.
  • The feeding sense: through the 16th–18th centuries 'pap' is the standard weaning food of English infancy, fed by pap-boat and pap-spoon; the word is everywhere in domestic and medical writing. The physicians and nursery manuals of the day prescribe 'thin pap' for invalids, and pap-feeding becomes a small institution of childcare. At the same time the plural 'paps' is the standard, even biblical, word for the breast — the Authorised Version of the Bible uses it — keeping the word tethered to nursing and dependence.
  • The 18th-century medical turn: pap takes on a darker tint as physicians begin to blame 'pap-feeding' for infant mortality and weak bones — babies fed on pap were said to grow rickets — and the word is implicated in the great debates over wet-nursing and infant care. The gentle food of infancy is now also a suspected poison.
  • The great figurative shift, 19th century: the modern sense is born. From the 1840s onward 'pap' comes to mean anything soft, undemanding, intellectually infantile — the newspapers 'feed pap', politicians dish out 'pap to the masses', reviewers dismiss mawkish novels as 'pap'. The metaphor is precise and pitiless: the public is treated as a baby, spoon-fed food that requires no chewing and no thought. This is the reading embedded in Vance's scene, where the alchemist spoon-feeds his homunculus, a creature that cannot yet think for itself.
  • The 20th century: the literal sense recedes as 'baby food' and commercial cereals (including 'Pablum', a Canadian trademark of 1931 that repeated the whole story in one word) take over the nursery; 'pap' survives mainly as the contemptuous noun, and 'Pap smear' (from Papanicolaou, unrelated) adds a confusing homograph to the confusion of the English lexicon. The trajectory is complete: from the softest, most loving word for a baby's food to the sharpest dismissal of what is fed to a passive mind — the word has done, in seven centuries, the exact journey that 'pablum' would compress into seventy years.
