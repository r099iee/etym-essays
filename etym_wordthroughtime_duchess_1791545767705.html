- 14th c. — Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman; denoted the wife of an English or French duke, celebrated as an exalted figure of chivalric reverence in Chaucer's The Book of the Duchess.
- 15th–16th c. — Associated with high feudal power and dynasty; female rulers in their own right (such as Anne of Brittany) commanded vast territories, while Renaissance drama framed the duchess as an emblem of tragic agency and courtly intrigue.
- 17th c. — Central to Jacobean tragedy (most famously John Webster's The Duchess of Malfi), where the title symbolized aristocratic dignity clashing with patriarchal tyranny and moral corruption.
- 18th–19th c. — Solidified as the supreme title of the British peerage; simultaneously adopted into French culinary art (pommes duchesse) and parodied in Victorian fantasy (Lewis Carroll's grotesque, pepper-sneezing Duchess in Alice).
- Early–mid 20th c. — Developed into Commonwealth and Australian political slang (the verb to duchess, meaning to flatter or co-opt through lavish hospitality), while British working-class slang used it affectionately or mockingly for a matriarch or haughty woman.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Prominent in contemporary global media surrounding royal marriages (e.g., Duchess of Cambridge, Duchess of Sussex), historical period dramas, culinary menus, and the enduring Australian political idiom.
