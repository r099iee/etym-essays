twang: (noun) A sharp, ringing, vibrating sound — the note made by a taut string that has been plucked or released, snapping back into vibration; (verb) to make, or cause to make, such a sound. The word also denotes a thin, nasal, vibrating quality of the voice and, by extension, a regional accent that rings with that quality. In the context passage. In "Next moment he heard a twang just beside his ear" (C. S. Lewis, "Prince Caspian"), the word names the exact acoustic signature of Susan's bow being loosed: the bowstring, drawn taut and released, snaps back and sets up a thin, high, ringing vibration. The nuance is instantaneous and percussive; the reader hears the sound before understanding its cause, and the twang itself becomes the pivot of the scene — the instant the arrow is away, the instant before the soldier throws up his arms and falls into the water. Lewis uses the word for its sharpness: it is not a thud or a crash but a precise, resonant, string-born note that cuts through the silence beside Peter's ear. The tone is onomatopoeic and faintly menacing — the bow has spoken, and the word makes the weapon itself vivid and present. Polysemy and modern senses. The word has three well-defined lives:
  • The string-sound proper (the oldest sense): a bowstring, lute string, guitar string, or banjo string plucked or released. As a verb: to twang a string; a string twangs. This sense covers every taut-string instrument and the weapon from which the word's earliest attestation draws its image.
  • The vocal sense (from the 17th century): a "nasal twang" is speech produced with a thin, vibrating, nasal resonance — the voice ringing through the taut membrane of the nose and mouth. A "regional twang" (Southern American, Australian, South African, and other accents) is an accent heard as sharp, pinched, and vibrating, like a plucked string.
  • The musical-jargon sense (20th century): "twang" is the standard word for the bright, ringing, often nasal guitar and banjo tone of country, rockabilly, surf, and bluegrass music; "twangy" describes the sound, and "a twang" can name a style, a guitar part, or a sound signature.
Derived and inflected forms include twanged (past), twanging (progressive and gerund), twangy (adjective, 20th century), twanginess (noun), and the jocular twangster (a player of twangy music). The semantic bridge. The bridge from string to voice is acoustic: both are vibrating membranes. A plucked string and a nasal voice share the same physics — a taut, resonating surface set into rapid vibration — so English simply extended the word for one to the other. The bridge from bow to instrument to accent is the same: wherever a taut membrane vibrates sharply, the sound is a twang. Collocations and register. Twang collocates with bowstring, guitar, banjo, wire, and with nasal, Southern, Australian (before the accent-sense); "with a twang" describes both sound and speech. The register is informal-to-standard, vivid and onomatopoeic, equally at home in narrative, music journalism, and dialect description. Synonym boundaries. The sound-vocabulary of English draws sharp boundaries here:
  • thud: dull, low, and heavy — the sound of impact, a body falling; twang is high, thin, and vibrating — the sound of a taut string released.
  • clang / bang: struck metal or a loud explosion; twang is softer in volume but sharper in pitch, and it is born of tension, not of impact.
  • snap / crack: dry, brittle sounds of breaking; a released bowstring does not break — it twangs, vibrating as the tension is released, and the vibration, not the fracture, is the point.
  • ping: a short, metallic, echoic note of a small struck object; twang is the string-sound proper, with a slightly longer, more nasal ring.
  • pluck / strum: the actions that produce a twang, rather than the sound itself.
  • twitch / twinge: the pull and the sharp sensation, sharing the tw- onset of tension words but naming neither the sound nor the vibration.
