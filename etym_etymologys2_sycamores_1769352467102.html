Non-Indo-European origin — the Greek elements are substrate loans, so there is NO PIE root for this word; the chain is presented from its earliest recoverable stage. Stage 1 — Ancient Greek sukon "fig" (attested 5th century BCE; of obscure, probably pre-Greek / Aegean substrate origin, not a native PIE word) + Ancient Greek moron "black mulberry" (same uncertain substrate stratum; cf. Armenian mor "blackberry"). Stage 2 — Ancient Greek sukomoros "fig-mulberry tree" — the tree Ficus sycomorus, a compound describing a tree with fig-like fruit and mulberry-like leaves (Classical Greek, 4th century BCE onward). Stage 3 — Latin sycomorus "the fig-mulberry tree" (Imperial Latin, 1st century CE; loan from Greek). Stage 4 — Old French sicamor / sicamour "the sycomore fig" (medieval French, from Latin). Stage 5 — Middle English sicamour / sycomore "the biblical fig-mulberry tree" (14th century; via Old French and the Wycliffite Bible translations). Stage 6 — English sycamore, referent transferred: (a) to Acer pseudoplatanus, the great maple, in 16th-century Britain; (b) to Platanus occidentalis and other American plane trees in 18th-century North America. —> sycamores (plural) standard modern form. Debate / nuance note: The Greek elements sukon and moron are not securely traced to Indo-European and are widely judged to be Mediterranean substrate loans — hence no Indo-European root should be asserted. The transfer of the name to maples and planes is a documented semantic accident (their broad lobed leaves resembled the biblical tree in European imagination), not a botanical or cognate relationship.
