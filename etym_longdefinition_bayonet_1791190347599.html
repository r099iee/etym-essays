A bayonet is a blade designed to be fixed to the muzzle of a rifle or musket so that the firearm doubles as a spear for thrusting in close combat; by extension the word names the practice and shock tactic of bayonet fighting itself.

Technically there are two families. The plug bayonet, earliest, had a tapered handle jammed directly into the barrel, preventing firing once fixed. The socket and later ring, stud, and knife bayonets attach alongside or under the barrel, allowing the soldier to shoot and stab. Modern knife-bayonets double as field knives, wire-cutters, and saws when detached. Drill distinguishes fix bayonets, charge bayonets, cross bayonets, and ground arms. The passage lists bayonet as the ultimate hardening of the stick in war: hoe in agriculture, lance and sword in war, and finally blade-on-gun where pike and firearm merge.

Tactically the bayonet's importance peaked in the 17th to early 20th centuries. Before repeating rifles, a volley followed by a bayonet rush often decided battles from Steinkirk to Borodino to Gettysburg. Armies cultivated cold steel morale, the belief that willingness to close with the bayonet proved discipline. World War I showed the limits in trench wire and machine-gun fire, yet bayonet drill persisted for aggression training. Today the weapon is rarely used to wound but remains issued for guard, crowd control, ceremony, and psychological effect; most casualties come from bullets, shells, and drones.

  • Plug bayonet (17th c.): dagger with tapering plug fitting bore; e.g. English Civil War and Restoration musketeers; obsolete because it blocks loading and firing.
  • Socket / ring bayonet (late 17th–19th c.): triangular or cruciform blade on slotted socket over muzzle; allows volley plus charge; Brown Bess, Charleville, Springfield patterns.
  • Sword and knife bayonet (19th c.–present): long blade with hilt and locking ring doubling as sidearm; Chassepot, Mauser, AKM, M7, M9 variants with sawback and wire-cutter scabbard.
  • Ceremonial and guard use: fixed bayonets for sentries, colors, funerals; chrome or polished blades for parade; symbol of readiness and discipline.
  • Figurative shock: at the point of the bayonet, imposed by force; bayonet strength, countable effectives; bayonet charge as metaphor for all-out aggressive push in business or politics.
  • Verb to bayonet: to stab with fixed blade; to compel at bayonet-point; past bayoneted, bayonetted spellings; stark, brutal register, used in war reporting and war-crimes law.

Polysemy is narrow because function dominates. All senses orbit fixing blade to gun: object, act of fixing, tactic of charging, and metaphor of coercion. The bridge from tool to terror is drill and memory: veterans' memoirs, from Waterloo to the Somme to Korea, recall the rattle of fixing, the glint in half-light, and the moral shock exceeding physical casualties. International humanitarian law treats bayonet wounds like other bladed wounds but remembers triangular blades myths about banning; in fact no specific ban exists, though serrated or barbed variants raise unnecessary-suffering scrutiny.

Usage notes: countable noun, plural bayonets; verb regular; compounds bayonet-fitting, bayonet-charge, bayonet-drill. Collocates with fix, fix and charge, bristling with, at bayonet point. Pronunciation stresses the first syllable with reduced middle vowel. Register is military-technical and historical; in civilian prose it signals organized violence rather than criminal stabbing. Distinguish from bayonet lug, bayonet mount in engineering where bayonet fitting means push-and-twist lock inspired by the same motion.
