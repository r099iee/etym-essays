Classical Latin Augury and Etymological Debate:

The English noun obscenity derives from the classical Latin noun obscēnitās (genitive obscēnitātis), meaning "foulness, repulsiveness, moral impurity, or lewdness", which was formed from the adjective obscēnus (or obscaenus). The ultimate etymological origin of Latin obscēnus is one of the most fascinating subjects in classical philology, with two primary scholarly explanations:

  1. Augural and Religious Origin (The Ill-Omened Theory): In ancient Roman religion and augury, obscēnus originally designated a sign, bird flight, or omen that was inauspicious, sinister, or ill-boding. Linguists (including de Vaan and Ernout-Meillet) link the second element to the Proto-Indo-European root *skai- ("left, sinister, ill-omened", cognate with Latin scaevus and Greek skaiós, "left-handed, clumsy, ominous"). Thus, ob-scaevus originally meant "confronting an ill-omened or sinister sign."
  2. The Folk/Literary Etymology (Filth Theory): Ancient Roman grammarians (such as Varro and Festus) connected the word to caenum ("filth, mud, dung"), interpreting obscēnus as "covered in filth or moral dirtiness." Another popular folk etymology connected it to Greek skēnē ("stage"), falsely claiming it meant "off-stage" (things too horrific to show on stage).

In classical Latin literature (in the works of Cicero, Virgil, and Livy), the word shifted from religious augury to general moral and aesthetic disgust, signifying anything repulsive, indecent, foul-mouthed, or shameful.

Renaissance Transmission via French into English (Late 16th Century):

In the 16th century, the term was adopted into Middle French as obscénité, denoting gross lewdness and foul language. The word was formally imported into Early Modern English in the late 1590s as obscenity (alongside the adjective obscene, c. 1593).

William Shakespeare was among the earliest English playwrights to utilize both the adjective and the noun. In Love's Labour's Lost (c. 1595), the pedantic schoolmaster Holofernes rails against coarse vulgarities, exclaiming against "such rackers of orthography" and noting an "obscenity." Similarly, in Richard II, the Bishop of Carlisle denounces a heinous crime as an "obscene deed."

19th-Century Victorian Law and Censorship:

In the 19th century, with the rise of mass-market printing, the British Parliament enacted the Obscene Publications Act of 1857 (Lord Campbell's Act). In the landmark 1868 case Regina v. Hicklin, Chief Justice Alexander Cockburn laid down the famous "Hicklin test", defining obscenity as anything having a tendency "to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences." This rigid Victorian standard was used for decades to censor literary masterpieces by Gustave Flaubert, James Joyce, D.H. Lawrence, and Radclyffe Hall.

20th-Century Constitutional Revolutions to Contemporary Times:

In 20th-century American jurisprudence, obscenity became the battleground for defining modern civil liberties. The U.S. Supreme Court gradually dismantled Victorian standards in cases like Roth v. United States (1957) and culminated in Miller v. California (1973), which established the three-prong Miller Test requiring proof that a work appeals to prurient interest, is patently offensive, and lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.

In the 1964 case Jacobellis v. Ohio, Justice Potter Stewart delivered his immortal pragmatic dictum: "I know it when I see it." Today, obscenity continues to function as an indispensable legal concept, a social category for profane language, and a powerful rhetorical indictment of moral and economic outrages.
