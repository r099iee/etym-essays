• Decorum: Similar because both mean correct social behaviour; differs because decorum emphasises outward fittingness — the seemliness of manner, dress, and gesture — closer to ceremonial beauty, whereas propriety is more purely ethical, the moral fitness of conduct to one’s station.
  • Etiquette: Similar because both mean rules of polite behaviour; differs because etiquette is far narrower — the codified rules of polite society, a mere list of manners — whereas propriety reaches into moral obligation.
  • Decency: Similar because both mean respectable conduct; differs because decency names a moral baseline — what is fundamentally respectable, often with a charge of sexual modesty — while propriety is broader and more social, about fittingness to role.
  • Seemliness: Similar because both mean fitting, correct behaviour; differs because seemliness is the more archaic synonym stressing aesthetic suitability — how things appear — while propriety stresses the moral fitness of conduct.
  • Fitness: Similar because both mean suitability of conduct to circumstance; differs because fitness is a general, almost mechanical word for appropriateness, whereas propriety carries the weight of social and moral convention.
  • Protocol: Similar because both mean correct procedure; differs because protocol is procedural, official, even diplomatic — written rules of formal procedure — whereas propriety is diffuse and unwritten, a whole climate of accepted behaviour.
  • Respectability: Similar because both mean standing in the eyes of society; differs because respectability names the status one enjoys, while propriety names the conduct that earns and maintains it.
  • Conventionality: Similar because both mean conformity to accepted norms; differs because conventionality emphasises mere custom and habit, while propriety can imply a reasoned, morally aware sense of what is fitting.
  • Correctness: Similar because both mean doing things the accepted way; differs because correctness is neutral and factual — right in the technical sense — without the moral and social weight that propriety carries in its strongest uses.
