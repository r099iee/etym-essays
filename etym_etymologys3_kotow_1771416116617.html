Kotow is one of the most famous Chinese loans in the English lexicon, yet its career is double-edged: it travelled first as a sober ethnographer’s term for an imperial ritual and then decayed, in popular usage, into a synonym for servile flattery. The word is built from the Mandarin compound kētóu (磕頭), literally “to knock the head,” a compound of 磕 kē “to strike, to knock against” and 頭 tóu “head.” The earliest layer of the concept, however, belongs to the Old Chinese form reconstructed as *khəp-do, whose meaning is precisely the gesture the novel describes: kneeling and bringing the forehead down to the ground in a controlled, repeated striking. In the Chinese textual record the ritual is far older than the modern word; by the Han period the court audience required the “three kneelings and nine knockings” (三跪九叩), and the verb kētóu appears in classical histories describing barbarian envoys and loyal ministers abasing themselves before the Son of Heaven. The compound is thus not simply a label for a gesture but a graded instrument of hierarchy, calibrated by the number of prostrations owed. Sound and form at each stage:
  • Old Chinese *khəp-do: “to knock the head,” the ritual gesture of kneeling and striking the forehead, attested under the graphs 磕頭.
  • Middle Chinese: the compound survives, the second syllable developing as tóu “head” (Middle Chinese *duw); the ritual word of the imperial audience is 叩頭 kòutóu.
  • Cantonese kau-tau and Hokkien khàu-thâu 叩頭: the southern coastal forms first heard by foreign traders at Guangzhou.
  • Portuguese cotau / k’o-t’ou (16th c.): transcription of the court prostration that carried the word toward Europe.
  • English kowtow, kow-tow, kotow (early 19th c.): the noun sense “the prostration performed before the Chinese emperor.”
From Middle Chinese the word bifurcated into the regional forms that Europeans would each record in their own way. The southern coast, where the ritual confronted Portuguese and later British merchants and missionaries, produced the Cantonese form kau-tau; it was this shape, transliterated through Portuguese and early English accounts, that gave English its kowtow and its rarer spelling kotow. The borrowing entered English in the first decades of the nineteenth century, initially as a noun naming the prostration performed before the emperor; the famous 1793 episode of Lord Macartney’s embassy—he bent the knee but refused the full kotow—fixed the word in diplomatic memory. By the 1870s the metaphorical extension was complete: a “kowtow” was any abject surrender of dignity, and “to kowtow to” meant to grovel before power, money, or opinion, a sense that dominates modern journalism and idiom. Socio-cultural and literary trajectory:
  • 18th century: travel and embassy accounts treat the kotow as an exotic, alien ritual of the Qing court, described with ethnographic distance.
  • 19th century: the word becomes a diplomatic flashpoint, a symbol of the clash between European protocol and Chinese court ceremonial after the Macartney (1793) and Amherst (1816) embassies.
  • 1870s onward: the figurative sense overtakes the literal one; newspapers apply “kowtow” to any servile surrender in politics and commerce.
  • 20th–21st centuries: kotow and kowtow coexist as accepted spellings; the word is used overwhelmingly in metaphor, while historians and cultural writers still invoke the literal ritual to explain Chinese social hierarchy.
Within the novel, the kotow carries the full weight of this history. When Dai-yu “would have fallen on her knees and made her kotow” to Grandmother Jia, the narrator is drawing on a gesture that encodes, in one physical motion, the entire moral economy of the Jia household: the junior member certifies her respect and her virtue through bodily surrender, and the elder, by intercepting the prostration with an embrace, declares that kinship outranks etiquette. The word functions here as a gravity well of social meaning—precisely because the ritual is habitual in the novel’s world, its interruption becomes extraordinary. The modern reader, hearing the idiom’s servile overtones, grasps at once the cost of the gesture that Grandmother Jia’s love, for a moment, spares her granddaughter.
