PIE *ger- "to turn, bend, curl" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; the precise form is debated — see note) → Proto-Germanic *krumbo- / *krumb- "crooked, bent, curved" (reconstructed; the ancestor of Old High German krump "crooked," German krumm "crooked, bent," Dutch krom "bent, crooked," Old Norse krumpinn "shrivelled, crumpled") → Old English crump "crooked, bent, curled, curved" (attested from the 9th–10th c. in glosses and prose; dialectal English crump kept the sense "crooked, bent" into modern times) → Middle English crumplen — a frequentative verb formed on the stem crump- with the iterative suffix -len ("to press or squeeze into folds, to bend, curl, wrinkle"; attested 15th c., c. 1480s–90s in the OED) → Middle English crumpled — past participle of crumplen ("pressed into folds, wrinkled, creased") → Modern English crumpled "crushed into creases and wrinkles" (the participle settled into general use as the standard adjective for crushed paper, cloth, and clothing; the figurative "collapsed, gave way" sense of the verb is attested from the 19th c.) → Modern English CRUMPLED "crushed to form creases and wrinkles" ◄ TARGET rootWord Debate / nuance note: The PIE etymon of the Germanic *krumbo- "crooked" family is not fully settled. It is commonly linked to the root *ger- "to turn, bend, curl" (also seen in Greek gurós "curved" and Latin gyrus "a circle, ring"), but the labial element *-mb- has led some scholars to posit a separate root *gremb- or to treat the Germanic forms as an independent expressive formation of obscure prehistory. The Old English → Middle English → modern path, by contrast, is fully attested and certain; the only uncertainty lies in the word's deep Indo-European stage.
