catacomb — the word in this passage and in the language. In the source sentence — "The place was bare, with the death-like emptiness of some long-deserted catacomb" — the word detonates a precise cluster of associations. A catacomb is an underground cemetery: a labyrinth of galleries and recessed burial niches hewn into rock, built to hold the dead in their thousands. The simile transfers all of that to the cave: the place is not merely empty but "death-like" empty — an emptiness with a history, the emptied-out hush of a place built for the dead and then abandoned to silence. The word contributes "bareness" twice over, for catacombs were stripped of bodies and goods by grave-robbers and by time; and it contributes monumentality, for a catacomb is not a chance hollow but a deliberate, engineered darkness, a city of the dead. "Long-deserted" reinforces aeons of abandonment, and "death-like emptiness" makes the silence itself feel like a residue of the departed — a vacuum left behind by the countless who once lay there. The tone is sepulchral and awe-struck: the explorers have not entered a cave but a tomb, and the geology of the tunnel has been transformed into a structure of human grief and death.

1. Primary sense (the defining one). An underground cemetery: a network of subterranean galleries and chambers with recesses (niches, loculi) cut into the walls to receive coffins or bodies. The word is usually used in the plural — the catacombs — because such cemeteries are systems of interconnecting galleries rather than single chambers; the singular catacomb denotes one gallery or one such cemetery treated as a whole. The primary sense is inseparable from its Roman and early-Christian history: the catacombs were the burial places of the ancient Church, and the word therefore carries religious, archaeological, and historical freight that no simple synonym shares.

2. Historical-specific sense — the catacombs of Rome. The famous underground Christian (and Jewish) burial galleries outside Rome, along the Appian Way and other consular roads, hewn in soft volcanic tufa from roughly the 2nd to the 5th centuries CE. They were used for burial, for commemorative meals (refrigeria), for veneration of martyrs, and — in periods of persecution — for worship and the celebration of the eucharist. The name is generalized from one particular site, the burial district "catacumbae" near the Basilica of San Sebastiano (see the etymology), and by extension from it the word came to name the whole phenomenon: the catacombs of Naples, of Paris (the famous ossuary, an 18th-century re-employment of abandoned quarries), of North Africa, of the early Christian world generally.

3. Extended sense (by metaphor). Any vast, buried, honeycombed network or system: "the catacombs of the city" (underground tunnels, sewers, forgotten basements), "the catacombs of the bureaucracy" (buried levels of a huge organization), "the catacombs of memory" (buried strata of the mind). In these extended uses the word keeps its implication of depth, darkness, labyrinthine extent, and long burial — a network you can get lost in, that exists beneath a living surface.

4. Modern technical and literary senses. In archaeology and art history the word remains precise, naming the actual ancient underground cemeteries and their art. The adjective catacombic (of or like catacombs) and the rare verb to catacomb (to bury or store in such a place) are attested. In everyday modern speech the word is uncommon outside archaeology, tourism, and gothic fiction; its power is precisely that it is a specialized, slightly foreign-sounding name that makes an underground place feel engineered, sacred, and saturated with death — exactly the effect the source passage purchases with a single simile.

5. The synonym field, carefully distinguished.
  • Tomb: a single burial chamber or grave — a point, not a network; no labyrinthine extent.
  • Crypt: a vault beneath a specific church, usually holding the bodies of the saintly or the elite; architecturally specific and above all ecclesiastical.
  • Vault: a general arched chamber — not necessarily funerary at all (treasure-vaults, bank-vaults).
  • Sepulchre: a lofty, chiefly biblical term for a burial place, usually monumental and individual.
  • Necropolis: literally "city of the dead" — an extensive burial complex, often above ground, lacking the underground honeycombed concreteness of the catacomb.
  • Ossuary: a container, building, or room for the bones of the dead — a charnel deposit rather than a network of graves.
  • Mausoleum: a grand above-ground tomb-building for an individual or family.
  • Cemetery / burial ground: the general, modern, above-ground terms, completely stripped of catacomb's depth, antiquity, and underground engineering.
  • Charnel house: a building where corpses or bones are deposited — shares the mass-death charge but not the underground labyrinth.
  • Undercroft: a vaulted room below a church, more architectural and less funerary than a crypt.
Historical note on the form: older English consistently used the plural catacombs (attested from the 17th century); the singular catacomb is a later back-formation used for a single gallery or by ellipsis, and it is the singular that the source passage deploys, giving the bare empty place the singularity of one vast, emptied vault.

6. The semantic bridge, stated plainly. A geographical hollow became a place-name, the place-name became the type of the thing, and the type became the metaphor: "hollow in the earth" → "the burial hollows near Rome" → "any underground cemetery" → "any buried, labyrinthine network." The descent of the word is a descent into burial — which is precisely what the simile performs for the cave.
