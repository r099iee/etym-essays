- [Classical Roman Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE)] — In Classical Latin, arduus denoted physically steep cliffs, high mountain summits, and fortified ramparts, quickly metaphoricalized by Cicero, Virgil, and Horace into tasks that were "hard to reach, difficult, toilsome" — [perception: a noble, elevated descriptor for steep topography and challenging civic or martial virtue]
- [Renaissance & Elizabethan Era (16th c.)] — Borrowed directly into Early Modern English by humanist scholars and translators; initially used both for precipitous geographical slopes and demanding intellectual labors — [perception: an erudite, high-prestige Latinate borrowing reserved for serious literature and translation]
- [17th c. Miltonic & Epic Literature] — Canonized by John Milton in Paradise Lost to describe the sheer, terrifying ascent of the fallen angels and the laborious climb of virtue — [perception: elevated into the grand style of English epic poetry, symbolizing heroic spiritual endurance]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Romanticism] — Adopted by Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Shelley) to express the sublime, agonizing labor of monumental artistic creation and philosophical introspection — [perception: revered as the ultimate adjective for high creative callings and heroic striving against human limitations]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Ubiquitous in high-register political, diplomatic, and exploratory journalism ("an arduous path to reconciliation", "an arduous recovery"), as well as mountaineering — [perception: a sophisticated standard term signifying sustained perseverance through deep hardship]
