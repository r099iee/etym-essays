The history of gnawed begins in a reconstructed Indo-European base *ghen- (or *gneh₂-), signifying scratching and scraping — the kind of abrasive action that leaves grooves in a surface. From this base the Germanic peoples built a verb of the sixth strong class, Proto-Germanic *gnagan-, meaning to scrape or wear something away with the teeth. Every major Germanic language inherited it: Old Saxon gnagan, Old High German gnagan, Middle Dutch gnaghen and Old Norse gnaga. In Old English it appears as gnagan, an unusually tactile verb carrying the full force of attrition: to bite gradually, to fret, to eat into. The class VI strong paradigm gave it a robust past tense (gnōg) and a participle (gegnagen), marking it as a thoroughly native, thoroughly physical word of everyday life — a word a farmer might use of a dog at a carcass or a rat in the grain-store. 1. The deep root and the question of expressiveness The Indo-European connection is real but debated in kind. Pokorny groups the Germanic gnag- cluster under the base *ghen- / *gneh₂- "to gnaw, scratch," and the wider Indo-European record offers plausible distant relatives built on the same scrape-and-bite notion. But many specialists also treat gnaw as part of an expressive "gn-"/"kn-" scraping-word set — guttural-plus-nasal verbs of biting and scraping recur independently in language after language, because the gnawing sound and action of teeth on a hard surface invites the same coinage everywhere. Neither view is disprovable; the honest account notes both, and neither changes the securely attested Germanic chain that follows. 2. Old and Middle English: the strong verb weakens Middle English inherited the verb as gnawen, still strong but already wobbling towards the weak conjugation that eventually produced gnawed; spellings such as gnagen and knawen show dialectal variation in the initial cluster. During the Early Modern period the pronunciation of the /ɡn/ cluster began to collapse: speakers stopped voicing the /ɡ/ before /n/ at the start of a word, so that gnaw came to be said simply as /nɔː/, while the spelling, jealously preserving the Old English g, kept a letter the mouth no longer used. The old strong forms gnew and gnowe were abandoned, and by the seventeenth century the weak past gnawed had won outright — a slow, steady conquest that matches, in its own way, the patient, wearing semantic content of the word. German walked the opposite path, dropping the g from the spelling as well (nagen), while Dutch hardened it (knagen). 3. The figurative turn Meanwhile the word developed its modern figurative life. The corrosion metaphor was ready from the start — a thing that eats away is a thing that gnaws — and by the sixteenth century hunger, care, doubt, fear and regret could gnaw at a person, and gnawing became the standard adjective for a persistent, corrosive mental pain. The semantic bridge is exact: the patient, unrelenting, inward-eating quality of the physical act is transferred wholesale to the emotions, so that a gnawing anxiety feels precisely like a rat at the vitals. This figurative sense has become, in modern English, the commonest use of the word: few speakers today have ever watched a rat gnaw, but everyone understands a gnawing worry. 4. The senses in summary
  • Physical (Old English–present): persistent biting that scrapes and wears away matter.
  • Corrosive (medieval–present): rust, acid, water, time eating into a surface.
  • Figurative (16th century–present): hunger, doubt, fear, guilt, pain gnawing at a person from within; gnawing as the stock adjective of inner erosion.
  • Participle gnawed (the card's form): the finished result of such attrition — visibly tooth-scored and half-eaten.
5. Literary life In Tolkien's hands the plain participle gnawed returns the word to its oldest, most bodily register. The mutton by the trolls' fire has been gnawed — tooth-scored, worried, abandoned like carrion — and the single participle does the work of an entire scene of characterisation: it tells us, without a single comment, that the eaters are beasts. The word's full biography — from the reconstructed Indo-European scratching base, through the strong Germanic verb of farm and barn, through the collapse of the /ɡn/ cluster and the loss of the old strong forms, to the modern metaphor of inner erosion — is compressed into that one barbaric detail, and it is precisely the word's ancient physicality that makes the point so forcefully.
