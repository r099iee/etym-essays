PICKAXES (singular pickaxe) are heavy hand tools with a T-shaped head of steel, pointed at one or both ends and mounted on a long wooden or fibreglass handle, swung with force to break rock, hard earth, frozen ground, or masonry. The head is typically curved: a pointed spike on one side for penetrating and prying, and a flat cutting edge or a second point on the other. The pickaxe is the emblematic tool of the miner, the quarryman, the road-builder, and the demolition worker — any trade that must break what the ground will not give up easily. It concentrates the whole force of a swinging arm on a single point, which is why it can split rock that a flat hammer could only bruise. Literary context — The Hobbit. In Tolkien's inventory of goblin industry, the word appears in a grim catalogue: "Hammers, axes, swords, daggers, pickaxes, tongs, and also instruments of torture, they make very well, or get other people to make to their design, prisoners and slaves that have to work till they die for want of air and light." The nuance is bitterly ironic and industrial. Set between weapons and instruments of torture, the pickaxe is simultaneously a tool of mining and a symbol of forced labour: the goblins' metalwork, praised with cold mockery ("they make very well"), is tainted by the knowledge that much of it is produced by prisoners and slaves worked to death underground. The word sharpens the horror by naming the very implement with which the enslaved are likely to have cut the tunnels in which they perish. It is the most utilitarian item in the list, and precisely for that reason the most damning. Polysemy and related senses. The pickaxe family has several branches:
  • The tool (core sense): a heavy, swung, pointed breaking tool used in mining, quarrying, road-building, excavation, and demolition. "They swung their pickaxes against the rock face."
  • Historical and archaeological use: pick-like implements of stone and antler appear from the Stone Age as the principal tools for breaking ground; the T-shaped form of the tool has remained remarkably stable for thousands of years — an object that solved its problem early and never needed to be re-solved.
  • Pickaxe as a verb (back-formation): "to pickaxe" means to break up ground or rock with a pick; the verb appears in the 19th century, a natural extension of the re-analysed noun.
  • Mountaineering and climbing sense: the ice axe (often called a pickaxe in older and translated usage), tipped with a pointed pick for breaking ice, is a standard piece of alpine equipment; the "romantic" image of the climber's pickaxe coexists uneasily with the grim miner's tool.
  • Symbol of forced labour: across recorded history the pickaxe has been the signature instrument of penal labour — prisoners, slaves, and convicts working quarries and mines — and it appears in literature and iconography as shorthand for brutal, unrewarding toil.
  • Folk-etymological compound: the spelling pick-axe re-analyses the word as a compound of pick + axe. This is historically false (see etymologyS2) but morphologically productive: it gave the word a transparent internal structure and, eventually, the verb to pickaxe.
  • Regional and technical variants: a pick in modern usage is essentially the same implement in a lighter, one-handed form; a mattock has a broad adze-blade instead of a point; a grub hoe and pick mattock blend pick and adze for particular soils.
Semantic boundaries. A mattock is a similar tool but with a broad adze-blade instead of a point, used for loosening soil. A hoe is a long-handled blade dragged toward the worker for weeding and tilling, far lighter and wholly agricultural. A crowbar is a straight iron bar used as a lever for prising, not for swinging blows. A hammer delivers blunt percussive force to a surface rather than penetrating it. A pick is essentially the same implement in a lighter, one-handed form. The pickaxe alone is the miner's and stone-breaker's tool proper — swung, pointed, and penetrating.
