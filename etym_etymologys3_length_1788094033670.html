1. The Indo-European root. Length belongs to one of the best-attested word-families in Indo-European. Its ultimate source is the PIE adjective *dl̥n̥gʰ- “long” (with full grade *dlongʰ-), traditionally derived from a verbal root *del- “to lengthen, extend” (Pokorny). The family is enormous and includes Sanskrit dīrghá- “long” (as in the classical epithet dīrghāyuṣ, “long-lived”), Greek δολιχός (dolikhós) “long”, and Latin longus “long” — the last of these is the source of English words like longitude, elongate and prolong, and its descendants in the Romance languages (long in French, largo in Spanish) show the same family dominating the “length” vocabulary of Europe. The Germanic branch of the family lost the initial *d- (*dlongʰ- > *lang-), which is why English “long” and “length” look so different from Latin longus: the two branches of the same root diverged in their initial consonants thousands of years ago.

2. The Germanic formation. Proto-Germanic inherited the adjective as *langaz “long” and built the abstract noun with the suffix *-iþō, giving *langiþō “length”. This is the same suffix that produced English width, depth, strength, warmth, health (from “hale”) and truth — the -th of abstract quality. Note the vowel alternation: in “long” the vowel is back (o), in “length” it is front (e), a relic of i-umlaut, the Old English sound change by which a following *-i- in the suffix fronted the vowel of the root: *lang-iþō gave lengþu. The alternation long/length is therefore the same phenomenon as strong/strength and broad/breadth: the adjective keeps the back vowel, the -th noun shows the umlauted front vowel.

3. Old English. The Old English noun is lengþu (also spelled lengð), meaning “length, extent, duration”. It is attested across the corpus: in the prose of King Alfred's circle, in the homilies of Ælfric, and in the poetry, where it measures both the extent of the sea-voyage and the length of days and lives. Its cognates appear in all the older Germanic languages — Old Saxon lengitha, Old High German lengida, Old Norse lengd — which is why the family is securely reconstructed for Proto-Germanic.

4. Middle English and the idiom. Middle English regularised the form as lengthe (with the -th spelling), and the 14th century produced the idiom that matters for this card: “at length”, in Middle English atte lengthe, “to the full extent”. From “to the full length” the phrase developed two meanings: “in full, completely, in detail” and “after a long time, at last” — the second arising because a process that runs to its full length takes time. Both senses are attested from the 14th century and survive intact in modern English: “he spoke at length” (in detail) and “at length they reached the hill path” (eventually), the sentence of this card from The Hobbit. Tolkien, a professional medievalist, uses the idiom in its full traditional range.

5. The 16th–18th centuries: measurement culture. The early modern period multiplied the word's technical senses as measurement became systematic: “a length” as a unit in horse racing (“win by a length”, 17th century), cable-length in seamanship, cloth-lengths in the textile trade, and “at full length” for a stretched-out body or a full-figure portrait. The 18th century added “go to great lengths” (make great efforts — from the image of travelling far) and the American coinage “lengthy” (1759), which began as a mild criticism of long-winded discourse and has remained slightly informal ever since.

6. The 19th–20th centuries: science and media. In the 19th century “length” entered the technical vocabulary of phonetics (vowel length, distinguishing words in Latin, Japanese, Finnish and classical Arabic), geometry and engineering; in the 20th, film and broadcasting gave it “feature-length”, “full-length”, “short-length” and “running length”. The verb “lengthen” (16th century) and the adjective “lengthy” complete the derivational family.

7. Conclusion. Length is a word of extreme age and extreme ordinariness: its root is attested across Indo-European from the Vedas to the Romance languages, its English form was fixed by the umlaut of Old English, its most famous idiom is a 14th-century phrase that still governs a sentence in The Hobbit, and its technical senses multiplied with every new measuring technology. It is a demonstration that even the most basic vocabulary carries a full Indo-European and English history.
