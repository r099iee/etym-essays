Root 1: pamperen "to cram with food" (origin uncertain)
1. Origin uncertain; possibly a frequentative formation from an unattested Germanic base or borrowed from a continental West Germanic source; the ultimate PIE ancestry, if any, is unknown and must be stated as such. Earliest regional forms: West Germanic *pamp-, Middle Dutch and Flemish pamperen "to cram, stuff" (attested in regional usage).
2. Middle English pamperen "to cram with food, overfeed, stuff" (attested late 14th c., c. 1390).
3. Early Modern English pamper "to overfeed; to indulge with rich food; to gratify to excess" (16th c.).
4. English pamper "to treat with excessive care, attention, and comfort" — sense widened from the stomach to the whole person (17th-18th c.).
5. pampered — past participle and adjective "overindulged, spoiled, softened by luxury" (16th-17th c. onward).
6. PAMPERED — present-day form.

Debate note: The Oxford English Dictionary and other authorities describe the origin of pamper as obscure or uncertain. Some connect it to English and Dutch dialectal pamperen "to cram, stuff," possibly a frequentative of a base meaning "to eat greedily" (compare dialectal pamp, pumph, "to cram"); others propose an ultimately imitative (echoic) origin suggesting the sound of stuffing food; and a minority view links the family to Latin pappare "to eat" (a reduplicative nursery word, from PIE *pa- "to feed, nourish," source of Latin pascere "to feed" and English "food," "fodder"). None of these connections is securely established, so the chain starts from the earliest attested form and marks everything earlier as uncertain.
