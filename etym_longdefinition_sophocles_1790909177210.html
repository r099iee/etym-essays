Sophocles (c. 497/6 – 406/5 BCE; Ancient Greek: Σοφοκλῆς, Sophoklês) was one of the three great canonical Athenian tragic playwrights of classical antiquity (alongside Aeschylus and Euripides) whose dramaturgy, poetic eloquence, and profound examination of human suffering established the foundational benchmark of Western dramatic art.

Born into a prosperous aristocratic family in the rural Attic deme of Colonus Hippius near Athens, Sophocles was renowned throughout his long life not only as a peerless dramatic genius but also as a charismatic civic leader, elected general (strategos, serving alongside Pericles during the Samian War of 440 BCE), imperial treasurer of the Delian League, member of the board of commissioners (probouloi) during the Peloponnesian War, and consecrated priest of the healing deity Amynos.

Revolutionary Theatrical Innovations:

  • Introduction of the Third Actor (Tritagonistes): Before Sophocles, Greek tragic performances were limited to two speaking actors and the chorus. By introducing the third actor, Sophocles transformed Greek theater, enabling intricate three-way dialogue, subtle psychological irony, and complex interpersonal conflict on stage.
  • Expansion & Integration of the Chorus: He enlarged the tragic chorus from twelve to fifteen performers, weaving their choral odes (*stasima*) more intimately into the emotional and thematic development of the dramatic plot.
  • Pioneering Scene Painting (Skenographia): Aristotle credits Sophocles with introducing painted scenery on the stage backdrop (skene), significantly enhancing theatrical illusion and environmental realism.
  • Abolition of the Connected Trilogy: Unlike Aeschylus, who presented three continuous plays telling a single grand mythic saga across a festival day, Sophocles composed independent, self-contained tragedies, concentrating dramatic tension into unified single works.

Surviving Masterpieces & Tragic Philosophy:

Out of more than 120 plays composed during his prolific sixty-year career, only seven complete tragedies have survived to modern times, each recognized as a towering classic of world literature:

  • The Theban Plays: Oedipus Rex (or Oedipus Tyrannus, celebrated by Aristotle in the Poetics as the consummate masterpiece of tragic plot construction, peripeteia, and anagnorisis), Antigone (the classic confrontation between divine moral conscience and state decree), and Oedipus at Colonus (his serene final tragedy composed in his ninetieth year).
  • Other Canonical Tragedies: Ajax (depicting the tragic loss of warrior honor), Electra (a searing portrait of filial grief and retributive matricide), Philoctetes (exploring betrayal, physical suffering, and moral compromise), and The Trachiniae / Women of Trachis (recounting the agonizing death of Heracles).

Philosophical & Cultural Legacy:

  • Sophocles famously observed that while Euripides portrayed human beings as they are, he depicted them as they ought to be—heroic figures of towering dignity confronting inescapable cosmic destiny, moral tragedy, and existential blindness.
