ETYMOLOGY ESSAY: TESTAMENTARY 1. THE PIE ROOT *tre-: THREE AS THE BASIS OF WITNESS Testamentary carries one of the most surprising etymologies in the English legal vocabulary: it descends from the Proto-Indo-European numeral *tre-, "three." The root is attested across the family — Sanskrit trí, Greek treîs, Latin trēs, Proto-Germanic *þrīz (English three) — and its ordinary career is entirely numerical. But Roman law perceived a deep truth in the number: a dispute between two parties is a deadlock, resolvable only by a third, neutral person. Roman jurists therefore built the noun for "witness" on the root for "three" plus the root for "stand": testis was etymologically "the third one who stands by" (a "tri-stander"), the independent observer whose presence turns a private claim into a public, verifiable fact. The precise reconstruction — a compound *tri-st-i- "standing as third" — is the accepted scholarly view but rests on reconstruction; a minority of scholars have proposed alternative origins, so the inner stage is labelled as debated. 2. LATIN testis, testari, testamentum From testis "witness" Latin formed the verb testārī, "to bear witness, to testify," and from it the noun testāmentum, "a will" — literally "a witnessed declaration," the formal, witnessed publication of a Roman citizen's final wishes. Roman inheritance law made the testament the centrepiece of property transmission: a citizen declared his heir before witnesses, and that witnessed declaration, sealed by law, bound the living after his death. The same word family produced a remarkable legal and philosophical vocabulary: testificārī (English testify), attestārī (English attest), prōtestārī (English protest), and dētestārī "to curse, calling a deity to witness" (English detest, which originally meant "to execrate" rather than merely "to dislike"). All of English's "testimony" words — testify, attest, protest, detest, testimony, testament — are products of this single Roman insight about the third person standing by. 3. TESTAMENT AND THE BIBLE: THE DOUBLE CAREER OF THE NOUN Latin testāmentum gained a second, sacred career when early Christian translators used it for the Greek diathēkē, "covenant, will," in rendering the biblical record: the "Old Testament" and "New Testament" are, etymologically, the "Old and New Witnessed Covenants." This biblical sense travelled into Old French as testament and into Middle English in the 14th century, where the noun carried both the legal sense (a will) and the religious sense (a covenant / a division of the Bible). The modern legal formula "last will and testament" is a relic of the period when both words (will and testament) were needed to cover the full sense. 4. FROM testamentum TO TESTAMENTARY The adjective testamentarius, "pertaining to a will," is attested in Late Latin (imperial and post-imperial legal texts, 4th-6th centuries CE), formed regularly from testāmentum with the suffix -arius ("pertaining to"). It passed into Old French as testamentaire and entered Middle English as testamentary/testamentarie in the 14th-15th centuries, at the same time that English law was absorbing the vocabulary of probate. The adjective has remained, ever since, the technical descriptor of wills and their administration. 5. SOUND AND FORM The chain trēs → testis → testārī → testāmentum → testāmentārius shows a succession of regular Latin developments: the cluster of "three" with the root for "stand" was reshaped into test-; the suffix -mentum (forming nouns of result) and then -arius (forming adjectives of relation) built the two later stages. In English, the Latin/French -arius/-aire became -ary, the standard English adjectival ending (library, military, ordinary). Modern English testamentary /ˌtɛstəˈmɛntəri/ preserves every stage in its spelling: test- (witness) + -ament- (the will) + -ary (adjective). 6. SEMANTIC DRIFT AND MODERN CAREER - Roman Republic: testamentum = witnessed will; the jurists' word for the third person standing by. - Late Latin: testamentarius = the adjective of wills in the legal codes. - Middle English (14th-15th c.): testament gains both legal and biblical senses; testamentary enters as a law word. - Early modern law: "last will and testament" is fixed; testamentary capacity, intent, and trust are developed by the common law and equity. - 19th-21st centuries: the adjective remains a workhorse of probate law while acquiring a solemn literary use for any final, witnessed declaration — "a testamentary memorial," as in the source sentence. 7. LITERARY ATTESTATIONS Shakespeare's legal vocabulary and the language of the King James Bible both deploy the word family. The phrase "last will and testament" resounds through English fiction from Defoe and Dickens to modern novels. In the source sentence — the emperor moved by "Dai-shan's testamentary memorial that he wrote on his death bed" — the adjective works exactly as Roman law intended: it converts a dying man's written words into a witnessed, binding, immortal declaration, and the emperor's tears are the measure of its power.
