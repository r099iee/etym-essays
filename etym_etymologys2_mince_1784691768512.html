1. Proto-Indo-European *mei- “to lessen, make small; small, less” (reconstructed root, c. 5th–4th millennium BCE approximate; reconstructible from Greek meiōn “less” and Latin minor/minus) 2. Proto-Italic *meinos “lesser, smaller” → Latin minuō, minuere “to lessen, make smaller” (attested 1st c. BCE) → Latin minutus (past participle, “made small, tiny, fine”; attested) 3. Vulgar Latin *minūtiāre “to make small, to chop small” (reconstructed, not directly attested; built on minūtia “smallness”) 4. Old French mincier “to cut into small pieces, chop fine” (10th–12th c.) 5. Anglo-Norman mincer (12th–13th c., the French of Norman England) 6. Middle English mincen “to chop or cut into very small pieces” (late 14th c., first recorded c. 1380–1400; already carrying the secondary sense “to speak or walk with affected daintiness”) 7. Modern English MINCE (verb “to cut very small”; British noun “minced meat,” 19th c.; figurative “to mince one’s words,” 17th c.) Note: menu, minutiae, and meiosis share the same *mei- root but descend by separate branches and are not links in the direct chain to MINCE.
