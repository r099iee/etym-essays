Part I — The “holy” element: from wholeness to sanctity.

The first half of halibut carries a deeply religious history. It goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *kailo-, which meant “whole, uninjured, sound, of good omen” — the idea of being complete and unbroken, and therefore blessed. That sense of wholeness produced the great family of Germanic “hale” and “holy” words: the root *hailaz gave English whole, hale, and health, while the derived *hailagaz — literally “made whole,” “blessed” — produced Old English hālig, the ancestor of holy. The step from “sound, complete” to “sacred” is a semantic shift of the first importance: in the pagan world what was whole and unimpaired was what was blessed by the gods, and when the Germanic peoples were christianised the same word was ready-made to translate the Christian idea of the sacred. Old English hālig became Middle English holy, with an old variant hali, haly preserved in exactly the kind of compound that was coined before the vowel settled — and halibut is one of those survivors, a fossil of an older pronunciation of holy frozen inside a fish’s name.

Part II — The “butte” element: the flatfish.

The second element is the everyday Germanic word for a flatfish, the thick, blunt, flattened fish that lies on the sea bed with both eyes on one side. Its origin is less secure than the first half’s: the word sits in the Germanic “blunt, thick, stubby” family — Old English butt, the “thick end,” Dutch bot, “flatfish,” Old Norse butr, “a log, a stump” — but no Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed with confidence, and some scholars suspect a pre-Germanic substrate word for the fish. What is certain is the shape: the halibut is the largest and thickest of flatfishes, so blunt and massive that the name “flatfish” barely does it justice. The fish itself was once staggeringly abundant in the cold northern seas, and it was the everyday food of fishing communities along the Atlantic coasts of Britain, Scandinavia, and North America.

The fusion: the holy flatfish.

Around 1400–1450 the two halves fused: halybutte, “holy flatfish.” The name was no accident of piety. The medieval Church forbade the eating of meat on fast days and holy days, and fish was the permitted substitute; the great flatfish was so plentiful and so sustaining that it became a staple of the holy-day table, and the English name celebrates that licence in two blunt syllables — the fish you were allowed to eat when meat was forbidden. The word thus encodes an entire chapter of religious and dietary history: the rhythm of the Christian calendar, the authority of the Church over what entered the mouth, and the practical ingenuity of coastal peoples who found in a giant fish the answer to fasting.

From common food to premium catch.

For four centuries halibut remained what its name promised: plain, abundant, everyday food. In the 16th and 17th centuries it was the cheap staple of Atlantic ports, salted or dried to feed crews and communities, with no gourmet pretension at all. The industrial fishing of the 20th century changed everything: overfishing collapsed the great Atlantic stocks, the fish became scarce, and the word’s register slid from “common food” to “premium seafood.” Modern halibut is a restaurant fish — pan-seared, grilled, or poached — and the quayside staple of the 1600s has become a delicacy. The plural halibuts has meanwhile settled into perfectly regular English: where salmon and trout keep the same form in singular and plural, halibut freely takes -s, so that a speaker can count the great flatfishes one by one.

Halibut in the Narnian oath.

When C. S. Lewis has Trufflehunter cry “Horns and halibuts!” he is doing something very old-fashioned and very deliberate. The phrase is a mincing oath — a substitute for profanity in which mundane or comic objects take the place of sacred names — and in pairing a stag’s horns with a pile of holy flatfish Lewis lets the old word’s religious and rustic history play at once: the fish that was holy enough to swear by in the medieval kitchen is holy enough to swear by in the Narnian wood. The plural is the joke’s engine: not one fish but a heap of them, great flat giants stacked like comic tribute, testifying to a world where even the sea’s largest fish can be summoned as witnesses. The etymology of halibuts — holy, whole, blessed, flat, thick, abundant — is the quiet source of all that warmth and humour.
