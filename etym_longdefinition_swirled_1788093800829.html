Swirled is the past tense and past participle of the verb swirl, denoting rapid, spiralling, eddying circular motion, or the act of causing such motion. It applies above all to fluids — water, wine, cream, smoke, mist, snow, dust, wind — but centuries of figurative use have carried it deep into the language of thought, feeling, memory and social life.

1. Core physical senses.

  • Intransitive ("the floodwater swirled through the streets"): to move round and round in a twisting, spiralling course, typically fast, unsteady, and driven by an external current or vortex rather than by the object's own effort.
  • Transitive ("she swirled the whisky in her glass"): to cause a liquid or loose material to rotate in spirals or eddies, agitating it into visible circular patterns.
  • Of natural forces: whirlwinds, tornadoes, whirlpools, eddies, firestorms and blizzards swirl; the participle suggests ceaseless, churning, slightly menacing rotation.

2. Extended and figurative senses.

  • Mind and emotion: thoughts, memories, fears and questions "swirl" in the head; the bridge is the felt experience of being caught in a vortex of mental activity one cannot control or escape.
  • Social information: rumours, gossip and speculation "swirl" around a person, scandal or event; the current of talk circulates endlessly, picking up and dropping details like debris.
  • Visual pattern: as a participle adjective, "swirled" describes matter marked with spiral, marbled bands — swirled icing, swirled glass, swirled marble, the swirled foam of a latte; the action is over and the trace of the motion remains.
  • Bodies in motion: dancers, skaters, falling leaves and hawks riding thermals swirl — graceful, whirling, unstable motion.

3. Grammar. Regular verb: swirl, swirled, swirled. The participle builds perfect tenses ("the river had swirled over its banks") and, very frequently, participial adjectives ("swirled smoke", "swirled colours"). The form is also used absolutely in literary narrative to open scenes of weather or chaos.

4. Register and nuance. Neutral in register but kinetic in feel: swirled differs from turned or rotated (smooth, axis-fixed rotation), from spun (even, rapid revolution), from circled (a defined round path) and from eddied (a smaller, water-specific vortex). It implies turbulence, fluidity and being carried along by the medium itself. In fiction it is a favourite verb for storms, rivers, smoke, crowds and inner turmoil, often with an undertone of overwhelming force — a dragon's wake, a sinking ship's water, a burning town's smoke all swirl in adventure prose.

5. One-sentence summary. To be swirled is to be moved — or to move something — in a rapid, spiralling, eddying circular current: literally of liquids, gases and loose matter, figuratively of thoughts, emotions and circulating talk, and, as an adjective, of any pattern that preserves the spiral traces of such motion.
