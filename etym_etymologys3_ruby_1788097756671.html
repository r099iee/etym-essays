From the Indo-European red root. Ruby is, at root, simply the Latin word for red. The Proto-Indo-European colour adjective *h₁rewdʰ- "red" — the root of English red itself, of Greek erythrós, of Latin ruber — yielded the Latin adjective ruber "red", from which Latin built the derived adjective rubeus "red, reddish". The gem word is Medieval Latin rubinus, in full rubinus lapis, "the red stone", used in the Latin of the high Middle Ages for the red corundum gem that ancient writers had called carbunculus, the "little coal". From rubinus, Old French produced rubi, which Middle English borrowed in the thirteenth or fourteenth century as rubi and rubie, settling as modern ruby; the Latin -inus suffix was dropped rather than retained, exactly as in the English treatment of other French gem and colour names.

The gem's ancient shadow and medieval glory. The ancients prized a red stone they called carbunculus, and Pliny the Elder describes red gems that glow "like a burning coal"; but the ruby's modern mineral identity is a product of the early modern and modern periods, because until the nineteenth century ruby, spinel and red garnet were not reliably distinguished. This is why old treasure inventories are full of "balas rubies" (pink-red spinels) and "Cape rubies" (garnets), and why the great Black Prince's Ruby of the British Crown Jewels is in fact a spinel. The sixteenth- and seventeenth-century trade in rubies from Burma, Ceylon and Siam reached Europe through the Portuguese and Dutch East India routes, and European lapidaries began to describe pigeon-blood colour, silk sheen and asterism with wonder; the ruby entered the iconography of crown jewellery everywhere, and church treasuries set ruby crosses and ruby-encrusted reliquaries as symbols of Christ's blood and of martyrdom.

Science: chemistry, synthesis, laser. The nineteenth century identified the gem's chemistry. Corundum is crystalline aluminium oxide, and the red colour was shown to come from chromium substitution, the same element that makes emerald green and red glass red; the ruby's place in the corundum family beside sapphire was fixed, and the "same mineral, different chromophore" rule entered the textbooks. In 1902 Auguste Verneuil's flame-fusion furnace made synthetic rubies cheap and perfect, and the ruby left the jeweller's case for industry: watch and chronometer bearings, instrument pivots, and in 1960 the world's first working laser, Theodore Maiman's ruby laser at Hughes Research Laboratories, which pumped a synthetic ruby rod with a flash lamp to emit the first coherent red light ever produced by a man-made device. The stone that had symbolised untouchable treasure became a workhorse of precision optics, a journey the word itself now records every time an engineer says "ruby laser".

Colour and culture. The colour sense is old and continuous: the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611) sets wisdom above rubies — "For wisdom is better than rubies", Proverbs 8:11 — and "ruby" has named deep reds ever since: ruby glass in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, ruby lips in the poets, ruby wine, and from the eighteenth through the twentieth centuries the fortified wine style of ruby port. Popular culture multiplied the gem's aura in the twentieth century: the ruby slippers of The Wizard of Oz (1939) made a pair of sparkling shoes the emblem of magical homecoming; the ruby wedding anniversary (40th) and ruby jubilees (40 years of a reign) made the stone a clock for durable affection; and Ruby became a common given name, peaking in English-speaking countries in the late nineteenth and again in the early twenty-first centuries.

Modern word-proper nouns. In 1995 the programmer Yukihiro Matsumoto released the Ruby programming language; as he has explained, it was named for the birthstone of a colleague, and chosen as a successor to the language Perl deliberately carrying the gem pun, which the RubyGems package manager then made permanent. In Japanese typography, "ruby characters" (ルビ), the tiny kana glosses printed beside kanji for young readers, take their name from an English typefounders' name for 5.5-point type, "ruby", itself borrowed from the gem by the same logic that named type sizes "pearl" and "diamond". The word has thus completed a remarkable circle: from PIE redness, through the medieval red stone, to laser physics, software and typesetting — always carrying the gem's double value of colour and worth, exactly as the ruby on the sword-hilt in Wilde's The Happy Prince glows with both beauty and cost.

  • Key stages: PIE *h₁rewdʰ- → Latin ruber "red" → rubeus "reddish" → Medieval Latin rubinus (lapis) "red stone, ruby" → Old French rubi → Middle English rubi/rubie → Modern English ruby.
  • Debated points: none in the chain itself; the ancient equation of ruby with carbunculus is only approximate, and "balas ruby" and "Cape ruby" are historical trade names later replaced by mineral classification.
  • Attestation anchors: Latin ruber (Plautus, 3rd-2nd c. BCE), Medieval Latin rubinus (12th-13th centuries), Old French rubi (12th century), Middle English rubie (14th century), Authorized Version of the Bible (1611), and Maiman's ruby laser (1960).
