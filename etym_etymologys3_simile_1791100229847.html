The word simile is a direct Latin borrowing whose root reaches to the deepest Indo-European vocabulary for sameness. PIE *sem- meant one, same, or together, yielding Greek homos (same, with rough breathing from s), Sanskrit sama (same, level), Germanic same, and Latin similis (like), simul (together), simplex (single-fold), and single via later French. The Italic form *similis added the adjectival suffix -ilis to the base simi-, producing like or resembling. Classical Latin used similis ubiquitously, from grammar to law, and its neuter substantive simile meant a like thing, a parallel, or a comparison. Cicero and Quintilian use simile and similitudo when classifying rhetorical figures, and the Vulgate uses simile to introduce parables: audite aliud simile, hear another parable.

  • PIE *sem-: unity and identity, one of the best-attested roots, surviving in English same, some, assemble.
  • Proto-Italic *similis: regular adjectival formation, like-ness as shared quality.
  • Classical Latin similis/simile: adjective and neuter noun, rhetoric and scripture, comparison and parable together.
  • Late Latin transmission: preserved in monastic grammar books and biblical commentary without phonetic rupture.

English borrowed the rhetorical sense twice. Middle English simile appears in the fourteenth century in Wycliffite and Chaucerian contexts for biblical parables, still pronounced with Latin weight. The Renaissance revival of Quintilian fixed the modern narrow sense: an overt comparison with like or as as opposed to metaphor. Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie (1589), Peacham, and later Blair and Campbell entrench simile versus metaphor as the elementary distinction, illustrated by Homer, Virgil, and the Psalms. Eighteenth-century elocutionists prized extended similes as tests of taste; Romantic poets both mocked and renewed them; Victorian novelists like Dickens could play on both the schoolroom and scriptural senses at once, letting a comic character venerate a simile as ancestral wisdom.

  • Medieval borrowing: ecclesiastical simile as parable, tied to preaching and exegesis.
  • Renaissance rhetoric: humanist textbooks Latinise English criticism, simile stabilised against metaphor.
  • Augustan codification: dictionaries from Blount to Johnson define simile as like-comparison, examples canonical.
  • Victorian popularisation: school grammars drill simile versus metaphor; Dickens parodies solemn reliance on saws.
  • Modern linguistics: simile analysed as hedged categorisation, mental mapping, and politeness, pronounced /sɪmɪli/ in RP with short vowels, distinct from smile.

Phonetically the borrowing is conservative: Latin simile [sɪmɪlɛ] to English /sɪmɪli/ with anglicised final vowel, stress on the first syllable, no Great Vowel Shift diphthongisation because the vowels are short. Orthography retains the Latin -e. No serious etymological debate attaches; the *sem- pedigree is textbook. The only nuance is semantic: modern handbooks restrict simile to like/as figures, while older and biblical use allowed any parallel or parable, the sense Dickens revives for comic reverence. That older breadth explains why translators still render Christ's parables as similes in footnotes, and why the wisdom of ancestors can live in a simile: a likeness remembered is reasoning remembered.
