Squaws (plural noun) is an archaic, historically prevalent colonial English term borrowed in the seventeenth century from Southern New England Eastern Algonquian languages, originally designating Indigenous North American women or wives. Over four centuries of colonial frontier violence, racial subjugation, and literary stereotyping, the term underwent profound semantic degradation, evolving from an everyday ethnographic loanword into an intensely offensive racial and misogynistic slur.

Historical Context & Sociological Application:

  • Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Context in Historical Literature: In Will Durant’s historical text, squaws is deployed in its mid-twentieth-century anthropological sense to describe women of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy. Durant highlights the profound political power exercised by Haudenosaunee women—such as their equal right to debate and vote in tribal councils and their authority to select and depose chiefs. There is a poignant historical irony in using a derogatory colonial loanword to characterize women who exercised constitutional authority far exceeding that of any European women of the same era.
  • The Degeneration into Pejorative Caricature: While early seventeenth-century New England colonists used the singular squaw and plural squaws as neutral translations of "woman" or "wife," eighteenth- and nineteenth-century frontier expansion stripped the word of dignity. White settlers utilized squaws to contrast native women with white women: white women were portrayed as delicate, civilized ladies, whereas indigenous women were caricatured as drudges, beasts of burden, or hyper-sexualized commodities.
  • Modern Status as an Offensive Slur: In contemporary English, squaw and squaws are universally recognized across indigenous communities, academic institutions, and federal bodies as deeply offensive, dehumanizing slurs. The term reduces Indigenous women to an ethnic and sexual trope, actively contributing to the historical erasure of their societal authority and reflecting ongoing systemic vulnerabilities captured in the Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women (MMIW) movement.

Polysemy, Colonial Jargon & Historical Toponymy:

  • Colonial Frontier Idioms: In nineteenth-century American frontier jargon, the term formed derogatory compound idioms:
    • Squaw man: A pejorative term used by white frontiersmen to stigmatize a white man who married an indigenous woman and lived among her people, implying a loss of racial status and masculinity.
    • Squaw winter: A North American meteorological folk idiom denoting a brief, mild cold snap occurring in early autumn, prior to Indian Summer.
  • Toponymic Proliferation and Federal Eradication: Settler colonialism embedded the term into over 650 official geographic features across the United States (e.g., *Squaw Valley*, *Squaw Peak*). In November 2021, the U.S. Secretary of the Interior, Deb Haaland (the first Native American cabinet secretary), issued Secretarial Order 3404, formally declaring *squaw* a derogatory term and mandating a federal reconciliation task force to rename all geographic sites bearing the moniker across public lands.
  • Botanical and Pharmacological Folk Names: Early American herbalists and botanical manuals adopted the word into common names for medicinal plants utilized by Native American women during childbirth or menstruation, including *squawroot* (Conopholis americana, cancer-root) and *squaw vine* (Mitchella repens, partridgeberry). Modern botany increasingly avoids these common names in favor of non-derogatory regional or scientific designations.
