In the Wodehouse sentence I may even have hummed a bar or two, a bar is a short section of music — a single measure or metrical unit, the amount of music between two vertical bar-lines on a written staff — and by extension any brief tuneful scrap that can be hummed in a moment. To hum a bar or two is to voice a couple of such phrases idly, suggesting light-heartedness, tipsiness or contentment leaking into song. In British usage bar and measure are synonyms for this unit (a bar of 4/4 contains four crotchet beats; a twelve-bar blues is a twelve-measure chord pattern), while American theory prefers measure for the unit and reserves bar for the dividing line itself. The context makes the musical sense certain: one hums tunes, not metal rods or tavern counters.

The musical bar is only one of a famously polysemous family, all radiating from the idea of a rigid rod or barrier. Understanding the musical sense requires seeing its neighbours, because Wodehouse constantly puns across them. A bar began as a straight piece of wood or metal, then any barrier made of such pieces, then specialised social barriers: the rail in a courtroom, the counter in a pub, the sandbank blocking a harbour, the stripe of colour, the prohibition (to bar someone), and the vertical stroke that bars off music into measures. The musical bar is therefore the barrier-line turned timekeeper: it fences beats into countable pens so players stay together.

  • Musical measure: the primary sense here — a bar of music, a two-bar phrase, a four-bar intro, to hum a bar or two. It implies a complete rhythmic thought, often with melody and harmony, countable for rehearsal (let's take it from bar 32). Its bridge from the rod sense is visual: the printed bar-line that segments the staff.
  • Rigid rod or stripe: a bar of iron, chocolate, soap, gold bullion; prison bars; a bar magnet — any straight rigid length or band. This is the etymological core from which barrier senses grow.
  • Barrier, obstruction and prohibition: a bar to progress, to bar the door, barred from the club, called to the Bar (the legal profession behind the courtroom rail). Here bar is something that blocks, excludes or qualifies.
  • Counter and establishment serving drink: the bar of a pub, to prop up the bar, barman, barfly — from the barrier-counter separating staff from customers, now metonymy for the whole trade of serving alcohol and for the rail of brass or wood itself.
  • Technical and sporting units: a bar of pressure (meteorological unit, ~atmospheric pressure), the crossbar of a goal, the horizontal bar in gymnastics, sandbar and sandbank in geography — each a rod-like or barrier-like formation in its domain.

Register of the musical bar is neutral and everyday among musicians, mildly technical elsewhere; to hum a bar is idiomatically modest and comic, minimising one's musical outburst as mere involuntary tunefulness. Wodehouse chooses it precisely for that modesty: the narrator, perhaps mellow after luncheon, admits to no more than a bar or two, as though fuller singing would be unseemly. The comedy depends on the reader instantly discarding the pub-counter and legal senses and hearing only the little fenced measure of tune.
