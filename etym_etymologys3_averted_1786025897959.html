AVERTED is the inflected past-tense and past-participle form of English avert, and its biography is therefore the biography of the verb. Avert is a Renaissance borrowing from Latin avertere, "to turn away", a compound of the prefix ab- ("away, off") and the celebrated verb vertere ("to turn"). Two distinct Indo-European roots converge in the Latin word, and the history of each illuminates how the modern form came to be.

1. The Indo-European roots. The prefix ab- descends from the Proto-Indo-European adverb *h2epo, "away, off, from", the same element that produced Greek apo "from, away" (source of English apostasy, apology, apogee) and Sanskrit apa "away". The verbal core vertere descends from Proto-Indo-European *wert-, "to turn, to wind", one of the most productive roots in the whole family. Its descendants are everywhere in the older European languages: Sanskrit vartate "turns", Latin vertere "to turn", and the Germanic set that yielded English -ward ("toward", "westward"), German werden "to become" (literally "to turn into"), and Old Norse verða. The Latin branch alone has given English a whole family of borrowings built on the participial stem vers-: version, convert, introvert, extrovert, advert, divert, reverse, anniversary ("the turning year"), adversary ("one turned against"), controversy ("a turning against"), and universe ("turned into one"). The root carries a core image of rotation, winding, and change of direction that the modern word still preserves.

2. Formation in Latin. Latin joined ab- to vertere to form avertere, whose past participle aversus "turned away" was already in common use by the first century BCE. In Caesar and Cicero the verb has the concrete senses "to turn (something) away, to divert" - a ship, an army, an attack - and a more abstract sense, "to turn (someone) from a purpose or opinion". Latin also derived the adjective aversus and the adverb aversa, so that the classical language already moved comfortably between the physical image (a face turned aside) and the figurative one (an evil turned aside).

3. Entry into English and the French trap. English borrowed avert in the late fifteenth century directly from the Latin verb, alongside the flood of scholarly Latinisms that enriched English in the Renaissance. A trap lurks here: French also had a superficially similar verb, avertir, but it meant "to warn, to inform" and derived from Latin advertere, "to turn toward" (the source of English advertise and advert). The two verbs are cousins, not the same word: one turns away, the other turns toward. English was careful to keep them apart, and avert entered the language cleanly with the meaning of the Latin original.

  • 15th-16th centuries: avert appears chiefly in the physical sense "to turn away the eyes or face", and in translations of classical and biblical texts it turns aside blows, plagues, and punishments.
  • 17th century: the preventive sense is firmly established - "to avert danger, to avert ruin" - and the word becomes part of serious political and moral vocabulary.
  • 18th-19th centuries: the physical and preventive senses coexist in literary prose; "averted eyes" becomes a staple of the novel of manners and the Gothic tale.
  • 20th century onward: the preventive sense comes to dominate. "War was averted", "the crisis was averted", "the disaster was narrowly averted" - journalism, diplomacy, and public health all press the word into service, and in epidemiology "averted deaths" and "averted cases" are now technical quantities.

4. Semantic drift summarized. The trajectory is a textbook case of metaphorical abstraction moving from concrete space to abstract time. First the word turns a physical object (the gaze, the face) away in space; then it turns an invisible object (thoughts, attention) away; finally it turns a future event (war, disaster) away in time - deflecting it before it arrives. The grammatical form AVERTED participates in all of these: as a past tense it reports the physical act ("she averted her eyes"); as a participle it reports the completed prevention ("the war was averted"). The word never lost its root sense of deliberate, timely redirection, which is exactly what the card's sentence exploits: war is not merely prevented but turned aside, like a face turned from something unbearable.
