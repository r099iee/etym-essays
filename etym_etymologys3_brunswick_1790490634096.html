Brunswick is the anglicised form of Low German Brunswik, modern High German Braunschweig, first recorded as Brunswik in 1031 in the consecration charter of St Magni. The name is a transparent Germanic compound: a personal name Brun- plus -wik, a trading settlement. The first element is almost universally taken as the short masculine name Bruno, borne by Bruno, son of Liudolf, brother of the Saxon duke, the Brunonen founder credited with establishing the place. Bruno itself continues Old High German brun, brown, shining, dark, dusky — the same adjective that gives modern brown, braun — used as a byname for a brown-haired or dark-armoured man and then as a full given name. The second element -wik continues Proto-Germanic *wika-, a settlement, trading place, specifically borrowed very early from Latin vicus, a quarter, village, row of houses. English wick in Berwick, Ipswich, Norwich and -wich are its cognates. Thus Brunswick literally means Bruno's wick, Bruno's trading hamlet on the Oker ford.

Phonologically the path is regular. Latin vicus /wiːkus/ was borrowed into Germanic before the High German consonant shift as *wika- with initial /w/ and medial /k/, attested in Old Saxon as wik, dwelling place, and in Old English as wic, camp, town. In the compound Brunes-wik, genitive of Bruno plus wik, intervocalic weakening and Low German retention of /w/ and /k/ gave Brunswik. High German second-sound-shift and diphthongisation later produced Braunschweig: brun- lowered and diphthongised to braun-, -swik with south-German sch- and vocalised -weig. English, borrowing through Low German, Dutch, and French diplomatic usage in the 16th–17th centuries, kept the older consonants and anglicised the vowels: Brunswick /ˈbrʌnzwɪk/, spelled with -ns- and -wick on the analogy of English wicks. The spelling with n before s preserves the genitive, while the final -wick shows English folk-assimilation to a familiar place-name ending.

  • Medieval lordship (11th–13th c.): from Brunonen foundation to Henry the Lion (1129–1195), who made Brunswick his residence, built Dankwarderode, cast the bronze lion (1166) as a declaration of Welf power, and endowed St Blasius. Chroniclers write Brunswic, Brunswik. The name already denotes a princely capital, mint, and market, not a mere hamlet.
  • Welf partition and duchy (13th–18th c.): after Henry's fall, Otto the Child creates Brunswick-Lüneburg (1235). Later partitions yield Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel and Hanover. English diplomats write Brunswick for the whole Welf complex. The 1714 succession makes the Duke of Brunswick-Lüneburg also King of Great Britain; House of Brunswick becomes a constitutional term in Acts of Parliament, sermons, and newspapers.
  • Military fame (1809–1815): the Black Duke Frederick William's Black Brunswickers in Spain and at Quatre Bras fix Brunswick in British popular memory as gallant German loyalty. War Office lists, medals, and elegies spell it Brunswick, never Braunschweig, cementing the English form.
  • London commemoration (1790s–1820s): the Foundling Hospital estate names its new Bloomsbury development Brunswick Square after Caroline of Brunswick (1768–1821), Princess of Wales. Rate-books, directories, and Ruskin's own birth record (8 February 1819 at 54 Hunter Street, Brunswick Square) entrench the square as a respectable address. Here evolution is socio-cultural, not phonetic: a German city-name becomes London bricks by royal marriage.
  • Global diffusion (18th–19th c.): colonial governors name New Brunswick (1784, for George III of the House of Brunswick), Brunswick, Georgia (1771), Brunswick, Maine, Ohio, and Australian suburbs. Emigrant gazetteers carry the anglicised spelling worldwide, while the German city itself officially remains Braunschweig.

Scholarly nuance must be stated. The Bruno etymology, though standard in Niemeyer, Förstemann, and the Braunschweig city archive, is not documentary in the strict sense: no charter says Bruno founded this wik; the inference rests on onomastics (a Bruno active in the region c. 1000), on the genitive -s-, and on the absence of any plausible common noun brun- that fits a river-ford market (brown water is possible but never cited in early forms). A minority suggestion linking brun- to Brunn, a well or spring, is phonologically possible but finds no early Brunn- spelling and is rejected by most place-name scholars. The responsible formulation is therefore: first element almost certainly the personal name Bruno, from the brown-adjective, with the usual caveat that early medieval place-naming rarely preserves an explicit founder deed. The -wik from Latin vicus is, by contrast, secure and paralleled in dozens of North Sea names.
