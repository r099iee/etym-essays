- Old English (ca. 8th–11th c.) - Attested as blǣstbylig (literally 'blast-bag' or 'blowing-belly', from Proto-Germanic *balgiz 'leather bag'); conceived as an airtight animal skin or pouch used to blow air into hearth fires and smelting furnaces.
- Middle English (ca. 12th–15th c.) - Evolved phonetically into blaest-bely and shortened to the plural/dual form belwes or balwes; the plural ending reflected the standard workshop design consisting of two paired leather chambers operated alternately to produce a continuous air stream.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - Spelling coalesced into 'bellows' (treated as a singular noun 'a bellows' or collective 'a pair of bellows'); flourished in Elizabethan drama (Shakespeare, Marlowe) as a vivid metaphor for stoking human passions ('bellows of lust', 'bellows of wrath') and as an everyday anatomical descriptor for the lungs and voice box.
- 18th to 19th c. - Indispensable powerhouse of the Industrial Revolution, driving gigantic steam-powered blast furnaces for ironworks and smelting; adapted into early photographic technology for the accordion-pleated 'camera bellows' that permitted precise focal adjustment between lens and plate.
- 20th to 21st c. - Replaced in heavy metallurgy by electric centrifugal blowers, but preserved in traditional artisan blacksmithing, wood-burning hearth care, pipe organ conservation, and macro-camera lenses; remains an enduring literary simile for heavy, exhausted breathing ('puffing like a bellows').
