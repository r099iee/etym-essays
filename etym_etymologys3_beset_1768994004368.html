The biography of beset begins at the oldest recognizable posture in the Indo-European lexicon: the root *sed-, "to sit." In Proto-Indo-European this was a simple, almost somatic verb — the sound of a body lowering itself to the ground — and every major daughter family kept it: Sanskrit sīdati, Greek hézomai, Latin sedeō, Old Irish saidid, Lithuanian sėdėti, Proto-Germanic *sitjaną. Because the root was so basic, it generated enormous metaphorical wealth wherever it landed. The Greeks made of it a "seat" of divinity (hédos) and of governance (sunédrion, the sitting-together that is a council); the Indians seated the pupil beside the teacher in the Upanishad, literally a "sitting down near"; the Romans built from it a whole vocabulary of institutions — the president "sits before" (praesidēre), the assessor "sits by," the session is a "sitting," and sediment is what "settles" out of a liquid. From the same root also come the plain bodily words: English sit, set, seat, settle, and saddle are all seated here.

The Germanic branch took the same root and gave it a causative twist. To make someone sit is to set them; hence Proto-Germanic *satjaną and Old English settan, "to set, to place." Here the decisive event occurs: Old English attached the prefix be-, meaning "around, about," and produced besettan, literally "to set about." At first the meaning was spatial and neutral — to place things around or upon something (and so, in the ornamental sense that survives in "a crown beset with jewels," to stud a surface with ornaments). But Old English and its continental relatives quickly hardened this into a military term: to beset a town was to surround it, to sit down before its walls and block every exit. The Old Saxon bisettian, Old High German bisezzan, and Dutch bezetten all carried this occupying, garrisoning force, and German besetzen still does to this day — the occupation sense that English has since let fade.

The entry of besettan into Middle English as bisetten marks the pivot of the whole biography. Through the 12th to 15th centuries the verb meant, above all, "to surround and besiege," and it appears throughout Middle English literature in genuinely military contexts — an army besetting a castle, a band besetting a traveller. It is in this period that the word also begins its migration inward: beside the literal siege, the verbs bisetten and besetten are used of diseases, griefs, and sins that "beset" a person — troubles that gather around one like a besieging force. The King James Bible and the English devotional tradition fixed this transfer permanently: "the sins which do so easily beset us" (Hebrews 12:1) gave English the famous collocation "besetting sin," the fault that surrounds and harries a person, and the usage is still alive today. The Reformation and post-Reformation centuries saw the military sense recede into the passive and the psychological and adversative senses rise: by the 18th century, to be beset was above all to be surrounded and pressed by difficulties, dangers, doubts, and enemies, with the "by" phrase naming the pressing agent.

Bullet points on the trajectory:
  • PIE *sed- "to sit" — the root's somatic core, c. 4500–2500 BCE.
  • Proto-Germanic *satjaną "to cause to sit, to set" — the causative turning.
  • Old English settan "to set, place" (8th c.) — English "set," "seat," "settle."
  • Old English besettan "to set about, surround, occupy, adorn" — the prefix be- "around" makes the word.
  • Middle English bisetten "to surround, besiege" (12th–15th c.) — the military sense dominant; the transfer to sins and griefs begins.
  • 16th–17th c. — the King James Bible fixes the moral-psychological sense: "the sin which doth so easily beset us"; "besetting sin" becomes a fixed collocation.
  • 18th–21st c. — the passive dominates: beset by difficulties, doubts, scandals, problems; the military sense survives only figuratively.
Sound and form notes: the verb shows the fate of a very old formation under the pressure of ordinary use. In Middle English it was a weak verb — bisette in the past — but the modern language has levelled the paradigm to the single form beset for present, past, and participle, a simplification shared by the "set" family to which it belongs. The prefix be-, once a fully productive Germanic preposition "around, about," survives in English as the fossilized prefix of a large family (before, behind, beside, below, become, beget, bemoan, besmirch), and beset is one of its most transparent members: to be set about on every side.

The passage's "beset by certain odd impressions" thus stands at the end of a three-thousand-year path from a body sitting down, to an army sitting down before a fortress, to a mind sitting ringed about by impressions that cannot be located or answered. The military past of the word is not dead in it: it is what gives the modern, purely psychological use its faint, ghostly strategy — as if the adventurers' own perceptions had thrown a cordon around them, exactly as the besiegers of an Old English town once threw a cordon around its walls.
