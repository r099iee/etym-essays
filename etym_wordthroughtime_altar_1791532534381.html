- Roman antiquity - ara and altare name the stone block of public and domestic sacrifice; the altar is civic, military and pagan, and its height marks the presence of the god.
- 10th-11th c. - Old English altar is borrowed with Christianity; it names the consecrated table of the Mass and carries the prestige of Latin learning.
- 12th-15th c. - Middle English auter and altar compete; the altar is the visual and emotional centre of the parish church, and "lead to the altar" becomes a metaphor for marriage.
- 16th c. - The Reformation turns the altar into a battleground: Protestants prefer "communion table", Catholics defend the stone altar, and the word acquires a confessional edge.
- 17th c. - Metaphysical poets make the altar a figure of the heart and of poetic sacrifice; George Herbert's "The Altar" is the classic example.
- 18th-19th c. - Gothic revival and missionary expansion multiply altars; the word becomes architectural, antiquarian and imperial, and the Oxford Movement restores altar ritual.
- 20th c. - The Second Vatican Council reorients the altar so the priest faces the people; altar girls are permitted, and the word becomes more ecumenical.
- 21st c. - "Altar" survives in multi-faith shrines, wedding language, and secular metaphors of sacrifice; it can sound solemn, archaic or ironically grand.
