Every pretence is a stretching. Its root is PIE *ten-, to stretch or extend — the same elastic ancestor that gives English thin, tender, tendon, tent, and extend. Latin built on it the verb tendere, to stretch, aim, or tend toward, and then the compound that matters: praetendere, literally to stretch something forth in front — to hold up, allege, or spread as a screen. Roman advocates pretended causes in court; Roman generals pretended — held forth — a show of force as cover.

Medieval lawyers turned the verb's feminine participle into a noun: praetensa, a thing alleged or put forward, a claim laid before the court. Old French inherited it as pretensse, claim or allegation, and Middle English borrowed it in the fourteenth century wearing legal robes — a pretence was first something one pretends in the old sense of asserts or claims, whether true or not.

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries darkened the meaning. In an age of plots, spies, and religious tests, claims put forward were so often false that the word slid from neutral allegation to habitual deceit: pretences of loyalty masking treason, pretences of sickness dodging musters, pretences of orthodoxy shielding heresy. Shakespeare's characters pretend — profess and allege — friendship while sharpening daggers, and the noun absorbed the whole theatre of conspiracy.

The novel completed the word's education. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century fiction made pretence the very air of social life: Fielding's hypocrites, Austen's polite deceivers, Dickens's Pecksniffs and Veneerings — entire plots turn on keeping up or dropping pretences. Meanwhile the law kept its own technical sense alive: obtaining money by false pretences became a canonical fraud offence, so that the same noun serves the charge-sheet and the drawing room.

  • Sound shifts: Latin prae- softened to French pre-; the participle ending -tensa passed through Old French -tensse into English -tence, with British spelling keeping noun -ce against verb pretend while American usage levels it to pretense.
  • Semantic drift: stretching-forth to legal allegation to excuse to sustained false show — the screen held before the court became the screen held before the world.
  • Socio-cultural context: courtroom pleading, religious persecution, polite society, and criminal fraud each claimed the word, leaving it poised between the statute book and the novel.
  • Literary attestations: chronicles of the Gunpowder age, Restoration comedy's pretenders, Austen's social camouflage, and our own conspirator keeping up the pretence for inquisitive folk — four centuries of Englishmen holding screens before secrets.

From a stretched canvas to a conspirator's smile, pretence has always named the same craft: holding something forth so that nothing behind it shows.
