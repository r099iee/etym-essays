- Old English — the hill itself: dūn names hills and hill-forts in the Saxon settlement of the chalk country.
- Medieval — sheep take the slopes: monastic and manorial flocks fold on the downs, and downland wool fills the Exchequer's sacks.
- Tudor–Stuart — the racing downs: Epsom, Newmarket's chalk neighbour, and Banstead bring horses to the turf, and the downs become sporting ground.
- 18th–19th c. — the poetic downs: painters and poets fix them as the emblem of open England, and the Southdown breed is perfected.
- 20th c. — Kipling's country: the Sussex downs become literary holy ground, then rifle ranges and tank grounds test them in two wars.
- Today — protected and prized: a National Park, rare chalk-grassland habitat, and still — as the source sentence promises — good sheep food.
