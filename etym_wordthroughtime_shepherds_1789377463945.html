- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Formed as the compound *scēaphierde* / *scēaphyrde*, strictly denoting an agricultural guardian of sheep against wolves, thieves, and exposure.
- Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.) - Phonologically compressed through trisyllabic shortening to *schepherde* / *schephurde*; deeply enriched by Christian devotional mystery plays (the Adoration of the Shepherds) and pastoral theology.
- 16th Century (Tudor Botanical Herbology) - The folk compound *Shepherd's-purse* (*Capsella bursa-pastoris*) is formally documented in English botanical treatises, comparing its triangular seed silicles to a shepherd's traditional leather purse.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romanticism & Political Metaphor) - Idealized in Romantic poetry (William Blake, William Wordsworth) as the archetype of peaceful rural innocence; transitive verb *to shepherd* emerges (c. 1820), meaning to guide or escort protective care.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Applied in astrophysics (*shepherd moons* confining Saturn's rings, Voyager missions 1979–1980) and sports tactics (Australian Rules football *shepherding*).
