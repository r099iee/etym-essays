lettrisme with a lowercase l denotes the general poetics of letter-worship, and with a capital L denotes the specific historical movement Lettrisme founded in Paris in 1942-1947 by Romanian-born poet Isidore Isou, joined by Gabriel Pomerand, Maurice Lemaître and later Guy Debord before his Situationist break. Its central doctrine held that poetry had exhausted semantic, anecdotal and prosodic resources from Baudelaire to Surrealism and must return to its atomic particles, letters as graphic shapes and phonetic noises, to build a new, so-called ciselant or chiselling beauty based on form rather than sense. In practice this meant hypergraphic poems made of invented alphabets, visual scores, koans of breaths, coughs and onomatopoeia, manifestoes, films that scratched the celluloid, and paintings where calligraphy replaced figuration.

The source context captures its philosophical ambition precisely: after World War II French lettrisme tried to give Dada-Surrealist letter-play a philosophic tincture, to build an expressive philosophy on the shapes rather than the meanings of letters. Isou proclaimed two phases of all art, an amplique expansive phase that perfects content and a ciselante self-destructive phase that pulverises form into particles, and claimed poetry had entered its second phase. Letters were thus celebrated as mystical, infantile, primitive matter uncorrupted by bourgeois signification, capable of a new community of creators who would chant, carve and scatter signs.

Beyond the sect, lettrisme names any1900s tendency to treat the alphabet as image and object, from Cubist collages incorporating stencilled letters to concrete poetry, to Pop art use of letters as graphic design motifs. Art historians therefore speak of a broader lettrist sensibility linking early Greek vase inscriptions, medieval illuminated initials, Baroque emblem books, Rimbaud's vowel colours, Mallarmé's typography, Dada sound poems by Hugo Ball, and digital ASCII art. In this wider sense lettrisme is not only Isou's group but a recurring counter-tradition to linear reading, insisting on seeing-at letters rather than reading-through them.

  • historical movement sense: Isou's Paris group 1942-1970s with manifestos, reviews, films and schisms, including Lettrisme, Lettrisme International and Situationism; similar because it is the proper-noun referent; differs because it is dated and doctrinal, not a timeless technique.
  • aesthetic doctrine sense: the theory that letters as sound-shape precede words and meanings and should ground a new poetry; similar because it states the philosophy; differs because it is portable to other arts and eras.
  • practice or work sense: a lettrist poem, film or painting made of dissociated letters, neographies and noises; similar because it names artefacts; differs because it is concrete rather than programmatic.
  • generalised art-historical sense: any foregrounding of script as image, from vase painting to Jasper Johns; similar because it shares graphic emphasis; differs because it lacks Isou's metaphysics and militancy.
  • pejorative sense: in French criticism, empty formalism accused of fetishising letters while saying nothing; similar because it describes the same objects; differs because it evaluates them as sterile wordplay.

Today lettrisme survives in histories of the avant-garde, in sound poetry festivals, in graphic design, and as ancestor to concrete poetry, Fluxus and language-centred digital poetics. To understand the term is to understand post-war Parisian hunger for a zero-degree language after propaganda and catastrophe, a hunger that believed salvation lay not in new messages but in new matter.
