1. The Indo-European root. The English participle sown closes a long chain that begins with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *seh₁- (traditionally written *sē-), "to sow, cast, throw". The root's core meaning was the physical throwing or casting of small objects, and from it the daughter languages built their verbs of sowing: Latin serere "to sow" (past participle satus, whence semen "seed" and, through Latin, English semen and seminary "a seedbed of learning"), Lithuanian sėti, Old Church Slavonic sěti, and the whole Germanic family. The root also produced a nominal formation in Germanic, *sē-ti- "seed", giving Old English sǣd and modern seed. Because the root is attested across the Italic, Baltic, Slavic and Germanic branches, its position in the PIE lexicon is secure.

2. Proto-Germanic and the Germanic verb. Germanic built a strong verb *sēaną "to sow": Old Norse sá, Old Saxon sāian, Old High German sāen, Gothic saian, and Old English sāwan. All reflect the same class VII strong-verb pattern, whose characteristic mark is reduplication in Gothic (saian, saisō). The Germanic agrarian economy — barley, rye, wheat and flax cereals — made sowing one of the most fundamental verbs of everyday life.

3. Old English: sāwan and sāwen. The Old English verb sāwan is a class VII strong verb: infinitive sāwan, past singular sēow, past plural sēowon, past participle sāwen. Old English texts use it almost exclusively of casting seed into tilled ground — the daily labour of the ceorl or peasant — and the participle sāwen appears in glosses and religious prose translating Latin seminatus. Already in Old English homilies, however, the figurative sense "to disseminate" is visible: Ælfric and the anonymous homilists speak of sowing the word of God in hearts, using the same verb.

4. Middle English: sowen. By Middle English the infinitive is sowen and the participle appears as sowen, sawen, souwen; the old class VII morphology was decaying, and the participle, when it survives, is the ancestor of modern "sown". Chaucer uses sowen both literally (of fields and gardens) and morally — in the Parson's Tale he warns that sins are sown by the devil and reaped in judgement. Middle English thereby married the literal and the moral senses that would define the modern word.

5. The sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: the rise of "sowed". From the 1500s English began converting archaic strong verbs to weak morphology, and "sowed" appears as a past participle from the sixteenth century, becoming common by the eighteenth. "Sown" never died but retreated into formal and literary register — a textbook case of doublet survival. Simultaneously the King James Bible (1611) fixed the figurative idiom: "They that sow in tears shall reap in joy" (Psalm 126:5), "whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap" (Galatians 6:7), and the vivid proverb "for they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind" (Hosea 8:7). These verses engraved "sow/sown" on English moral imagination for centuries.

6. The figurative flowering in literature. Elizabethan and Stuart writers exploited the metaphor: Shakespeare uses sow/sown for planting, begetting and moral causation; Milton's Paradise Lost describes the earth "sown" with its first fruits after the Fall. The Parable of the Sower (Matthew 13) remained the master-text, so that to "sow" always faintly echoes the sower scattering seed on good and stony ground alike.

7. The modern profile. Over the past three centuries the participle has been pulled in two directions. In everyday speech and American English, "sowed" dominates the perfect and passive constructions; "sown" survives as the marked, formal, literary member of the pair. In journalism and politics the figurative formula "seeds of doubt were sown" is ubiquitous, and metaphors of planting, seeding and sowing remain among English's most productive ways of describing causation, education and propaganda. In horticulture, "direct-sown" is standard technical usage. Finally, the word retains its ancient phonetic shape /səʊn/, the direct descendant of Old English sāwen, and in high literary register — as in Tolkien's "Over snow by winter sown" — it keeps the gravitas of two and a half millennia of agriculture, scripture and poetry.
