- 13th century literal taste - enters English from French meaning to taste or have flavour; the verb savour first works on the tongue, naming the tasting and relishing of food.
- 14th-16th century figurative flavour - "to savour of" extends from taste to character: a thing savours of something when it is tinged with, smacks of, or betrays a quality — in this period often a good or neutral quality.
- 17th-19th century moral detection - the construction "savours of" becomes the standard way to say that a quality is perceptible in a situation, usually a discreditable one (as here: prying "savours of impertinence"); the metaphor is of a taste detected on the tongue.
- Contemporary use - the verb survives mainly as "to savour" meaning to enjoy or appreciate fully (savour the moment), while the "savours of" sense of perceiving a taint is now literary and old-fashioned.
