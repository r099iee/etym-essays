PIGEONHOLING is a compound of two English words, pigeon and hole, each with its own independent chain. The compound is a pure English formation of the early modern period. CHAIN FOR PIGEON (onomatopoeic, non-PIE): pīpiō, pīpiōnis (Late Latin, "a young chirping bird," from the onomatopoeic verb pīpīre "to chirp, peep," imitative of a bird's cry; attested 2nd–4th c. CE) → Old French pijon / pigeon ("young dove," borrowed from the Latin accusative pīpiōnem, attested 11th–12th c.) → Middle English pijoun / pigeon ("a young dove," borrowed from Anglo-French and Old French, attested 14th c.) → English pigeon (the bird; also, from the 16th c., figurative "a dupe, a gullible person") → used in the compound pigeonhole. CHAIN FOR HOLE: *kel- / *kelh₁- (Proto-Indo-European, "to cover, conceal, hide"; also the source of Latin cēlāre "to hide" and English hell, from the sense "the concealed place") → Proto-Germanic *hulą ("a hollow, a covered or concealed cavity," zero-grade of the root with -n/nom. -l- suffix; reconstructed) → Old English hol ("a hollow, a hole, a cave," attested 8th–9th c.) → Middle English hole ("a cavity, an opening," attested 12th–13th c.) → English hole ("an opening or hollow place"). COMPOUND AND VERBAL STAGES: English pigeonhole (noun, "a small recess or compartment in a desk or cabinet," first attested 1680s; the compartments were likened to pigeon nests in a dovecote) → to pigeonhole (verb, "to file papers in pigeonholes," attested 1840s; figurative "to assign to a fixed restrictive category" from the later 19th c.) → English PIGEONHOLING (gerund, "the act of assigning to rigid categories," in use from the late 19th c.). Note on debated points: the onomatopoeic origin of Latin pīpiō is generally accepted but not directly attested as a stage; the identification of Old English hol with PIE *kel- "to cover, conceal" is standard (via the sense "concealed cavity") though the exact ablaut grade is debated. The compound stage (1680s, then 1840s verb) is well documented in English dictionaries.
