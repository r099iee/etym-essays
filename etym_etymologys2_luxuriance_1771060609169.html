Derivation of luxuriance PIE *leug- "to bend, to twist, to turn aside, to break" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *loukso- "twisted, bent aside, dislocated" (regular development of *leug- in Italic, c. 1500–800 BCE) → Latin luxus "dislocated, thrown out of joint; extravagant, excessive, luxurious" (attested 1st c. BCE; the medical sense 'dislocated' in Celsus, the moral sense 'excess, extravagance' developed in classical Latin, e.g. Cicero and Livy) → Latin luxuria "rankness, excessive growth, luxury, wantonness, excess" (derived noun on -ia, attested 1st c. BCE; also spelled luxurias) → Latin luxuriāre "to grow rank, to be luxuriant, to be wanton, to run riot" (denominal verb, attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. Lucretius and Virgil use luxuriant of vines and corn growing wildly) → Latin luxuriāns, luxuriantem "growing rank, running riot, luxuriant" (present participle, attested 1st c. BCE–CE) → Middle French luxuriance "luxuriance, rankness" (16th c., from the Latin participle stem; the French noun and adjective luxuriant establish the modern form) → English luxuriance "rankness or abundance of growth, luxuriousness" (borrowed 17th c., first recorded c. 1600s; also the adjective luxuriant, 16th–17th c.) → luxuriance (Modern English) Debated / nuanced points. The PIE root *leug- 'to bend, twist' is securely attested by Latin "luxus" (dislocated), Greek "lugrós" (mournful, crooked), Sanskrit "rujáti" (he breaks), and the Germanic "lock" (of hair, literally a twist), but the precise sense-development from 'bent aside' to 'extravagant excess' is reconstructed by semantic inference rather than by direct attestation: a thing "out of joint" was metaphorically "out of bounds, beyond measure," hence 'excess.' A minority tradition instead separates Latin "luxus" (dislocation) from "luxus" (luxury) as two homonymous roots; most modern authorities (de Vaan, Ernout–Meillet) now treat them as a single root with one semantic trajectory. The moral sense 'wantonness, lust' prominent in Romance ("lujuria," "luxure") belongs to the same Latin "luxuria" and was present in English as "luxurie" before the word was narrowed to botanical and aesthetic abundance in the 17th–18th centuries.
