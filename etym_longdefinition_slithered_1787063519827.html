Core meaning. Slithered is the past tense and past participle of the verb to slither. To slither is to move smoothly and quietly with a long, gliding, often twisting motion — the motion characteristic of a snake, a worm, or a slow slippery slide — or, of people and things, to slide unsteadily and inelegantly over a wet or slippery surface, often losing balance. The word always implies motion along a surface (not through the air), a degree of friction or slipperiness, and usually a sinuous, writhing, or uncontrolled quality.

Primary senses.

  • Animal locomotion: a snake, eel, worm, or lizard slithers — it advances by serpentine gliding, pressing its body against the ground with a continuous wavelike motion. This is the sense most English speakers now think of first.
  • Unsteady human motion: a person slithered down a slope, across mud, or on ice — slid awkwardly and out of control, with the feet slipping, as in the source sentence (“their feet slithered on stones rubbed smooth and slimed”).
  • Quiet, stealthy motion: by extension, to slither can mean to move quietly and secretively, like a snake, into or out of a place (“she slithered out of the room”).

Secondary and extended senses.

  • Figurative for fear or cold: a feeling can “slither down the spine” — a creeping, physical sensation of dread or cold that moves like a snake.
  • Political / moral metaphor: in modern comment, a person can “slither” out of an obligation or “slither into” a position — moving evasively, snake-like, with moral slipperiness.
  • Objects and liquid: loose cables, mud, ice sheets, or a flood of small things can slither; the word captures slow, slidey, semi-fluid motion.
  • Literary nature writing: “slithered” is occasionally used of roots, fungal threads, ice, or streams creeping along, in nature prose rather than scientific registers.

Collocations. Slither pairs with subjects of reptiles (“the snake slithered”), with surfaces (“slithered across the ice”, “down the muddy bank”), and with physical sensations (“a chill slithered down his spine”). In news prose it appears for evacuations in mud and debris (“survivors slithered across the wreckage”). The participle slithered is frequent in fantasy, horror, and nature writing.

Slither vs sliver. The near-homophone sliver (a thin splinter or slice) is unrelated in origin, though modern speakers sometimes pun on the pair; a “slither of cake” is a playful British spelling of “a thin slice” that borrows the snake word’s sound.

Grammar and register. Slither is a regular verb and is used intransitively, usually with a prepositional phrase (“slithered across the floor”) or adverb (“slithered away”). The noun is also slither (“a slither of ice”). The word is more vivid than slide: it adds texture, sinuosity, and often unease or comedy. It is standard English, neither slang nor dialect, though it was long a word of everyday physical description before the snaky sense came to dominate.

Register and tone. Slither is more vivid and specific than slide: sliding is smooth and often deliberate; slithering is slippery, sinuous, or uncontrolled, and usually conveys unease, repulsion (for snakes), or physical comedy (feet sliding on mud). The verb is frequent in nature writing, children’s literature, horror, and fantasy; in the source passage, feet slithering on dragon-polished stones combines both slippery sliding and the snake-like atmosphere of the dragon’s hall.
