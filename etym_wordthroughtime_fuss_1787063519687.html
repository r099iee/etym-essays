- 1701: first recorded, of obscure (likely imitative) origin; it means a fluster, a bustle, needless busy commotion. - 18th c.: "to make a fuss" = to create pointless excitement; "fussy" = hard to please, over-particular; the word spreads across dialects and social classes. - 19th c.: "to make a fuss of/over someone" — doting, pampering attention; the Victorian nursery gives us the fussy baby and fussing parents. - 20th c.: "no fuss" (straightforward, without ceremony), "fuss-free", "fussbudget" (one who frets over trivia); still mildly dismissive but affectionate. - Modern: both "complaint" ("make a fuss" = protest) and "pampering" ("make a fuss of" = lavish attention); the core note of unnecessary excitement persists.
