Deprecate in current standard English means to express earnest disapproval, to deplore or protest against as undesirable, and in a specialized technical sense to mark software, syntax or standards as obsolete, discouraged and slated for removal while still temporarily available. The truncated source context uses it in the older literary sense: to plead against, to seek to avert by prayer or argument, to depreciate as motives.

The general disapproval sense dominates educated prose: we deprecate violence, deprecate personal attacks, deprecate the use of clichés. It is stronger than dislike and more formal than disapprove. A deprecating remark expresses regret and censure. To deprecate competition and play as motives for writing would be to argue they are unworthy, to warn them away, to treat them as low motives beneath purpose. The fragment suggests the author resists doing this.

The computing sense, now ubiquitous, is narrower: developers deprecate an API endpoint, a tag like center, a function like gets, meaning do not use in new code, it will disappear. Deprecation warnings announce the transition. This borrows the pray-against force: the manual pleads with programmers to avert future breakage.

  • Earnest disapproval: to deplore, to frown upon, to express regret and censure, e.g. the committee deprecated personal abuse.
  • Archaic prayer against: to pray to avert evil, to intercede to ward off, e.g. I deprecate the wrath to come; obsolete except in liturgy.
  • Protest and plead against: to urge reasons against a course, to remonstrate, e.g. he deprecated hasty action.
  • Self-deprecation: deprecating manner, smile or tone that belittles the self to avoid boastfulness; often confused with depreciating.
  • Computing obsolescence marking: to declare a feature deprecated, discouraged but still functional, pending removal.
  • Confusion with depreciate: many writers use deprecate for belittle or lower in value, properly depreciate; dictionaries now note the overlap as common but proscribed.

Polysemy bridges run through Latin precari, to pray. To de-precari was to pray against, to try to avert by entreaty. English first kept the pray-against sense, then generalized to protest earnestly against, then to disapprove generally, then by metaphor to warn programmers away from a feature. The self-deprecating branch comes from praying against pride in oneself: to deprecate oneself is to talk oneself down as if warding off envy.

Usage cautions are famous. Strict style guides insist deprecate means disapprove while depreciate means lower in price or belittle. To say the currency deprecated is wrong; to say his deprecating remarks lowered morale blurs the line. Self-deprecating humor is correct for self-belittling humor, though self-depreciating appears. In the prompt, deprecate these behaviors as motives means to disparage and dismiss them as explanations, a formal literary use that would today often be phrased as discount, dismiss or devalue, showing how close the two verbs have grown.

Extended note: grammar guides devote entries to the deprecate versus depreciate pair because spellcheckers miss it. Garner Language-Change Index rates deprecate for depreciate at Stage 3, widespread but condemned. Self-deprecating versus self-depreciating shows similar drift, with the former correct for modest humor. In software lifecycles, deprecated, obsolescent, removed form a pipeline: documented discouragement, runtime warnings, eventual deletion. To deprecate competition and play, in the source fragment, thus means to pipeline them out of literary theory, a move the author will oppose by rehabilitating play as central motive.
