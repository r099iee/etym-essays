OREB — Milton's spelling of Horeb — is a Semitic toponym of transparent meaning and no Indo-European ancestry. It descends from the Biblical Hebrew proper name Ḥōrēb, formed on the well-attested Semitic verbal root ḥ-r-b, "to be dry, waste, desolate." The name is simply the substantive "dry place, waste, desert." The same root gives Hebrew ḥārab, "to be dried up, desolated," and the noun ḥorbā, "waste, ruin, desolation," and, in the neighboring Arabic of the Semitic family, ḫariba, "to be ruined, deserted," and ḫarāb, "ruin, waste" — the very word heard in countless Arabic toponyms and in the loanword sense of "kharab" as "broken-down, ruined." The name Ḥōrēb is thus a bald piece of desert description: the mountain of God, the "mountain of the dry waste," named by desert peoples for what it was — a barren, waterless, solitary peak — and then invested by revelation with what it became. In the Bible Ḥōrēb appears throughout the wilderness narrative as the mount of theophany. It is at Horeb that Moses, keeping the flock of his father-in-law Jethro "behind the desert," sees the bush that burns without being consumed and hears the voice that names itself "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3). It is at Horeb that Moses strikes the rock to give water to the murmuring people (Exodus 17:6). And to Horeb Elijah, fleeing Jezebel, travels forty days to the mount of God, where God passes by not in wind, earthquake, or fire but in a still small voice (1 Kings 19) — the desert mount of the quietest, most inward revelation in the Old Testament. The giving of the Law in Exodus 19–20, meanwhile, is located at Sinai; Deuteronomy, however, repeatedly places the covenant and the lawgiving at Horeb (Deuteronomy 1:6; 4:10, 15; 5:2). This duality is exactly what Milton's line "On Oreb, or of Sinai" so economically registers, following a long exegetical tradition that treated the two names as alternatives for one holy mountain. Modern historical criticism explains the double naming through the sources of the Pentateuch: the Elohist source prefers "Horeb," the Yahwist and Priestly sources prefer "Sinai," and the later editors preserved both traditions side by side. The names may name the same mountain, two different peaks of the same massif, or two adjacent mountains; the traditional identification places the events at Jebel Musa in the southern Sinai Peninsula, but the location is not archaeologically certain, and some scholars prefer sites in northwest Arabia. What is not in doubt is that in the finished Bible the two names coexist, and that Christian tradition from the fourth century (the pilgrim Egeria, the monks of St. Catherine's) fixed the holy mountain in the southern peninsula. The English forms of the name preserve the split in the Vulgate. Jerome rendered the Hebrew as Horeb, and that is the form English Bibles standardized — Tyndale, Geneva, and the King James Version all give "Horeb." The Vulgate also transmitted a variant without the initial aspiration, Oreb, and it was this form, Latinate and metrically convenient, that Milton adopted in Paradise Lost (1667): "That on the secret top Of Oreb, or of Sinai, didst inspire / That Shepherd." The line deliberately pairs the desert mount with Sinai, the lawgiving mount, and sets both against the pagan "Aonian Mount" of the Muses: the true source of Milton's epic song is the Spirit that inspired Moses the lawgiver on the secret, cloud-veiled peak of the wilderness. The word has since divided in register. Horeb is the scholarly and standard form, used in translation, commentary, and criticism to name the Elohist's sacred mount; Oreb survives as a fossil of Miltonic diction, essentially confined to Paradise Lost and to readers and poets who echo it. Yet even in Milton the original topographical meaning works beneath the surface: the dry waste mountain, the barren peak of the wilderness, is precisely the place where God chooses to be heard — the still small voice on the mount of desolation, the fire in the bush of the waterless waste. The etymology of the name is thus not an antiquarian footnote but the key to the narrative itself: revelation descends on the dry place, and the desert is transformed into the holiest ground in the Old Testament. Sound and orthographic notes:
  • The Hebrew ḥeth (ח) is a voiceless pharyngeal fricative; Greek rendered it ch- (Χωρήβ Chōrēb), and English normally renders it h- (Horeb), while the variant Oreb drops it entirely, reflecting a Latin spelling without the initial aspiration.
  • Milton scans Oreb as two syllables, matching the rhythm of the line and giving the name a weight the one-syllable form might lack; the modern pronunciation is /ˈɒɹɛb/.
  • The penultimate Hebrew vowel ē (tsere) became the Latin/English e, and the final ḇ (softened b) became the English b.
In sum, Oreb is the poetic spelling of a name that means "dry waste," the desert mountain that became the mount of the burning bush, the rock-water, the still small voice, and the twin of Sinai — and that, in Milton's verse, still holds the secret top of biblical inspiration.
