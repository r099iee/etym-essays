Stage 1 — PIE *per- "forward, through, beyond" (a root of great productivity in Indo-European; its extended grade *pro- "before, forward" gives Greek pró, Sanskrit prá, Latin pro "before", and English for, from, forth). Stage 2 — Latin prope "near, close to" (adverb and adjective; comparative propius "nearer", superlative proximus "nearest"). DEBATED: the formation of prope from *pro- is standard but not entirely certain, since its comparative and superlative forms draw on a different stem; some scholars treat prope as an isolated Italic form. Stage 3 — (Vulgar / Late) Latin *propiāre "to bring near, to approach" (a denominative verb built on prope; reconstructed, not classically attested, hence the asterisk). Stage 4 — (Vulgar) Latin *repropīāre "to bring back near, to draw near again, to recall to the face" — re- "back, again" + *propiāre "to bring near" (reconstructed. DEBATED: the intermediate form is not directly attested; the reconstruction is standard in the OED and Etymonline but rests on inference from the Old French outcome). Stage 5 — Old French reprochier "to blame, accuse, upbraid, cast back in one's teeth" (11th–12th centuries), with the noun reproche "reproach, shame". Stage 6 — Middle English reproche / reprochen "reproach, shame; to reproach, upbraid" (13th century, via Anglo-Norman). Stage 7 — English reproach "blame, disgrace" (noun) and "to charge with fault" (verb); reproachful "full of reproach, expressive of blame" (16th century, formed with the suffix -ful). Stage 8 — English reproachfully "in a manner expressing reproach" (adverb formed with the suffix -ly) → FINAL NODE: REPROACHFULLY.
