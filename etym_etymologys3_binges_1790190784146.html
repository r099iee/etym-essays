The semantic and etymological evolution of binges is one of the most colorful examples in the English lexicon of a humble rural woodworking craft term transforming into university slang, expanding into high-society literary fiction, entering clinical psychiatric nomenclature, and ultimately defining digital twenty-first-century entertainment habits.

1. Rural Dialect Origins: The Cooper's Soak:

  • The Northamptonshire Dialect Attestation: Unlike words inherited through classical Greek, Latin, or Norman French literary traditions, binge emerged directly from English provincial vernacular. In the 1854 Glossary of Northamptonshire Words and Phrases compiled by Anne Elizabeth Baker, the verb to binge is defined as: "To soak a wooden vessel in water, that the wood may swell and prevent its leaking. 'A churn or a tub is said to be binged.'"
  • Dialectal Etymon: Dialectologists connect this usage to a regional Germanic expressive stem (*bing- / *beng-) related to swelling, saturation, and water-soaking. The cooper's practice of soaking dry barrels before filling them with ale or beer was a standard rural task across the English Midlands and Lincolnshire.

2. University Argot and Naval Slang: Soaking the Vessel:

  • The Oxford Metaphor: Around the 1850s, undergraduate students at the University of Oxford and officers in the Royal Navy adopted the dialect word as humorous academic slang. By a vivid anatomical analogy, a carousing scholar preparing for a heavy night of drinking was compared to an empty wooden tub being "binged" or soaked in liquid until thoroughly saturated.
  • Noun and Verb Shift: Within a decade, binge transitioned from a transitive verb of soaking to both an intransitive verb meaning "to drink heavily" and a countable noun meaning "a heavy drinking bout or drunken spree." An 1854 student publication recorded: "A man who gets drunk is said to have had a good binge."

3. Interwar High Society and Comedic Literature:

  • Expansion Beyond Alcohol: By the late Victorian and Edwardian eras, the term had broadened from purely drunken carousing into a general descriptor for celebratory parties, joyful sprees, shopping trips, and lively social outings.
  • The Wodehousian Social Binge: The word achieved iconic literary status through the comedic masterpieces of P. G. Wodehouse in the 1920s and 1930s. In Wodehouse’s world, members of the Drones Club regularly attend binges at casinos, restaurants, and country manors. In this high-society comedic register, a "binge" was an innocent, exuberantly festive occasion characterized by white mess-jackets, dancing, practical jokes, and high spirits.

4. Medicalization and Psychiatric Taxonomy in the Late 20th Century:

  • Binge Drinking: In the 1960s and 1970s, public health researchers formalized the colloquial phrase binge drinking to establish epidemiological thresholds for acute, hazardous alcohol consumption over short intervals.
  • Binge Eating Disorder: In clinical psychiatry, the term was co-opted into formal nosology (eventually codified in the DSM-IV and DSM-5 as Binge Eating Disorder). Here, binges lost their celebratory connotations and came to denote severe, distressing episodes of compulsive, dysregulated eating accompanied by psychological distress and loss of executive control.

5. The 21st-Century Digital Revolution: Binge-Watching:

  • Media Streaming Metaphor: Around 2012–2013, with the rise of on-demand digital platforms (such as Netflix releasing entire television seasons simultaneously), the term underwent an unprecedented cultural expansion in the form of binge-watching. Consuming multiple television episodes consecutively became an international cultural phenomenon.
  • Semantic Reshaping: This modern digital usage shifted the societal perception of binges back toward acceptable recreation, illustrating how a word that began with soaking wooden beer barrels in Victorian England became the defining verb of global streaming entertainment.
