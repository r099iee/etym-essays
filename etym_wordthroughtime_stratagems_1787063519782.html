- c. 1490: English borrows "stratagem" from French "stratagème" and Latin "strategema" — strictly a military term: a trick or device of war for deceiving the enemy.
- 16th–17th centuries: stays within military and political writing — "stratagems of war," used in translations of Livy, Plutarch, and the Greek tacticians; the plural "stratagems" collects lists of such tricks, as in Frontinus's Stratagems.
- 18th century: begins to broaden beyond the battlefield — any clever scheme, plot, or device in business, courtship, and politics, though still formal and slightly grave.
- 19th century: becomes a staple of adventure fiction and melodrama — "his stratagems were foiled"; the word gathers a theatrical, slightly archaic flavour.
- 20th–21st centuries: continues as a formal or literary term for cunning schemes; used in espionage thrillers, chess commentary, and rhetoric about deceptive politics; the plural "stratagems" retains the flavour of a whole set of clever tricks.
