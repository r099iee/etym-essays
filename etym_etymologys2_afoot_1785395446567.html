Linear derivation chain for AFOOT (a compound of the prefix a- "on" and the noun foot): 1. Proto-Indo-European *pṓds (genitive *ped-és) "foot" — the reconstructed ancestor of the "foot" words across the family (Greek poús, Latin pēs, Sanskrit pád-, English foot). No single attested form for PIE; reconstructed on the basis of these descendants. 2. Proto-Germanic *fōts "foot" (early 1st millennium CE) — from PIE *pṓds via the regular Germanic sound shift and the rounding/fronting of the vowel; the base of all the Germanic "foot" words (Old High German fuoz, Old Norse fótr, Gothic fōtus). 3. Old English fōt "foot" (attested from the 8th c., e.g., in the glosses and in Beowulf) and the preposition on "on, upon, in" (from Proto-Germanic *ana, PIE *h₁én "in, on"), combined in the set phrase on fōte "on foot, walking", attested in Old English prose. 4. Middle English a fote / a-fote "on foot" (12th–15th c.): the Old English prepositional phrase contracts, with on reduced to the unstressed prefix a-, exactly as in aboard, asleep, afloat. 5. 16th c. — afoot acquires the figurative sense "in motion, astir, in preparation, in progress", attested from c. 1560 in phrases such as "what is afoot?" (i.e., what is stirring, what is being planned). Final node: AFOOT. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGE: the derivation itself is uncontroversial — afoot is transparently on + foot, and the literal sense "on foot, walking" is securely attested before the figurative sense. The nuance lies in the figurative bridge: handbooks note that the shift from "walking" to "in progress" proceeds through the idea that what is on foot is moving, and that the word long carried a military tint (an army afoot, a war afoot) before generalizing to plots and plans. The PIE form *pṓds is a reconstruction, not an attested word, and the exact vocalism of the PIE etymon is debated (full-grade *pṓd- vs. zero-grade *pd-); the stages above use the standard accepted reconstruction.
