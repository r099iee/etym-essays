*bher- (to carry, bear; Proto-Indo-European, reconstructed — one of the most widespread roots of the family, attested in Sanskrit bhárati "carries," Greek phérō "I carry," Latin ferō "I carry, bear," Old English beran)
→ Proto-Germanic *beraną (to carry, bear, bring forth; with the regular shift of PIE *bʰ- to Germanic *b-)
→ Proto-Germanic *farbaraną (to hold back, abstain, endure; the prefixed compound: for- "away, off" + *beraną)
→ Old English forberan (to endure, abstain, restrain oneself, hold back; attested from the earliest English records, c. 9th–10th c.)
→ Middle English forberen (to forbear, endure, refrain; 12th–15th c.)
→ English forbear (v.) (to refrain from, to hold back, to be patient; early modern)
→ English FORBEARANCE (forbear + -ance; first recorded 16th c.) ◄ TARGET

Note: PIE *bher- "to carry, bear" has reflexes in every major branch — Sanskrit bhárati, Greek phérō (whence phosphorus "light-bearer," metaphor "carrying over"), Latin ferō (whence fertile, transfer, confer), and the Germanic *beraną behind English bear, birth, burden, and bier. The English prefix for- is the same element as in forgive, forbid, and forsake, meaning "away, off, refraining," and it gives the compound its core sense "to hold oneself back." The Sanskrit bhárati "carries" is a sister reflex, not an ancestor.
