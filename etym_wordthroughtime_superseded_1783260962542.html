- Late-15th-century borrowing - supersede entered English from Old French superseder and Latin supersedere (sit above), where its earliest legal sense was postpone, defer, the verb governing a supersedeas, a writ that stayed proceedings.
- 17th-18th-century semantic shift - the sense of sit above hardened into occupy a superior or later position and thereby set the older thing aside, so the word became the standard term for one law, rule, or practice being officially replaced by a superior successor.
- Contemporary sense - a calm, formal, almost judicial word for orderly replacement by a better thing, carrying no aggression: technologies are superseded, constitutions supersede earlier ones, and the tone is one of inevitable progress rather than violent ousting.
