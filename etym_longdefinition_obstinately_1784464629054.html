Obstinately means in a stubborn, fixed, unyielding way: adhering to an opinion, request, or course of action despite opposition, explanation, or inconvenience. As an adverb it reports how a person speaks, acts, or holds a belief — colouring the verb with the quality of the adjective obstinate. The word carries not only firmness but a slightly abrasive persistence: refusal to be talked off the point. When Bilbo asks for the gold, the dragon, ownership, and risks 'all plain and clear,' and says so obstinately while putting on his business manner, the word marks hobbit tenacity misapplied (or well applied) to adventure logistics: he will not be soothed by song and map alone; he wants terms. The tone is comic resolve, bourgeois caution in a legendary room; the adverb is both character portrait and narrative irony, because obstinacy here is prudence wearing a mulish face. Primary senses
  • In a stubborn, fixed, unyielding manner; refusing to change one's opinion, intention, or course of action despite argument, evidence, or inconvenience — the central sense (he obstinately refused, she held obstinately to her belief).
  • With reference to things rather than persons — in a way that resists treatment, solution, or change (an obstinately persistent problem, an obstinately high fever), transferring the human refusal to yield onto non-human difficulty.
  • Manner-adverb of character — describing how a person speaks or behaves in a way that suggests mulish persistence; in narrative prose it paints a character who will not be moved, for better or worse.
Polysemy and semantic nuances
  • The double valence — the whole family (obstinate, obstinacy, obstinately) can be admiring or condemning depending on context: an obstinate refusal to surrender is heroic; an obstinate refusal to listen is a fault. The etymology — 'standing firm against' — is morally neutral, and context decides whether the fixed stance is resolve or mulishness. This is exactly why resolutely exists as the positive counterpart.
  • Formal register — obstinately is more formal and Latinate than the native stubbornly; it belongs to narrative, moral, and intellectual prose, where it carries a slightly evaluative edge of moral or intellectual critique (obstinate error, obstinate refusal, obstinate heresy).
  • Moral and religious history — in medieval and early modern usage obstinacy was a serious matter: sermons and treatises denounced the obstinate sinner, the obstinate heretic, the obstinate rebel who refused the counsel of church or state. The word retains a faint echo of this gravity.
  • Adverbial intensification — like most -ly manner adverbs it is often placed before or after the verb ('said he obstinately,' 'obstinately refused'), and it combines with a range of verbs of speech, belief, and action (obstinately insisted, obstinately clung, obstinately denied).
Near-synonyms and boundaries
  • Stubbornly — the closest everyday synonym; obstinately is more formal and slightly more pejorative.
  • Resolutely and determinedly — more positive and goal-oriented; they praise the fixedness that obstinately may blame.
  • Persistently — stresses repetition over time (acting again and again); obstinately stresses fixed refusal to yield to persuasion.
  • Pigheadedly — cruder, informal, and fully pejorative; obstinately is formal and can be sympathetic.
  • Firmly — can be calm, admirable, and neutral; obstinately carries friction and a hint of excessive resistance.
The card form OBSTINATELY is the adverb (obstinate + -ly), preserving the text's description of how Bilbo speaks — with the fixed, mulish clarity of his business manner.
