• Caesar: Similar because both are Roman titles derived from personal names, used of rulers and emperors; differs because "Caesar" began as the family name of Julius Caesar and became a junior or dynastic title (and later "Kaiser", "Tsar"), while "Augustus" was the senior, sacral title of the emperor and is not a family name.
  • Emperor: Similar because both denote the supreme ruler of an empire and can refer to Augustus as the first holder; differs because "emperor" is a generic English title for any imperial ruler, while "Augustus" names the specific Roman title and its first bearer.
  • Princeps: Similar because both were used of the Roman emperor as the leading citizen ("first citizen"); differs because "princeps" was the deliberately republican, constitutional mask Augustus preferred, while "Augustus" was the religious-majestic title that expressed his divine authority.
  • Imperator: Similar because both are Latin titles borne by the Roman emperor; differs because "imperator" originally meant "victorious commander" acclaimed by troops and later became an imperial title, while "Augustus" was the sacral honorific of increased divine favour.
  • Octavian: Similar because both denote the same historical person; differs because "Octavian" (from "Octavius") was his name before 27 BC, the name of the victorious faction-leader, whereas "Augustus" is the name he took with the title, marking the beginning of his reign and the Principate.
  • Monarch: Similar because both denote a sole supreme ruler; differs because "monarch" is a generic term for a hereditary king or queen, while "Augustus" is a specific Roman sacral title, and Augustus's own constitutional fiction was that he was not a monarch.
  • Sovereign: Similar because both denote a supreme ruler with ultimate authority; differs because "sovereign" is a legal-political term applicable to any supreme power (including states), while "Augustus" is an individual title tied to Roman history.
  • August (adjective): Similar because both descend from the same Latin word and share the meaning "venerable, majestic"; differs because "august" is a common adjective in English describing any awe-inspiring dignity, while "Augustus" is a capitalized proper name and title.
  • The Venerable / venerable: Similar because both express the awe and respect that the title "Augustus" was meant to command; differs because "venerable" is a general adjective of respect (and a specific ecclesiastical title), not the specific Roman sacral concept of divinely increased majesty.
  • Autocrat: Similar because both denote a ruler with absolute personal power; differs because "autocrat" is a later, more frankly despotic term, while "Augustus" masked autocracy in republican and religious forms.
  • Dictator: Similar because both were Roman supreme magistracies in which one man held absolute power; differs because "dictator" was an emergency, temporary, constitutional office that Augustus carefully avoided, while "Augustus" was a lifelong sacral title.
  • First citizen: Similar because both describe Augustus's self-presentation as primus inter pares; differs because "first citizen" is an English gloss of "princeps" emphasising republican equality, while "Augustus" emphasises religious majesty and increased divine status.
