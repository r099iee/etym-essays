The noun ENFRANCHISEMENT is best understood as a legal and political term whose history begins not in the abstract language of rights but in the concrete world of war, conquest, and land. Its ultimate source is the reconstructed Proto-Germanic noun *frankō, “javelin” or “spear,” which was taken as the name of the Frankish confederation, the Germanic peoples who crossed the Rhine and established a kingdom over much of post-Roman Gaul. As conquerors, the Franks alone enjoyed the full freedom that came with victory; their very name, Frank, became in the language of the conquered Gallo-Romans a byword for the free man — the man who was not a serf, not bound, not servile. Thus Old French franc, the ethnic adjective, came to mean simply “free, exempt, of noble or privileged standing,” and, by extension of the free man's supposed candor, “open, sincere, honest.”

Feudal foundations. The medieval stage of the word's life is firmly rooted in feudal law. Old French built enfranchir from the prefix en- (from Latin in-) and franc, meaning “to make free, to set free,” and the verb was applied above all to the legal act of manumission — the release of a serf or villain from servitude — and to the granting of the freedom of a borough, which carried the rights to trade, to own property, and to participate in municipal government. Anglo-Norman and Middle English took the verb as enfraunchisen, and the nominal suffix -ment, from Latin -mentum via Old French, produced enfraunchisement, the name of the ceremony and legal process by which a person was declared free or admitted to citizenship. In this medieval sense the word is a concrete, procedural term: enfranchisement was an act performed by a lord or a town, with charters, oaths, and witnesses, not a general slogan.

The constitutional transformation. The modern transformation came with the rise of constitutional politics. As representative government spread from the eighteenth century onward, enfranchisement narrowed and elevated itself from the old feudal act to the political admission of whole classes into citizenship. In Britain the successive Reform Acts of 1832, 1867, and 1884 each extended the franchise to new groups of men, and each was described in the language of enfranchisement; the Chartist movement of the 1830s and 1840s made manhood suffrage its central demand. The most famous English use is Harriet Taylor Mill's essay The Enfranchisement of Women (1851), which John Stuart Mill praised and partly rewrote, and which gives the word in the card's context its exact occasion: an article on women's enfranchisement becomes the pretext for “another encomium” upon his wife's powers.

Modern extension and the antonyms. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word has been extended beyond gender and class to any excluded group admitted to civic or political rights — the enfranchisement of former slaves after the American Civil War, the enfranchisement of colonial peoples in the era of decolonization, the enfranchisement of prisoners' votes in modern debate. Its antonym, disenfranchisement (or disfranchisement), the withdrawal or denial of the vote, has become equally common in political commentary, so that the two words now define the two poles of democratic inclusion.

  • Sound and form. The word keeps its French spelling of the fifteenth century almost unchanged: the en- prefix, the -franch- stem (from franc), and the nominal -ment. In pronunciation the stress falls on the second syllable (“en-FRAN-chise-ment”).
  • Register drift. From a procedural term of medieval law the word became a solemn constitutional keyword in the age of reform, and today it is a formal, elevated noun of political discourse — one would rarely use it of a minor privilege such as a library card.
  • Literary attestations. Beyond Harriet Taylor Mill, the word appears throughout Victorian reformist prose, in constitutional historians such as Macaulay, in the American debates over the Fifteenth Amendment, and in suffragist rhetoric on both sides of the Atlantic.

The word thus carries within it a compressed history of Western liberty: from the spear of the conquering Frank, to the free man of feudal law, to the citizen of the modern democratic state. To enfranchise is, etymologically, to make free in the manner of a Frank — and enfranchisement is the long, laborious, legal making-freedom of a people.
