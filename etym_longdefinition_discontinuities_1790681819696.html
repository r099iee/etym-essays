discontinuities is the plural of discontinuity, denoting multiple instances of lack of continuity — breaks, gaps, ruptures, or interruptions in what would otherwise be a continuous series, process, argument, texture, or historical development. In its most general sense it means simply that things do not join up: there is a cut, a jump, or an interval where sameness, flow, or logical connection fails. A crack in a wall, a missing chapter in a record, a logical non sequitur, and a sudden evolutionary leap can all be described as discontinuities.

In academic and technical use the term carries stronger, domain-specific weight. In mathematics and physics it denotes points at which a function, material, or process is not continuous — e.g., jump discontinuities, removable discontinuities, or phase boundaries. In geology it denotes unconformities and faults where strata do not follow in unbroken succession. In history, philosophy of science, and cultural theory — as in Foucault's archaeology or Kuhn's paradigm shifts — it denotes radical breaks with prior traditions rather than gradual evolution, and in behavioral science, as in the source context, it denotes methodological and conceptual mismatches: C-B-S thinking exhibits discontinuities with other disciplines, i.e., it does not cohere, align, or articulate smoothly with them.

  • Core physical sense: a perceptible interruption in material or spatial continuity — a gap, fissure, joint, or fault. Example: discontinuities in rock strata indicating erosion or tectonic movement.
  • Temporal / narrative sense: breaks in time, sequence, or story — missing years, ellipses, abrupt transitions. Bridges historically from the spatial sense via the metaphor TIME IS SPACE.
  • Logical / argumentative sense: failures of coherence or entailment — contradictions, non sequiturs, category errors. The source sentence uses this sense: self-contradictions within C-B-S thinking plus discontinuities with neighbouring fields.
  • Mathematical-technical sense: precisely defined points of non-continuity in functions, distributions, or media — classified since the 19th century into removable, jump, and essential discontinuities, each with distinct analytic consequences for limits, integration, and modelling.
  • Historical-epistemological sense: Foucault, Bachelard, and Kuhn gave the word a celebrated secondary life to mean epistemic ruptures: moments when a discipline's concepts, methods, or objects change so sharply that no cumulative story can bridge old and new regimes without distortion.
  • Psychological-behavioral sense: perceived gaps between stimulus and response, intention and action, or one explanatory level and another — e.g., discontinuities between neural, behavioural, and cultural accounts of mind.

Grammatically, discontinuities is a regular count plural in -ies (consonant + y → ies), contrasting with mass uses of discontinuity as a property ("the discontinuity of the record"). It collocates with reveal, expose, bridge, overcome, mark, exhibit, manifest and with prepositions with, between, in, across: discontinuities with other fields (external mismatch), discontinuities between stages (internal gap), discontinuities in a series. Register is formal and analytic rather than colloquial, where speakers would prefer gaps, breaks, jumps. Its rhetorical force is diagnostic and often critical: to allege discontinuities is to allege failure of integration, whether as neutral observation or as accusation of incoherence.
