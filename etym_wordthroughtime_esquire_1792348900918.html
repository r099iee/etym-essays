- [13th–14th c. Feudal Middle English] — borrowed from Anglo-Norman as a designation for a knight's personal attendant, armor-bearer, and chivalric apprentice - [perception: respected as a noble youth in training for full knighthood and martial brotherhood]
- [15th–17th c. Early Modern English] — evolved into a distinct socio-legal rank in English heraldry, denoting landowners, magistrates, and the heads of ancient gentry families situated immediately below knights - [perception: an official mark of gentle birth, landed authority, and heraldic entitlement]
- [18th–19th c. Georgian & Victorian Eras] — expanded into an epistolary courtesy title appended to the names of respected gentlemen on postal correspondence (e.g., "John Smith, Esq."), while formally retained by barristers and justices of the peace - [perception: an indispensable badge of social respectability, distinguished from mere tradesmen]
- [Late 19th–20th c. American Transition] — specialized almost exclusively in the United States legal profession as a post-nominal title for licensed attorneys-at-law of either gender - [perception: a formal signifier of professional bar certification and legal authority]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Register] — crystallized in popular culture via Esquire magazine as the quintessential modern gentleman of taste and sophistication, while general epistolary use in the UK gradually receded into archaism - [perception: primarily a professional legal credential in the US and an evocative, vintage honorific in international English]
