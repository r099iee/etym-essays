- 14th century: English compounds "undercut" (verb) from under + cut — literal "to cut from beneath," used of wood, meat, and branches.
- 15th–16th centuries: figurative extension — "to undermine, to destroy secretly" (plans, reputations, honour), with no commercial meaning yet.
- 17th–19th centuries: in swordplay and fighting, an "undercut" is a rising cut from below — Tolkien's sense; in 19th-century boxing a rising blow from below is called an "undercut" before "uppercut" displaces it in the 1930s.
- Late 19th century: the commercial revolution adds "to undercut" = "to undersell a competitor with lower prices"; from the 20th century this is the dominant business sense, applied to prices, bids, and wages.
- 20th century: figurative "to weaken, to deprive of force" (undercut an argument, undercut authority) renews the old undermine sense.
- 2010s: "the undercut" becomes a fashionable men's hairstyle (short shaved sides, long top) — the word's journey from sword stroke to haircut shows how radically meanings can repurpose.
