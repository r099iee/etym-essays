1. BALE

A large, dense bundle of goods, tightly compressed and bound with cord, rope, or wire — the standard packing unit of trade for cotton, hay, wool, straw, paper, and cloth. In the passage, Bellman takes an electric torch "from his bale of belongings": the word turns expedition gear into cargo — heavy, packed, corded, the raw logistics of survival made tangible.

  • Primary mercantile sense — a compressed, bound mass of merchandise: "a bale of cotton," "a bale of hay," "a bale of paper." The bale is both a unit of quantity and a unit of transport.
  • Extended sense — any bulky bound pack of personal effects or provisions, as here.
  • Back-formation verb "to bale" — to compress and bind into bales ("baling hay"); separately, an unrelated homograph "to bale out" means to scoop water out of a boat, and in aviation "to bail/bale out" means to parachute to safety — a different word, from Old French baille (a bucket).
  • Distinction from "bale" (evil) — Old English bealu "evil, misery" (surviving in "bale-fire" and in Tolkien's archaisms) is a completely different word, accidentally identical in spelling and unrelated in origin.


2. PRYING

The participial adjective of "to pry": searching, peering, or inquiring in an inquisitive, intrusive, or impertinent way. In the passage Bellman "turned its prying beam into the shadows" — the torch's light is itself given the quality of an intrusive eye, wedging into the dark as a crowbar wedges open a locked thing.

  • Primary sense — given to or marked by intrusive curiosity: "prying eyes," "prying questions," "a prying neighbor." Almost always negative in modern usage.
  • Older neutral sense — simply to look closely, strain the eyes, or peer narrowly (13th–16th c.); the blame was acquired later, in the 17th century.
  • Second, confusingly identical verb "to pry" — to force open with a lever ("pry the lid off"; the US spelling of British "prise"); the two homophones have influenced each other, so that a "prying eye" feels like a lever working on secrets.
  • Noun "pry" — an act of prying; also the lever itself (a "pry bar," US usage).
  • Context and texture — BALE — the torch drawn "from his bale of belongings" makes the travellers' gear a compressed, corded mass; the expedition's supplies are stowed like merchandise, so that the explorers carry their world into the cave's darkness as cargo. In trade the bale was even standardized — a bale of cotton weighed several hundred pounds in the plantation economy of the American South — so that "bale" is not merely a bundle but a measure, a quantity with economic gravity; the word imports that weight into the passage.
  • Context and texture — PRYING — the torch's beam is "turned into the shadows," and calling it "prying" gives the light an eye and a purpose: it looks where the darkness wishes to keep its secrets, and the cave's floor, "worn smooth as if by vanished waters," refuses to yield its history until the light forces it. The adjective is almost exclusively critical today; a "prying beam," like a "prying eye," is an intrusion given personality and intent.
