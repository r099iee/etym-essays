ENCOMIUM is a noun denoting a formal, composed, and usually public expression of high praise — a tribute, eulogy, or panegyric delivered in speech or in writing. In the passage behind this card, an article on the enfranchisement of women “was made the occasion for another ENCOMIUM”: John Stuart Mill used the publication of a serious political essay as the pretext for another lavish celebration of his wife Harriet Taylor's intellectual gifts. The nuance of the word matters: an encomium is not casual or spontaneous praise but a set-piece of commendation, worked up and delivered, the kind of tribute one offers at a banquet, in a dedication, or in a review. The sentence quietly insinuates that the praise may have been disproportionate to the occasion — a political article on women's suffrage becomes the vehicle of personal homage — and so the word names both the genre of the tribute and the slightly suspect surplus of admiration that so often attends it.

Primary sense — a formal speech or writing of praise. In ordinary modern use, an encomium is any elaborate speech, essay, poem, or review composed to sing someone's or something's praises. It implies deliberate, ornamented composition rather than spontaneous feeling, and a public or semi-public setting. One writes an encomium of a colleague at retirement, of a master's work, or of a nation's achievements; a reviewer's essay that is all admiration may be called an encomium rather than a criticism.

  • Classical literary sense — the choral ode of praise. In Greek literature, the enkōmion was a fixed lyric genre: a formal ode sung in honour of a victor at the games, a ruler, or the dead, celebrated during the festive procession (kōmos) that gave the word its name. This is the root sense from which all later meanings descend.
  • Rhetorical sense — the oratorical eulogy. In Roman and Renaissance rhetoric, the encomium became one of the set exercises of praise, a model speech or composition celebrating a person, a city, an institution, or even a virtue — the rhetorical handbooks taught its structure from the subject's birth, family, deeds, and virtues onward.
  • Satirical and ironic sense. Because an encomium is a manufactured tribute, the word lends itself to irony: a mock encomium praises ironically (as Erasmus's In Praise of Folly and the Greek Encomium of Helen by Gorgias), and in ordinary usage calling a piece an encomium can faintly suggest flattery, extravagance, or praise in excess of desert.

Register and collocations. ENCOMIUM is formal and bookish, at home in literary criticism, biography, and ceremonial oratory. It collocates with verbs of bestowal and delivery: “pronounce,” “deliver,” “offer,” “write,” “heap.” One delivers an encomium, pronounces it, or writes it in honour of a subject. Its adjectives are “lavish,” “glowing,” “rhetorical,” and, increasingly, “faintly ironic.” The plural is encomia (classical) or encomiums (Anglicised).

Distinctions and semantic boundaries. The encomium is close to the eulogy, but the eulogy is specifically praise of the deceased delivered at a funeral, whereas an encomium may praise the living and need not involve mourning. It overlaps with the panegyric, which in classical usage was a formal public speech in praise of a ruler or city and leans toward public oratory and flattery of power; the encomium may be spoken or written and is the more general word. Against the compliment — a small, casual courtesy — the encomium is a composed literary set-piece; against the tribute, which may take the form of gifts, memorials, or acts, the encomium is always a verbal composition. In the context of the Autobiography, calling Mill's praise an encomium is a pointed way of saying that it was more than justified celebration — it was a formal, almost literary, genre of homage.
