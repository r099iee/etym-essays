LINEAR CHAIN (PRAGMATIC) 1. Greek πράσσειν / πράττειν (prássein / práttein, "to do, act, accomplish, fare, manage") — the verbal base, attested from the Archaic/Homeric period onward. Its deeper PIE ancestry is uncertain and disputed; no single secure PIE etymon is universally accepted, so the chain begins with Greek itself. 2. Greek πρᾶγμα (prâgma, "deed, act, affair, matter, thing done"; plural πράγματα, prágmata, "affairs, business, circumstances") — attested from classical Greek (5th century BCE). 3. Greek πραγματικός (pragmatikós, "fit for business; active; skilled in affairs; relating to facts or deeds") — attested from classical Greek onward. 4. Latin pragmaticus (skilled in business or law; businesslike; as a noun, a legal adviser or practitioner) — attested from the 1st century BCE onward; a Greek learned loan in Latin. 5. English pragmatic (adjective) — borrowed via the Latin/French learned channel in the early modern period (16th century). Early English senses: "officious, meddlesome, interfering" and "relating to affairs of state" (e.g., "pragmatic sanction"); the modern dominant sense, "practical, results-oriented," was consolidated from the 19th century onward, reinforced by the philosophical school of Pragmatism. The noun pragmatics in the modern linguistic sense dates to the 20th century. SISTER LOANS from the same Greek action-root family: praxis (πρᾶξις), practical, practice (πρακτικός, praktikós). NOTE ON DEBATE: There is no universally accepted PIE reconstruction for the Greek verb; older handbooks sometimes linked it to *perə- or similar roots, but these are speculative and not standard. The historically attested path is Greek → Latin → English, with the "meddlesome" and "results-oriented" senses developing within English itself.
