- 14th c. - curt enters Middle English from Old French court, "short"; the sense is purely physical and spatial - a curt staff is a short staff, a curt man a short man - with no rudeness yet implied.
- 16th–18th c. - the word lingers on the edge of the vocabulary; its energy passes into the verb curtail ("to cut short"), while curt itself remains an uncommon adjective meaning merely "shortened."
- c. 1825 - the decisive transfer: curt is first recorded meaning "rudely brief in speech"; the spatial idea of being cut short is applied to words, and a curt reply becomes one docked like a horse's tail - stripped of courtesy, hedging, and softening.
- 19th–20th c. - the abstract noun curtness appears, naming a fixed behavioural quality of clipped, peremptory, faintly contemptuous brevity; it enters the vocabulary of the essayist and novelist as a clinical, diagnostic authorial verdict on a character's manner.
- 20th–21st c. - curtness stays a formal and literary noun, typical of contexts of rank, office, and strained acquaintance; everyday speech prefers "he was short with me," while curtness remains the blade-sharp word for conversation cut short.
