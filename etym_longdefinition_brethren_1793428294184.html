Brethren is an archaic, formal, and ecclesiastical plural of brother, used almost exclusively to designate fellow members of a shared profession, religious congregation, fraternal society, or ideological community, rather than biological male siblings. Morphologically, it represents one of the rarest grammatical survivors in Modern English: an ancient cumulative double plural combining an internal vowel mutation (i-umlaut) with an external weak noun plural suffix.

In the source passage from Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726), Lemuel Gulliver recounts the crisis that redirected his career back toward maritime voyages: ‘But my good master Bates dying in two years after, and I having few friends, my business began to fail; for my conscience would not suffer me to imitate the bad practice of too many among my __________.  Having therefore consulted with my wife, and some of my acquaintance, I determined to go again to sea.’ Here, brethren refers specifically to Gulliver’s professional colleagues in London—the community of surgeons and medical practitioners organized under the Company of Barber-Surgeons. Swift uses the term with biting Augustan irony: while brethren nominally invokes Christian charity, mutual trust, and honorable guild solidarity, Gulliver uses it to expose the pervasive commercial corruption, charlatanism, and unethical practices of his medical contemporaries, which his conscience refused to copy.

Core meaning and the great plural schism: During the transition from Middle to Early Modern English, the plural forms of brother bifurcated along semantic lines. The regularized plural brothers (formed with the productive northern suffix -s) became the exclusive term for blood relations and kinship. Conversely, brethren was specialized for non-biological, covenantal bonds—individuals united by a common oath, shared faith, mutual professional mysteries, or collective historical struggle.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Professional and guild colleagues (primary historical sense in the text): Fellow practitioners of an art, trade, or learned profession, particularly those belonging to a chartered guild, legal inn, medical faculty, or academic senate. Guildsmen were sworn ‘brethren of the mystery,’ obligated to support one another while maintaining standards of trade.
  • Ecclesiastical and congregational address (liturgical sense): Fellow believers within the Christian church, celebrated in biblical passages and liturgical invocations such as the Book of Common Prayer’s familiar opening address: ‘Dearly beloved brethren, the Scripture moveth us in sundry places...’
  • Specific denominational titles (proper noun usage): The formal name of several historical Protestant denominations emphasizing non-violence, communal fellowship, or simple biblical doctrine, such as the Plymouth Brethren, the Moravian Brethren (Unitas Fratrum), the Bohemian Brethren, and the Church of the Brethren.
  • Fraternal and secret societies (lodges and brotherhoods): Initiated members of fraternal orders such as the Freemasons, the Ancient Order of Foresters, or the Independent Order of Odd Fellows, where members address one another as ‘brethren’ under solemn ritual oaths.
  • Ideological and civil rights solidarity (oratorical sense): Rhetorical address invoking the universal kinship of oppressed peoples, trade union comrades, or citizens fighting in common cause for liberty and equality (e.g., in the stirring rhetoric of Martin Luther King Jr. and 19th-century abolitionists).
  • Ironic, satirical, or conspiratorial usage (literary register): Cynical reference to members of a corrupt clique, self-serving cartel, or political cabal who protect one another against public accountability, directly echoing Swift’s sardonic tone in Gulliver's Travels.

Register, connotation, and stylistic resonance: Brethren belongs to a high, solemn, and archaic register. It carries an aura of ancient tradition, sacred covenant, and collective responsibility. In secular modern speech, using brethren outside formal religious, lodge, or legal contexts sounds intentionally elevated, biblical, or humorously arch.
