swelling. The biography of swelling begins with an ancient root of two faces. The Proto-Indo-European base *swel- is usually divided by scholars into two homophonous roots: one meaning "to burn, to glow," the other "to swell, to rise." The glow-sense left its mark in Sanskrit svarati, "to shine, to resound," and in Greek selas, "brightness, flash"; the swell-sense produced the great Germanic family — Proto-Germanic *swellaną, Old English swellan, Old Norse svella, German schwellen, Dutch zwellen — all meaning "to grow in volume, to rise up." Old English even kept the burning homophone alive in swelan, "to smoulder," which the OED notes may be distantly linked to the swell word; whether the two faces were ever one root is a question no evidence can finally settle, and the honest statement is that the root's prehistory is complicated by the homophone. From its earliest recorded use, to swell described the living distension of flesh, water, and bread: a wound swelled, a river swelled in flood, dough swelled in the oven. The core image is growth from within, under pressure — matter pressing outward against its own surface — and it is this interiority that has always distinguished swelling from mere growing. In Old and Middle English the verb carried this physical sense through the centuries, and the present participle swelling (attested from the fourteenth century) added the adjectival sense "puffed up, growing." The word then began its long migration into the figurative. In the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries swelling pride, swelling rage, and swelling sound entered the language: the same inward pressure, spilling over from matter into emotion and volume. The heart swells with joy; music swells to a climax; anger swells in the breast — each use still faintly carrying the old physics of the body filling from within, the way a sail fills with wind. The nineteenth century produced two further turns. In the language of medicine, swelling fixed itself as the standard term for a localized enlargement of tissue — the everyday clinical word for the body's response to injury and inflammation, a use matched in Hindi by sūjan (सूजन), the swelling of a twisted ankle, and by the everyday phrase suj jānā, "to swell up." In American slang of the late nineteenth century, meanwhile, the word performed an unexpected somersault: "a swell fellow" meant an excellent, elegant fellow, and "swell" became a term of pure praise — a new dress, a fine party, a good time — with the pressure entirely drained out of the word. That slang sense glows in the prose of the period and survives today as a mild, nostalgic intensifier, a fossil of the Gilded Age. In the passage, the participle recovers its oldest and largest energy. The cloud, "swelling and towering swiftly," is matter inflating from within at the abruptness of the released genie — not accumulation but dilation, bulk gained faster than the eye can follow, the living, breathing organism of the weather inflating itself against the sky. The word's whole journey — from a PIE root through the flood-waters and swelling wounds of Old English to the clinical register of modern medicine and the cheerful slang of nineteenth-century America — is concentrated in that single participle: growth under pressure, organic, liquid, and suddenly enormous.
towering. Its story begins with a turning. The Proto-Indo-European root *twer- meant "to turn, to whirl, to stir," and its branches kept that motion everywhere: Sanskrit tvarate, "he hastens" — one who turns quickly — is a reflex; Latin turma, "a squadron," is a "whirling" troop of riders; and Latin turba, "a crowd, a commotion," gave English turbulent, turbid, and turmoil. Then comes the crucial turn of fortune, the point where the root leaves motion behind. Greek tyrsis, "tower, stronghold," is a word of disputed origin: it appears in the oldest Greek texts for the round towers and walled acropoleis of the Aegean, and modern scholarship, following the work on Pre-Greek substrate vocabulary, treats it most probably as a loan from the Mediterranean languages spoken before Greek — a non-Indo-European word for a non-Indo-European kind of building, the circular bastion of the Aegean citadels. On the older hypothesis it was attached directly to PIE *twer-, as if the tower were "turned" or "whirled" into shape; the connection is probable at best and cannot be proved. If tyrsis is indeed a substrate word, then tower's Indo-European ancestry is second-hand only, entering through the Greek loan into Latin. The probable relative Tyrsenoi — the Greek name for the Etruscans, "people of the towers" — has been read as evidence that the word travelled with the tower-builders themselves. Whatever its deeper origin, the word crossed into Latin as turris, "tower," a perfectly ordinary classical word, and from Latin it passed into the Romance languages: Old French tour or tor, the ancestor of modern French tour, which English adopted in the Middle English period as tour, our tower. The noun arrived with the full architectural weight of medieval Europe — the castle's keep, the church tower, the bell-tower, the fortified height that watches over the surrounding land. From the noun, English built the verb to tower, "to rise to a great height," and then the participle towering, attested from the seventeenth century, meaning "rising like a tower, that overtops its surroundings." From its first appearance the participle was static and monumental rather than airborne: towering describes the position of holding height, not the motion of gaining it, and its dignity is architectural even when applied to the natural world — towering cliffs, towering pines, towering clouds. The figurative turn came early and proved permanent. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries towering attached itself to passion and to scale of mind: a towering rage, towering ambition, towering intellect — height converted into magnitude of feeling and achievement, the vertical image standing for the immeasurable. A towering figure is someone so eminent that others stand in his shadow; a towering achievement is one that overtops its field. In the passage the participle works at full concrete power: the cloud "swelling and towering swiftly" is not merely tall — it is tall the way a fortress is tall, a monolith on the planet's rim, an architectural presence with the menace and magnificence of a genie grown vast. From a whirling PIE root to the still, overwhelming height of the cloud, the word has travelled from motion to monument, and it holds its height as if it had never moved.
