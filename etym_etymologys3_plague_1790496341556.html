plague begins as a blow and ends as a bacterium — with three millennia of theology, panic, and schoolroom mischief in between. Its root is the vivid PIE *plak-, to strike, to beat, which gives Greek plege (blow, stroke), plessein (to strike), apoplexia (a stroke — being struck down), Latin plangere (to beat the breast in grief, whence complain, plaintive), plaga (a blow, then the wound a blow leaves, then any calamity), and Germanic echoes in flake (a struck-off splinter). A plaga in Caesar is a sword-cut; in Cicero it is already a political disaster; in the Vulgate it is God's fist.

  • Vulgate plagae and the theology of striking (4th–5th c.): Jerome, translating Exodus, renders the Hebrew makkot (blows) as Latin plagae: decem plagae Aegypti, the ten blows of Egypt — blood, frogs, gnats, flies, murrain, boils, hail, locusts, darkness, death of the firstborn. Each is a stroke laid on Pharaoh. Patristic preaching generalises the image: famine, war, and pestilence are all plagae Dei, blows of God for sin. The semantic shift from single wound to mass calamity happens here, inside church Latin, and never reverses: a plague is no longer what one soldier does to one enemy but what Heaven does to a people.
  • Old French plaie/plage to Middle English plage (12th–14th c.): Norman French brings plaie (wound, sore — modern French plaie) and the learned form plage (calamity). Middle English plage (Chaucer, Langland, Wyclif) means both a sore on the body and a sorrow on the land; spelling wavers between plage, plague, plawe because scribes confuse Latin plaga (blow) with plaga (tract, region — a different word behind pelagic). The intrusive -u- that gives modern plague (/pleɪɡ/) is a 15th-century Anglo-French flourish, like tongue for tonge — a silent letter advertising learnedness while the pronunciation stays /pleɪɡ/.
  • The Black Death names the word (1347–1352): when Yersinia pestis arrives from the Crimea to Sicily, Marseille, and Weymouth, contemporaries call it the pestilence, the mortality, the great death; within a generation retrospective chronicles fix it as the plague, and thereafter plague in English means epidemic first and wound second. Successive visitations — London 1361, 1563, 1603, 1625, 1665; Marseille 1720; the Third Pandemic from Yunnan in the 1890s — keep the association lethal. Plague orders, plague stones (hollowed stones filled with vinegar for coins), quarantine (forty days, from Italian quaranta), lazarettos, and Bills of Mortality build an administrative vocabulary around the single noun, while Defoe, Pepys, and nursery rhymes (ring-a-ring o' roses) build its literary afterlife. Bacteriology in 1894 finally isolates the bacillus (Yersin and Kitasato), but the name, forged from a fist-blow, stays.
  • From pestilence to pestering: alongside the dying, the verb quietly generalises. To plague someone in 1500 is to smite them with disease; by Shakespeare (a plague on both your houses) it is already to curse; by Austen it is to tease and importune (do not plague me with questions). The schoolroom specialisation — the plague of the class, our little plague, Ruskin's plague and pet — belongs to this domestication: the deadliest word in the language tamed into a governess's fond complaint. Modern collocations (plagued by injuries, plagued with guilt, a plague of flies, a plague of notifications) keep both poles alive: we still hear the blow even when we only mean the bother.

Ruskin's aunt, refusing evangelical gloom with laughter, is therefore a walking etymology: a plaga — a blow — to solemnity, a small sweet epidemic of high spirits in a school built for penitence. The teachers who called her a plague and kept her as a pet understood, better than they knew, that every plague begins as a stroke — and that some strokes waken rather than kill.
