Derivation chain for wager: 1. Proto-Indo-European *wedʰ- 'to pledge, to bind oneself, to engage, to give security' (traditional root, also written *wedh-; attested in Latin vas, vadis 'surety, bail' and in the Germanic pledging words; the exact vocalism of the root is debated, but the core sense 'to pledge, to stand surety' is well established). 2. Proto-Germanic *wadją (neuter ja-stem noun) 'pledge, guarantee, security, earnest-money' (reconstructed from the Germanic daughters: Old English wedd 'pledge, security', Old High German wetti 'pledge, fine, price', and Gothic wadi 'pledge, security' — the Gothic form is attested in the Gothic Bible, e.g. Mark 1:44, in the sense of an offering or pledge). 3. Frankish *waddi 'pledge, security deposit, earnest money' (the West Germanic form of the same word as borrowed into Gallo-Romance; reflected indirectly in Medieval Latin vadium / wadium 'pledge, surety, security'). 4. Old North French / Anglo-French wage (noun) 'pledge, security, deposit, reward, challenge, stipend' (the form with initial /w/, preserved in Norman French, where Central French had gage with /g/; Central French gage 'pledge, pawn, wages' is the source of English gage, while the Norman wage is the source of English wage 'payment for work' and wager). 5. Anglo-French wager (verb) 'to pledge, to guarantee, to engage, to wager' (formed from wage) and wageure / wager (noun) 'wager, bet, guarantee'. 6. Middle English wager (noun) 'a pledge, a security, a bet, a stake risked on an uncertain outcome' (attested from the 14th c.) and wager (verb) 'to wager, to bet'. 7. Modern English WAGER (noun 'an act of betting; a stake risked on an uncertain outcome'; verb 'to bet, to risk; to venture a confident prediction') — the exact target word. Note on doublets and cognates: English wage 'payment for work' comes from the same Old North French wage (a pledged payment), so wager and wage are etymological twins; English gage 'a pledge, a security' comes from the Central French gage of the same Germanic source; and the native English cognate of the whole family is Old English wedd 'pledge', which survives in to wed and wedding — to wed is, etymologically, to pledge oneself.
