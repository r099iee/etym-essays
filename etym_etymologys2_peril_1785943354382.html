PIE *per- 'to try, to venture, to risk; to lead or pass across' (one of the most prolific roots of the family; also the source of Latin per 'through', peritus 'experienced', experiri 'to test' - and of English for, far, and experience) -> Latin periculum 'trial, test, attempt; risk, danger' (also spelled periclum; noun in -culum built on the same stem as experiri; attested from the 2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus and in the phrase periculum facere 'to make a trial, to run a risk') -> Old French peril 'danger, risk' (also perill) -> Anglo-Norman and Middle English peril 'danger, jeopardy' (attested c. 1225 in Ancrene Wisse; also spelled perile) -> English PERIL 'serious and immediate danger'.

Note on the debate: PIE *per- splits into two broad sense-clusters - 'forward, through, beyond' (as in Latin per, English for, German ver-) and 'try, venture, risk' (as in periculum, experiri 'to test', periclitari 'to be in danger'). Some scholars treat these as two originally distinct homonymous roots that merged; others as one root with 'passing through' developing the 'venturing through danger' sense. The Latin and Romance chain for peril itself is secure; only the sense-branching at the PIE level is debated.
