- Mid-16th-century entry as a learned Latin borrowing - used exclusively of the great Roman waterworks, so the word arrived in English already freighted with the prestige of antiquity and named only an archaeological monument, never a living machine.
- 17th-18th-century generalization - extended from the Roman structures to any artificial channel for conveying water, and applied to the new civic engineering of the modern age, where it gained a neutral, technical tone and lost some of its purely antiquarian colour.
- 19th-century monumentalizing - the aqueduct became a byword for Roman civil engineering and public-spirited infrastructure, celebrated in histories and guidebooks as proof that civilization is measured by what it leaves behind in stone.
- Contemporary sense - a standard technical word for water-supply structures of any era, and now metaphorically extended to anything that carries a continuous flow, from a conduit of ideas to a pipeline of data, while still retaining its Roman grandeur.
