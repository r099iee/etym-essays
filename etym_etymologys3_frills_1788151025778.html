1. A word without a pedigree (1590s). Few common garment-words arrive as suddenly as frill. English texts of the 1590s begin using it for a ruffled or fluted edging on clothing with no recorded prior form: the OED declares the origin obscure, and the usual appeals — a native dialect word, an imitation of the quivering flutter of the cloth, a loan from Dutch or Flemish — remain hypothesis. The suddenness itself tells a social story: Elizabethan and Jacobean fashion was erupting into ruffs, cuffs, and standing collars, and the language coined or seized a word for the small trembling edge that pleated cloth makes.

2. The ornamental century (17th c.). Through the seventeenth century the frill spread from the neckline down the shirtfront and out to the cuffs; the word also reached for anything crimped or curled, from hair to paper edges. Dictionaries give the verb ‘to frill’ = to curl, to quiver, matching the restless, fluttering character of the decoration. In this period there grew the connotation the word never lost: frills are pretty, fussy, and unnecessary — delightful on a sleeve, telling on a budget.

3. Victorian profusion (18th–19th c.). The nineteenth century was the frill’s great season. Sleeves, collars, petticoats, caps, curtains, and cushions carried frills; ‘frills and furbelows’ became the canonical phrase for trimming of every kind. The word slid easily from the sewing-room into prose: by mid-century one could speak of a speech ‘stripped of its rhetorical frills’, and journalists mocked novels ‘written with all possible frills’. The transfer was natural because the ornament was detachable: cut the frill and the garment still serves, so the frill came to name whatever could be cut away — the adornment that a task, a text, or an institution could shed without ceasing to function.

  • Dress: cuffs and jabots, baby frocks, and upholstery kept the word in daily domestic use.
  • Prose and oratory: ‘rhetorical frills’, ‘a plain style without frills’ made the figurative sense standard long before commerce adopted it.

4. The commercial turn (20th c.). The decisive event was the birth of the compound ‘no-frills’. From the early twentieth century, goods and services were advertised ‘without frills’; after the Second World War, and above all with the budget airlines of the 1970s and the deregulated 1980s, the label became a business model: function at minimum cost, ornament dropped. The word metonymized an entire economic philosophy — frills as the antonym of essentials.

5. Specialist branchings. The same flared-edge image went technical. The frilled lizard (Chlamydosaurus kingii, 1825) carries a neck frill raised in display; darkroom workers of the early twentieth century spoke of ‘frilling’ when the gelatin emulsion curled off the edge of a photographic plate. Both are the quivering edge of the sixteenth-century fabric, naturalized into biology and chemistry.

6. The word in culture. In Will Durant’s source sentence ‘the laces and frills … the arts and comforts, of civilization’, frills are the surplus that defines a civilization against mere survival — and the modern idiom guarantees the irony: no-frills civilization is a civilization stripped to its functions. The word today therefore carries a double valuation: charming on a cuff, dismissive in a business plan, and everywhere a reminder that ornament is what a culture adds after it has become secure in its necessities.
