rapped: (verb, past tense and past participle of rap) Struck sharply and quickly, with a short, crisp, percussive blow — often with a flat object against a hard surface, so that the contact makes an audible sound. The word emphasises speed, sharpness, and sound over force: a rap is quick, dry, and brief. In the context passage. In "He blew, and rapped them on the floor, to get off the dust" (C. S. Lewis, "Prince Caspian"), the word describes Peter's brisk treatment of the royal shield and sword: he blows on them and raps them on the floor to knock off the dust of ages. The nuance is quickness and sharpness: the blows are short, light, and dry, producing a crisp sound rather than a heavy impact — exactly the motion that would dislodge dust without damaging the metal. The tone is matter-of-fact and practical; Lewis gives the gesture a boyish briskness, and the word's percussive sound mirrors the action. In this context rapped also carries a faint sense of casual ownership — these are Peter's gifts, and he handles them with the easy confidence of a child, blowing and rapping the dust off the royal weapons before fitting the shield to his arm and slinging the sword by his side. Polysemy and modern senses. The word rap has grown a remarkable family of senses from the single blow:
  • The physical strike (the card's sense): to strike sharply and quickly; a rap is such a blow. Collocations: rap on the door, rap on the table, rap someone's knuckles (punishment), rap a stick on a desk.
  • The reprimand: "a rap on the knuckles" is a figurative punishment or rebuke; to take the rap (1904) is to accept blame or punishment, often for someone else's offence.
  • Sharp speech: to rap out an order or command (18th century) is to utter it sharply and crisply; a rapping style of speech is staccato and commanding.
  • Charge and blame: a rap can be a charge, censure, or criminal accusation — "a bum rap" is an unjust accusation, and a "rap sheet" is a criminal record.
  • Talk and music (20th century): in African-American vernacular, to rap is to talk, converse, or banter; by the 1960s–70s it meant to speak rhythmically over a beat, and rap music (named as a genre around 1979) made the word global — rapper, rap battle, gangsta rap, old-school rap. The musical rap extends the percussive blow-sense to voice and rhythm: the rapper "hits" the beat with words.
  • Inflected and derived forms: rapped (past and past participle — the form on this card), rapping (gerund and participle), rapper, rappy, rap sheet, rap battle.
The semantic bridge. The whole family is one continuous metaphor: the blow becomes the message. A sharp strike becomes a sharp rebuke, a sharp rebuke becomes sharp speech, sharp speech becomes rhythmic speech, and rhythmic speech becomes a genre of music — every link is the same percussive sharpness transferred to a new medium. Collocations and register. Rap collocates with on the door, on the table, on the knuckles, out (orders), take the (rap), rap sheet, rap music, rapper. The register spans the homely (rapping at a door), the formal (rap out a command), and the colloquial (to rap = to talk; rap music). Synonym boundaries. The boundary-synonyms are finely graded:
  • tap: lighter, gentler, often repeated — a delicate touch; rap is harder, sharper, and more decisive, a single crisp blow that makes a sound.
  • knock: the standard word for striking a door, usually repeated and moderate in force; a rap can be one sharp, quick blow and always emphasises the sound.
  • thump: heavy and dull — the sound of a soft-mass impact; rap is sharp, dry, and brief.
  • smack: loud and flat — the open-hand blow; rap is the crisp report of a firm object meeting a surface.
  • strike / hit: the general verbs of impact, carrying no information about force or sound; rap specifies a quick, sharp, audible blow.
  • thwack / whack: heavier, more swinging blows; a rap is short and staccato.
  • crack: sharper still, often of whips or breaking; rap is the contact-sound of hard objects, not of breaking.
