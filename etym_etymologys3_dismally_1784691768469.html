Dismally is the faint survivor of an astrological superstition. Its biography begins not with gloom but with brightness, for its oldest element is the Proto-Indo-European root *dyēw-, "bright sky, daylight", one of the most productive roots of the entire family. *dyēw- lies behind the day itself: Latin diēs "day"; Greek Zeus, the sky-god; Sanskrit dyāúṣ "sky, heaven" and devá "god"; Hittite šiwatt- "day". In Latin the root yielded the everyday noun diēs, and in the calendar of the medieval church diēs entered one of the oddest phrases in the history of English: diēs malī, "evil days". The adjective malus, "bad, evil", which supplies the second element, has its own obscure pedigree — some scholars connect it to a PIE *mel-, "to deceive, do wrong", but the etymology is genuinely disputed, and the word may belong to a pre-Latin layer of the Italian peninsula. What matters is the phrase: in late medieval calendars the two inauspicious days of each month — twenty-four in the year, popularly called the "Egyptian days" because they were credited to Egyptian astrological learning — were marked as diēs mali, days on which no enterprise should begin, days on which the almanac itself foretold calamity. The Anglo-Norman form dis mal carried the idea into England, and by the early fourteenth century dismal named these unpropitious dates. The semantic journey that followed is a classic case of widening. From a noun meaning a doomed calendar date the word passed to an adjective meaning "calamitous, unlucky", and from there to any state of gloom, dreariness, or sinister darkness, shedding almost all its astronomical content. The shift is easy to follow: a day marked in the almanac as unlucky is a day you dread; the dread seeps from the date to the weather, the news, and the mood; by Early Modern English dismal meant simply "cheerless, gloomy, dreary", and the calendar was forgotten. The word passed from astrological record to poetic gloom, and in the sixteenth century the addition of -ly — the native Germanic suffix that forms manner-adverbs from adjectives — produced dismally, a formation made in England rather than borrowed from French: dismal the adjective was French-derived, but the adverb is English work. What the word retains from its astrological cradle is a flavour of inescapable bad luck. To fail dismally is not merely to fail badly; it is to fail as though the day were written against you, as though the enterprise had begun on an evil date. And in Tolkien's sentence the flavour is deployed with perfect irony. Bilbo hopes the night's festivities were "part of his bad dreams"; the washing-up declares itself "dismally real". The adverb turns the physical fact of dirty crockery into something almost oracular — the day is marked, the verdict is in, and the only comfort (that it was all a dream) is refused. Dismally thus preserves, in a single adverb, a thousand years of European calendar-magic, and hands it to a hobbit contemplating a sinkful of dishes. The word's journey from the brightest of roots (*dyēw-, the shining sky) to the most leaden of modern adverbs is one of the great ironies of English semantic history: the daylight root ended up naming darkness, and the happy day became the unlucky one.
