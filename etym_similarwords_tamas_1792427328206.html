• Jāhnavī: Similar because both are sacred rivers celebrated in the Rāmāyaṇa and classical Sanskrit sacred geography; differs because Jāhnavī (the Ganges) is a grand, celestial waterway representing cosmic descent, universal salvation, and public ritual bathing, whereas Tamasā is a secluded, tranquil forest tributary symbolizing ascetic solitude, inner reflection, and the birth of poetic sentiment.
  • Sarayū: Similar because both are venerated waterways flowing through the kingdom of Kośala near the capital of Ayodhyā; differs because the Sarayū is the majestic civic river of royal dynastic life, state rituals, and ultimate earthly departure, whereas the Tamasā marks the immediate forest frontier and the initial threshold of exile.
  • Mandākinī: Similar because both are peaceful, forest-shaded streams intimately tied to Rāma’s ascetic exile; differs because the Mandākinī flows past Mount Citrakūṭa and is celebrated for mountain pastoral beauty and contented hermitage life, whereas the Tamasā evokes the poignant sadness of the first night of separation from society.
  • Godāvarī: Similar because both are prominent riverine landscapes in epic narrative where holy sages establish hermitages; differs because the Godāvarī is a vast, roaring southern river flowing through the perilous deeper wilderness of Pañcavaṭī, whereas the Tamasā is a gentle northern tributary situated just outside civic borders.
  • Kāverī: Similar because both are venerated hydronyms whose waters are praised for spiritual purification in Indian tradition; differs because the Kāverī is the supreme agricultural mother-river of southern India sustaining extensive temple civilizations, whereas Tamasā is uniquely celebrated as the quiet incubator of Sanskrit literary art.
  • Revā: Similar because both are ancient Sanskrit hydronyms celebrated in epic poetry for their scenic, holy banks; differs because Revā (the Narmada) is a massive east-west dividing river famed for its rugged rocky cascades and independent pilgrimage tradition (parikramā), whereas Tamasā is renowned specifically as Vālmīki's poetic sanctuary.
