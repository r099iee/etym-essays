• Pine: Similar in being a tall, conical conifer with needle-like leaves; differs because pine is evergreen with long clustered needles, while the larch alone sheds its needles, turning golden and bare each autumn.
  • Fir: Similar in the familiar Christmas-tree silhouette; differs because fir keeps its soft, flat needles year-round, whereas the larch goes bare in winter and re-greens in spring.
  • Spruce: Similar as a conifer of the same northern forests; differs because spruce has short, stiff, four-sided needles and downward-hanging cones, while the larch has soft needles, small upright cones, and a deciduous habit.
  • Tamarack: Similar as the very same tree in North America; differs only in name and region — tamarack is the Algonquian word for the American larch, identical in habit and genus.
  • Cedar: Similar as a large ornamental conifer; differs because cedar is an evergreen with scale- or awl-shaped leaves and famously fragrant, insect-repellent wood, unlike the soft deciduous needles of the larch.
  • Yew: Similar as a needle-bearing conifer; differs because the yew is a small evergreen with dark flat needles and red berry-like cones, a different genus with an entirely different growth habit.
  • Deciduous tree: Similar in losing leaves each autumn; differs because the general term covers all broadleaf trees that shed foliage, while the larch is the exceptional conifer that behaves like one.
