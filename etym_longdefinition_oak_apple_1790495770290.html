oak-apple, hyphenated exactly so in Ruskin, is first a natural object and second a political emblem, and the source sentence needs both. Though I always wore a gilded oak-apple very piously in my button-hole on the 29th of May — John Ruskin in Praeterita confessing a childhood Tory yearning for a true Restoration that Charles II had not effected — means the boy pinned to his coat a round oak-gall, gilded for the day, as Royalists had done every Oak Apple Day since 1660 to remember Charles's hiding in the Boscobel oak after Worcester (1651) and his restoration on his birthday, 29 May 1660. The oak-apple is thus at once a wasp's nursery and a king's relic: an insect-gall the size of a small apple, and the badge of restored monarchy. The hyphen marks the compound as established (modern books often write oak apple or oakapple); the lower case marks it as common noun, not title.

In natural history an oak-apple (also oak apple gall, oak nut) is the smooth, round, apple-like gall formed on oak leaves, buds, or twigs when the female oak-apple gall-wasp (Biorhiza pallida in Britain, Amphibolips species in America) lays eggs and injects chemistry that hijacks the tree's growth. The agamic (asexual) generation makes the familiar 2-5 cm green-to-red, spongy, juice-filled ball in spring on leaves and catkins; the sexual generation makes small clustered root-galls in winter. Cut open, the gall shows radiating fibres, a central larval cell, and a grub that will chew out in autumn. Old pharmacopoeias confused it with the true Aleppo gall (from Quercus infectoria, rich in tannin for ink and dye); the British oak-apple is poorer in tannin, spongier, and useless for ink, but children have collected, thrown, and dissected it for centuries. Country names are oak-nut, oak-ball, sham-apple, cuckoo-apple.

  • Botanical head sense: the gall itself: e.g. to find oak-apples on the pedunculate oak, the oak-apple is spongy within, wasps emerge from oak-apples in October. Always countable, often plural (oak-apples strew the path), with adjectives of size and colour — green, rosy, marble, woolly oak-apples. The diagnostic is the single central cell with one grub, distinguishing it from marble-galls (many cells) and spangle-galls (flat discs).
  • Restoration-emblem sense (source sense): oak-apple as badge: e.g. to wear an oak-apple on the 29th of May, a gilded oak-apple in the button-hole, Oak Apple Day. Parliament in 1660 made 29 May a perpetual thanksgiving for the Restoration; custom required oak-boughs on doors, oak-leaves in hats, and an oak-apple or sprig for the coat. To wear it was to declare for Church and King; to refuse (as Dissenters and later radicals did) was dissent. Ruskin's gilding is childish piety intensifying loyalism into sacrament.
  • Calendar-custom sense: Oak Apple Day (Royal Oak Day): e.g. Oak Apple Day at Upton-upon-Severn, thegrovely rights at Great Wishford, beating the bounds with oak-apples. The day survives in a handful of parishes (Fownhope, Membury, Aston-on-Clun with its Garland) where the church is decked, the bells rung, and schoolchildren given holidays. Elsewhere it died with the 1859 repeal of the observance Act, leaving only the Chelsea Pensioners' parade (Oak Apple Day is Founder's Day at the Royal Hospital).
  • Literary-moral sense: sham fruit: preachers and poets use oak-apple for fair-seeming emptiness — ambition's oak-apples, the oak-apples of preferment — because the gall looks like fruit but holds only a maggot. The trope is common in 17th-century sermons against Popery and ambition.
  • Dyer-ink confusion (obsolete technical): early herbals call any round oak-gall an oak-apple and prescribe it for ink, tanning, and medicine. Modern economic botany restricts true gall-ink to Aleppo and Chinese galls; the British oak-apple is noted only to be rejected as too poor.
  • Modern ecological sense: field guides list oak-apple under cynipid galls as a keystone microhabitat: inquilines, parasitoids, birds, and fungi all exploit it. To record oak-apples on a survey is to record oak health, wasp cycles, and parasitoid webs.

Grammatically oak-apple is a countable compound noun, plural oak-apples, with attributive use (oak-apple wasp, oak-apple gall, oak-apple day). Spelling varies historically — oak apple, oak-apple, oakapple — but Ruskin's hyphen is the 19th-century standard and is retained here exactly as required. Pronunciation is /ˈəʊkˌæpəl/ with equal stress on oak and secondary on apple. Register splits: naturalists say it plainly, historians capitalise the Day, children gild it.
