The biography of NOUMENON begins not in Germany but in the prehistoric speech of the Indo-Europeans, with the root *gneh3-, "to know, to come to know, to recognize." From this single root the family's branches grew in parallel across the Indo-European world: the Hellenic line produced gignosko (γιγνώσκω), "I come to know"; the Italic line Latin noscere, "to know," with its derivatives notion, cognition, recognize; the Indo-Iranian line Sanskrit jna-, "to know," and Hindi gyan, "knowledge"; the Germanic line Old English cnawan, our own know, and German kennen, "to know." Around this knowing-root, Greek also developed the nominal stem noos or nous (νοῦς), "mind, intellect, thought" — and from it the verb noein (νοεῖν), "to perceive with the mind, to think, to apprehend by intellectual intuition." The seed of the later philosophical term is already visible here: knowledge is treated as an act of the mind's own perceiving, not a passive reception of the senses.

There is, however, an honest caveat at this very stage. The derivation of nous from the knowing root *gneh3- is traditional but not secure. Modern authorities such as Robert Beekes class noos as of uncertain etymology, and an older rival theory connects it with the PIE root *nes-, "to return to oneself, to come to oneself," which would make the nous the "coming-to-oneself" faculty, the mind turning back upon itself. Either way the later chain is unaffected: noein "to perceive with the mind" is securely attested from the sixth century BCE onward, and the participle built on it is the direct ancestor of our word.

From noein came the present passive participle nooumenon (νοούμενον), whose neuter gender lets it function as a noun meaning "a thing being thought of" or "an object of intellectual apprehension." In classical Greek this was a modest grammatical form, but it carried philosophical weight: for Plato the highest realities — the Forms — are precisely the objects grasped by the nous, and for Aristotle the "active intellect" (nous poietikos) is the principle that thinks them. The participle named the object-side of intellection: not what is perceived, but what is conceived. Even the earlier usage of the divine Nous by the Pre-Socratics — Anaxagoras' cosmic Mind that set all things in order — belongs to the same semantic field: the nous as the organ and principle of pure thought.

The great semantic leap happened in the late eighteenth century. Immanuel Kant, writing in Königsberg, needed a word for the object as it is in itself, independent of all sensible intuition. In the first edition of the Kritik der reinen Vernunft (1781) he set the phenomenon — the thing as it appears to the senses, structured by space, time, and the categories — against the noumenon, the thing as thought, the "thing-in-itself" (Ding an sich) that reason can think but never empirically know. Kant himself distinguished two senses: the noumenon in the negative sense is simply "the thing insofar as it is not an object of our sensible intuition"; in the positive sense it would be "the object of a non-sensible, purely intellectual intuition" — an object only a divine or intuitive intellect could know. Precisely because human beings have no such intuition, the positive noumenon is for us a mere limiting concept, and the noumenon as a whole remains unknowable. By employing the Greek participle rather than a Latin calque, Kant deliberately invoked the ancient vocabulary of nous and of the intellectual intuition that the Greek philosophers had prized.

From German the word crossed into English around 1796, during the first wave of Kantian enthusiasm among British readers, and it was taken up with relish by the idealist tradition — Coleridge deployed it in his prose, and later the British idealists, Bradley and his school, built entire systems on the noumenal as ultimate reality. In the twentieth century the term migrated into literary criticism and aesthetics. It is exactly here that Empson's sentence belongs: "A poem is a noumenon rather than a phenomenon" asserts that the poem's true being is not its sensory surface — the printed letters, the heard rhythms — but the ideal, mind-constructed object the intellect alone can grasp, the meaning that is thought rather than perceived. The word thus moves from Kant's epistemology of perception to a literary epistemology of reading.

Today noumenon remains a high technical term of philosophy — Kantian scholarship, epistemology, and literary theory — with the adjective noumenal doing most of the work in contemporary prose. Its plural noumena and its pairing with phenomenon are fixed; its register is rarified, and its occasional appearance outside philosophy usually carries a quasi-mystical aura of hidden, ultimate reality. The orthography is a story of Latinization: Greek nooumenon, accented on the second syllable, was spelled Noumenon in German and English, the Greek -ou- rendered as -u-, and the English word settled on the forms noumenon and noumenal.

Summary of the eras:
  • PIE (c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE): *gneh3- "to know" — root of Greek gignosko, Latin noscere, Sanskrit jna-, English know.
  • Greek (c. 8th c. BCE onward): noos/nous "mind, intellect" (derivation from *gneh3- debated; alternative *nes-); noein "to perceive with the mind."
  • Greek participle (5th-4th c. BCE): nooumenon "a thing being thought of"; Platonic and Aristotelian use; Anaxagoras' cosmic Nous.
  • German coinage (1781): Kant's Noumenon in the Kritik der reinen Vernunft; negative and positive senses; Ding an sich.
  • English (c. 1796): noumenon enters English; British idealism (Coleridge, Bradley) adopts it.
  • 20th century: Empson and literary theory — the poem as noumenon, meaning thought rather than perceived.
