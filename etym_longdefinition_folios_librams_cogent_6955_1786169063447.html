folios: large sheets of paper folded once in half to form two leaves (four pages) of a book, or large-format books printed on such sheets; in accounting and ledger management, page or sheet numbers used for cross-referencing transactions; in botany, a leaf or the leaf-bearing structure.

librams: heavy, ornate, ancient, or highly specialized books of magic, esoteric spells, and sorcerous lore — a literary coinage by Jack Vance adapted from Latin liber, "book" (and ultimately "inner bark of a tree").

cogent: clear, logical, forceful, compelling, and irresistibly convincing; in jurisprudence, evidence or reasoning that compels intellectual agreement and leaves no room for reasonable doubt.

Section A. Comprehensive Lexical Scope of FOLIOS

The noun folios (plural of folio) operates across bibliography, printing history, accounting, and botany.

  • Bibliographical format and the book trade: A folio is a book format in which large sheets of paper are folded once down the centre, creating two leaves (four pages). Historically the folio was the grandest, largest, and most expensive format in early printing — the format of Shakespeare's First Folio (1623) and of great legal, religious, and scientific reference works.
  • Single leaf or sheet: Before the format sense, a folio was simply a leaf of paper or a sheet of parchment, especially a numbered page in a manuscript or printed book.
  • Accounting and ledger management: In bookkeeping, a folio is a page of a ledger, and "folio numbers" are reference numbers used to cross-index entries between journals and ledgers.
  • Botany: From Latin folium ("leaf"), the stem appears in foliage, defoliate, exfoliate, portfolio (a case for carrying loose leaves), and foliolate.
  • Register: In modern usage, "folio" names both the large-format book and the single printed sheet; the word keeps its leafy heritage — a book that still remembers it is a stack of vegetable leaves.

Semantic bridge: Latin folium meant "leaf" — of a plant and of paper; the printer's sheet was a leaf, folded to make a folio volume, and so the word moved from the botanical world into bibliography and accounting.

Section B. Comprehensive Lexical Scope of LIBRAMS

The noun librams (plural of libram) is a deliberate literary coinage of Jack Vance, used in The Dying Earth sequence and its Turjan stories.

  • Vancean fictional sense: A libram is a formal, ancient, leather-bound book of magic, setting forth spells and sorcerous lore — heavier, older, and more arcane than an ordinary book. Vance constructs it from Latin liber, "book."
  • Etymology in the fiction: The coinage deliberately reaches past the modern Romance words (French livre, Spanish libro) to the Latin root and its still-older sense of the inner bark of a tree — a book imagined as bark peeled and unpeeled layer by layer.
  • Non-standard status: Libram is not a standard dictionary word of English; it is an artful archaism that readers recognize for its manufactured Latin depth.

Semantic bridge: Latin liber originally named the inner bark of a tree, the surface on which the Romans wrote; the bark-word became the book-word, and Vance's coinage restores the ancient, fibrous sense of writing material.

Section C. Comprehensive Lexical Scope of COGENT

The adjective cogent operates across logic, rhetoric, jurisprudence, and everyday argument.

  • Logic and rhetoric: Clear, logical, well-reasoned, and powerfully persuasive — an argument or explanation that drives the hearer irresistibly to assent ("a cogent explanation").
  • Jurisprudence: Evidence or reasoning so compelling that it forces intellectual agreement and leaves no reasonable doubt ("cogent proof," "cogent testimony").
  • Philosophy: In formal logic, a cogent argument is a strong inductive argument with all true premises — the inductive counterpart of a valid deductive argument.
  • Everyday register: "Cogent" is a formal but not rare word for any reasoning that is both clear and convincing; it applies to reasons, arguments, evidence, explanations, and cases, and almost never to people or emotions.

Semantic bridge: Latin cogēns (present participle of cōgere, "to drive together, compel") meant literally "driving together, compelling." An argument is cogent because it drives its parts together into one whole and compels assent — the same root that gives English coerce and coaction.
