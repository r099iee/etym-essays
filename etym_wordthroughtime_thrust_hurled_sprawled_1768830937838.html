- Medieval English — "to press or push with sudden force": from its first appearance in English, thrust means a sharp, directed, concentrated push, and the past tense is already identical to the present.
- 15th–18th centuries — the weapon-word: thrust becomes the verb of swords and spears, the penetrating jab that stops dead on the -t, and of forcing oneself forward into another's place.
- 19th–21st centuries — the engine-word: thrust is the mechanical push of pistons, engine cylinders, and rockets; as a noun it also names the main drive or point of an argument, while keeping the instantaneous, timeless hardness of a lance.
hurled
- Medieval English — "to throw with force, to rush": hurl enters English as a word of violent projection, of bodies and boulders flung with overwhelming power.
- 16th–18th centuries — the catapult's verb: to hurl a spear, a stone, or a blow, and to hurl words — abuse and insults shouted with ballistic force — so that hurl becomes the throw of giants and storms.
- Contemporary — hurl is pure ballistic release: the hurled thing has no say in its own trajectory, and the word is kept distinct from hurtle, the word of forcible collision.
sprawled
- Earliest recorded English — "to move convulsively": sprawl begins as the writhing of a body out of control, limbs jerking against the will.
- 17th–19th centuries — the posture takes over: sprawled comes to mean lying or fallen with the limbs flung open, an undignified, unheroic scattering of the body.
- 20th–21st centuries — the shape of lost form: urban sprawl extends the word to anything that spreads without plan or direction, the sprawled body and the sprawled city as one.
