- Old English: wefan is a plain household craft verb — making cloth at the loom; socially low, domestic, unglamorous; already used of spiders weaving their webs.
- Middle Ages: the age of craft guilds makes 'weaver' a proud urban trade and a surname; the moral and theological metaphor grows — God and the Fates are weavers of destiny; 'woven' fate becomes a stock image.
- 16th–17th centuries: the figurative explosion — Spenser and Shakespeare weave plots, tales and spells; the loom of destiny and the spider's web of deceit are established idioms; Bottom the weaver puns on 'web' throughout A Midsummer Night's Dream.
- 18th–19th centuries: the Industrial Revolution makes weaving a factory word (Kay's flying shuttle, 1733; the power loom) — the weaver turns from artisan to operative; meanwhile 'the fabric of society' metaphor spreads through political prose.
- 19th–20th centuries: a second 'weave' (to move from side to side) surfaces in nautical and sporting English and becomes everyday for rivers and traffic; the old textile verb stays alive in craft and idiom.
- 20th–21st centuries: 'a weave' (noun) = hair extensions, from 1980s Black hair culture into the mainstream; 'the web' returns the word to digital imagery; 'weave' now runs from loom to lyric to lorry-driver.
