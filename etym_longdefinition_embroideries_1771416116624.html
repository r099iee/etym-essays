Embroideries (the plural of embroidery) denote ornamental patterns or pictures worked directly onto a fabric ground with a needle and thread — the art and craft of embellishing cloth with stitched designs. In the specific literary context from which this card is drawn (a translated scene of Dream of the Red Chamber), the word describes the sumptuous needlework adorning the garments of a great lady, which together with sparkling jewels makes her “gleaming like some fairy princess.” The embroideries are the visible signs of rank and wealth: every flower, phoenix, or cloud motif is the product of countless hours of skilled labour, precious dyed silk thread, and often gold or silver thread, and their richness is a direct index of the household’s economic and social standing. A reader who does not know that embroideries are also a form of portable wealth will miss half the sentence.

The word carries a broad field of related senses, and these should be enumerated exhaustively:

  • The concrete textile art: decorative stitching applied to cloth, whether functional (strengthening seams, binding edges) or purely ornamental (pictorial scenes, floral sprays, monograms, heraldic devices). This is the primary and oldest sense of the plural noun in English.
  • The craft and its product collectively: “embroidery” (uncountable) as a form of needlework practised in homes, workshops, nunneries, and professional ateliers; the plural embroideries collects particular finished pieces, as here.
  • Figurative embellishment of narrative: since the 16th century, “to embroider” a story or account has meant to embellish it with invented or exaggerated detail, to add decorative flourish to the truth; embroideries in this abstract sense are the fanciful additions themselves.
  • Costume and fashion terminology: in dressmaking, embroideries are the applied decorative elements (beading, sequins, appliqué, couched threads) that distinguish haute couture and ceremonial dress from plain cloth.
  • Ecclesiastical and heraldic registers: in church vestments, altar cloths, and regimental or family banners, embroideries carry liturgical or emblematic meaning: figurative scenes, coats of arms, and mottoes worked in metal thread.
  • Technical jargon in restoration and museums: conservators speak of an object’s “embroideries” as a countable set of textile components whose threads, grounds, and stitches must be documented and preserved separately.
  • Scientific / microstructure metaphor: in anatomy and biology, finely patterned surface structures (the network of vessels in a membrane, the venation of a leaf) are described with the metaphor of needlework, borrowing the craft term for intricate surface patterning.

Historically, the semantic range has widened from a single concrete activity to a family of metaphorical senses. In medieval Europe, embroidery was among the most prestigious of the decorative arts, its costliness rivalling that of goldsmiths’ work; church and crown commissioned “opus anglicanum” and other celebrated styles. The 16th-century figurative sense (“to embroider a tale”) arose naturally because the craft adds ornament over an existing surface — exactly as rhetorical embellishment adds ornament over a plain report. In the 18th and 19th centuries, embroidery became the archetypal “accomplishment” of the middle-class young lady, and the plural embroideries came to suggest samplers, household linens, and the genteel parlour arts. Today the word spans both folk craft and fine art (contemporary artists exhibit “embroideries” in galleries), and the pejorative modern use of “embroidering the truth” keeps the rhetorical sense alive.

Against near neighbours the boundaries are instructive. Brocade is a woven fabric whose raised pattern is produced on the loom, so design and ground cloth are born together; embroidery is added afterward, upon an existing fabric, by needle and thread. Tapestry names a large-scale pictorial hanging, usually woven, in which the picture fills the entire field; embroideries are typically smaller surface decorations on garments, furnishings, or small hangings. Appliqué is a form of embroidery in which cut-out fabric shapes are stitched onto a ground, but the broader term embroidery includes all free-thread stitchery. Quilting is decorative stitching that also fastens layers together, whereas embroidery is ornament alone. In sum, the core of embroideries remains: stitched patterns and pictures applied to cloth — with a rich halo of metaphorical, ceremonial, and technical senses layered over that concrete foundation.
