The verb ripened begins in the fields, not in the study. Old English rīpe meant 'mature, ready for reaping, fit for harvest,' and it belonged to a distinctly West Germanic family: Old Saxon ripi, Middle Dutch and Dutch rijp, Old High German rifi, and Modern German reif, all meaning 'ripe.' The word's core sense was agricultural readiness — grain standing yellow and full, fruit at the moment it could be gathered — and it sat side by side with the verb rīpan, 'to cut down grain,' which survives in English as reap. The older handbooks derive the Germanic adjective from an Indo-European root *rep- (or *reip-), 'to pluck, strip, tear off,' on the reasoning that the ripe thing is the thing ready to be plucked from the stem, and they adduce Greek ereptesthai 'to feed on, to tear down' and Baltic forms such as Lithuanian aprepti 'to take, touch' as distant relatives. But the connection is not absolutely certain: the modern dictionaries note that *rīpiz may be an independent West Germanic formation, and the cautious account is to say that the word is West Germanic with a plausible but not guaranteed Indo-European background. The German verb raffen 'to snatch, gather up' and the Gothic skauda-raip 'shoe-lace' (a plucked strip) belong to the same reaping family.

What happened next in English is a small grammatical event with large consequences. In Middle English the adjective ripe was fitted with the verbal suffix -en (from Old English -nian), producing ripen, 'to become ripe,' recorded from the early fourteenth century (c. 1325); the transitive sense, 'to make ripe' (as the sun ripens the wheat), is attested from the sixteenth century, and the past participle ripened could then be used either of crops and fruits literally or of anything metaphorically brought to fullness. The word's early career was overwhelmingly agricultural and natural:

  • In medieval husbandry and recipe literature ripen described the natural action of sun and season on grain, fruit, and grapes — the god-given process by which the harvest comes to readiness.
  • By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the metaphorical extension was established: men ripen into maturity, plans and intentions ripen into action, time ripens opportunity; the organic image of coming to fullness spreads across the moral and social vocabulary.
  • In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the figurative use flourished in biography and the novel, and it is precisely this ripe, organic, wholesome image that the biographer of John Stuart Mill reaches for: 'The acquaintance rapidly ripened into intimacy and the intimacy into friendship.'
  • In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word also gained technical extensions — ripening cheese, wine, and tobacco; ripening eggs and rubber; a 'ripening' of political or economic conditions — while never losing its root sense of harvest-readiness.

Modern English retains both strands in full vigour: the literal 'the fruit ripened' and the figurative 'the plan ripened,' 'a ripened talent,' 'conditions ripened.' Throughout, the word has carried a connotation of naturalness and inevitability — what ripens does so of its own nature, in its own season, without forcing — and it is precisely this connotation that the context exploits, smuggling an air of wholesome inevitability into a swift and scandal-adjacent Victorian attachment. The adjective ripe and the verb ripen continue side by side, the pair forming one of the clearest examples in English of a word whose whole history, from the PIE field of plucking to the Victorian drawing-room, can be read in a single season's growth.
