BLOTTED — evolution essay

The verb "to blot" is a late medieval newcomer to English, and its history is largely the history of the noun from which it grew. The noun "blot" first appears in Middle English in the 14th century (c. 1369, in the age of Chaucer), meaning a spot, a dark discoloration, a stain — above all the inky mark left on a page by a slip of the pen. Its origin is obscure: the leading hypothesis derives it from Old Norse blettr, "a spot, a blemish," which would make it a loan from the Scandinavian speech that so fertilized northern English after the Viking settlement of the Danelaw; but the OED and Etymonline both mark the word as of uncertain origin, and some scholars hear in "blot" an imitative or expressive creation — a word that sounds like the soft, absorbing splat of ink on paper. No Proto-Indo-European root can be securely reconstructed, so the word's known history begins not with an ancient etymon but with a medieval stain.

From the noun the verb was a natural growth. To "blot" (15th century) was to make such a mark, to smear or stain a surface; almost at once the word took on the absorbing, transitive function for which we still use it — to blot is to soak up liquid, as blotting paper drinks the excess ink from a freshly written line (hence the "blotter," 19th century). The moral metaphor was equally quick: a reputation could be "blotted," a name stained, a blot upon the honor of a family — the heraldic imagination of a coat of arms defaced by a stain. From the late medieval and early modern centuries the phrase "to blot out" hardened into its modern shape: to efface, erase, obliterate. In the language of Scripture and of the pulpit, sins and iniquities are "blotted out" (Psalm 51), names are "blotted out of the book of life," memories and faces are struck away as though wiped by a thumb. The King James Bible (1611) gave the phrase a permanence that has never faded: "blot out my transgressions," "blot out their names."

Through the early modern period the word carried both literal ink and figurative erasure. Seventeenth-century dictionaries record "to blot" as a schoolroom and scrivener's verb, and the act of "blotting out" a line with a stroke of the pen became the standard figure for deletion. It is worth noticing how the word's physical register shaped its figurative one: a blot is not a clean erasure. Unlike "erase," which suggests a deliberate rubbing away of a pencil line, "to blot out" is a messy, absorbing act — something dark and wet spreads over what it removes, and the thing removed disappears into the stain itself. This is exactly the sense exploited in the passage before us, where the sand-storm "blotted out the sun": the sun is not merely hidden but drowned, swallowed into a murky twilight "colored like dried blood."

In the 18th and 19th centuries the word continued to specialize. The noun "blot" became the fixed expression of blemished perfection: "a blot on the escutcheon" (the stain on the heraldic shield), "a blot on the landscape" (something that spoils a view), "a blot on civilization." In the Victorian schoolroom the phrase "to blot one's copybook" arose, naming the ruin of a previously clean record by a single bad mark or misdeed. Meanwhile narrower, technical survivals kept the word alive: in backgammon a "blot" is an exposed, vulnerable single piece — the oldest sense of "an unprotected mark" — and in the 1970s the word took an unexpected scientific lease of life, as "blotting" in molecular biology (Southern blot, Northern blot, Western blot) came to name the transfer of nucleic acids or proteins onto a membrane, a laboratory joke that has preserved the ink-and-paper metaphor in the vocabulary of genetics.

Through all this the perception of "blotted" has shifted from a simple spot to a total, staining obliteration. The participle today names a state of effacement: something has been covered, absorbed, swallowed — a memory, a line, a sun. Its literary life is long and distinguished, from the Psalmists through Milton to the modern novel, and its register remains more physical, messier, and darker than its rival "erased." The word that began as a medieval ink-stain has become our standard image for the disappearance of things into darkness, and in the passage it does precisely that: the zoorth rises, blots out the sun, and leaves the travelers in a twilight the color of old blood.

ERE — evolution essay

"Ere" is one of the oldest words in English, and its history is a story of retreat: from the everyday adverb of a whole people, it has dwindled to a deliberate, fossilized archaism, kept alive by the Bible, poetry, and fantasy prose. Its roots reach back to the deepest layer of the Indo-European family. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *h₂éyer- (also written *ayer-) meant "day, morning," and from it descend, in Greek, ἦρι (ēri, "early, at dawn," attested in Homer); in Indo-Iranian, Vedic Sanskrit áhar / áyar ("day") and Avestan ayar ("day"); and, in Germanic, a form *airi- meaning "early, at the time of morning." The family is ancient and widespread: it is a morning-root, and the notion "early" is literally "at daybreak."

In Proto-Germanic the root produced *airi, the adverb "early," and — critically for our word — its comparative *airiz, "earlier," used with the sense "before in time." Old English inherited this as the form ǣr, spelled with a long ǣ: it served as adverb ("previously, soon"), preposition ("before," of time), and conjunction ("before that"), and it was the plain, workaday word of daily speech — ǣr dæges, "before daybreak"; ǣr þām þe, "before that." It is among the best-attested words of Old English prose and poetry, appearing throughout Beowulf, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and the homilies. Its close relatives flourished across the whole Germanic family: Old Saxon ēr, Old High German ēr, Gothic air — all meaning "early" or "before." The positive degree of the root survives in English "early" (Old English ǣrlīce, from ǣr + -līce), and its superlative ǣrest ("first") survives in "erstwhile" ("at an earlier time, former").

In Middle English the word was simply er, still the plain word for "before," used freely in narrative, chronicle, and common speech ("er the day," "er that I go"). But across the 16th and 17th centuries it receded from the spoken language, and its modern history is a story of deliberate preservation. Two forces kept it alive. The first is Scripture: the King James Bible (1611) and its predecessors made "ere" a word of sacred prose — "ere I die," "ere ever the earth was" — and biblical style fixed it in the English ear as a word of gravity and doom. The second is poetry and its archaism: Spenser, Milton, the Romantics, and after them the Victorian poets and the whole modern tradition of fantasy and historical fiction (Tolkien above all) drew on "ere" for its slow, antique cadence. "Ere the fading of the stars," "ere long," "ere the moon wane" — the word became a stylistic costume, worn deliberately.

Its fixed phrases preserved the word in the modern period: "ere long" (before long), "ere now," "erewhile" (a while before, formerly — a fossil even in the 19th century), "erenow," and in dialect and nautical speech "ere" lingered in such forms as "afore." By the 20th century the word was essentially fossilized in ordinary speech: no one says "ere" spontaneously anymore, but every reader of poetry, the King James Bible, or high fantasy recognizes it instantly, and it continues to function as a register-marker, lending sentences a biblical and epic weight. The passage before us uses it in exactly this way — "the sun ere they reached the bottom of the ancient slope" — placing the travelers' descent in a deliberately archaic, legend-haunted time. "Ere" thus shows, in miniature, how a language's ordinary vocabulary can be retired from daily work and reborn as literature: a word of the morning that became the everyday word for "before," and is now a deliberate echo of an older, grander English.

SINISTER — evolution essay

The history of "sinister" is a study in how a spatial word becomes a moral one. Its story begins in Latin, where sinister meant simply "left, on the left side" — a word whose pre-Latin past is obscure: no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed, and de Vaan's etymological dictionary marks the origin as uncertain, possibly a substrate or loanword (some scholars have floated links to roots meaning "half" or "the weaker side," but none has won acceptance). In Latin the word was already doing double duty, for in addition to the neutral spatial sense, sinister carried secondary senses of awkwardness, clumsiness, and perversity. The Latin-speaking world, like almost all cultures until very recently, treated the left hand as the unhandy, unlucky, and impure hand: the right (dexter) was the skilled hand, the hand of greeting, oath, and precedence, and "rightness" itself became the word for correctness — compare dexterity, "skill," from dexter, "right."

The omens complicated things in an interesting way. In Roman augury the left side could be either favorable or unfavorable depending on the augur's orientation: an augur facing south had the lucky east on his left, so the left (sinistrum) could be the auspicious quarter; but the popular, everyday associations of left-handedness with clumsiness and bad luck ran the other way, and it is these popular associations, strengthened by Greek parallels (Greek σκαιός, "left," also meant "awkward, unlucky"), that shaped the word's fate. The bridge from "left" to "evil" is thus cultural rather than linguistic: because most people are right-handed, the left hand is awkward; and because the awkward is distrusted, the left became the side of ill omen, impurity, and the demonic — the side on which, in many traditions, the devil or an evil spirit stands.

English borrowed the word directly from Latin, not through French, around 1400, at first as a purely spatial heraldic and anatomical term: "sinister" named the left side of a coat of arms or the left hand. The moral sense was not far behind. Within the 15th century "sinister" begins to appear meaning "unlucky, inauspicious, evil-omened," and by the 16th–17th centuries the modern meaning — "menacing, threatening evil, suggestive of harm" — is fully established. Two institutions cemented the moral turn. The first is heraldry: the bend sinister, the diagonal band drawn across the shield from the bearer's upper right to lower left, was used to mark illegitimate descent, the badge of the base-born child — a spatial mark made shameful. The second is the literary language: in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama "sinister" is already a word of villainy and dark design, used of plots, looks, and intentions. By the 18th and 19th centuries the physical "left" sense had vanished from ordinary use entirely, surviving only in specialized registers — heraldry, anatomy ("sinistral," left-handed; "sinistrorse," spiraling left), and the coinages of science (sinistrodextral, "left-to-right") — while the everyday word is purely moral: a sinister smile, a sinister motive, a sinister shadow, a sinister plan.

Modern usage has extended the word from outright evil to mere suggestiveness: "sinister" no longer requires actual wrongdoing, only the impression of it — a wrongness in appearance, atmosphere, or implication, which is exactly the register of the passage, where a twilight "colored like dried blood" and a shadowed cave are charged with a sinister quality without any overt evil action. The word thus shows a complete arc from a neutral spatial term ("the left hand") through a superstitious taboo (the unlucky left) to a fully abstract moral adjective (menace, wrongness), retaining beneath it, in the technical vocabularies of heraldry and medicine, the fossil of its original meaning: the left side, still labeled with the old word for ill omen.
