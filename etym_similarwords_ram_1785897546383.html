• Battering ram: Similar because it is the same siege engine and literally the same word; differs because the full term names specifically the heavy beam swung in a frame against gates and walls, while bare "ram" ranges over the animal, the verb, and all the mechanical senses.
  • Tup: Similar because it is also an uncastrated breeding male sheep; differs because "tup" is a regional British word (chiefly northern and Scottish dialects) for the breeding ram, used mostly in farming and livestock contexts, while "ram" is the standard, all-purpose term.
  • Ewe: Similar because it is an adult sheep of the same species; differs because a ewe is female, kept for milk and lambing, whereas a ram is male, bred for stud and associated with force and aggression.
  • Wether: Similar because it is a male sheep; differs because a wether is a castrated male, kept for wool and meat rather than breeding — the docile counterpart of the aggressive ram.
  • Lamb: Similar because it is a sheep of the same flock; differs because a lamb is the young animal of either sex, whereas "ram" denotes the mature, uncastrated male.
  • Billy goat: Similar because it is a horned, aggressive male animal that butts; differs because a billy goat is a goat, not a sheep, and its headstrong, rank behaviour belongs to a comic rather than a majestic register.
  • Aries / the Ram (zodiac): Similar because the sign and constellation literally are the Ram; differs because Aries is the proper astrological and astronomical name, while "the Ram" is the English gloss of the same symbol, used outside specialist horoscope writing.
  • Piston: Similar because it is a machine part that delivers strong, repeated force; differs because a piston moves back and forth inside a closed cylinder as part of an engine, while a ram is the moving shaft or the whole impact-delivering device, and may be driven hydraulically, pneumatically, or by weight alone.
  • Hydraulic ram: Similar because it shares the word and the job of exerting huge force; differs because the full name denotes a specific pump or press that uses fluid pressure (including Montgolfier's self-acting water pump), while "ram" alone covers the animal, the verb, and every mechanical sense.
  • Pile-driver: Similar because it is a heavy machine delivering repeated massive impacts; differs because a pile-driver lifts and drops a weight to sink piles, whereas a ram presses, punches, or rams by direct drive rather than by dropping a weight.
  • Shove: Similar because it is a push; differs because a shove is informal, human, and moderate, while to ram implies great speed, weight, and impact — the shove of a machine, not of a shoulder.
  • Butt: Similar because a ram butts with its head and one can butt like a ram; differs because "butt" denotes the head-strike of animals in general (goats, oxen, deer) and playful human contact, whereas "ram" as a verb stresses driving force into or against something until it gives way.
  • Ramrod: Similar because it is the tool that literally rams, once used to pack gunpowder down a barrel; differs because "ramrod" names a specific rod and, figuratively, a rigid unbending person (stiff as a ramrod), while "ram" itself never lost its animal or verb senses.
  • Collide / crash into: Similar because to ram is a kind of collision; differs because "crash" and "collide" describe accidental or mutual meeting with impact, whereas "to ram" implies a deliberate driving of one thing into another.
