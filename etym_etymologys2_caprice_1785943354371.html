Single chain for CAPRICE (reaching English via Italian and French):
  • Reconstructed Proto-Indo-European *kapro- — "he-goat" (traditional reconstruction; c. 4500–3000 BCE).
  • Latin caper (masc.) "he-goat" and capra (fem.) "she-goat" (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE).
  • Italian capra "goat" (or, in the rival analysis, capo "head" + riccio "hedgehog, curly") + suffix -iccio → capriccio "a sudden start, shiver, shudder, whim" (16th c.).
  • French caprice — "caprice, whim" (17th c.), a loan from Italian.
  • English CAPRICE — "a sudden, unaccountable turn of mind" (borrowed c. 1650s–1660s; bold target).
Notes on nuance and debate: the etymology of Italian capriccio is genuinely contested. The older sources favour capo "head" + riccio "hedgehog" — hair standing on end in fright — while many modern dictionaries prefer derivation from capra "goat," with the goat's leaping as the image of a sudden start; both analyses are attested in early commentary and neither is finally settled. Behind Latin caper/capra lies the reconstructed PIE *kapro-, but authorities such as de Vaan note that the goat-word has no secure Indo-European etymology and may be a loan from a Mediterranean substrate; the connection to Greek kápros "boar" is sometimes added. The musical form keeps the Italian spelling capriccio; the English noun uses the French-derived spelling caprice.
