CHAIN for WARGS: 1. PIE *wergʰ- "to press, oppress, choke, strangle" (a root of violence and weight; distinct from PIE *werg- "to work", an unrelated homophone-root that gives English work) — reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE. 2. Proto-Germanic *wargaz "strangler; criminal, outlaw" (the man condemned to death by strangling or hanging, then any accursed outcast of human law) — reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–100 CE. 3. Old Norse vargr "outlaw, evildoer"; by famous taboo-euphemism also "wolf" (the feared name of the wolf being avoided, and the plain Norse word úlfr displaced in everyday use) — attested from the Viking Age, 9th c. onward; the euphemism survives in modern Swedish varg "wolf". 4. Modern English Warg "the evil wolf of Middle-earth" — J. R. R. Tolkien's revival and adaptation of the Old Norse word in The Hobbit (1937), and The Lord of the Rings (1954–55). 5. WARGS (plural; the modern card word). NOTE ON THE DEBATED / PARALLEL STAGES: the Old English branch, wearg "criminal, felon" (surviving dialectally as wari/weri "wretch"), is a West Germanic sibling of Old Norse vargr, not its ancestor; Tolkien drew on the Norse form and its wolf sense. The gaming respelling worg (Dungeons & Dragons, mid-1970s) is a separate re-borrowing of the word, not a stage of this chain. Warg is NOT a cognate of wolf: PIE *wergʰ- and PIE *wḷkʷos "wolf" are entirely distinct roots — the taboo substitution of *wargaz for *wulfaz is a semantic event, not an etymological one. Latin urgēre "to press, urge" and Lithuanian var̃gas "need, distress" are cognate reflexes of the same root; no secure Indo-Iranian reflex is established and none is invented.
