1. Indo-European epistemological roots and the Germanic causative shift. The technical literary noun kenning (plural kennings) descends from the deeply rooted Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ǵneh₃-, which designated the fundamental human cognitive act of knowing, recognizing, and perceiving. This root produced some of the most philosophically and scientifically vital vocabulary across all Indo-European daughter families:

  • Indo-Aryan: Vedic Sanskrit jñā- (ज्ञा, "to know"), yielding jñāna (ज्ञान, "knowledge, wisdom, gnosis") and prajñā (प्रज्ञा, "higher insight, discernment").
  • Hellenic: Greek gignṓskein (γιγνώσκειν, "to recognize, know"), yielding gnôsis (γνῶσις, "knowledge") and gnṓmōn ("judge, indicator"), source of English gnostic, agnostic, and diagnosis.
  • Italic: Latin (g)nōscere ("to know, learn") and nōbilis ("knowable, well-known, noble"), producing English know, noble, notion, cognition, and recognize.
  • Germanic: In accordance with Grimm's Law, the voiceless palatovelar stop *ǵ shifted to the voiceless velar stop *k, producing the Proto-Germanic preterite-present verbal base *kunnaną ("to know, understand, be mentally capable of"), which yielded Old English cunnan (source of Modern English can, cunning, and con) and cnāwan (source of Modern English know).

From this basic cognitive root, Proto-Germanic developed a specialized causative-factitive weak verb: *kannijaną, literally meaning "to make known, cause someone to know, declare, proclaim, or recognize." In Old Norse, through regular North Germanic phonological developments (including the loss of the infinitive nasal suffix and i-umlaut of the root vowel *a to *e caused by the *j* in the following syllable), *kannijaną emerged as the vital verb kenna.

2. Semantic specialization in Old Norse skaldic culture. In everyday Old Norse, kenna meant "to perceive, recognize, feel, or know." However, within the highly aristocratic and ritualized context of early medieval Scandinavian court poetry (the skaldic tradition), kenna acquired a crucial specialized grammatical and aesthetic meaning: "to designate, characterize, or name an object by an epithet or circumlocution." To attach a descriptive marker to an entity was to "mark it for recognition" (kenna við).

By appending the productive Germanic deverbal feminine substantive suffix -ing (Old Norse -ing / -ung, which forms abstract nouns of action or process), the skaldic tradition created the technical noun kenning (plural kenningar). Originally meaning simply "a recognition, a sign, a perception," the word crystallized into the formal designation for a metaphorical circumlocution composed of a base-word (grundorð) and a determinant (kenniorð). When a skald referred to a warrior as an "uprooter of the wave-steed" or blood as the "dew of the raven's beak," he was deploying a kenning—a deliberate puzzle requiring the listener to utilize their cognitive "ken" to identify the true referent.

3. Medieval codification by Snorri Sturluson. In the early 13th century (c. 1220–1225 CE), as Christianity became thoroughly established in Iceland and the memory of ancient pagan mythology began to fade, the prominent scholar and chieftain Snorri Sturluson realized that subsequent generations of poets would no longer understand the complex mythological allusions underlying the classic verses of ancient skalds like Egill Skallagrímsson or Bragi Boddason. In response, Snorri composed the Prose Edda, dedicating its second major book, the Skáldskaparmál, to an exhaustive classification of poetic diction. Snorri defined the kenning as an art form wherein an object is called by an alien name accompanied by a descriptive identifier, cementing the word forever as a formal category of literary poetics.

4. English dialect survival and 19th-century academic revival. While Old English had inherited the sister cognate verb cennan ("to declare, acknowledge") and the noun cenning, Anglo-Saxon scribes never used their native word to denote the poetic compounds of Beowulf; they simply referred to poetry as leoð-cræft ("song-craft") or scop-cræft. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, Latin and French rhetorical terms (such as metaphor, allegory, and periphrasis) dominated English courtly education, while the native Germanic root survived in everyday Northern English and Lowland Scots dialect speech as the verb ken ("D'ye ken?", meaning "Do you know?").

It was not until the late 19th-century Victorian philological renaissance that the noun kenning entered standard English. Translators and comparative linguists—most notably Sir George Webbe Dasent in his translations of the Prose Edda (1842) and Icelandic sagas, followed by scholars of Old English editing Beowulf—adopted the Old Norse word directly into English literary criticism. They recognized that Latin terms like "metaphor" failed to capture the unique, formulaic, riddling nature of Germanic compounds. Throughout the 20th century, the term broadened beyond academic philology into creative writing, celebrated by modernist poets and fantasy writers as an elemental pillar of poetic defamiliarization.
