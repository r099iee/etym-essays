- Old English stēap "high, lofty, towering" — first a word of height, not slope; a stēap hill is a high hill.
- Middle English stepe — the sense shifts from "tall" to "rising sharply, precipitous" — the angle, not the height, becomes the core meaning; "steeple" (a tall spire) splits off as the height-word.
- 16th–17th c. — steep solidifies as "sharply inclined": steep hills, steep paths, steep cliffs; the compound steep-walled begins to appear for vertical-sided valleys and bays.
- 17th–19th c. — steep-walled enters geology, geography, and travel writing for canyons, gorges, coves, and bays whose sides rise almost vertically.
- 19th–20th c. — figurative steep: "a steep price", "that's a bit steep!" = exorbitant, unreasonable — a 19th-century slang extension; the walled compound stays purely topographical.
- 21st c. — steep-walled is a standard descriptive compound of landscape and architecture — a steep-walled bay, a steep-walled canyon, a steep-walled city — always evoking near-vertical enclosure.
