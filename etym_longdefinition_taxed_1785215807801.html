TAXED is the past tense and past participle of the verb tax, and the word in this card carries a distinct, archaic-literal sense: it does not mean "levied a tax" but "accused, confronted, or challenged — charged with a fault or duty." In the sentence "His father openly taxed him with being in love with another man's wife," the verb describes James Mill's stern, public confrontation of his son John Stuart Mill over his attachment to Harriet Taylor: the father pressed the charge home face to face, treating the fault like a debt or a duty owed.

Primary sense: to impose a levy or burden

In its dominant modern sense, to tax is to impose a charge or levy on persons, property, income, or goods — to require payment to a state or authority: "to tax income," "to tax imports," "heavily taxed." This is the sense anchored in the noun tax, "a compulsory contribution to state revenue," and it is the sense that most speakers meet daily. The verb is transitive and may take a person ("the state taxes its citizens") or a thing ("the state taxes wealth").

Secondary sense: to charge with a fault (archaic-literal)

Derived by metaphor from the assessing of money is the moral sense: to tax a person with a fault is to charge them with it, to press an accusation home to their face, treating the fault as if it were a debt owed and collectable. The construction is almost always with the preposition with: "taxed him with X," "tax someone with neglect, ingratitude, or lying." The register is formal and literary; in modern everyday English the construction is old-fashioned, surviving mainly in historical narrative and rhetorical prose — which is exactly why it appears naturally in the biography of the Mills.

Tertiary sense: to strain or burden

A third, purely figurative sense extends the fiscal image to demands on strength or resources: "the long journey taxed his strength," "the crisis taxed her patience." Here the word means "to put a heavy demand on, to strain to the limit," and it is fully current in ordinary speech, particularly in the passive: "the audience's attention was taxed by the long speech."

Polysemy and the historical bridge

All three senses are joined by a single Latin ancestor. The verb comes, through Old French taxer, from Latin taxāre, the frequentative of tangere, "to touch": literally "to touch repeatedly," and so "to appraise, assess, fix the value of." The assessment of money (the fiscal sense) and the assessment of a person's conduct (the accusatory sense) are the same gesture of appraisal aimed at two different objects; the straining sense is the fiscal image extended once more, loading a demand onto a person or resource. The sixteenth-century leap from "assess a levy" to "charge with a fault" is the key to the word's double life.

Register, grammar, and usage

The verb is weak and regular: tax, taxed, taxed. The accusatory construction "to tax someone with something" is marked formal and literary, and the object may be a person or an abstract matter ("taxed with ingratitude"). The family includes taxation, taxable, taxpayer, and the noun tax. In the Mill passage the word is used in its archaic-literal sense with the full force of public moral confrontation: the adverb "openly" signals that no discretion or tact was used — the accusation was delivered frankly and before others.
