WORMED is the past tense and past participle of the verb worm, which in its modern dominant sense means to extract information or a secret from someone by gradual, persistent, and often devious means - typically in the phrase 'worm something out of someone'. To worm a fact out of a reluctant witness is to obtain it not by direct questioning but by slow, patient, sideways pressure: flattery, feigned weakness, casual questions, and endless persistence, until the guarded information slips out. The verb is built on a vivid animal metaphor: the worm moves blindly, underground, and unstoppably; it does not confront its obstacle but burrows around and through it. The past participle wormed therefore describes a successful act of stealthy extraction - information that has been 'wormed out'.

In the supplied literary context - 'Having just [wormed] out the information that a motion for reinstatement had been approved, he had been dashing about ever since' - the word performs this extraction sense with full force. The character has obtained, by secret and persistent means, a piece of guarded bureaucratic intelligence, and the word 'wormed' tells us that it was not given freely but pried loose. The contrast between the slow, subterranean worming and the subsequent frantic dashing is part of the sentence's charm: the tedious work of extraction is done, and now the character is energised by the prize.

The full polysemy of the verb is best surveyed sense by sense.

  • Figurative extraction sense (modern dominant) - To get a secret, truth, or confession out of someone by gradual, persistent, devious probing: 'to worm a secret out of a witness'.
  • Figurative insinuation sense - To work one's way gradually and stealthily into a position, a group, or a person's favour: 'to worm one's way into confidence' or 'into the inner circle'.
  • Literal physical sense - To move, crawl, or wriggle like a worm; to creep slowly and sinuously through narrow spaces. Used especially of snakes, caterpillars, and people moving covertly along the ground.
  • Veterinary / zoological sense - To treat an animal for intestinal worms (to 'worm a dog' is to give it a vermifuge); the past participle wormed describes a dewormed animal.
  • Nautical sense - To worm a rope is to wind small cord spirally between its strands (in the old phrase 'to worm, parcel, and serve'), protecting it from wear.
  • Mechanical / rare sense - To twist or coil into a spiral like a worm-thread, or to wind a thread around a screw (the ancestor of the noun 'worm' in worm gear).

Boundaries: wormed is distinguished from its near-synonyms by combining slow, bodily persistence with deviousness and a note of the unheroic. To extricate something is to free it skilfully and often heroically from a tangle; to worm something out is to obtain it slyly, by infiltration, without ever confronting the obstacle head-on. The word always carries a faint moral shadow - the means are low, subterranean, even parasitic - and this shadow is exactly what the supplied sentence exploits when it describes information that had to be wormed rather than asked for.

Register and idiom: outside the fixed phrases 'worm out' and 'worm one's way into', the verb is now literary rather than everyday; it sounds slightly old-fashioned, with a vivid, almost grotesque physicality. Its nominal relatives - the worm itself, wormy, dewormed, and the computer 'worm' (a self-spreading malicious program, so called because it burrows through networks) - keep the metaphor alive in contemporary speech, so that even modern listeners feel the ancient image of the creeping, burrowing, unstoppable creature behind the verb.

The full history of the verb's senses shows a gradual slide from the physical to the social. When first used of humans, worm described literal crawling - moving on hands and knees, creeping along the ground. From crawling came the idea of working one's way slowly into a restricted space, and from physical space the metaphor jumped to social space: to worm oneself into a household, a court, or a monarch's confidence, a favourite image of Renaissance and Restoration satire. Only then did the modern sense attach to information itself - not working one's way in, but drawing a secret out - completing the transfer of the worm's motion from the body to the tongue and the ear. Used in the past tense, wormed packs the whole process into a single finished fact: the patience, the deviousness, the slippery approach, and the obtained prize are all present at once. It is a small word with an enormous and ancient image inside it, and its use in the supplied sentence is a masterstroke of compression.
