Frankish / Germanic (c. 5th–8th c.)
*but
"stump, end, blunt tip"
↓
Old French (12th c.)
but / bute
"end, goal, target, mound of earth"
↓
Middle French (14th–16th c.)
butte
"small mound, hillock behind archery target"
↓
North American French (Voyageurs) (18th c.)
butte
"isolated steep-sided hill on western plains"
↓
American English (early 19th c., c. 1805)
butte
"isolated flat-topped hill with steep sides"

Note: Derived from Old French butte ('mound, target-bank'), itself rooted in Germanic *but ('stump, blunt end') or related to Old French bouter ('to push, strike'). French-Canadian fur trappers and voyageurs applied the word to western American landforms. Meriwether Lewis recorded it in 1805, and Washington Irving popularized it in Astoria (1836).
