- Middle English verb - "Tinken" (14th c.) imitates a sharp metallic ring; it soon gains the frequentative "-le" to become "tinklen", a smaller, lighter, repeated ringing.
- Sound-sense established - "Tinkle" spreads across bells, glasses, ice and coins; the noun "tinkle" (the sound itself) is attested by the 17th century.
- A mocking register - The King James Bible's "tinkling cymbal" (1611) makes the word carry a hint of hollow showiness — bright but empty.
- Gentle euphemism - In the early 20th century "to tinkle" becomes a polite, childish word for urinating, borrowing the very sound.
- Two-way split - Modern English runs both senses in parallel: a delicate ringing sound in literary prose, and an informal bodily euphemism in speech.
- In Tolkien - "A glint and a tinkle" (1937) shows the pure sound-sense in action: small gold striking gold in the dark, bright and musical.
