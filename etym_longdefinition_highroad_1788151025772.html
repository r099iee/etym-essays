highroad (noun) — /ˈhaɪrəʊd/. (1) A main public road; a first-class thoroughfare for long-distance travel. (2) Figuratively, the principal, most direct or most reliable course to a result — the sense Will Durant reaches for when he says a nation lies "on the highroad of the world’s trade": that is, directly on the main artery along which the world’s commerce moves, so that geography itself showers it with opportunity. (3) In modern idiom, "the high road" is the morally superior, principled course of conduct, the opposite of the low road of expedients and personal attacks.

I. The literal sense — the chief public way

The highroad is the road of first rank in a district or kingdom: broad, well-kept, built for carts, coaches and heavy traffic, linking the great towns with one another. It is distinguished in the old road-hierarchy from the lane, the byway, the bridle-path and the mere track, and in Britain it was historically the king’s highway, maintained in principle by the public and open to all lawful travellers. English literature before the railway age is full of the highroad as the emblem of movement and connection: the stagecoach rumbling along the Great North Road, the packhorse train on the drove-road, the pedlar and the post-boy on the mail route. In American English the same object is normally called the highway, while highroad retains its currency chiefly in Britain and in older prose; the two words are etymologically near twins (high + way versus high + road).

II. The figurative sense — the main course

From "main road" the word passes naturally and early into metaphor. The highroad to a goal is the surest, most straightforward, often the most honourable channel to it: the highroad to success, to wealth, to ruin, to peace. The metaphor depends on the geography of roads: goods, armies, news and ideas all travel fastest on the great routes, so that to be on the highroad is to be where decisive events move. This is precisely Durant’s meaning — nations like Athens, Carthage, Florence and Venice flourished partly because they stood on the trade-routes of their age; geography, "though it can never create" civilization, "smiles upon" those who lie on the highroad of the world’s commerce.

III. The moral sense — taking the high road

  • The spatial opposition high/low has carried moral weight in English since the Middle Ages: to be "high" is to be elevated, exalted, lawful, virtuous; to be "low" is to be base, crooked, degraded. The high road, as the public and lawful way, picked up the same charge: thieves, it was said, preferred the dark byways, while honest men travelled the open road.
  • Modern political and diplomatic idiom has consolidated this: to take the high road is to campaign or act on substance, decency and magnanimity rather than savaging one’s opponent; to take the low road is the disreputable alternative. In this idiom the word’s literal, roadlike character has almost vanished — "high road" now functions as a synonym for "the principled option."

IV. Secondary extensions and technical uses

  • Trade and economics. "The highroad of trade" is a fixed collocation in 19th- and 20th-century prose for the principal channel of commercial activity; "highroad to prosperity" for the surest route to economic success. The phrase survives in journalism, usually with a slightly literary sheen.
  • Strategy and geography. Strategists speak of holding the high road or the high ground: the elevated line of advance that commands a region. The image is military — the heights dominate the valleys — and its metaphorical twins are "commanding position" and "the upper hand."
  • Transport history (technical). In civil engineering and historical geography, the highroad is any road of the first order in the medieval-to-modern system, as opposed to the secondary and tertiary roads; modern equivalents are the trunk road, the arterial road and the motorway or freeway. The science of road classification, from the Roman viae publicae to the modern hierarchy, is essentially the history of deciding which routes deserve to be called high.
  • Railway and later extensions. The pattern proved so productive that the railway age borrowed it: the main line of a railway was "the high road of iron," and the information age produced the "information superhighway." In each case the linguistic move is the same — extend the name of the principal physical route to the principal channel of any activity.
  • Syntax. The word is countable, but in figurative use it nearly always occurs in the singular with the definite article: on the highroad of trade, the highroad to ruin. The plural (highroads) is rare and always literal.

V. Why the word fits the sentence

Durant’s choice of highroad over highway, route or trade-route is deliberate: the highroad is not merely a route but the route, the one on which the world’s business flows, with all the old dignity of the king’s highway gathering around the phrase. The word therefore does double duty — factual geography and moral elevation — which is exactly why the sentence feels both concrete and grand. It also keys the whole passage’s governing metaphor, in which geography is a smiling patron who "nourishes" civilization as a road nourishes a migrating trade.
