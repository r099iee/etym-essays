Meddling is the present participle and verbal noun of to meddle: interfering in other people's business, handling things one has no right or invitation to handle, busying oneself where one is neither wanted nor competent. In the source sentence Saruman takes ill any meddling, which tells the reader as much about him as about the meddlers — pride that treats every outside touch as trespass.

The word is wholly pejorative in modern use. One never meddles helpfully; helpers assist, advise, or intervene, while meddlers intrude. The choice of meddling over neutral involvement already convicts the actor: it declares the interference unwelcome, unskilled, or self-serving before any evidence is heard.

Core meaning in detail: To meddle is to mix oneself into affairs that are not one's own — a neighbour's quarrel, another wizard's counsels, the ordering of a realm. The gerund meddling names both the habit (he is given to meddling) and the instance (he takes ill any meddling). Characteristic companions are adjectives of resentment: unwelcome meddling, officious meddling, foreign meddling, and the political colossus of the modern age, election meddling.

From mixing to interfering — the bridge: The ancestor of meddle meant simply to mix or mingle, from Latin miscere, to mix. How did mixing become intrusion. Through the kitchen and the battlefield: to mix oneself into a dish is to stir it, to mix oneself into a quarrel is to stir that too, and uninvited stirring spoils both. By the fourteenth century the physical sense of blending had faded and the social sense of intrusive blending dominated, leaving the modern word where every touch is a trespass.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Unwelcome interference (primary modern sense): prying, intruding, managing others without mandate. The bridge from mixing is social chemistry: an uninvited ingredient ruins the mixture, so the mixer is blamed.
  • Tampering with objects or systems: to meddle with the controls, the evidence, the thermostat — unauthorised handling that risks harm. The bridge keeps the hand in the mixture: fingers in machinery stir trouble as surely as fingers in soup.
  • Political or electoral interference (dominant public sense today): covert action by outsiders in elections or government, as in charges of foreign meddling. The bridge scales the neighbour's intrusion to the nation: the ballot box as a household whose affairs are its own.
  • Busy officiousness (character sense): the meddling neighbour, the meddling mother-in-law of proverb. The bridge is habitual aspect: meddling as a standing disposition rather than a single act, the person who cannot leave any pot unstirred.
  • To have to do with, to engage in combat (archaic, 14th–17th c.): knights meddled in battle, meaning mingled or contended there, and lovers meddled together. The bridge preserves the old neutral mixing before pejoration drained it, precious evidence of the word before it soured.
  • Trifling or playing amorously (obsolete): to meddle with a maid once meant to dally. The bridge runs through mingling bodies rather than mingling business, a euphemism the language has since abandoned.
  • Medication confusion (folk overlap, not true sense): speakers sometimes hear meddle inside medicine and medication, but the resemblance is accident: medicine comes from Latin mederi, to heal, while meddle comes from miscere, to mix. The false bridge is worth naming precisely to refuse it.

Register and connotation: meddling is the resentful word of the powerful and the private alike: Saruman resents it from pride, householders from love of quiet, nations from love of sovereignty. It flatters the speaker's boundary while blackening the intruder, which is why accusations of meddling so often reveal the accuser — here, a wizard whose deep knowledge has curdled into ownership, for whom counsel itself has become trespass.
