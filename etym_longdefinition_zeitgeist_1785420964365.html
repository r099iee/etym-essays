ZEITGEIST is a German loanword meaning literally "spirit of the time": the defining spirit, mood, intellectual climate, or cultural temper of a particular historical period. It is a compound of Zeit, "time," and Geist, "spirit," and it names the idea that each epoch has a distinctive collective mindset that shapes its art, thought, taste, and politics — a current larger than any single individual, which individuals may voice but never fully control. In the passage from William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity, the Zeitgeist "in general" — with T. S. Eliot's criticism "in particular" — was "calling for a reconsideration of the claims of the nineteenth-century poets": the phrase pictures an impersonal, epochal momentum pushing Victorian romanticism aside in favour of the newly recovered merits of Donne, Marvell, and Dryden. The nuance is of a force of history itself, not of any single critic; Eliot gives voice to the age, but the age is the agent. The senses and uses in full:
  • Core sense. The prevailing intellectual, moral, and cultural climate of a period: the spirit of the age, the temper of the times. Used typically with the definite article: the zeitgeist, the spirit of the age.
  • Historiographical and philosophical sense. In historicism and the philosophy of history, the Zeitgeist is the determining spirit of an era that shapes its institutions and ideas; Herder, Hegel, and the nineteenth-century historical school developed the concept, and Ranke's historicism asked how each age was to be understood in the light of its own Zeitgeist.
  • Literary-critical sense. In criticism, the Zeitgeist is the dominant taste and set of assumptions of an age — the current that "calls for" a revaluation of older poets, as in Empson's sentence.
  • Cultural and journalistic sense. In modern journalism, media, and marketing, the word names the prevailing mood or fashion of a moment, often diluted toward "current trend" or "vibe": capturing the zeitgeist, in the zeitgeist.
  • Related concepts. Weltgeist (world-spirit), Volksgeist (spirit of a people or nation, associated with Herder and German Romantic nationalism), and Geistesgeschichte (intellectual history) form a family of German spirit-compounds.
  • Derived and playful forms. Zeitgeisty (adjective, informal, "of the moment"), and the anglicized use of the word as a semi-naturalized loanword, at first printed in italics and later without.
Distinctive features: ZEITGEIST is one of the few German words fully naturalized into English — usually printed without italics in modern usage — and it preserves German capitalization, which English reserves for proper nouns. Its singular strength is that it names a concept in a single word that English can otherwise only approximate with the phrase "spirit of the age" or "climate of opinion." Its weakness is the same coin's other face: in loose modern usage it can mean little more than a fashionable trend, losing the philosophical depth that Herder and Hegel gave it. Register runs from the august and epochal in philosophy and criticism to the breezy in lifestyle journalism.
