Mingling is the present participle of mingle, a Middle English frequentative of meng to mix, from Old English mengan and Proto-Germanic *mangijaną, ultimately plausibly from PIE *menk- to knead, press, work dough. Its history is one of softening: from laborious kneading to gentle blending to sociable circulating.

PIE *menk- meant to knead, crush, press, seen in Old Church Slavonic mąka flour, Lithuanian minkyti to knead, and possibly Greek massein to knead. The semantic core is working a mass with hands to combine. Proto-Germanic *mangijaną meant to knead together, to mix, with causative or iterative force. Old English mengan, gemengan meant to mix, combine, confuse, mingle, used for dough, peoples, sounds and affairs. Beowulf and chronicles use it for mingling of blood, battle and fate.

Middle English is decisive. Old English mengan developed a frequentative minglen / menglen with -l- implying repeated small mixings, parallel to wrest ~ wrestle. By c. 1400 minglen replaces mengen in most dialects, with vowel shift e > i before ng. Early senses: to mix ingredients, to blend wines, to mingle tears with laughter, to mingle among company. The participle mingling appears early for ongoing blending, as in Chaucer and Lydgate for mingled joys and sorrows.

  • Phonetic evolution: PIE *menk- > PG *mang- by regular vowel gradation e > a in Germanic; OE mengan with front vowel by i-mutation from *-ij-; ME frequentative suffix -l- gives minglen; Great Vowel Shift and loss of final -en give modern mingle; -ing participle regular.
  • Culinary to social bridge (14th–16th c.): From mixing foods and medicines, as in the source context mingling new plants with familiar flesh and fish, to mixing people: to mingle with guests, to mingle bloodlines. The source preserves the older alimentary sense.
  • Moral and emotional mingling (16th–18th c.): Shakespeare, Milton and the King James Bible use mingle for mingled hopes, mingled yells, mingled perfumes. Connotation becomes aesthetic: complexity, richness, bittersweetness.
  • Modern sociability (19th–present): To mingle at a party means to circulate, chat lightly, blend into company. Singles mixers, art openings and conference coffee breaks institutionalize mingling as skilled sociability. Gerund mingling also denotes cultural mixing: mingling of races, traditions, genres, celebrated or feared depending on ideology.
  • Technical extensions: Chemistry and physics speak of mingling currents, mingling streams; computing of mingling data; music of mingling styles. All retain gradual, incomplete blending rather than total fusion.

Disputed points are minor. The PIE attribution to *menk- is accepted by many but marked uncertain in some handbooks because Germanic *mang- could be an independent expressive formation; Kluge and OED note the frequentative origin as clear but the deeper root as probable rather than proven. The semantic development from kneading to socializing is, however, fully documented in dated quotations and uncontroversial. Mingling as mingle + -ing adds only aspectual meaning of ongoing action.
