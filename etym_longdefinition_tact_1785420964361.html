TACT is an uncountable noun meaning adroitness and sensitivity in dealing with others or with difficult issues; social discretion; the delicate intuitive skill of saying and doing the right thing so as to avoid giving offence while still managing the situation. In the passage above, William Empson writes that "an honest man erected the ignoring of 'TACT' into a point of honour" — the scare-quotes distance the narrator from the convention, and the nuance is adversarial and intellectual: tact is presented as the polite self-censorship, diplomatic reserve, or social gloss that the honest critic flouts in order to speak unpalatable truths. Empson is defending plain-spoken scholarship against a code of gentlemanly reticence, so the word's tone here is deliberately challenged rather than endorsed: it stands for prudence that can shade into cowardice, the smoothness that prefers harmony to truth. The word's history is a famous semantic migration from the physical to the social. Tact derives from Latin tactus, "touch," the past participle of tangere, "to touch," and its original English sense, recorded from the seventeenth century, was the sense of touch itself — the physical faculty of feeling. In the eighteenth century, at the height of the culture of politeness, the word shifted ground: just as the fingers can read the texture of a surface, so the discerning mind was said to have a "touch" for nuance in aesthetics, morality, and conversation, and tact came to mean the mental delicacy by which one grasps what is fitting, what should be said, and how to say it. The whole modern word is that metaphor made noun: tact is a "touch" applied to people and situations. Polysemy and allied senses:
  • Core sense: intuitive adroitness in dealing with others, saying and doing the right thing to avoid offence and preserve harmony. Bridge: the Enlightenment metaphor of the mind's "touch" for the fitting.
  • Historical/physical sense: the sense of touch itself (now archaic except in technical or poetic use). Bridge: the original Latin meaning of tactus preserved in the borrowed word.
  • Criticised/contested use (this card): tact as polite self-censorship, diplomatic reserve, the social gloss the plain-speaking critic ignores. Bridge: the negative valuation of tact as prudence shading into cowardice.
  • In diplomacy and business: the practical art of handling sensitive negotiations with discretion. Bridge: extension of the social "touch" to institutional and professional settings.
  • Privative tactless: lacking the "touch," blunt, offensive without meaning to be. Bridge: the negative of the same faculty.
Register ranges from neutral to formal, and the word belongs to the vocabulary of manners, education, business, diplomacy, and criticism. It is uncountable and typically collocates with show, have, display, exercise, lack, and with modifiers such as diplomatic, considerable, great, or no; fixed phrases include with tact, a matter of tact, tact and diplomacy, and tactless for its absence. The word is uniquely grounded in feeling for the other person's sensibilities — a "touch" in the social sense that its near-synonyms lack. Diplomacy is the strategic, often institutional art of negotiation between parties; discretion stresses secrecy and the prudent concealment of what should not be said; finesse implies skillfully executed subtlety in performance or negotiation; tact, by contrast, is the private, intuitive social grace of one who instinctively knows how to handle the feelings of others. In Empson's usage the word is placed in inverted commas and held up to scrutiny, made to stand for the very politeness the honest scholar proudly ignores — a reminder that the same faculty that oils social life can also, at its worst, blunt the truth.
