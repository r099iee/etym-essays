Proto-Germanic (reconstructed c. 500 BCE)
*lut- / *lūt-
"to bow, stoop, cower, skulk"
(expressive frequentative verbal base)
↓
Middle Dutch / Middle Low German (c. 13th–14th c.)
loteren / leuteren
"to wag about, totter; linger, dawdle"
(frequentative of loten 'to shake, be loose')
↓
Middle English (late 14th c.)
loteren / loyteren
"to waste time, linger idly, play the vagrant"
(imported via Flemish weavers or Hanseatic trade)
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
loiter + -ing
"dawdling, hanging about in public places"
(codified in Tudor vagrancy statutes)
↓
Modern English (18th c.–present)
loitering
"lingering aimlessly; wandering unhurriedly"

Note on historical debate: Philologists debate whether Middle English loteren was directly borrowed from Middle Dutch loteren ('to totter, be loose, dawdle') through Low Countries textile merchants settling in eastern England, or if it survived natively from an unrecorded Old English frequentative *lotrian related to lūtian ('to lurk, lie hidden'). The sudden appearance of the word in late 14th-century texts alongside other Flemish borrowings strongly supports the Low German/Dutch loan hypothesis, where the physical sense of 'wobbling or wagging loosely' transitioned into 'idle, unsteady hanging about'.
