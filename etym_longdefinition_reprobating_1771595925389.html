reprobating is the present participle of the verb "to reprobate", and it names the act of expressing or feeling strong disapproval of someone or something; of condemning; of declaring a person or thing unworthy or evil; and of rejecting after examination as unsound, false, or blameworthy. In the passage from John Ruskin the word performs a precise and formal act. His pamphlet was "intended to be a short pamphlet, reprobating the manner and style of those critiques" — that is to say, its whole purpose was a reasoned, considered condemnation, directed not merely at particular errors but at the critics' habitual way of writing. The present participle gives the sentence an active, ongoing quality: the pamphlet itself reprobates, and condemnation is its function, not a single event but the document's continuing character. The word's tone is judicial and severe without being hot; reprobation is what a court, a synod, or a stern reviewer pronounces after weighing the evidence and finding the object wanting. Where "denouncing" is a shout and "condemning" is a sentence, "reprobating" is a verdict delivered in measured language; it implies that the disapproval has been tested and earned. The full modern polysemy of the word deserves careful enumeration.
  • Sense 1 — to express strong disapproval of; to condemn. This is the core, primary sense, alive in English since the sixteenth century. One reprobates a practice, a text, a manner of proceeding, or a habitual style, and the object is usually regarded as morally or intellectually defective.
  • Sense 2 — to reject after examination as unsound, false, or blameworthy. This is the older and more technical sense, and it is the sense that unlocks the word's anatomy: to reprobate is to put a thing to the test (Latin probare, "to test, to prove") and, finding it wanting, to cast it out. The testing image survives visibly in the related English words prove, probate, and probation.
  • Sense 3 — to declare to be unworthy or evil; to denounce as depraved. In moral and religious writing the object is not merely an action but a person or a class of persons, and the verb carries the weight of a total moral verdict.
  • Sense 4 — theology: the Calvinist doctrine of reprobation. In Reformed theology reprobation is God's eternal decree of rejection of the wicked, who are passed over in the decree of election; to reprobate in this sense is God's own act of disowning. The noun reprobate names one who is thus rejected, and by extension one who is depraved and beyond hope of moral amendment. This theological residue is what gives the secular uses of the word their special solemnity.
  • Sense 5 — noun and adjective. As a noun, reprobate means a thoroughly depraved or wicked person (a drunken reprobate, a scandalous reprobate); as an adjective it means morally abandoned or depraved. The verb is now rare in ordinary speech, but the noun and adjective survive in literary and jocular registers, and the participle reprobating survives as the formal, verdict-like form.
  • Sense 6 — obsolete and historical. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries to reprobate also meant to disprove, to refute, or to argue a thing down (literally "to prove against"). This sense has died out, but it explains the word's kinship with reprove, which through Old French means to scold or blame, and with reprobation as formal disallowance.
  • Sense 7 — contextual shade in Ruskin. The word quietly dignifies a quarrel with critics into a matter of principle rather than of pique: the disapproving verdict is tested and earned, delivered in measured language, and it is deliberately contrasted with the louder denouncement and the heavier condemnation.
The historical semantic bridge that connects these senses is the idea of a trial. The Latin root probare means to submit something to a proof; probus means good, upright, so that to prove a thing is to establish its worth. The prefix re- ("back, against") turns the proof into a test that goes the other way: reprobare is to test a thing and find it fails, to prove against it. English inherited that double verdict in a single family — prove, approve, disprove, reprove, reprobate — and the theological writers of the sixteenth century completed the work by making the failed test God's judgment on the wicked. From that point on, to reprobate was never merely to dislike; it was to have examined and rejected. In usage the word has a clear register. It belongs to elevated prose, to sermons, to political and literary controversy, and to the language of formal censure. One says "reprobating the manner and style of those critiques", "reprobating the use of public funds", "reprobating the behavior of the unruly crowd", "reprobating the illegal methods of the campaign". It is collocated with nouns of conduct, method, manner, and style rather than with persons in familiar contexts, and it keeps a faint, antique gravity that makes it feel at once precise and old-fashioned. Modern alternatives — condemning, criticizing, objecting to, calling out — are blunter or milder, and none of them preserves the word's double idea that a rejection has been tested against the evidence before it is pronounced.
