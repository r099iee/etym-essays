- (c. 800s–1000s) - Old English slōg/slōh: the everyday strong past of slēan “to strike, smite, kill” — warriors slay and are slain in chronicle and epic; the core sense is still striking.
- (1100s–1400s) - Middle English slou/slow/slewe: still the plain word for killing in battle, romance, and chronicle; “strike” fades to the background.
- (1500s–1600s) - Spelling settles as slew; the King James Bible consecrates it (David slew Goliath; hundreds of occurrences) — the word begins to sound scriptural and heroic.
- (1700s–1800s) - Retreats from speech into literature, sermons, ballads, and epic; slew becomes the dignified past tense of heroic killing.
- (1800s US) - A separate noun slew (a great number), from Irish sluagh, begins its American career — a soundalike that occasionally muddles readers.
- (1900s–) - Slay/slew/slain remain the register of epic and fantasy prose (Tolkien: “whose father you slew in Moria”); modern slang present slay (“you slay!”) prefers weak slayed, leaving slew purely epic.
- (today) - Instantly understood, never casual: the ceremonial past tense of killing — with a busy homograph, a slew of, meaning “many”.
