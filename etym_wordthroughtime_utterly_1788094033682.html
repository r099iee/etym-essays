- (Old English, c. 900) - The raw material is spatial: ūt "out" and its comparative ūtera "outer" — purely about position, nothing to do with degree.
- (1200s–1300s) - The adjective utter slides from "outer, exterior" to "extreme, thoroughgoing": the utter end of the earth is the farthest end; utterly (the adverb) appears meaning "completely, truly."
- (1300s–1500s) - Utterly becomes the standard English intensive of totality in law, religion, and chronicle: lands "utterly wasted," sins "utterly forgiven," enemies "utterly destroyed."
- (1600s) - The King James Bible consecrates it (scores of occurrences: "utterly destroyed," "utterly consumed"); Shakespeare uses it for absolute emotional and physical states; it is plain formal prose, not yet archaic.
- (1700s–1800s) - Ubiquitous in oratory, law, and fiction; begins to sound bookish as "totally" rises in colloquial speech; "utter" as an adjective of ruin becomes a novelist's staple.
- (1900s–) - In speech, totally and completely dominate; utterly retreats to emphatic, formal, or literary use, specialising in finality and ruin ("utterly devastated," "utterly alone") — the register of Tolkien's "fall utterly short of the reality."
- (today) - Perceived as complete but formal; its power is extremity — the adverb that leaves nothing out, nothing beyond.
