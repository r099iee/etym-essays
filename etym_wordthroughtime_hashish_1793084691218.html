- Medieval Arabic — dry herb and hay: ḥašīš names any cut herbage, then narrows to hemp as the intoxicating herb above all others.
- Crusades era — a slur becomes a legend: Sunni polemic calls Nizari Ismailis hashishiyya, and Crusaders carry home tales of drugged killers.
- Marco Polo, c. 1300 — the garden of paradise: his travel account fixes the tale of youths drugged into obedience, and Europe believes it for centuries.
- 1798–1801 — Napoleon in Egypt: French troops pick up the habit, alarm their commanders, and carry hachisch and its prohibition back to France.
- 1840s Paris — the poets experiment: the Club des Hachichins tastes dawamesk paste, and Baudelaire writes of artificial paradises.
- 20th c. — prohibition and counterculture: the resin is outlawed worldwide, then romanticised again by travellers on the hippie trail to Afghanistan and Morocco.
- Today — partly legal, partly illicit: the old word survives in law, medicine, and memory, while new extracts and edibles inherit its chemistry under new names.
