Every battle in English begins with a beating. The ancestor of embattled is Latin battuere, ‘to beat, strike’ — a soldier's word, probably borrowed from Gaulish Celtic, that marched with the legions and conquered the Romance languages: French battre, Italian battere, Spanish batir all descend from it, and from its Vulgar Latin offspring *battualia (‘military exercises, combat drills’) comes the whole family of battle, battalion, battery, combat, and battlement.

From beating to battle — Old French bataille: By the 11th century bataille means both the fight and the body of men doing the fighting — a battalion. William's knights at Hastings fought in three batailles. The Normans carry the word to England, where Middle English bataile swiftly becomes the standard term for pitched combat, pushing native bead and hild (Old English poetic words for battle) into extinction.

The en- prefix — two births:

  • Architectural birth (14th c.): embataillen first means ‘to furnish with battlements’ — to crown a wall with the alternating solid merlons and open crenels through which defenders shoot. The earliest citations describe castles and city walls: stone prepared for battle.
  • Military birth (15th–16th c.): the verb extends to ‘to array for battle, marshal in fighting order’ — troops prepared as battle. Chronicles describe ‘embattled hosts’ drawn up in the field, and the past participle becomes the adjective we know.

Literary centuries: Shakespeare's age loves the word in both senses — castle walls and ranked spears. Milton arms heaven itself: ‘embattled angels’ in Paradise Lost. Gray's Elegy (1751) gives the architectural sense its loveliest line — ‘the moping owl does to the moon complain…’ beside no embattled tower, pointedly. And Emerson's Concord Hymn (1837) — ‘here once the embattled farmers stood, and fired the shot heard round the world’ — plants the citizen-soldier image at the root of American memory.

The headline sense (20th c.): Modern journalism discovered that embattled dignifies any losing struggle: the embattled president, the embattled pound, the embattled coach. The battlefield metaphor flatters its subject — no longer merely ‘troubled’ or ‘failing’, they are a brave garrison holding the walls. Tolkien, characteristically, reaches past the cliché back to the word's martial youth: his embattled army is no metaphor for a cabinet crisis but a host of silver spears yelling as they spring — battle-joy, not battle-weariness. The word's future seems secure: as long as newspapers need a dignified synonym for ‘in trouble’, and as long as fantasy needs armies glad for war, embattled will keep both its thrones — the newsroom's and the battlefield's — without either sensing the other's claim; few adjectives serve headline and epic so equally well.
