UNABASHEDLY is a combination of two inherited strands of very different age and origin: the native English/Germanic negative prefix un- and the Romance word abash. The two chains are given separately, then joined at the adjective. CHAIN 1 — the prefix un- (native Germanic): 1) PIE *ne- / *n̥- "not" (reconstructed; the oldest and most widespread negative in Indo-European, reflected in Sanskrit a-/an-, Greek a-/an-, Latin in-, Gothic un-, Lithuanian ne-, Old Irish an-) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Germanic *un- "not" (reconstructed) — the ordinary negative prefix of the family. 3) Old English un- "not" (attested from c. 700 CE) — used to negate adjectives and participles. 4) English un- "not" (still productive) — the prefix added to abashed to make unabashed. CHAIN 2 — the stem abash (Romance): 1) *bassus "low, short, thick-set" (Late Latin adjective; NO SECURE PIE ETYMOLOGY — widely suspected to be a Pre-Roman substrate word of Italy, possibly Oscan; its origin is debated and unknown) — attested from the 1st c. CE. 2) Latin *bassiāre / Vulgar Latin bassiare "to lower, to let down" (reconstructed denominative verb) — source of the Romance "to lower": Italian abbassare, Spanish abajar, French baisser. 3) Old French baissier "to lower, let down, abase" (attested from the 10th c.). 4) Old French esbaissier "to astound, dismay, discomfit" (es- intensive + baissier; 12th c.) — to "bring a person's gaze down," to destroy composure. This verb was later conflated with a second Old French verb, esbahir "to astonish" (probably from Vulgar Latin *batare "to gape, be open-mouthed"), and both contributed to the English abash — the precise mixture is debated. 5) Anglo-Norman abaissier / esbaïr "to lower, to confound" — the Channel form that crossed into English. 6) Middle English abaissen "to put to shame, disconcert, confuse" (early 14th c.). 7) abashed (past participle, mid 14th c.) "embarrassed, ashamed, disconcerted." 8) unabashed (adjective, coined 1670s) "not disconcerted or ashamed." 9) UNABASHEDLY (adverb, attested c. 1840s) "in a manner free from shame." NOTE ON DEBATE: The exact Old French background is debated. Standard dictionaries derive abash from esbaissier (es- + baissier, ultimately *bassus "low"), but the English spelling with -sh- shows the influence of esbahir "to astonish" (from a separate gape/stare root), and the two French verbs were thoroughly mixed in Anglo-Norman. The Germanic *ne- chain, by contrast, is uncontroversial.
