bray for a donkey's cry has nothing to do at first with grinding in a mortar, though the two have shared a spelling for centuries. The donkey word comes from Gaulish shouting through Latin and French; the mortar word comes from Germanic crushing through French. Their convergence in Middle English is accident, but a happy one: both involve violent noise and pressure.

  • Imitative Gaulish base: Late Latin bragire to cry out is generally taken as a borrowing from Gaulish *brag-, itself imitative of a loud burst — compare Old Irish braigim to fart noisily, Welsh bragal to bray. The root is sound-symbolic rather than strictly lexical: br- + guttural suggests eruption. Roman soldiers and Gallo-Roman peasants would have heard asses, trumpets, and drunks all bragire.
  • Old French braire: by the 12th century braire means to cry, clamour, and specifically to bray like an ass. Bestiaries and fabliaux fix the ass as the type of the harsh crier; humans who braient are coarse, drunk, or in pain. The form shows regular loss of intervocalic g: bragire > braire, with infinitive in -aire.
  • Middle English borrowing: breien / brayen appears c. 1300, first for asses, then for men crying harshly and trumpets blaring. Chaucer's contemporaries know the ass's bray as proverbial for discordant preaching and boasting. Pronunciation moves from French -ai- to English /eɪ/, spelled ay.
  • Early Modern expansion and homonym clash: 16th–17th century writers use bray for donkey, human laugh, and brass. Simultaneously a second bray — to pound in a mortar — enters from Old French breier / broyer to crush, of Germanic origin (cf. Old High German brehhan to break). Tyndale and the King James Bible use both: asses bray for want of fodder (Job 6:5), fools are brayed in mortars (Proverbs 27:22). Context alone disambiguates, and the mortar sense soon becomes technical and rare, leaving the donkey triumphant.
  • Natural history and satire: 18th–19th century naturalists describe the bray's mechanism — alternate inhalation and exhalation through larynx and nasal cavities producing hee-haw audible for miles — while satirists weaponise it. To bray is to expose oneself as asinine: politicians bray, fops bray, congregations accused of braying psalms. Dickens, Thackeray, and French naturalists in translation use it for rural pathos and urban mockery alike.
  • Modern stability: today the verb is regular (bray – brayed – brayed), the noun countable (a bray, brays), the phonetics stable /breɪ/. Children's books teach hee-haw as the donkey's English voice, field guides list bray alongside neigh and bleat, and political columns still dismiss braying laughter. Maupassant's il se mit à braire, rendered began to bray, sits squarely in this long line: a stretched neck, a grating burst, pity mixed with comedy.

Note on the mortar homonym: if encountered, to bray in a mortar derives not from Gaulish shouting but from Frankish crushing. Dictionaries list them as bray v.1 and bray v.2. Only v.1 concerns the donkey in the story.
