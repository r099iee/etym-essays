1. Proto-Indo-European roots and the chromatic archetype of redness. The name Rohiṇī descends from one of the most widely attested and stable color roots in Proto-Indo-European: *h₁rewdʰ- (with ablaut variants *h₁rowdʰ- and zero-grade *h₁rudʰ-), signifying "red, ruddy, copper-colored," or "to glow with reddish hue." This root supplied nearly all major Indo-European language families with their foundational terms for redness, blood, and rust: Greek erythrós (ἐρυθρός, "red"), Latin ruber and rūfus ("red, ruddy"), Old Church Slavonic rъdrъ ("red"), Gothic rauþs, Old High German rōt, and Old English rēad (Modern English red). Across ancient pastoralist societies, the color red carried potent cosmic symbolism, embodying vital arterial blood, life force, the blazing dawn, and the fertility of soil and cattle.

2. The Indo-Iranian and Vedic transition: from color adjective to bovine sanctity. In Proto-Indo-Iranian, the root yielded *HrawtH-, giving rise to Avestan raoiδita- ("red") and Old Persian forms. In the Vedic speech of northern India, the root split into two dialectal variants: the classical lōhita- (with lateral liquid l-) and the archaic priestly róhita- (with rhotic liquid r-). In the Ṛgveda, róhita primarily functioned as an adjective meaning "reddish, chestnut, or copper-colored," frequently applied to the fiery celestial steeds of Agni (the Fire god) and Sūrya (the Sun god).

The feminine substantive róhiṇī was generated through the addition of the primary relational suffix -in- followed by the feminine marker -ī, with the dental nasal regularly undergoing retroflexion (n → ṇ) under the influence of the initial rhotic consonant (the famous phonological process of nati in Pāṇinian grammar):

  • The bovine mother — In archaic Vedic pastoral culture, cows were categorized strictly by their hide colors. A rohiṇī was specifically a prized "red cow." Because red cows were intimately associated with sacrificial offerings, dawn light, and the life-giving milk of the cosmic mother, the word acquired deep religious sanctity.
  • Astronomical crystallization — In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā (TS 4.4.10) and Atharvaveda (AV 19.7), the astronomical catalog of twenty-seven nakṣatras (lunar mansions) was formally codified. The fourth asterism, dominated by the brilliant 0.85-magnitude red giant star Aldebaran, was designated as Rohiṇī precisely because its vivid, reddish-orange glare mirrored the sacred red cow of the heavens. Furthermore, during the late 3rd to early 2nd millennium BCE, the vernal equinox occurred near this asterism, making Rohiṇī the cosmological dawn of the solar year, ruled by Prajāpati, the Lord of Creation.

3. Anthropomorphic mythologizing and the legend of Candra's wives. As Vedic sacrificial ritual evolved into the grand narrative literature of the Epics (Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa) and the Purāṇas, the constellations were systematically anthropomorphized into conscious personalities. Rohiṇī and her sister asterisms were reimagined as the twenty-seven daughters of Dakṣa Pracetasa, bestowed upon the Moon god Candra (Soma) in holy matrimony. The physical astronomical reality—that the moon appears particularly luminous and lingers noticeably against the broad Hyades star cluster—was translated into the famous romantic myth of Candra's uncontrollable favoritism toward Rohiṇī, provoking Dakṣa's curse of consumption and explaining the lunar phases.

4. Literary elevation in the Rāmāyaṇa and classical Kāvya. In the Rāmāyaṇa of Vālmīki, Rohiṇī transcends mere astronomy to become the ultimate aesthetic motif for conjugal fidelity. When Princess Sītā rejects comfortable royal protection in Ayodhyā to follow Rāma into the tiger-infested wilderness of Daṇḍaka, the poet compares her to Rohiṇī cleaving faithfully to Candra. This simile became a mandatory trope across classical Sanskrit kāvya literature; poets such as Kālidāsa, Bhāravi, and Harṣa repeatedly invoked Rohiṇī as the sublime embodiment of romantic loyalty that never wavers, regardless of whether her lord waxes in glory or wanes in sorrow.

5. Puranic synthesis, Āyurvedic medicine, and modern legacy. During the development of the Purāṇas, the name Rohiṇī bifurcated into multiple vital streams. In Vaishnava sacred history, she became the heroic senior wife of Vasudeva who gave birth to Balarāma in Gokula. Concurrently, Āyurvedic physicians adopted rohiṇī as a botanical and diagnostic taxon, denoting red-stemmed healing herbs and pediatric throat inflammations. In the modern era, Rohiṇī remains one of the most beloved and auspicious feminine given names across South Asia, while the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) christened India's earliest experimental satellite and rocket programs the "Rohini series," cementing the ancient red asterism as an enduring emblem of cosmic exploration and cultural heritage.
