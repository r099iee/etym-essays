Stumbled is the past tense and past participle of the verb “stumble,” which means to trip or lose one's footing while moving, and — by metaphor — to make a mistake, falter, or discover something by accident. The word covers a physical action and a large family of figurative uses, all built on the image of a foot catching or a step going wrong.

Primary sense — to trip while moving. To stumble is to catch one's foot on something, or to move so unsteadily that one nearly falls. It is less severe than falling: a stumble is a near-fall, a lurch of balance that is usually recovered. In the source context, “he was nearly overcome, and stumbled blindly on in great pain and fear,” the word carries exactly this sense of impaired, staggering movement — a person barely able to continue, tripping forward through darkness and pain.

Secondary senses.

  • To move or progress unsteadily: staggering, lurching onward, often through adverse conditions (“stumbled through the storm,” “stumbled over the rubble”).
  • To falter in speech: stumbling over one's words means hesitating, tripping on syllables, losing fluency in speaking, reciting, or performing.
  • To make a mistake or lapse: to err in conduct, judgement, or a task — a moral, professional, or intellectual stumble (“the company stumbled badly last quarter”).
  • To encounter by chance: “stumble on / upon / across” means to find or meet something unexpectedly and often accidentally — the luckiest sense of the word (“I stumbled upon a brilliant café”).
  • To fall into sin or error (archaic and Biblical): in religious language, to stumble is to fall from righteousness or to be caused to fail in one's faith; a “stumbling-block” is anything that causes such a fall.
  • To totter or threaten to collapse (archaic): of thrones, empires, or fortunes “stumbling” toward ruin.
  • Transitive (rare): to cause to trip — “the rope stumbled him.”

Modern collocations and register. The word is used across registers. In sport and exercise: “he stumbled but kept his balance.” In finance: “shares stumbled on news of the layoffs.” In politics: “the campaign stumbled over its own contradictions.” In everyday speech it is most common in the chance-discovery formula “stumbled upon.” As a participle it also works as an adjective-like form (“a stumbled step” is rare; the adverb “stumblingly” is more usual).

Degree and nuance. “Stumbled” is milder than “fell” (which implies loss of balance to the ground) and more accidental than “staggered” (which implies instability from weakness, drink, or shock). It implies a brief, recoverable loss of control, which is why its figurative uses all describe a transient lapse rather than a catastrophe: a stumble in a career, a stumble in a speech, a stumble into good luck.

Historical and literary use. The word carries a long literary and scriptural pedigree. In the King James Bible, “stumble” and “stumbling” render Hebrew and Greek words for tripping and offence (“they shall stumble,” “a stone of stumbling”), giving the participle an archaic solemn ring that writers have echoed ever since. In prose narration, “stumbled on” (“he stumbled on down the dark passage”) is a favourite for conveying exhausted, desperate movement, exactly as Tolkien uses it in The Hobbit.
