The sluggard begins life as two mediocrities that combine into a masterpiece of insult. The first is Middle English slugge, a sluggish, slow-witted fellow, attested in the late fourteenth century and of famously murky origin: it may echo the snaillike slug of the garden, it may imitate the very sound of dragging feet, or it may be kin to Scandinavian words such as Norwegian slugg, a heavy slow person, carried by Norse settlers and North Sea trade. Scholars have never settled the matter, which suits a word about never getting anywhere. The second ingredient is the suffix -ard, a piece of Norman French that English borrowed along with the Conqueror's vocabulary of contempt — coward, drunkard, dullard, laggard — and swiftly naturalised as its favourite way of turning a weakness into a permanent identity.

Put slug and -ard together and the late medieval Englishman had a new noun of maximum reproach: not one who is slow but one who is a Slow, professionally and incorrigibly. The timing was perfect, because the English Bible was about to make it unforgettable. Miles Coverdale and then the Geneva and King James translators rendered Solomon's warnings with Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise, and the ant-versus-sluggard tableau entered every cottage: the tiny industrious insect against the sprawling human disgrace. Preachers anatomised the sluggard's timetable — his late rising, his long dinners, his hand that hideth in his bosom — and conduct writers made early rising a spiritual duty, so that every lie-abed in Christendom heard the word from the pulpit before he heard it at home.

Ages of the idler:

  • Medieval moralising (14th–15th centuries): Langland's Piers Plowman parades Sloth as a priest too drowsy for his office, and catechisms list sluggardliness among sloth's children. The noun already spawns kin: sluggardy for the condition, sluggish for the temperament.
  • Tudor and Stuart colour (16th–17th centuries): Shakespeare never quite writes sluggard into a famous mouth, but his age is thick with it — pamphlets against vagrant sluggards, sermons against sluggard Sabbaths, and householders roaring it up the stairs exactly as in the source sentence. The spelling firms up while pronunciation keeps the hard double-g deliberation of the slug.
  • Work-ethic centuries (18th–19th centuries): Franklin's Poor Richard, Wesley's Methodists, and Smilesian self-help all conscript the sluggard as the anti-citizen: the boy who rises at five owns the day, the sluggard forfeits it. Industrial discipline gives the old sin a new ledger, and the schoolroom cane enforces what the pulpit once preached.
  • Psychological century (20th century): Doctors and managers retire the moral vocabulary — low motivation, underperformance, executive dysfunction — and the sluggard retreats to comedy: cartoons, pantomime dames, and fond parental roars on Saturday mornings. Tolkien's use belongs here, a deliberate archaism that tastes of nursery and chapel.
  • Survival today: The word persists because no modernism matches its music: the slug-glide of the first syllable, the growled -ard finale. Fitness trackers may count our steps, but only sluggard makes staying in bed sound like a character in a morality play — which, for one shouted morning moment, it is.

Sound shifts and kin: Phonologically the word barely moved in six centuries: the vowel shortened and settled, the final -ard kept its full French resonance rather than reducing, preserving the deliberate scorn. Its great family remains the -ard insults — coward from coue, tail; drunkard; dullard; laggard, its closest sibling, built on lag exactly as sluggard is built on slug — plus the adjective sluggish and the noun slug itself, mollusc and bullet alike, all trailing slime behind them through the dictionary.
