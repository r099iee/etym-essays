**The short, quick history of SCURRYING** Unlike most English verbs, *scurry* has no long single-branch ancestry; it is a young, partly onomatopoeic word born of a rhyme in the eighteenth century. Its full story therefore runs in two streams — one Germanic, one Latin — that meet and fuse into the modern verb, and then into its present participle *scurrying*. The word has been in English for barely two hundred years, yet its two parents are of very different ages. **Stream one: the Germanic element of haste** - Reconstructing backward, philologists posit a Proto-Germanic base *hurza- meaning "to move with haste, to whir," an imitative formation that survives in Middle English hurren "to vibrate rapidly, buzz" — the word for the whirring of insects' wings — and in continental relatives such as German hurren "to whir, move fast" and Old Swedish hurra "to whirl round." - The modern verb hurry is first attested in the 1590s, in the plays of Shakespeare. Its origin is debated: it may be a variant of harry (to harass, to rush), or it may spring directly from the buzzing, whirring sense of Middle English hurren. Either way, it established in English the plain idea of rapid, urgent motion. - This stream gives scurrying its energetic, bustling core — the sense of movement made in haste, with a small, busy, agitated quality that still lingers in the word. **Stream two: the Latin-derived element of running out** - The second parent is the verb scour, "to move about hastily in search, to range over." Its lineage is long and impeccably Latin: PIE *kers- "to run" (reconstructed) → Latin currere "to run" → excurrere "to run out, sally forth" → Old French / Anglo-French escourre "to run out, range over" → Middle English scour. - This stream contributes the idea of ranging, darting movement — motion that covers ground quickly and searchingly — and, through the shared currere, the word links scurrying to a vast Romance family including course, current, and courier. **The fusion: hurry-scurry and the birth of scurry** - Around 1732, English speakers coined a rhyming, reduplicative phrase — hurry-scurry, also spelled hurry-skurry — to capture confused, flurried bustle. The second element skurry was coined or reshaped under the influence of scour, which rhymes with hurry and matches its sense of fast, ranging motion. - The pairing is purely rhythmic and echoic: two hasty syllables, one Germanic and one Latin-flavored, bound together by rhyme — the same pattern that gives tick-tock and ding-dong their memorability. - The phrase was a success as an idiom of disorderly haste, and its second element proved durable enough to stand alone. - By 1810 the verb scurry had been back-formed from the phrase: to scurry is to hasten along, to move precipitately, to scuttle. By 1823 a noun scurry followed, meaning a hurried, bustling movement. **Sound and meaning in the new word** - As a back-formation, scurry inherited exactly the right phonetic shape for its sense: the initial sk- cluster (as in scamper, scuttle, skitter, scamper) is itself sound-symbolic in English for quick, light, scraping motion, and the final -y gives it a small, fussy, almost diminutive ring. - Because its birth was imitative and reduplicative, scurry has always suggested something small, busy, and slightly undignified in motion — the scuttle of mice, the bustle of a crowd, the swarming rush of insects — rather than the dignified haste of a messenger or the urgent speed of a racer. - That connotation settled early and has never left: scurrying is what startled creatures do, and what harried servants and panicked crowds do; it is never a stately verb. **Semantic drift and social life** - In the 19th century, scurrying attached itself to the small and domestic — servants, mice, messengers, clerks — the vocabulary of the Victorian household in a hurry. - In the 20th–21st centuries, the word broadened to cover commuters, office workers, insects, and (in Tolkien) goblins, but it kept its diminutive, furtive, comic edge: a scurry of feet is always a little frantic and never heroic. - In ecological and zoological writing, scurrying became the standard gait-word for small mammals, insects, and crustaceans — short, quick, irregular footfalls — a technical use that simply formalizes the popular image. **Literary life and Tolkien** - Hurry-scurry appears in the eighteenth-century idiom and travels through Dickensian prose as a word of flustered bustle. - The verb scurry and its participle scurrying enter literary English in the 19th century and are used whenever writers want quick, small, agitated motion. - In The Hobbit (1937), the goblins who come scurrying round the corner in full cry are exactly the creatures this young word was made for: many, loud, and frantic, their brief panicked motion snapping short before the cold gleam of the elven swords — a word of two hundred years, put to work in a story of Middle-earth, and doing precisely what it was coined to do.
