Pacified is the past participle of pacify, whose lineage runs from an Indo-European pact through Roman imperial peace to modern calm and euphemistic killing. The ultimate root is PIE *peh₂k- meaning to fasten or join, source of Latin pax, pacis peace, paciscor to agree, pangere to fix, and English pact, appease, pay. Peace in this etymology is not inner serenity but a fastened bargain — a treaty pinned between parties. To that noun Latin added -ficare, the combining form of facere to make or do (PIE *dʰeh₁- to put, do), yielding pacificare to make peace, already in classical authors carrying the double edge of negotiated settlement and imposed subjugation.

Classical usage already shows the euphemistic slide visible in this card. Caesar and Livy use pacare and pacificare for reducing Gaul or Spain to peace — that is, conquering, disarming and taxing them. The Augustan Pax Romana is peace through victory, and Tacitus bitter epigram that Romans make a desert and call it peace captures the coercive undertone. Late Latin and Christian writers extend pacificare to reconciling God and man, calming strife and soothing conscience, restoring the gentle pole. Both poles — tender calming and military subduing — enter Old French pacifier (12th–13th c.) meaning to make peace, appease and settle, and Middle English pacifien (15th c.) meaning to appease wrath, calm seas and quiet quarrels.

Phonetic development is conservative because the word travelled through learned borrowing rather than popular sound erosion:

  • Latin to French: pacificare loses the -are ending stress pattern to Old French pacifier, with c before a retained as /k/ then assibilated in later French to /s/; English borrows the French stem with initial p-a-c intact.
  • French to English: ME pacifien, pacifyen adapts the -fier ending to English -fy on the model of edify, modify, justify; stress settles on the first syllable PAC-i-fy, and the past participle adds regular -ed as pacified /ˈpæsɪfaɪd/.
  • Modern spelling: c before i voices to /s/, y marks the vowel /ɪ/ to /aɪ/ shift in the stressed-to-unstressed pattern; no irregular allomorphy unlike peace / appease.

Semantic history in English shows oscillation between soothing and subduing. Early Modern citations (15th–17th c.) favour appeasement: pacify anger, pacify conscience, pacify a storm. Eighteenth-century imperial administration revives the Roman military sense: to pacify a province, a tribe, the frontier — meaning to suppress resistance, disarm and govern. Nineteenth-century colonial literature, including the source of this passage, uses both at once with dark irony: ducks gently held under water till they were pacified means till they drowned and stopped struggling — calming by killing, rendered with the detached adverb gently. Twentieth-century counter-insurgency entrenches the euphemism: pacified villages, pacification programmes in Vietnam, Algeria and Malaya, where calm statistics mask coercion.

Key attestations trace the widening:

  • Middle English (c. 1450): Lydgate and Malory use pacify for appeasing lords and quarrels.
  • Shakespeare (c. 1600): Othello and histories use pacify for calming passion and settling rebellion.
  • 18th-c. imperial dispatches: pacified districts in India and North America meaning militarily secured.
  • 19th-c. anthropology: passages like this one apply pacified to animals drowned or stupefied, extending military euphemism to hunting technique.
  • 20th-c. coinages: pacifier as baby dummy (American) and rubber teat that calms by sucking; Pacific Ocean from Magellan Mar Pacifico peaceful sea; pacifist, pacifism (19th–20th c.) for principled refusal of war, inverting the coercive pole.

In this card the participle functions as resultative adjective: held till they were pacified equals held until dead and motionless. The gentleness is technical — a steady underwater grip that avoids splashing and alarming other ducks — not merciful. Modern readers feel the euphemism sharply because pacified now triggers associations with counter-insurgency and with soothing babies, making its application to drowned ducks startlingly flat. That flatness is the point of the source style: primitive ingenuity narrated as calm procedure.
