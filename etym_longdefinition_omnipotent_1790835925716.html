Omnipotent (adjective; noun: the Omnipotent) describes an entity that possesses unlimited, infinite, and universal power, capable of accomplishing all things within the realm of logical possibility. In theology, philosophy, and political theory, omnipotence represents the absolute zenith of power—unconstrained by physical limitations, temporal resistance, or external opposition. Functioning as a proper noun with the definite article (the Omnipotent), it serves as a solemn epithet for God.

The word encompasses several profound dimensions across theological, philosophical, and literary thought:

  • Theological Attribute of the Divine: In monotheistic theology (Judaism, Christianity, Islam), omnipotence is one of the essential, incommunicable attributes of God (alongside omniscience, omnipresence, and omnibenevolence). It signifies that God is the ultimate ground of all existence and causal agency, whose will brought the cosmos into being ex nihilo (out of nothing) and sustains it continually.
  • Scholastic Philosophy and the Boundaries of Power: In medieval and classical scholastic philosophy (notably analyzed by St. Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae, Question 25, and William of Ockham), the precise definition of omnipotence was subjected to rigorous logical analysis. Scholastics distinguished between:
    • Potentia absoluta (absolute power): God's abstract ability to do anything that does not involve an intrinsic logical contradiction (such as making a square circle or undoing the past).
    • Potentia ordinata (ordained power): The power God actually exercises in governing the universe in accordance with divine wisdom, justice, and natural laws.
  • Political and Sovereign Supremacy: In constitutional and legal theory, the concept of absolute, unchecked authority is frequently described through the lens of omnipotence (e.g., "the omnipotence of Parliament" in British constitutional theory, as formulated by A.V. Dicey, denoting the legal doctrine that Parliament can make or unmake any law whatsoever).
  • Literary and Figurative Hyperbole: Applied poetically to irresistible, overwhelming natural, emotional, or psychological forces that conquer human resistance (e.g., "the omnipotent force of love", "omnipotent nature", "the omnipotent power of public opinion").
  • Substantive Noun Epithet: Used as a noun to refer directly to the Almighty Creator ("prayers addressed to the Omnipotent").

Historical Semantic Bridges and Metaphoric Shifts:

The word is a direct borrowing from Latin omnipotens (genitive omnipotentis), an ancient classical compound formed from the adjective omnis ("all, every") and potens ("powerful, able, capable", the present participle of posse / pote sum, from the PIE root *pótis, "master, lord, able").

  • From Roman Epic to Latin Vulgate: In classical Roman poetry (Virgil's Aeneid, Ovid's Metamorphoses), omnipotens was originally applied as an epic epithet to Jupiter (Iuppiter omnipotens) and Father Olympus. St. Jerome and early Christian translators adopted this elevated classical term into the Latin Vulgate to translate the Hebrew divine title El Shaddai ("God Almighty") and the Greek Septuagint title $\Pi\alpha\nu\tau\mathrm{o}\kappa\rho\acute{\alpha}\tau\omega\rho$ (Pantokratōr, "Ruler of All").
  • From Divine Majesty to Universal Metaphor: In Early Modern English (Shakespeare, Milton, Addison), the word retained its supreme theological majesty while expanding metaphorically to describe the absolute sovereignty of moral duty and cosmic providence over human anxieties.
