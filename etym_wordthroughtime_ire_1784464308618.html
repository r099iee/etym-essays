13th-Century Romance Borrowing - Ire enters Middle English in the 13th century from Old French ire, the epic anger-word of the chansons de geste, the roaring wrath of Roland and Charlemagne, high and heroic.
Religious and Royal Register - Through the medieval and early modern period ire flourishes in religious, legal, and poetic texts — the ire of God, of kings, of wronged lords — an anger of high status, not of quarrelsome neighbours.
18th-19th Century Literary Survival - As anger becomes the everyday term, ire retreats to poetry and elevated prose, where it carries the archaic grandeur of kings, gods, and monsters, losing ground in common speech.
Contemporary Sense - Today ire is a somewhat literary word for intense anger or wrath, used for effect in prose ("drawing the ire of critics"), concise and high-register compared to everyday anger.
