CHAIN 1 — HERE: Stage 1 — PIE demonstrative particle *ki-/*ko- "this, here" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the finger pointing at what lies near the speaker; also the source of Latin cis "on this side" (cisalpine, cisgender), Greek sēmeron "today" (lit. "this day"), and Lithuanian šìs "this".
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hēr "in this place" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English hēr "here" (attested c. 7th c.).
Stage 4 — Modern English here "here" (unchanged in sense).
NOTE: Sanskrit hyas "yesterday" is sometimes loosely cited beside here but descends from the separate root *gʰdʰyés (cognate with Latin heri, Greek khthes) and must not be grafted onto this branch.
CHAIN 2 — TO: Stage 1 — PIE directional particle *de/*do "to, toward" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — a bare grammatical gesture of motion toward a goal; cf. Greek -de in hóde "this one here".
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *tō "to" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English tō "to" (attested c. 7th c.).
Stage 4 — Modern English to "to", with the emphatic twin too "also" (lit. "to it as well") (16th c.).
NOTE: No secure Indic cognate of the to-particle is established.
CHAIN 3 — FORE: Stage 1 — PIE root *per- "forward, in front, before" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — source of Sanskrit puras "in front", purā "formerly", pūrva "foremost; eastern" (Hindi pūrv/pūrab "east", Odia pūrba), Greek pró "before" (prologue, prophet, program), Latin prō "before, forward" (proceed, provide).
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *furai "before, in front" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English fore "before, in front of" (attested c. 7th c.); sibling for.
Stage 4 — Modern English fore "before; the front part" (in before, forth, forecast, forearm).
NOTE: The same root *per- underlies "forward" and "before" — the spatial-to-temporal mapping is ancient.
COMPOUND CHAIN — HERETOFORE: Stage 1 — Middle English (14th c.): hēr + tō + fore welded into here-to-fore "up to before this, until now" (attested c. 1300s as here-to-fore / here tofore); the same analogical impulse produced its twin hitherto (hither + to).
Stage 2 — Modern English heretofore "until now; before this time" (spelling closed from the 18th c.).
NOTE: The compound is a Middle English coinage, not an inherited word; its three members are old, but their fusion into one temporal adverb is English's own work.
