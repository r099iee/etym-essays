WHINNYING (present participle of the verb whinny; also the noun whinny) CORE SENSE. To whinny is to make the high, soft, tremulous, vibrating call of a horse — a sound produced through a partly closed throat, halfway between a breathy snort and a full neigh, often rising in pitch as it is repeated. As a noun, a whinny is that call itself. The word belongs to the equine sound vocabulary of English, but it occupies a precise emotional register: where the neigh is loud, full-throated, and commanding, the whinny is soft, high, anxious, and repeated — the cry of a horse that is nervous, frightened, lonely, or eager for attention, rather than the trumpet of a confident stallion. LITERARY CONTEXT (The Hobbit, ch. 3). In the storm scene, the terrified ponies stand "with their heads down and their tails between their legs" while some of them are whinnying with fright. Tolkien's choice of whinnying rather than the louder neighing gives the moment a note of nervous, almost pleading animal distress rather than bold alarm. The word pictures the sound trembling through the crashing thunder of the stone-giants' game — a thin thread of living fear against the dead roar of the storm. It is one of the few points in the book where the reader is given the emotional interior of the pack animals: their panic is voiced, fragile, and helpless. The progressive form adds duration and repetition: the sound does not ring out once but shivers on, over and over, which is precisely the acoustics of a frightened horse. POLYSEMY: THE FULL RANGE OF SENSES. - The equine call (primary sense). The soft, high-pitched, tremulous cry of a horse, especially of greeting, eagerness, loneliness, or nervousness. Horses whinny when their companions are led away, when they hear a familiar voice, when they anticipate feed, and when they are frightened. This is the sense in the Hobbit passage. - The human laugh metaphor. In prose, a whinny describes a high-pitched, tremulous laugh resembling a horse's nicker — laughter that rises and shakes, often nervous or uncontrolled. Historical bridge: sound-transfer from the equine cry to a similarly pitched human sound; the shared feature is the soft, vibrating, half-suppressed quality, quite unlike a belly-laugh or a guffaw. - Figurative uses of the verb. To whinny can describe any high, thin, quivering sound that resembles the horse's call — the whinny of wind, of a violin string, of a creaking hinge in literary prose. Bridge: onomatopoeic extension from the imitative base to any acoustically similar noise. - "Whinny" as a synonym for a soft call in rustic/country register. In dialect and pastoral writing the word describes the friendly, welcoming cry of a horse to its handler, with a note of affection. ON THE SOUND ITSELF. A whinny is made by the horse forcing breath through a partially closed larynx while the head is raised and the mouth slightly open; the result is a complex, rapidly rising and falling tone that humans hear as tremulous and "questioning." English vocabulary for equine cries is finely graded: neigh (Old English hnægan) is the loud, full-throated, trumpet-like call, proud and commanding, audible across a field; whicker and nicker are the low, soft, throaty, friendly mutterings of greeting or anticipation of food — quiet, intimate, contented; bray belongs to the donkey and ass, a harsh, sawing, discordant blast; squeal is the high, sharp, shrill protest of a hurt or fighting horse; snort is the explosive expulsion of air through the nostrils, often of alarm or challenge. Whinnying occupies the anxious middle ground: high-pitched yet soft, tremulous yet insistent. REGISTER AND CONNOTATION. Whinnying is literary, pastoral, and gently intimate. It belongs to the vocabulary of the stable and the farm, of story and of verse, rather than to the technical manuals of the veterinary surgeon (who would speak of vocalization). The word carries a note of tenderness and vulnerability — the horse as a creature with a voice of its own, nervous and alive — which is exactly why Tolkien uses it to let the reader hear the ponies' fear in the thunderstorm. WORD FAMILY AND DERIVATIVES. whinny (verb and noun); whinnying (present participle and noun, the act or the sound); whinnied (past tense); whinnyer (rare). The verb belongs to the great wh- family of English imitative words for sounds of breath and air — whine, whistle, whiz, whir, wheeze, whisper, whelp — all of which begin with the same onomatopoeic cluster for a forced, thin, airy sound. Historically whinny was formed from whine by the diminutive-frequentative suffix -y/-ie (compare doggie), signalling a repeated, small, soft version of the sound: not a whine but a series of little whines. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS (summary). - neigh — loud, full-throated, commanding; whinny — soft, high, anxious, repeated. - whicker / nicker — low, soft, friendly muttering of contentment or greeting; whinny — higher, more tremulous, more urgent. - bray — harsh, discordant, sawing, belongs to the donkey; whinny — soft and quivering, belongs to the horse. - squeal — sharp, shrill, a protest of pain or fighting; whinny — quivering and pleading, a cry of fear or need. - snort — explosive expulsion of air, of alarm or challenge; whinny — a voiced, trembling call through a partly closed throat. IN THE HOBBIT, the word is pure animal pathos: the thin, repeated, trembling voice of terror rising against the thunder, the sound by which the reader hears the ponies' helplessness.
