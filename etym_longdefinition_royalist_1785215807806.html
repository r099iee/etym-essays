ROYALIST — a supporter of the crown, in politics and in temperament A royalist is a person who supports a monarch, monarchy, or a reigning royal house, or who is associated with or devoted to the king and his cause. In the reading passage — "a living-romance heroine of the royalist volition and questionable destiny" — the word is used with striking figurative force as an adjective: Carlyle describes Harriet Taylor Mill's will as "royalist," meaning proud, imperious, self-possessed, and aristocratically unbending, as if her very character carried the regal authority of a monarch or the unswerving loyalty of a partisan devoted to an exiled sovereign. The word thus has two lives — one strictly political and historical, one temperamental and romantic — and both are in play in modern English. SENSE 1 — The historical and political sense In its primary, historically anchored meaning, royalist names a partisan of the crown, a supporter of monarchy against its opponents:
  • The word is born in England in the 1640s, in the English Civil War, where royalists were the Cavaliers who fought for King Charles I against the Parliamentarian Roundheads.
  • It was applied across Europe to all defenders of monarchy: the Jacobites who fought to restore the exiled Stuart kings, the French royalists who defended the Bourbon monarchy during the Revolution, the Spanish Carlists, and the Italian supporters of the crown in the Risorgimento.
  • In modern political science it remains the standard, largely neutral term for one who upholds monarchy as a form of government, and it is used of contemporary monarchist movements from Thailand to the United Kingdom.
SENSE 2 — The figurative, temperamental, and adjectival sense Because the vocabulary of monarchy is rich in connotations of dignity, pride, and command, royalist has developed a secondary, non-political life:
  • Used adjectivally of a quality, a bearing, or a will, royalist means proud, haughty, imperious, self-possessed, and aristocratic — as in Carlyle's "royalist volition," where the will of a romance heroine wears the lofty dignity of a queen.
  • The register is romantic and high-flown: a "royalist" temperament defies circumstance with self-command, without any literal loyalty to a crown.
  • This sense sits close to regal and aristocratic, but keeps the partisan energy of the political word: it is a will that not only looks royal but fights, with loyalty, for its own sovereignty.
SENSE 3 — Associated and derived uses The word belongs to a small family that shares its root and its politics:
  • Royalist (noun) — a supporter of the crown; (adjective) — of, relating to, or supporting a monarch or monarchy.
  • Royalism (noun) — the doctrine or practice of supporting monarchy.
  • Royal, royalty, royalist, and the surname Royale all descend from the same Old French roial "kingly," and the -ist suffix marks the believer or partisan, as in loyalist, socialist, capitalist.
  • In French and other Romance languages the parallel royaliste / royalista is current for the same political sense.
THE SEMANTIC FIELD AND ITS BOUNDARIES
  • A monarchist is the modern, neutral political-science term for anyone who supports monarchy as a form of government, without emotional loyalty to a particular dynasty; royalist is the older, more romantic, more partisan word.
  • A loyalist is one faithful to an established authority or cause — in the American Revolution, to the British Crown — and can apply to non-royal allegiances, whereas a royalist is specifically devoted to the king.
  • A Cavalier is the colourful historical name for a royalist partisan of the English Civil War, with overtones of gallantry and swagger; royalist is the general term.
  • A Tory is a specific party label (originally the nickname for the court party of the late 17th century) that has evolved into a political party name, not a synonym for royalist.
  • Regal and aristocratic are pure temperament adjectives denoting stately, noble bearing, with no political commitment whatsoever; royalist, even in its figurative use, keeps the energy of allegiance and command.
COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER
  • Typical phrases: "royalist forces," "royalist cause," "royalist sympathies," "a staunch royalist," "royalist volition" (Carlyle), "French royalists," "the royalist faction."
  • Register: historical and political writing is its home; the adjective use in literary and biographical prose is deliberately elevated, romantic, and slightly archaic.
  • In the Carlyle passage the political term is pressed into service for a psychological portrait — the whole point being that Harriet's will behaves like a monarch: proud, sovereign, and unbending in a "questionable destiny."
WHY THE WORD MEANS ALL OF THIS The journey from the 1640s battlefield to Carlyle's drawing-room portrait is continuous: the royalist is defined by devotion to the king and by a pride that will not bend, and that double definition — partisan loyalty plus aristocratic self-command — is exactly what allows the word to describe a temperament rather than a party. When Carlyle calls Harriet's volition "royalist," he compresses the entire history of the word into a single adjective: a will that serves its own crown and bows to nothing else.
