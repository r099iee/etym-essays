Syracuse (Italian Siracusa; Greek Συράκουσαι, Syrakousai) is the English name of the Greek city founded on the south-eastern coast of Sicily and of the modern Italian city that occupies the same site. In the source sentence — “...the discovery of the tomb of Archimedes at Syracuse” — it denotes the city where Archimedes was born, worked and died, and outside whose walls Cicero found his tomb in 75 BCE. It is a place name, with no common-noun sense in English, and its reference is fixed by geography and history rather than by lexical definition.

What is denoted

  • The Greek city: founded, according to Thucydides, in 733 BCE by Corinth under the oikist Archias, on the island of Ortygia, whose freshwater spring, the fountain of Arethusa, made the site habitable. Within two centuries it was the largest Greek city in the west.
  • The tyrant city: under Gelon (485–478) and Hieron I (478–466) Syracuse dominated Greek Sicily; after the democratic interlude of 466 it survived the great Athenian expedition of 415–413, then passed under Dionysius I (405–367), who walled the plateau of Epipolae and built the fortress that made the city almost impregnable.
  • The Roman city: captured by Marcellus in 212 BCE after a two-year siege, Syracuse became the capital of the province of Sicilia, a city of Greek institutions, Latin administration and celebrated artworks carried off to Rome.
  • The modern city: Siracusa, capital of its own province, rebuilt in Baroque style after the earthquake of 1693, and since 2005 a UNESCO World Heritage site together with the rock necropolis of Pantalica.

What makes it memorable

  • Archimedes: the city's most famous citizen, whose engines held off the Roman fleet and whose tomb Cicero recovered from the brambles.
  • The Athenian disaster: the destruction of the expedition of 415–413 BCE, narrated by Thucydides in Books VI and VII, is one of the great set pieces of ancient historiography and fixed Syracuse in the Greek imagination as the city that broke Athens.
  • Plato: he visited Syracuse three times, drawn by the hope of turning a tyrant into a philosopher-king; the experiment failed, and he is said to have been sold into slavery on one occasion.
  • The theatre: the great Greek theatre carved into the hillside, still used for classical drama, and the quarries known as the Latomia del Paradiso, including the cave Caravaggio nicknamed the Ear of Dionysius.

Derived and transferred uses

  • Syracusan: the adjective and demonym, used of the city's coins, its constitution and its mathematicians.
  • Siracusa: the Italian form, increasingly used in English travel writing and on maps, often alongside the English name.
  • Transferred names: Syracuse in New York State, named in the 1820s after the Sicilian city; Syracuse University; several smaller American townships. These are transfers of the name, not extensions of its meaning.
  • Allusive use: “a Syracuse” or “the Syracuse of the north” appears occasionally in journalism as a loose metaphor for a provincial city with an outsized ancient past.

Register and collocations

The name is neutral and learned. It collocates with ancient, Greek, siege, tyrant, Archimedes, Cicero, Ortygia and Athenian expedition. In English it carries a faint air of the Grand Tour and of classical scholarship; there is no pejorative sense, and no colloquial one.

Why it matters in this reading

The sentence mentions Syracuse almost in passing, as the address of a grave. But the choice is not accidental: Syracuse is where Greek mathematics, Greek tyranny and Roman conquest intersect, and Archimedes' tomb outside its walls is the perfect emblem of that intersection. A Roman quaestor, educated on Greek texts, reads a Greek geometer's epitaph in a cemetery outside a Greek city that Rome now governs.
