Elk-teeth joins two ancient Germanic words whose histories diverge then reunite on a beaded dress. Elk continues Old English eolh, eolas elk, from Proto-Germanic *alxiz, *algiz, attested as Old Norse elgr, Swedish alg, German Elch. Julius Caesar already notes Germanic alces (Latinized). The deeper Indo-European source is debated: most connect it to *h1el- reddish, tawny, the deer-colour root giving Greek elaphos deer, Armenian eln hind, Welsh elain hind. Others posit a northern substratum animal name *algh- with no further kin. What is certain is continuity of a large cervid word shifting reference as fauna changed.

In Britain elk after medieval extinction came to mean the Irish giant deer in antiquarian use, while in North America colonists transferred elk to the wapiti (Cervus canadensis), Shawnee wapiti white rump, and in Eurasia modern elk often means moose (Alces alces). American elk-teeth thus almost always means wapiti ivories, two per animal, creamy and prized. Phonetics: OE eolh /eolx/ simplifies /lx/ to /lk/ in Middle English elk, stable since.

  • Elk attestations: Caesar alces, OE glossaries eolh, 16th-century Gesner elk, 19th-century Catlin elk-tooth dresses valued at 100+ teeth.
  • Cultural shift: British extinct beast to American game animal to fraternal emblem (1868 Order of Elks takes elk-teeth as badge).

Teeth continues Old English toth (plural teth) from Proto-Germanic *tanths, from PIE participial *h3dont- the eating one, from *h1ed- to eat (Latin edens, Greek odont-, Sanskrit dant-). Ablaut and Verner alternation give Germanic *tanth- with nasal. Old English shows umlaut plural teth becoming modern teeth /ti:th/ versus singular tooth /tu:th/ after Great Vowel Shift divergence. Hyphenated elk-tooth / elk-teeth follows regular noun-noun compounding (catfish, dogtooth), pluralized on head (teeth). Trade jargon lexicalizes the plural because teeth circulated in pairs and dozens, rarely singly.

  • Sound shifts: PIE *d- to Germanic *t- by Grimm, *-ont- to *-anth-; OE fricative voicing tooth /th/ versus teeth /th/ preserved.
  • Semantic stability: always hard mouth structures; monetary use adds value without changing anatomy.
  • Literary attestations: Beowulf toth, Chaucer teeth, Lewis and Clark journals elk tusks as currency, Parkman and Catlin frontier rates.

Together, elk-teeth illustrate how an anatomical relic becomes wealth when culture prizes rarity and shine. Industrial coinage and game laws ended monetary use, but jewellery and regalia keep the compound alive, now with imitation alternatives for ethical craft. The source rate - two teeth to pony - preserves a moment when zoology was economy.

Modern wildlife management sustains limited bull harvests, so ivories remain available as byproducts, while Native artisans revive quill-and-tooth dresses for powwows and museums. Collectors distinguish old trade teeth with patina and hand-drilled holes from modern gold-mounted novelties. Whether as biology, money, or emblem, elk-teeth endure because two small teeth condense hunt, beauty, and value in one portable object.
