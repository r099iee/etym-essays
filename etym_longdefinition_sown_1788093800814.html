sown (pronounced /səʊn/) is the past participle of the strong verb sow, "to scatter seed over the ground for growth", and it is also used as a participial adjective. It belongs to the small surviving remnant of English's Old English class VII strong verbs: sow – sowed – sown, alongside the later, competing weak form sowed which has challenged it since the sixteenth century. In the phrase "Over snow by winter sown" (J. R. R. Tolkien, The Hobbit) the word carries its full participial force: snow that winter has scattered or spread abroad, exactly as a sower casts seed.

Primary sense — agriculture. At its core, sown describes seed that has been deliberately cast, broadcast or planted into prepared ground so that it will germinate:

  • Of seed: placed in or on the soil with the intention of growth — "barley sown in April", "seed sown too deep will rot".
  • Of land or fields: covered or planted with seed — "sown fields", "land sown with clover". The subject can be the crop (a field sown with wheat) or the ground itself (the acres were sown).
  • Of the act itself: the participle presupposes a sower; sowing is the oldest form of deliberate cultivation, historically done by broadcasting seed by hand and then harrowing it in.

Secondary sense — strewing and covering. By a natural physical extension from casting seed, sown means "scattered or spread over a surface", with the ground-metaphor still faintly alive: a lawn "sown with daisies", a sky "sown with stars" (a phrase beloved of Victorian poets), a path "sown with gravel". The bridge is purely physical — to sow is to throw small particles so that they lie scattered over an area — and the boundary between the agricultural and the merely strewn sense is often blurred in poetic usage.

Tertiary sense — figurative dissemination. Since the sixteenth century, and decisively reinforced by the King James Bible of 1611, sown means "implanted, disseminated, or introduced so as to develop later", used of abstract things:

  • "Seeds of doubt, division, discord or suspicion were sown" — the standard journalistic and political formula; the thing sown is a cause that will produce a visible effect in time.
  • Sown doctrines, rumours and teachings: spread widely and deliberately.
  • Providential idioms: "you reap what you sow" (Galatians 6:7), "sow the wind and reap the whirlwind" (Hosea 8:7), "sow dragon's teeth" (from the myth of Cadmus — to plant the source of future conflict).

The semantic bridge for every figurative use is temporal and agricultural: sowing is an investment whose harvest comes later and is proportional to what was planted. Hence morality, prophecy, rhetoric and politics all borrowed the verb early and never gave it back.

Sense inventory and register.

  • sown (formal/literary) versus sowed (conversational, more frequent in American English): both are correct past participles; "sown" retains the older strong-verb morphology and a faintly biblical or poetic gravity, which is exactly why Tolkien chose it for his verse.
  • As a pure adjective: "sown land" = land under cultivation from seed, opposed to fallow, pasture or waste.
  • Collocations: "sown with" (covered with), "freshly sown", "newly sown", "sown broadcast" (agricultural jargon: scattered without rows), "direct-sown" (horticulture: seed planted where it will grow rather than in trays).
  • Figurative extensions in modern usage: "to seed" ideas, "seeding" markets and "planting" thoughts are all re-borrowings of the same ancient metaphor through the noun seed.

Literary and cultural echoes. The verb sow carries two millennia of biblical weight: the Parable of the Sower (Matthew 13), "They that sow in tears shall reap in joy" (Psalm 126:5), and Paul's harvest of the flesh against the harvest of the Spirit (Galatians 6:7-8). Shakespeare plays freely on the word for both planting and begetting, and Milton's Paradise Lost uses "sown" of the earth's first produce after the Fall. Tolkien's line "Over snow by winter sown" deliberately fuses the literal scatter of snowfall with an archaic, sonorous participle, making winter itself a sower — a quiet, powerful image of nature broadcasting seed across the season.

Grammar note. Because sown is a participle it appears (1) in perfect constructions with have or be ("has sown", "was sown"), (2) in the passive voice ("the seed was sown"), and (3) as an adjective before or after nouns ("sown acres", "a field sown with rye"). It never inflects for number or gender. The verb is transitive in its agricultural core, and the participle is genuinely formed from the past-participial stem sāwen of Old English, surviving into the present as the literary doublet of "sowed".
