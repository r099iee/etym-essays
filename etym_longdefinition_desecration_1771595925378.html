desecration (noun): The action of treating something sacred with violent disrespect; the deliberate stripping away of holiness from a person, thing, or place; profanation, unhallowing. In the Preface to Modern Painters John Ruskin writes: "Where the blame has been warranted and deserved by the desecration of the highest powers of the human mind." The theological word is startling in its setting, and the shock is deliberate: Ruskin is not describing a desecrated church but the misuse of "the highest powers of the human mind" — the faculties of perception, imagination, and truth that, in his moral aesthetics, stand to the artist as the altar stands to the priest. To debase those powers in pursuit of falsehood is, in his vocabulary, a species of sacrilege; hence blame is not merely earned but "warranted and deserved," the doubled legal-moral phrase confirming that the desecration is a real offence with real accountability. The word asks the reader to feel the violation as a desecration — an unhallowing of what ought to remain set apart and pure. Polysemy and related senses:
  • Religious sense (primary, oldest): the violent defilement or profanation of a consecrated thing — a church, altar, temple, grave, or holy object. "The desecration of the cathedral" — the sense in which the word entered English in the seventeenth century, as the exact antonym of consecration.
  • Secular extension (19th century): the contemptuous violation of anything held in high reverence — a monument, a memory, an ideal, an institution, "the desecration of the flag." This is Ruskin's sense: the highest powers of the human mind are treated as though they were profane.
  • Reverential and emotional sense: the ruining of something precious to a person or community, even where nothing is religious — "the desecration of a quiet place," "the desecration of a memory" — transferring the sacred/ordinary boundary from religion to value.
  • Technical theological sense (contrast): in Catholic and Anglican theology, desecration is the act that undoes a consecration, and a desecrated object may require rites of reconsecration; the noun sits at the head of a paired vocabulary with consecration, sanctification, and hallowing.
The historical bridge is the boundary between the sacred and the common. Latin sacer meant both "holy" and "accursed" — the ancient taboo was two-sided, a thing set apart for blessing or for doom — and consecration sets a thing apart for the gods while desecration tears it back. The prefix de- is the reversive: desecration is, etymologically, the "undoing of the sacred." The word entered English in the seventeenth century as the theological antonym of consecration; its nineteenth-century secular extension, in which Ruskin is a pioneer, transferred the altar-vocabulary to ethics, so that the misuse of the mind's highest powers came to be felt as an unhallowing. In modern usage the religious sense remains, but the word is at least as often applied to monuments, memory, ideals, and the natural world — wherever a community draws the line between what is holy and what is merely useful. Register and usage notes:
  • Desecration is the active crime; profanation is both the act and the resulting state.
  • Sacrilege is narrower and more technical — the specific offence of violating or stealing consecrated things; desecration is the broader act of making unholy, of which sacrilege may be a species.
  • The word is intense and rhetorical; it is not used of minor damage or mild disrespect, but only of violations felt to be grave.
  • Its antonym chain — consecrate/consecration, sanctify/sanctification, hallow — makes it part of a large and ancient vocabulary of the sacred and its undoing.
