Lineated is an adjective meaning marked, scored, or furnished with lines, whether as visible stripes, grooves, or wrinkles on a surface, or as text arranged in successive lines on a page or screen. In its most literal physical sense it describes anything ruled, streaked, striated, or veined with fine linear markings, as in a lineated leaf, a lineated shell, a lineated face furrowed by age, or the lineated woodpecker named for the white stripes running down its neck. The core image is linearity made visible, a surface or body organized by parallel or consecutive strokes that the eye can follow.

In the source context, lineated is used in its textual and prosodic sense, where writing as a consecutive lineated notation means writing laid out left to right, down a line, left to right, in the standard alphabetic block of modern print. Here lineated does not mean striped but means set into lines, broken into the horizontal rows that define prose and verse on page and screen. The author contrasts this consecutive lineated order with the suppressed agenda of voice, rhythm, and performance that linear print flattens, arguing that the computer screen for the first time allows that suppressed counterculture of intonation, timing, and emphasis to be expressed visually through spacing, breaking, and diagramming rather than forced into uniform justified lines.

In poetics and rhetoric, lineated carries a technical opposition between lineated verse and run-on prose, where to lineate a text is to decide where each line ends, and a lineated poem is one whose line-breaks are deliberate rhythmic and semantic units rather than mere word-wrap. Teachers speak of lineated versus paragaraphed presentation of the same sentence to show how breaking into lines changes breath, stress, and meaning, while editors speak of lineated manuscripts where every verse or speech begins on a new line. In art history, palaeography, and natural history the sense remains concrete, describing hatching, engraving, bark fissures, geological striations, and animal coloration formed in lines.

  • Physically striped or furrowed sense: marked with thin lines, grooves, or colour streaks, e.g. a lineated beetle elytron, a lineated rock surface, a lineated forehead. Polysemy bridge: directly from Latin linea as linen thread to any thread-like mark, transferring sewing-thread image to any narrow elongated mark on skin, shell, stone, or feather.
  • Textually arranged-in-lines sense: set out as successive horizontal lines, e.g. lineated prose notation, a lineated transcript, lineated verse. Polysemy bridge: via metonymy from the drawn rule to the row of script resting on an invisible rule, so that to be furnished with lines comes to mean to be organized into rows for reading.
  • Prosodic and analytical sense: broken for breath and voice, e.g. to present a speech lineated by intonation units rather than as a solid paragraph. Polysemy bridge: via functional extension where line-break is no longer neutral wrapping but a notation of pause, pitch, and emphasis, turning layout into a score for oral performance.
  • Taxonomic and descriptive epithet sense: in binomial names such as lineatus, lineata, lineated woodpecker, lineated parakeet, denoting species distinguished by striped plumage or skin. Polysemy bridge: via scientific Latin borrowing where lineatus as striped becomes a fixed diagnostic epithet, narrowing general lined to species-defining streak pattern.

Grammatically lineated is most often a participial adjective from the verb to lineate, to mark with lines or to divide into lines, with the -ed ending marking completed state rather than past action. It is gradable only weakly, one text is more heavily lineated than another, and it combines with adverbs such as finely, faintly, boldly, consecutively, or densely. Register is formal, technical, and literary rather than colloquial, appearing in ornithology, botany, geology, bibliography, and poetics, where plain lined would sound too domestic. To call writing consecutive lineated notation, as here, is to stress the cultural contingency of the line itself, that left-to-right rows are not natural speech but a technology of literacy with expressive costs and alternatives.
