- c. 1300 — vainglory appears as a religious term: the sin of seeking praise from men instead of from God, with the emphasis on the emptiness of the prize.
- Early 15th c. — vainglorious is coined as its adjective; it describes persons, sermons and works infected by that sin, and remains chiefly homiletic.
- 16th–17th c. — Reformation polemic and the stage keep the word busy: the vainglorious soldier and the vainglorious courtier are stock figures of satire.
- 18th–19th c. — the adjective is secularised and enlarged: empires, monuments, titles and political rhetoric are now called vainglorious, and the religious frame fades.
- 20th–21st c. — rare and deliberately archaic in tone; it survives as a literary and journalistic term of contempt for self-advertising leaders and national myths.
