The story of blister is a story of swelling: a word whose root means "to blow up, inflate" travelled from medieval French into English and then spread from the skin to paints, metals, plastics, and rhetoric.

Part 1: the Germanic root and the "blow/swell" family. The ultimate source is a Germanic root meaning "to swell, blow up, inflate," reconstructed as Proto-Germanic *blēs- (to blow) / *blēstr- (a blowing up, swelling) — the family that produced Old English blāwan "to blow," blæst "blast," blædre "bladder," and blain "a sore, swelling." The Indo-European ancestor is *bhel- (to swell, blow, bloom) — a root that also gave Sanskrit bhal- (to shine, swell), Greek phlydaina "a blister" (from phlyō "to swell, boil over"), and Latin flāre "to blow" and inflare "to inflate."

Part 2: the French bridge. The immediate ancestor of English blister is Old French blestre "a swelling, pustule, tumor," borrowed into Anglo-Norman and Middle English around the 13th century. The Middle English forms are blister and blyster (attested c. 1300). The French word itself was a loan from Germanic (Old French borrowed heavily from Frankish and other Germanic sources), so the word travelled: Germanic → Old French → Middle English, returning the "swell" concept to the island it had left centuries earlier.

Part 3: the skin senses. In Middle and Early Modern English, blister meant strictly a fluid-filled swelling of the skin — a result of burning, rubbing, or disease. Medical texts of the 14th–16th centuries used it for burns and plague-blisters; the verb "to blister" (raise a blister) is attested from the 15th century. The blister was also a deliberate medical instrument: blistering plasters (cantharides) were applied to raise counter-irritant blisters — a therapy that kept the word active in medical vocabulary for centuries.

Part 4: extension to materials. By the 17th century the word jumped from skin to inanimate surfaces: paint and varnish "blister" when trapped air or heat lifts the film into bubbles; "blister copper" and "blister steel" are named for their blistered, bubbled surfaces. This extension is pure metaphor — the same "swollen bubble" image applied to a new substrate — and it set up the word's modern industrial career.

Part 5: the packaging revolution. In the 20th century, the rise of thermoformed plastics gave English blister pack (1940s–50s): a transparent plastic bubble sealed to a backing card. The humble skin-blister word became a term of retail technology, showing how a vivid concrete noun can be re-purposed to name new inventions whenever the old image fits.

Part 6: rhetoric and idiom. Alongside the physical senses, the adjective blistering (from the verb, "to burn or scald") developed the rhetorical sense "scathing, ferocious": a blistering attack, a blistering pace, blistering heat. The proverb in the source context — "a jibe won't raise a blister" — is a small piece of folk wisdom in the long tradition of measuring words against physical wounds: sticks and stones may break bones, but words cannot raise a blister. The word thus carries, even in ordinary use, an implicit contrast between the superficial physical wound (blister) and the claimed invulnerability of the hardened speaker.

  • Sound shifts: PGmc *blēs-/*blēstr- → OF blestre → ME blister (the French borrowing preserved the Germanic cluster); modern /ˈblɪstə/.
  • Key attestations: ME blister/blyster (c. 1300); verb "to blister" (15th c.); blister copper/steel (17th c.); blister pack (20th c.).
  • Semantic drift: skin bubble (medieval) → surface bubble (materials) → plastic bubble (packaging) → scathing heat/criticism (blistering).
  • Socio-cultural factors: medieval medicine and burning injuries, 17th-c. metallurgy, 20th-c. plastics industry, proverbial folk wisdom about words vs wounds.
