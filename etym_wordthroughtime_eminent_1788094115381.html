- 15th century – purely spatial: “projecting, standing out physically”, of hills, towers, rocks, and bodily protuberances; a neutral descriptive adjective borrowed with the participial sense of Latin ēminēns.
- 15th–16th century – metaphorical transfer to persons: “rising above others in rank, virtue, or learning”; the spatial image of standing out from the crowd becomes a social judgement of superiority, and the word gains a strongly laudatory charge.
- 16th–17th century – abstraction into rank and dignity: the noun eminence comes to mean “exalted position”; “His Eminence” becomes the fixed honorific of cardinals (papal decree, 1630), giving the whole word-family an aura of ecclesiastical and courtly solemnity.
- 17th–18th century – legal fixation: “eminent domain” enters English legal vocabulary from Grotius’s dominium eminens (1625), shifting “eminent” into the register of state power and property law, where it means “supreme, paramount”.
- 18th–19th century – scholarly prestige: “eminent” solidifies as the standard epithet of scientists, judges, divines, and public men; collocations like “eminent scholar” and “eminent Victorian” make it a marker of institutional reputation — and satirists (Swift, Pope, Fielding) begin exploiting its pomposity.
- 20th century – technical narrowing and popular confusion: anatomy fixes “eminence” for bodily prominences (thenar eminence); “eminent” is increasingly confused with “imminent” and “immanent” in popular prose; scare-quoted “eminent” appears in journalism as irony chips away at its solemnity.
- 21st century – formal register with ironic undertow: still honorific in academic and official use, but in media and everyday speech it often carries a faintly old-fashioned or tongue-in-cheek flavour; “eminence grise” keeps the shadowy-adviser sense alive in political journalism.
