Dubious is a word whose entire modern personality — cautious hesitation, polite suspicion, quiet disapproval — is encoded in its Indo-European birth: it descends from the Proto-Indo-European numeral for two. Understanding that ancestry explains why a word meaning "of two minds" became the everyday English adjective for anything we cannot quite trust.

1. The Indo-European root. The chain begins with PIE *dwóh₁ ("two"), which produced the Latin cardinal duo ("two"). From duo Latin built the adjective dubius, attested from about 200 BCE in the comedies of Plautus, with the sense "doubtful, uncertain, hesitating." Its formation is transparent to a Latinist: a stem based on du- ("two") plus a suffix conveying "being in a state of two-ness," so that the original image is of a person pulled two ways, unable to settle on one answer. The internal details of that suffix are debated — the standard analysis posits *du-bhi-wos — but the conceptual core is not in doubt: dubius literally pictures the mind divided between two possibilities.

2. From adjective to adjective. Latin already possessed the fuller form dubiosus ("full of doubt, dubious"), an adjective built on dubium ("doubt, a point in doubt") with the familiar suffix -osus ("full of"). This is the form that medieval and Renaissance scholars carried forward, and it is the direct ancestor of the English word.

3. The loan into English (1540s). English dubious appears around 1540 as a learned borrowing, the kind of word introduced by humanists translating Latin and French works. For its first century it was a bookish term for "doubtful, uncertain," applied to questions, conclusions, and propositions. The earliest senses are objective: something is dubious because the evidence is not settled.

4. The psychological turn (17th century). In the seventeenth century the adjective extends from things to persons: a person can now be dubious, i.e. "inclined to doubt, hesitant, wavering." The word begins to describe an attitude rather than a property, gaining the modern pattern "to be dubious about something." This is the sense in which the speaker in Tolkien's passage quietly files away uncertain dragon-stories.

5. The moral shade (18th–19th centuries). During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word deepens into the moral register. A "dubious reputation," "dubious character," or "dubious dealings" now implies questionable honesty and the whiff of impropriety. The shift is a natural escalation: if a matter is doubtful in truth, it is a short step to suspecting that someone is hiding something. The word becomes a favourite of novelists for the shady gentleman whose past will not bear close inspection.

6. The ironic turn (19th century onward). The nineteenth century adds the characteristically British ironic construction — the "dubious honour," "dubious distinction," or "dubious pleasure." Here the word is used against the grain: the honour is real, but it is one nobody would envy. The joke depends entirely on the word's negative colouring and has made the construction a permanent fixture of English.

7. Modern usage. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries dubious flourishes in journalism and fact-checking (dubious claims, dubious provenance, dubious statistics), in law and finance (dubious transactions), and in everyday scepticism ("that sounds dubious"). It remains slightly formal, slightly polite, and reliably negative — the ideal word for saying "I do not believe this" without being rude.

8. Summary. From a PIE numeral for two, through Latin's image of a mind split between two paths, to a word for uncertain claims, shady reputations, and ironic honours — dubious is a two-millennia-long lesson in how the mathematics of two-ness became the psychology of doubt.
