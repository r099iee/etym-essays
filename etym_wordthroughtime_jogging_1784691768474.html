- 16th century - from the 1540s "jog" meant to shake up and down or nudge with a small jerk — the sense still alive in "jog one's memory" — a word born of short, percussive movement.
- 17th-19th century - the verb stepped sideways into locomotion: to "jog along" or "jog off" was to travel at an easy, bouncing trot, and Shakespeare's "Jog on, jog on, the foot-path way" fixed its light, rhythmical sound in verse; "jogtrot" then became a byword for any humdrum routine of life.
- 20th century to modern - the word performed an extraordinary transfer: from 1948 "jogging" named running slowly and steadily for exercise, and by the late 1960s it was a mass pursuit — the horse's jog-trot became the citizen's morning jog.
