1. An uncertain beginning
Unlike most English vocabulary, the family of trifling cannot be traced to a Proto-Indo-European root. The trail begins, as far as scholarship can follow it, in Old French of the 12th–13th centuries, where trufle (also spelled trufe, truffle) meant "mockery, jest, trickery, deceit." The origin of this Old French word is itself obscure: it may be an expressive or onomatopoeic formation, a dialectal variant, or — as many dictionaries cautiously allow — connected with the Latin word tuber "a swelling, a protuberance," the source of the modern truffle mushroom. The difficulty is that in Old French the same form trufle named both the act of mockery and the mushroom, and etymologists disagree over whether these are one word or two unrelated words that happened to converge. The honest account is that the word's deeper ancestry is unknown; this is one of those cases where an English word of French origin simply has no demonstrable root behind it.

2. From mockery to smallness
What is certain is the direction of the English semantic development. Middle English took the word as trufle and then trifle, and its first recorded meanings were those of the Old French original: a false tale, a trick, a piece of mockery. To "trifle with" someone was to deceive or make sport of them. But the word then slid down a slope of depreciation: a thing that is mocked is a thing not taken seriously; a thing not taken seriously is a thing of no worth; and by the 14th–15th centuries trifle meant "a thing of small value or importance," the sense that dominates today. This is the classic semantic move from the moral to the quantitative — from "something you laugh at" to "something tiny." The verb moved the same way, from "to mock, to deceive" toward "to deal with idly, to play, to waste time": Shakespeare's "trifle with" still carries the old sting of deception, while modern "trifling away one's time" carries only idleness.

3. The adjective trifling and its rise
The participial adjective trifling is recorded from the 16th century, meaning "of the nature of a trifle; unimportant, frivolous; and, of people, mocking or lightly jesting." It became a workhorse of moral and comic prose: the Renaissance moralist warns against "trifling pursuits"; the satirist describes "a trifling fellow." In Early Modern and later English the adjective divided into the two great uses that persist — the neutral "a trifling sum," "a trifling detail" (small, negligible), and the pejorative "a trifling person" (unserious, unreliable). The legal spirit is ancient in the family: English courts developed the doctrine de minimis non curat lex, "the law does not concern itself with trifles," so that a "trifling" injury or sum is treated as beneath litigation — a memorable fusion of Latin doctrine and English word.

4. The dessert and the mushroom
Two homonymic relatives complicate the picture. The English trifle, the dessert of sponge cake, fruit, custard, and cream, is first recorded in the 17th century and is named simply as an "easy, unimportant" confection — a dish so simple it was thought of as a trifle; the adjective trifling never refers to it. The truffle mushroom, meanwhile, descends from the same Old French trufle and, on the usual account, from Latin tuber; it is a separate thread of the same tangled skein, a reminder that the mockery-word and the mushroom-word shared a form in Old French before going their separate ways in English.

5. Modern life
In modern English trifling survives in all registers. It is standard in ordinary speech ("trifling details," "a trifling amount"), formal in legal prose, and colloquially reinvigorated as an insult — "trifling," "triflin'," for a person who is deceitful, shallow, or wastes others' time — a sense that reaches back, remarkably, to the Old French original of mockery and deceit. The word has thus come full circle: begun as mockery, it was tamed into smallness, and in slang it has regained its original harshness.
