Abyssinians is the plural of Abyssinian, a demonym used as both noun and adjective: "a native or inhabitant of Abyssinia", the historical English name for the Ethiopian highlands and, by extension, the Ethiopian Empire. The singular is Abyssinian; the plural Abyssinians names the people collectively and is the form used in the source. In the reading — "The Abyssinians were so meticulous in this regard that when a boy fell from a tree upon his companion and killed him..." — the word names a people credited with an exacting customary scale of compensation for injury and homicide.

Strictly, Abyssinia denoted the Christian, Semitic-speaking highland core of the Ethiopian state, especially the Amhara and Tigrayan provinces, rather than the whole territory of modern Ethiopia. Modern Ethiopia also includes the Oromo, Somali, Afar, Sidama, Wolayta and many other peoples, most of whom were not called Abyssinian in the older literature. The loose Victorian habit of treating "Abyssinian" and "Ethiopian" as interchangeable is therefore imprecise, though it is very common in nineteenth-century writing.

What the name denotes

  • A historical polity and region. The Ethiopian Empire, with its highland heartland, its Solomonic dynasty and its Christian church; the term covers roughly the northern and central highlands, not the lowland peripheries.
  • A cultural and religious identity. Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity, the Ge'ez liturgical and literary tradition, and the Amharic and Tigrinya languages are the markers that older writers had in mind when they spoke of Abyssinians.
  • A people in the plural. "The Abyssinians" can mean the inhabitants of the country collectively, or, more narrowly, the highland Christian elite that ruled it.
  • An adjective. "Abyssinian coffee", "the Abyssinian campaign", "Abyssinian Christianity" — attributive uses that refer to the country, its products or its institutions.

Historical outline

The name belongs to a region with one of the deepest state traditions in Africa. The Kingdom of Aksum flourished from roughly the first to the seventh century CE, trading with Rome, Arabia and India and adopting Christianity in the fourth century under King Ezana. After Aksum's decline came the Zagwe dynasty, then in 1270 the Solomonic dynasty, which traced its legitimacy to Solomon and the Queen of Sheba and ruled, with interruptions, until 1974. The highland kingdom preserved its independence through the medieval and early modern periods, repelled Egyptian and Italian encroachments, and in 1896 defeated an Italian army at Adwa, the decisive event that secured international recognition of Ethiopian sovereignty.

Italy occupied the country from 1936 to 1941, and Emperor Haile Selassie returned from exile to rule until the revolution of 1974. Since 1995 the state has been a federal republic. Throughout these changes, "Abyssinia" remained an English exonym, increasingly literary or nostalgic, while "Ethiopia" became the standard political name.

Law and the passage's claim

Ethiopia possesses a genuine and sophisticated legal heritage. The Fetha Nagast ("Law of the Kings"), a thirteenth-century Coptic compilation of canon and civil law translated into Ge'ez, was consulted in Ethiopian courts into the twentieth century; later emperors issued comprehensive codes in the 1930s and 1950s. Customary compensation for homicide and injury, negotiated between kin groups, was also widespread. The specific anecdote in the reading — a bereaved mother sending another son into the tree to fall upon the killer's neck — belongs to an older comparative-law tradition that delighted in such exact talionic puzzles. It should be read as an illustration of the principle of measured retribution, not as a quotation from an Ethiopian statute; the ethnographic accuracy of the story is uncertain.

Secondary and transferred senses

  • The Abyssinian cat. A short-haired breed with a ticked, "wild" coat, developed in Britain in the later nineteenth century and romantically said to descend from a cat brought back from Abyssinia. The breed name is genuine, the origin story doubtful.
  • Abyssinian well. A narrow driven tube well with a pointed screen, used where the water table is shallow; the name reflects nineteenth-century military engineering in the region.
  • Rastafari usage. "Abyssinia" has symbolic force in Rastafari, where Ethiopia, the land of Haile Selassie, figures as Zion; the name appears in songs, greetings and devotional language.
  • Older racial and ethnographic writing. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century anthropology sometimes classified "Abyssinians" as a distinct "race" or "type". Such usage is now obsolete and should be treated as a historical artefact.

Register and sensitivity

Today Abyssinian is not offensive, but it is dated and externally imposed. Ethiopian and Eritrean readers generally prefer Ethiopian, or the self-designation Habesha, which carries cultural rather than strictly territorial meaning. In historical writing the term remains useful because it names the pre-modern Christian highland polity that "Ethiopia" only partly describes. The plural Abyssinians is therefore best used with a clear historical anchor, as the reading does.
