Old English Formation - Satst continues the Old English second-person singular past form (þū sætst, "thou sat"), a regular inflected strong-verb form of sittan used in everyday Old English speech.
Early Modern Sacred Usage - In Early Modern English satst flourishes in the Bible, Shakespeare, and Milton as the intimate "thou"-form of the past tense, addressing God, loved ones, or a single soul with reverence; Milton uses it of the Spirit brooding on the abyss.
18th-19th Century Retreat - As the thou-paradigm dies out of standard speech, satst disappears from the living language and is preserved only in the King James Bible, liturgy, and archaizing poetry.
Contemporary Sense - Today satst is archaic and poetic, instantly signalling early modern or biblical diction, while modern English uses sat for all persons.
