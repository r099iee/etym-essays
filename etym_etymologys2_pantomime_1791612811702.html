Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*peh₂nts- / *panto-
"all, whole, entire"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 5th c. BCE)
πᾶς (neut. πᾶν, gen. παντός)
"all, every, each"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 4th c. BCE)
μῖμος (mîmos)
"actor, mime, imitator"
↓
Hellenistic Greek (c. 1st c. BCE)
παντόμιμος (pantómīmos)
"imitator of all things,"
"dancer playing all roles"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
pantomīmus
"solo dramatic dancer in dumb-show"
↓
Middle French (c. 16th c.)
pantomime
"dancer, theatrical mime"
↓
Modern English (c. 1600s)
pantomime
"silent dumb-show; holiday comedy"

Note: Greek pantómīmos combines pan- ('all') and mîmos ('imitator, actor'). The PIE root for mîmos is uncertain, likely onomatopoeic or expressive (*mey- 'to imitate, counter'). Latin pantomīmus originally designated the solo actor-dancer before the term shifted in English to denote the dramatic spectacle itself.
