morose (adj.) — Sullen, ill-tempered, gloomy, or sourly withdrawn in disposition. In Macaulay's History of England (1848–61), the word completes his rebuttal of nostalgia: those who measure their age against an imaginary golden age "may talk of degeneracy and decay," but "no man who is correctly informed as to the past will be disposed to take a morose or desponding view of the present." Morose here is a temperamental verdict — a way of looking at the present that refuses to be pleased. Core sense. A morose person is habitually sullen and gloomy, inclined to ill-temper and sourness, withdrawn from cheer, hard to please. The word describes a settled disposition or a prevailing mood rather than a momentary irritation: moroseness is a character that turns everything sour. Applied to moods, atmospheres, and faces ("a morose expression"), it names the visible, brooding absence of good humour. All senses and uses.
  • (1) Sullen, ill-tempered, gloomy (core, from the 16th c.). "A morose old man," "morose temperament." The dominant modern sense.
  • (2) Hard to please, fastidious to the point of peevishness (early and classical sense). Retained from Latin morosus "self-willed, full of one's own ways, hard to please"; in older English and in literary echo, morose can still mean "obstinately attached to one's own habits, difficult to satisfy."
  • (3) Psychopathological use (19th–20th c.). In older psychiatric writing, morose appears in descriptions of melancholic and depressive states — "morose melancholia," a patient "morose and withdrawn."
  • (4) Legal/archaic echo — slow, dilatory. Latin morosus (from mora, "delay") also meant "slow, dilatory" in some contexts; this sense is a false friend with the mos-derived word and survives only in etymological discussion.
  • (5) Applied to things and atmospheres (19th c. onward). "A morose sky," "morose silence," "a morose landscape" — the adjective extends to anything that seems sullenly cheerless.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. Latin morōsus is formed from mōs, mōris "custom, manner, habit, humour," plus the suffix -ōsus, "full of." Etymologically, then, a morose person is "full of his own customs" — wholly governed by his own habits and self-will. In classical Latin the adjective ran a revealing course: it could mean "self-willed, obstinate" and "fastidious, hard to please" (a morose critic of style, an old man who will have things his own way), and only in later moralising usage did it harden into "sullen, ill-tempered, gloomily cross." The semantic engine is habit: a person so fixed in his own ways that he is affronted by everything outside them, and so turns sour, withdrawn, and resentful. That is why morose names a character, not a passing mood — and why Macaulay's coupling of "morose or desponding" is precise: the one refuses to be pleased, the other has given up hope. Register and usage notes.
  • Register: literary-to-standard; stronger and more judgmental than "gloomy," more inward and heavy than "grumpy."
  • Collocations: "morose disposition," "morose silence," "morose expression," "grow morose," "morose and withdrawn."
  • Near-synonyms by nuance: sullen (silent stubbornness), sulky (childish petulance), surly (active rudeness), dour (stern severity), glum (dull cheerlessness), saturnine (heavy, sardonic gloom) — see similarWords.
  • Antonyms: cheerful, genial, affable, sunny, jovial, sanguine.
  • Related forms: moroseness, morosity (both nouns).
