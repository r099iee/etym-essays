Vexing is a word whose whole history is a study in the transfer of a jolt from the body to the mind. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *weǵh-, "to carry, to travel in a vehicle" — a root of roads, wagons, and transport that spread across the Indo-European world with extraordinary vitality: in Sanskrit it gave váhati, "he carries," and vāhana, a sacred mount or vehicle; in Germanic, Grimm's Law turned the initial glide-and-velar into *wegaz, "road," whence English way, wagon (*wagnaz), wain, and weigh (*wegan, "to carry," hence "to weigh"); and in Italic it produced the Latin verb vehere, "to carry, convey" — source of English vehicle, convey, vector, and voyage.

The decisive Latin formation was vexāre, a frequentative verb built on vehere: where vehere meant to carry once, vexāre meant to keep carrying back and forth, to haul repeatedly, to shake and toss. The physical core is unmistakable — the violent jolting of a springless Roman wagon over rutted roads, a small boat tossed by a storm, a body shaken by force. Roman writers — Caesar, Cicero, and the historians — used it both physically and morally: an army "vexed" a province by plundering it; the sea "vexed" a fleet. From the very beginning the word had an edge of violence and repetition that the simple "carry" never had.

The transfer to the mind was natural and quick. To vex a person came to mean to toss their thoughts about, to harass, to disturb their peace. Latin vexāre thus carried both the physical jolting and the psychological harassment into the Middle Ages, and the word entered the Romance languages with both meanings. Old French vexer (12th century) and Anglo-Norman vexer carried it into Middle English around 1330 as vexen; the earliest English senses are serious and forceful — severe tormenting, demonic affliction, military oppression, legal harassment. In medieval religious writing the devil "vexes" the faithful; in the chronicles, armies and tyrants "vex" the land.

English history since the 14th century shows a progressive softening and domestication:

  • 14th–17th centuries: vex retains its heavy register — affliction, torment, harassment. The 1611 King James Bible uses it of affliction and grief; "vexed with cares" is the standard expression of a disturbed mind.
  • 18th century onward: the word softens dramatically in ordinary use. In the polite domestic fiction of the era of this card — Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, where Mrs. Bennet cries "You take delight in vexing me" — the word has become the sharp, small-scale irritation of domestic teasing, a witty husband's jabs at a nervous wife, far from the demonic torment of medieval English.
  • Legal retention of the strong sense: the law kept the older force alive in vexatious litigation — lawsuits brought purely to harass an opponent — a technical survival of the word's violent past.
  • Modern usage: vexing is now a literary, slightly formal adjective for persistent mild-to-moderate annoyance, applied chiefly to problems, questions, and situations rather than to persons tormenting each other: "a vexing problem," "the vexed question of..."

The word's career is thus a perfect record of the civilizing of irritation in English — from the jolting wagon and the storm-tossed ship, through the tormentor and the devil, to the teasing husband and the vexing puzzle. Its sound has helped: the short, sharp veks- of "vex" is itself a little jolt, a percussive onset and a sibilant snap that enacts the irritation it names. In Mrs. Bennet's mouth, it catches the exact pitch of her nerves — a word that is no longer torment, but not yet mere annoyance, still carrying in its etymology the memory of being shaken.
