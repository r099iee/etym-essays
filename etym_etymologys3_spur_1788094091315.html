An instrument named for the ankle. The English word spur is a direct inheritance from Proto-Germanic *spuron, reconstructed from Old English spora/spura, Old Norse spori, Old High German sporo (modern German Sporn) and Middle Dutch spore (modern Dutch spoor-related forms). The standard etymology connects the Germanic word with the Indo-European base *spere- 'ankle', attested in Greek sphuron 'ankle': the spur is, quite simply, the instrument worn on the ankle — the heel-spike. (An alternative school links it instead with the Germanic base of spurn, 'to kick', making the spur 'the kicking instrument'; both derivations are respectable, and the chain above follows the mainstream view.) The word is native Germanic: there is no Latin or French ancestor, though Latin calcar 'spur', from calx 'heel', shows that other Indo-European languages named the instrument by the same body part.

  • Old English (8th–11th centuries): OE spora, spura is attested from the early glosses onward in the plain sense 'spur for urging a horse'. Horse-culture was central to Germanic warrior society — the horse was a mark of rank — and the spur, like the sword, was part of the equipment of the noble rider. The word is thoroughly concrete at this stage: a metal point or spike fastened to the heel.
  • Chivalry and the golden spurs (12th–15th centuries): In medieval England the spur became a symbol of knighthood. At the ceremony of knighting, golden spurs were buckled on the new knight (in contrast to the plain iron ones of common riders); 'to win one's spurs' — originally to be knighted — became the idiom for earning any distinction through merit. The spur's function (urging the horse on) and its prestige (the knightly badge) together prepare the word for metaphor: from the 14th century 'spur' begins to mean anything that urges or incites.
  • The figurative goad (16th century): By the 16th century the abstract sense is fully established: 'the spur of necessity', 'the spur of honour', 'the spur of ambition' — the instrument's function has become a general noun for incitement. The idiom 'on the spur of the moment' ('on a sudden impulse', first recorded 1801) completes the transfer: the rider's instant heel-touch becomes the type of unreflecting sudden action.
  • The mountain's point (17th–18th centuries): Meanwhile the shape of the instrument — a projecting point — generates a family of geometric senses. From the 17th century topographical writing uses 'spur' for a lateral ridge or projection descending from a mountain, a sense familiar from adventure prose and, later, from Tolkien's 'great spur of the Mountain flung out southwards'. The cock's spur (the horny spike on a bird's leg) is equally old; the botanical sense — the tubular nectar-bearing projection of a flower — arrives with the naming of the larkspur (1570s), whose spurred flowers were said to resemble a lark's heel.
  • Industrial and scientific expansion (19th–20th centuries): The 19th century multiplies technical senses: railway 'spur lines' (short branch lines) appear with the American railroad boom; geology and surveying use 'spur' for ridges and rock projections; architecture applies it to the bracket at the base of a column; anatomy and medicine speak of the 'calcaneal spur', a bony outgrowth on the heel-bone — a usage that completes a neat circle, since the word began at the heel.
  • Phonetic history: OE spora/spura (short o or u) becomes ME spure/spore; the u-variant wins, and its short vowel before r develops in early modern English into the NURSE vowel, giving modern /spɜː/. The spelling spur is fixed by the 16th century. Modern use is split: everyday speech favours the figurative senses ('a spur to action'), while the riding instrument survives in equestrian contexts and the landscape sense persists in geography and narrative prose.
