Four separate root words — each with its own complete chain. CHAIN 1 — detritus: 1. PIE *terh₁- "to rub, to grind, to thresh, to turn" (reconstructed; the root of friction, attested across the family) — Greek teirō "to rub, wear down, distress," Sanskrit tirati "rubs through, crosses," Latin terere "to rub, grind." 2. Latin terere "to rub, grind, wear away" (attested) — the direct Italic verb; its family gives English "trite" (worn smooth by use), "contrite" (heart ground down), "attrition" (a wearing away), "tribulation" (from tribulum, the threshing-sledge). 3. Latin dēterere "to rub off, to wear away" (dē- "off, away" + terere; attested in classical Latin, e.g. Pliny). 4. Latin dētrītus "worn away, worn down" (past participle of dēterere; attested in classical Latin). 5. New Latin detritus (scientific coinage, c. 1794, in geology) — "waste matter worn away from rock"; passed into French as détritus. 6. English detritus (attested 1795 in geology; generalized to "debris, worn-away fragments" by the 19th century). CHAIN 2 — anterior: 1. PIE *h₂ent- "front, forehead, in front" (reconstructed); the adverbial *h₂énti "in front, before." Cognates: Sanskrit ánti "opposite, near," Greek antí "against, in front of," Hittite ḫanti "in front," Tocharian ānte "forehead." 2. Latin ante "before, in front of" (preposition and adverb; attested in the earliest Latin records, e.g. the Twelve Tables, 5th c. BCE). 3. Latin anterior "in front, foremost" — a comparative formed on the stem of ante (attested in Latin of the Imperial period and Late Latin). 4. English anterior (attested 1571 in English; originally anatomical and spatial, "located at the front"; the temporal sense "earlier, prior" developed alongside). CHAIN 3 — Cyclopean (compound of kýklos "circle" + ōps "eye"): 3a. The element kýklos: 1. PIE *kʷel- "to turn, move around" (reconstructed) → reduplicated *kʷekʷl- "wheel, circle." 2. Greek kýklos "circle, ring, wheel, round" (attested in Homer, 8th c. BCE) — source of English "cycle," "cyclone," "bicycle." 3b. The element ōps: 3. PIE *h₃ekʷ- "to see; eye" (reconstructed) — Greek ōps "eye, face, voice" (in compounds: ōps appears in kýklōps; also ophthalmós "eye," autópsia "seeing with one's own eyes"). 4. Greek Kýklōps "the Cyclops" — Homeric, 8th c. BCE — literally "round-eyed," from kýklos "circle" + ōps "eye"; plural Kýklōpes. 5. Greek Kyklṓpeios "of the Cyclopes, Cyclopean" (adjective formed on Kýklōps). 6. English Cyclopean (adjective; attested 17th c., applied to the vast irregular masonry of Mycenae and Tiryns, which the ancient Greeks believed built by the Cyclopes). CHAIN 4 — conduit: 1. PIE *dewk- "to lead, to draw, to pull" (reconstructed; LIV *dewk-) — Sanskrit dócati "to draw, milk," Gothic tiuhan "to lead," Latin dūcere "to lead." 2. Latin dūcere "to lead, draw, conduct" (attested in early Latin) — source of English "duct," "duke" (leader), "education" (a leading out). 3. Latin condūcere "to lead together, bring together, assemble, hire" (con- "together" + dūcere; attested). 4. Latin conductus "led together, assembled" (past participle of condūcere) — re-used in Medieval Latin as conductus "an escort, a hired troop; a channel or aqueduct for water." 5. Old French conduit "a channel, pipe, aqueduct; a conduct, escort, guidance" (attested) → Middle English conduit (13th c.). 6. English conduit (attested 14th c.; modern technical senses: a pipe for fluids, an electrical duct, any channel of conveyance). No stage is guessed in any of the four chains; where an origin is debated (the element katá of the related catacomb, or the exact reconstruction of *kʷel-/*h₃ekʷ-), the standard, most widely accepted form is given with its evidence.
