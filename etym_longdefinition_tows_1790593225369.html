Tows in the sentence the boat tows you along means pulls, drags, or hauls you forward while you stand on a board holding a rope. It is the third-person singular of the verb tow, meaning to draw something behind one by means of a line, cable, or other attachment, using superior motive power to impart motion to a passive or semi-active load. The tower provides propulsion; the towed provides weight and direction to be overcome.

The core sense is straightforwardly physical and ancient: one vessel tows another, a car tows a trailer, a tug tows a barge, a horse tows a canal boat. The towed object may be on water, land, or in air, as when a plane tows a glider. The verb implies continuous traction over distance, not a single yank, and a flexible connection, typically rope, hawser, chain, or tow-bar, rather than rigid pushing. To tow is therefore to lead from the front by pulling, as opposed to push from behind, carry on top, or guide alongside.

Extended and specialised senses include:

  • Nautical towing: to tow a disabled ship into harbour, to take in tow, to tow astern or alongside. Maritime law distinguishes towing for salvage versus ordinary towage, with precise liabilities.
  • Roadside towing: to tow away illegally parked cars, to call a tow truck, to tow a caravan. Here towing is institutional, punitive, or assistive, performed by specialised vehicles with winches and flatbeds.
  • Canal and river towing: historically horses towed narrowboats from the towpath, the path alongside the canal reserved for towing animals, preserving tow in landscape vocabulary even after engines replaced horses.
  • Aviation and sport towing: to tow a glider, to tow a banner, to tow a water-skier or aquaplaner. The Wodehouse aquaplaning scene is exactly this: a motor-boat tows a rider, who is not self-propelled but towed into planing speed.
  • Figurative towing: to tow children along, to tow a reluctant companion, to be towed into a scheme, metaphorically dragging a person or project by influence, authority, or enthusiasm, often with comic reluctance.
  • Noun tow: a tow-rope, a tow-truck, to give someone a tow, to have someone in tow, meaning literally under traction and figuratively in attendance or under control, as in she arrived with three admirers in tow.

The polysemy bridge from boat-pulling to car-pulling to person-dragging is continuity of force geometry. All tows share an agent ahead, a patient behind, a taut link, and forward motion. Whether the link is hemp, steel, or social obligation, the physics of leading by pulling remains. This is why in tow so naturally means accompanied by, as if tied: followers are imagined as trailers. The comedy in Wodehouse often exploits this geometry, with masterful characters towing reluctant Berties, aunts towing nephews, and motorboats towing dignified gentlemen who would prefer not to be towed.

Semantic neighbours clarify boundaries. Tow differs from pull in implying sustained transport of a separate vehicle or person, not just exertion; from drag in implying controlled, useful haulage rather than reluctant scraping; from haul in being less heavy and more directional; from tug in being steadier and longer-distance rather than short sharp jerks; from lug in lacking the sense of personal carrying. To tow is purposeful, vehicular, and connective: it creates a temporary train of two units moving as one.

Register is neutral, practical, and transatlantic, equally at home in harbour regulations, motoring manuals, and holiday anecdotes. It is neither slangy like yank nor formal like traction. Its very plainness makes it ideal for explanatory prose: you stand on a board, holding the rope, and the boat tows you along needs no ornament, only clarity of mechanism. Mastering tows is mastering the English verb for assisted motion by traction.
