Valour is great courage in the face of danger, especially in battle — courage under arms, the bravery of a soldier or warrior measured in combat. The British spelling valour (American valor) carries a distinctively elevated, ceremonial register: it is the word of epic, chronicle, military citation, and grand narrative rather than of casual speech, where bravery and courage dominate. In Gibbon's sentence — 'The frontiers of that extensive monarchy were guarded by ancient renown and disciplined valour' — valour is not wild heroism or single-combat glory but courage that has been drilled, organised, and made systemic: the professional fighting quality of the Roman legion rather than the berserk impulse of the barbarian charge. The epithet 'disciplined' is decisive; it converts a personal virtue into an institutional capacity. In the sentence's economy, 'ancient renown' is the empire's memory and reputation, while valour is its present, embodied force; together they hold the frontiers at the rim of the 'extensive monarchy' while the interior enjoys peace. Boundary work among near-synonyms is essential. Courage is the broad moral capacity to face fear; it need not be martial and can describe moral, social, or intellectual bravery. Bravery often stresses conspicuous, momentary daring. Prowess emphasizes skill and outstanding deeds more than courage in danger. Gallantry adds chivalric or courtly colour, sometimes shading toward show. Fortitude is endurance under suffering rather than aggressive front-line energy. Valour specializes in martial courage under arms — courage that is publicly valued, 'worth-showing,' the soldier's worth measured on the battlefield — and Gibbon's 'disciplined valour' restricts it further to trained, unit-level Roman fighting quality, the courage of an institution rather than an individual. Polysemy and modern usage:
  • Military decorations and citations: 'medal for valour,' 'Cross for Valour,' 'Victoria Cross for Valour' — valour as the highest formally recognized battlefield courage.
  • Historical sense of 'worth': Latin valor began as 'worth, value, strength,' and an older English strand used valour (and valor) for intrinsic worth or value; this monetary sense survives chiefly in the separate word value, while valour itself specialised toward martial courage.
  • Chivalric romance and epic: valour as the knightly virtue — prowess in arms joined to honour, loyalty, and courtly duty, central to medieval romance and chronicle.
  • Register and spelling: elevated, literary, ceremonial, and historical; rare in colloquial speech and ordinary journalism except in set phrases and military contexts. British -our vs American -or; the related adjectives are valiant (from French vaillant) and valorous (English Latinate).
  • Figurative extension: 'moral valour,' 'valour of the spirit' — courage transferred from the battlefield to endurance of any kind of danger or trial, though the martial core remains primary.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the word descends from Latin valēre 'to be strong, be well, be worth, prevail'; its abstract noun valor meant 'worth, value, strength.' Under the pressure of medieval chivalric culture, especially in Old French, this sense of 'worth' specialised toward 'bravery in battle' — the worth of a man measured under arms. The shift is the decisive semantic event in the word's history: from generic strength and value to the publicly recognised virtue of the fighter. Register and collocations: valour is elevated, ceremonial, and historical — standard in military citations, heraldry, epic and romantic narrative, and grand historiography, and markedly less common in everyday speech. Chief collocations: 'disciplined/military/personal valour,' 'deeds of valour,' 'medal/cross for valour,' 'valour in the field,' 'conspicuous valour,' 'valour in battle.'
