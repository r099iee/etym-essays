• Large-format books: Similar because both denote oversized books; differs because this is a descriptive phrase, whereas folios names the precise historical format (a sheet folded once).
  • Sheets / Leaves: Similar because a folio is literally a sheet or leaf; differs because sheets and leaves are the raw materials of books, whereas folios are the finished large-format volumes or the numbered pages.
  • Codexes / Codices: Similar because both are large, important bound books; differs because codex is the technical term for a manuscript book as opposed to a scroll, whereas folio names a printing format.
  • Quartos: Similar because both are book formats based on sheet-folding; differs because a quarto is a sheet folded twice (four leaves), smaller than a folio.
  • Octavos: Similar because both are book formats; differs because an octavo is a sheet folded into eight leaves — much smaller, the common book size, the opposite of the grand folio.
  • Atlas volumes: Similar because both are very large format books; differs because atlas names a book of maps specifically, whereas folio names any large-format book.
  • Grimoires: Similar because both are ancient books of magic; differs because grimoire is a real historical word for a magic manual, whereas libram is a 20th-century authorial coinage built on Latin liber.
  • Tomes: Similar in suggesting heavy books of lore; differs because tome means a large heavy book, whereas libram specifically means an ancient magical book in Vance's coinage.
  • Spellbooks: Similar because both mean books of spells; differs because spellbook is plain and descriptive, whereas libram is archaic and pseudo-Latin, suggesting greater antiquity and gravity.
  • Manuscripts: Similar because both are old handwritten books; differs because manuscript is a neutral technical term for any handwritten document, whereas libram names the magical book of a fantasy world.
  • Incunabula: Similar because both are early books; differs because incunabula specifically means books printed before 1501, whereas libram is a coinage for ancient magical books regardless of printing.
  • Compendiums: Similar because both are substantial collections of knowledge; differs because compendium stresses a summary of gathered learning, whereas libram stresses the esoteric magical content.
  • Convincing: Similar because both describe what wins belief; differs because convincing is the general everyday word, whereas cogent specifically describes arguments or evidence that compel intellectual assent.
  • Compelling: Similar because both mean irresistibly forceful; differs because compelling can apply to stories, films, or people (emotional force), whereas cogent is restricted to reasoned argument and evidence.
  • Persuasive: Similar because both mean able to win agreement; differs because persuasive may use charm, rhetoric, or emotion, whereas cogent compels by logic and clarity alone.
  • Forceful: Similar because both mean having driving power; differs because forceful can describe personality and style, whereas cogent is confined to the force of reasoning.
  • Sound: Similar because both describe reliable reasoning; differs because sound means logically valid and well-founded (a sound argument), whereas cogent adds the idea that it compels belief by its force.
  • Valid: Similar because both describe arguments that hold up; differs because valid is the logical term for a formally correct argument, whereas cogent describes the persuasive force of a good argument.
  • Logical: Similar because both involve reasoning; differs because logical describes the method (conforming to logic), whereas cogent describes the result (compelling conviction).
  • Reasonable: Similar because both describe arguments that make sense; differs because reasonable is moderate and unremarkable, whereas cogent is strong and commanding.
  • Trenchant: Similar because both describe sharp, forceful reasoning; differs because trenchant means cuttingly incisive (often of wit or criticism), whereas cogent is about clean, compelling logic.
  • Incisive: Similar because both describe penetrating, effective reasoning; differs because incisive stresses sharp cutting through confusion, whereas cogent stresses the compelling force of the whole argument.
  • Powerful: Similar because both describe great force; differs because powerful is broad and applies to many things, whereas cogent names specifically the force of a convincing argument.
  • Weighty: Similar because both describe arguments of serious force; differs because weighty suggests importance and gravity, whereas cogent suggests clarity and compelling logic.
  • Irrefutable: Similar because both describe arguments that cannot be denied; differs because irrefutable means impossible to refute, a stronger, more absolute claim, whereas cogent means highly compelling without necessarily being unanswerable.
