Proto-Germanic *ragga- "shaggy, rough, tufted, unevenly projecting" (reconstructed; a base denoting rough, hairy, projecting surfaces) → Old Norse rögg "a shaggy tuft of coarse wool, rough hair" (attested in Old Icelandic sources, 13th c.) → Middle English ragge "a torn strip of cloth, a shred, a tattered garment" (Scandinavian loan adopted in the Danelaw dialects, attested 14th c.; the Old English cognate ragu "lichen, shaggy moss" shows related forms were already present on the native side) → Modern English rag, plural rags "tattered fragments of cloth; ragged clothing; by extension any shred-like remnant" (the sense used in the text, of wind-torn strips of cloud). RELATED SIDE-BRANCHES: the same Old Norse rögg gave English rug "coarse woollen covering" (16th c.); the adjective ragged "rough, jagged, torn" was formed in Middle English from ragge, perhaps with the Old Norse adjective rǠgðr "tufted" behind it, and survives in modern ragged "frayed; uneven, straggling". DEBATE / NUANCE: The derivation of ragge from Old Norse rögg is the standard account and is well supported, though the Old English cognate ragu shows that related native forms existed; the exact route of the fourteenth-century form — direct Scandinavian loan versus reinforcement of a native word — is not fully recoverable from the record.
