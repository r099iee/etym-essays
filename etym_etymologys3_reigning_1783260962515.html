The chain that produces reigning begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *h₃reǵ-, "to make straight, to direct, to rule." Its original physical sense was concrete: to draw a straight line, to stretch something out, to set a course straight ahead, and from that came the idea of guiding and commanding. The daughter languages spread the root across almost the whole family: Greek orégō means "I stretch out, I reach," preserving the physical sense; Sanskrit rā́jan means "king" — from *h₃rēǵ-ōn-, "the director" — and lives on in Hindi राजा rājā "king" and Odia ରାଜା rājā; the Celtic languages built *rīg-, "king," the element we still hear in the Gaulish chieftain Vercingetorix, "great king of warriors"; and the Germanic branch, without taking the kingship sense, kept the root as *rehtaz, "straight, right, just," which is our own native English word right and German recht. It is a striking distribution: one root, and across Europe it means at once to straighten, to direct, to be right, and to be king.

Latin developed the root into a complete vocabulary of rule. The verb regere means "to keep straight, to guide, to govern"; from it come rēx "king" (the director, the one who draws the line), rēgīna "queen," rēgnum "kingship, kingdom," rēgula "rule, ruler, straight edge," rēctus "straight, right," and regimen "steering, government." The verb rēgnāre, "to be king, to exercise royal rule," was formed from rēgnum, and it is this verb that matters for our word: through the whole history of the Roman empire rēgnāre described the act of occupying the throne, and Latin's royal vocabulary passed wholesale into the Romance languages — French roi "king," règne "reign," régner "to reign" — all of it descending from the straightening root.

  • Sound change — the participial suffix: English reigning is simply the present participle of the verb reign, formed with the productive suffix -ing (Old English -ende); no further phonetic change is involved at the English stage.
  • Loan path: Latin rēgnāre → Old French reignier/regner → Middle English reignen (c. 1225) → reign; the noun reign followed the same route. The word reached English with the Norman-French royal and legal vocabulary.
  • Semantic narrowing of the participle: as an adjective reigning came to mean specifically "currently in possession of the throne," the present occupant of royal office, distinct from the active, day-to-day exercise of government in ruling.
  • Modern metaphors: from the same participial construction English developed reigning champion (sport), reigning beauty, and reigning fashion — the current, acknowledged, uncrowned holder of pre-eminence, by analogy with a monarch presently on the throne.
  • Homophone caution: reign "rule" (from Latin rēgnāre) is etymologically unrelated to the identical-sounding rein, the strap for guiding a horse, which descends from Latin retinēre "to hold back"; the king reigns, but the horseman merely reins.

The word reached English in the thirteenth century through Old French: Latin rēgnāre became Old French reignier or regner, and Middle English took it as reignen, attested from about 1225. The noun reign followed, and from the verb English formed the present participle reigning, used as an adjective for whoever is currently on the throne. The modern word has kept the full range of its Latin ancestry: in law and constitutional history it describes occupancy of the throne by title, and in Macaulay's sentence the "reigning dynasty" is precisely the dynasty whose title, after the Revolution of 1688, was held to rest on the rights of the people. From the same participial construction English developed the metaphorical reigning champion of sport, the reigning beauty, and the reigning fashion — the current, acknowledged, uncrowned holder of pre-eminence. One caution completes the biography: reign must never be confused with the identical-sounding rein, the strap for guiding a horse, which descends from a wholly different Latin verb, retinēre "to hold back"; the king reigns, but the horseman merely reins.
