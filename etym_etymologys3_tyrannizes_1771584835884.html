The story of tyrannizes begins in the Aegean, not in the Indo-European homeland. Unlike almost every other abstract political noun in English, the family of tyranny has no Indo-European pedigree: the Greek noun týrannos (τύραννος) appears suddenly in the seventh century BCE — the age of the so-called tyrants — and is transparently a loanword from the non-Greek world of Asia Minor, perhaps Lydian, perhaps Luwian, in any case a Pre-Greek substratum word for a lord or ruler. Beekes classifies it as Pre-Greek; some scholars have proposed a Luwian tarwan(a)- "power" or a Lydian form, but none conclusively. This is why no PIE etymon can be reconstructed: the word was borrowed, not inherited, and forcing a PIE root onto it would be a scholarly error.

In early Greece the word was not yet the insult it became. A tyrannos was a sole ruler who had seized power without constitutional title — often a populist reformer, like Cypselus of Corinth or Peisistratus of Athens — and the term, used by poets such as Archilochus, could be perfectly neutral. It was Plato and Aristotle who turned it into a term of moral and political abuse: the tyrant is the man who rules for his own pleasure, unchecked by law, the living antithesis of the lawful king (basileus). From the noun Greek formed the verb tyrannízein (τυραννίζειν), "to act as a tyrant, to rule despotically," using the verbal suffix -ízein that English would later import as -ize — so that even the morphology of tyrannize declares its Greek manufacture.

Latin took tyrannus with the pejorative load already installed, and from the noun the late language grew the verb tyrannizāre, which passed into Old French as tyranniser in the medieval period and thence into Middle English as tyrannisen in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The plain noun tyrant arrived in English separately, via Old French tirant (with the vowel weakening y > i that also produced Italian tiranno and Spanish tirano), but both words share the single borrowed stem *tyrann-. The English verb tyrannize, attested from about the 1590s, keeps the full Aristotelian charge: to exercise absolute, lawless, self-serving power. It takes the preposition over and a human object, and in modern usage it has broadened from the state to any domain of unchecked authority — the tyrant father, the tyrant boss, the tyrant husband — so that a spoilt child may be said, with comic grandeur, to tyrannize a household, exactly as Lady Wang jokes in The Story of the Stone. What was once the precise vocabulary of Greek constitutional theory has become the everyday (and sometimes playful) word for petty domestic despotism.

Along the way the word acquired a rich literary life of its own. The Renaissance stage made the tyrant a stock figure of ambition and cruelty — Marlowe's Tamburlaine and the Richard III of Shakespeare's history plays rule as living illustrations of the Aristotelian type, and the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries teem with English works on "tyrannical government," from anti-monarchical pamphlets to royalist defences, each using the family of tyrant, tyranny, and tyrannize as a political weapon. The modern age has extended the metaphor in every direction, from the "tyranny of the majority" of Tocqueville and John Stuart Mill to the tyranny of fashion, of the clock, of work, of opinion; and the verb has kept pace, so that to tyrannize no longer requires a crown — a parent, a headmaster, a spoiled child, even a habit may tyrannize over those in their orbit. The word has thereby circled from the archaic Greek marketplace, where a tyrannos could still be a popular reformer, through the philosophical laboratories of Plato and Aristotle, the imperial chancelleries of Rome, the courts and dictionaries of early modern England, to the half-joking, half-worried remark of a Chinese matriarch about her "little monster" of a son — a single borrowed stem, *tyrann-, carrying the whole weight of that history.
