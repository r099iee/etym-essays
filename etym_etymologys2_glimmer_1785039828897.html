Single root word: GLIMMER. The chain runs from a well-attested PIE "shine" root through a Germanic frequentative formation; the English history is clear from Middle English onward. Proto-Indo-European *ghel- "to shine, gleam, glow" (reconstructed root; the source of a vast family of light- and colour-words: Greek khloros "greenish-yellow," Sanskrit hiraṇya "gold," English gold, yellow, glass, glow, gleam, glint, glitter, glisten, glad "bright") → Proto-Germanic *glim- / *glaim- "to shine, gleam" (reconstructed base; cf. Old Norse gljá "to shine," Old Saxon glīmo "brightness," Old High German glīmo "glow-worm; gleam," Middle Dutch glimmen "to shine faintly") → Middle Low German glimmeren / Middle Dutch glimmen "to shine faintly, flicker" (frequentative verbs, with the iterative suffix -er-: the repeated, trembling motion of a faint light) → Middle English glimeren / glimmeren "to shine faintly, flicker" (verb; recorded from the late 14th century; the earliest English sense was actually "to shine brightly," shifting to "shine dimly" in the early 15th century) → Early Modern English glimmer (verb "to shine with a faint unsteady light"; noun "a faint wavering light" recorded from 1580s; glim "a light, lantern" in cant from 1670s) → Modern English GLIMMER (noun and verb; also figurative "a faint trace of hope, understanding, or an idea"; mineral name for mica). Note on nuance: the shift from "shine brightly" to "shine dimly" in early 15th-century English is the opposite of what one might expect; the frequentative suffix -er-, which repeated the action into a series of small trembling gleams, seems to have re-imagined brightness as unsteadiness, and the dimness then became the word's permanent centre. Cognate check: German glimmen "to glow faintly, smoulder" preserves the same root with the same weak-light semantics.
