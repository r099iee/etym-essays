BOAST appears in Middle English around 1300 as both noun bost, meaning boast, glory, noise, arrogance, presumption, pride and vanity, and verb bosten, to brag, in Kyng Alisaunder according to the OED. The OED verdict is that boast is of unknown origin, with noun and verb found before 1300 and their mutual relation and source alike unknown. Phonetic history shows Middle English long o surviving as modern oa, which rules out native Old English *bast, *bost or *bost sources that would have given different modern vowels, pointing instead to a later foreign introduction analogous to coast, toast and host, though no Old French *boster has been found. Speculative links to Welsh bostio and Gaelic bosd are dismissed as borrowings from English, while a North Germanic derivation from Proto-Germanic *bausuz, inflated, swollen, puffed up and proud, remains a modern conjecture without OED endorsement.

Form and sound development is therefore short and stable:

  • Middle English bost and bosten: spellings bost, boste, boost and Scots boist show long o with variable final e; noun and verb coexist without clear derivation direction.
  • Loss of inflectional e: early modern levelling drops final e, giving boast as both noun and verb; the oa spelling fixes after 1600 to mark the long vowel, pronounced with the distinctive GOAT diphthong.
  • Regular weak verb: boasted and boasting follow without ablaut; third singular boasts, noun plural boasts; stress remains initial and monosyllabic.
  • Phrasal extensions: to boast of and to boast about govern the vaunted thing, to boast that introduces a clause; inanimate subjects as in the hotel boasts develop in the eighteenth century without formal change.

Semantic movement runs from noise to pride to possession:

  • Medieval noise and vaunting: bost is loud self-praise and turbulent pride, close to bluster; to bosten is to speak big, threaten and exult, often condemned in moral and religious writing.
  • Early modern threat and glory: Shakespearean boast can mean to threaten, to brag of deeds and to vaunt lineage; the noun boast becomes both the act and the thing boasted of, as his boast was his sword.
  • Eighteenth and nineteenth century social boasting: Austen and folk-tale worlds treat boasting of riches to kin as provocation and envy-bait; the prompt brother fears the enriched sibling will dare to boast of riches to him, so Misery is weaponised to ruin him first.
  • Modern possession and marketing: towns, hotels and phones boast views, features and speeds, a positive inanimate sense where to boast means to possess and display; psychology distinguishes benign sharing from narcissistic boasting, while social media revives the moral debate over bragging versus updating.

Literary attestations are continuous: Kyng Alisaunder around 1300, Chaucerian vaunting, Tudor interludes, Shakespearean boasts and threats, Restoration rakes, Austen drawing-rooms, Russian folk tales in English where Misery punishes boasting wealth, and modern advertising where every product boasts. The prompt infinitive, dare to boast of his riches to me, preserves the classic construction boast of plus object plus audience, where daring marks that boasting is a social challenge inviting ruin.
