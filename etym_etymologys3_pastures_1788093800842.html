1. The Indo-European root. The whole family descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *peh₂- “to protect, feed, shepherd”, which also underlies Latin pānis “bread” and pābulum “fodder”. The specifically Latin branch built an inchoative present on the extended stem *peh₂-s- with the suffix -sk-: pāscō, pāscere “to feed, to drive to pasture, to graze”, attested from the earliest Latin literature (Plautus, 3rd–2nd century BCE). The semantics are revealing: the Roman verb means both “to feed” and “to lead out to graze” — one continuous activity in the herder's day, from the stall to the field and back.

2. The shepherd and the pasture in Latin. From the past participle pāstus arose the agent noun pāstor “shepherd” (literally “feeder”), which Latin Christianity then promoted to a title for bishops — the shepherd of souls — giving English pastor and pastoral (cf. the Vulgate's shepherds of Luke 2:8, pastores, in the Nativity story). The place-noun pāstūra “a feeding, pasturage, grazing land” is built on the participle with the productive suffix -tūra (the same suffix as in nātūra, cultūra, creātūra), and it is attested in the agricultural writers of the early Roman Empire, notably Columella (1st century CE). Latin also had a parallel formation, pascua “pastures”, from the same verb; it was pāstūra, however, that the Romance languages continued.

3. Old French and the -ure suffix. Gallo-Romance developed pāstūra into Old French pasture (12th century), “fodder, pasture, pasturage”. The sound changes are regular: Latin long ā in the stressed first syllable became Old French a; the medial cluster -st- survived; and the suffix -ūra became -ure, exactly as in creature, nature and picture. English took the word through Anglo-Norman and Middle French.

4. Middle English. Middle English pasture (also pastur, pastour) is attested from about 1300, in the double sense “fodder, grass eaten by cattle” and “grazing land, meadow”. It entered a language that already had native words for the concept: Old English lǣs and lǣswe “pasture, meadow” (recorded in charter boundary clauses, and surviving into modern dialect as leasow). The Romance loan gradually became the standard term, though the native words lingered for centuries in regional speech. A verb pasturen “to feed, graze” follows in the 14th century.

5. Early Modern English: the sound of the word. Three sound changes shaped the modern pronunciation. First, the Old French -ure suffix entered English as -ure and later weakened; second, the cluster /tj/ in this suffix underwent yod-palatalisation in late Middle and Early Modern English, so that pasture came to be pronounced with /tʃ/ — exactly as in nature /ˈneɪtʃə/ and picture /ˈpɪktʃə/; third, the stressed vowel, Middle English short [a] before /st/, was lengthened to the “broad a” [ɑː] in 17th–18th-century London speech, the same change that gives RP /pɑːst/ for past and /ˈkɑːsl/ for castle. The result is Modern English /ˈpɑːstʃə/.

6. The figurative turn: 16th–17th centuries. The word's literary career began in the 16th century. Miles Coverdale's Psalter (1535) rendered Psalm 23 with “he shall feed me in a green pasture”, and the Authorised Version (1611) fixed the immortal “green pastures”. John Milton closed “Lycidas” (1637) with “Tomorrow to fresh woods, and pastures new”, coining the idiom pastures new for a new field of endeavour — the phrase survives in exactly this form today, always in the plural.

7. Modern English. In the 19th–20th centuries the agricultural core stayed intact while the idioms multiplied: greener pastures (better opportunities; the “grass is greener” proverb transferred to pasture imagery), to put out to pasture (to retire, originally of worn-out horses and cattle, then of people — with an ironic, gently mocking tone), and the marketing usage pasture-raised. In ecology and agronomy the plural pastures anchors a technical vocabulary of permanent and ley pastures, rotational grazing, overgrazing and pasture degradation. The word has thus travelled from a Proto-Indo-European verb of feeding, through the Roman farm and the French manor, to biblical comfort, a Miltonic idiom and a modern food label — a full arc from shepherd's field to cultural metaphor.
