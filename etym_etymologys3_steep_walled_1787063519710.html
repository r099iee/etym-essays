THE COMPOUND AND ITS TWO INDEPENDENT HISTORIES

steep-walled is a modern descriptive compound, but each half carries a long, independent history, and the compound itself is a vivid meeting of the two: an Old English word for height and steepness meets an English word for enclosure whose ultimate ancestor is a Roman palisade. The essay below traces both lines in full.

PART ONE — STEEP: FROM TOWERING HEIGHT TO SHARP ANGLE

The adjective steep descends from a Proto-Indo-European root *(s)teup- "to push, strike, stamp" — a percussive, stabbing root whose image is something that stands up abruptly, juts out, or spikes upward. Cognates in the family include Greek týptō "to strike, beat" and, in Germanic, the verbs meaning "to knock" and "to push" (Old Norse steypa "to cast down, topple"). From this root the Germanic branch built the adjective *staupaz "steep, lofty, towering, high" — reconstructed on the secure evidence of Old English stēap, Old Norse steypʀ, and Old High German cognates — and the semantic bridge, though inferred rather than attested, is straightforward: that which "sticks up" or "stands up" is high and towering; a towering face of rock is, by the same image, steep. Old English stēap (8th–11th century) carried the full old range: "high, lofty, towering" — of mountains, towers, and heaven itself — and also "steep, deep", the two halves of the same vertical image (a stēap cliff is both tall and sheer; a stēap pool is deep, going down as abruptly as the cliff goes up).

Middle English stepe / steep preserved both senses, but the modern language narrowed the word: by Early Modern English, steep meant almost exclusively "rising or falling sharply, inclined at a great angle", while the height-sense split off into the separate word steeple — the tall church tower is literally "the steep one", the high one. The angle now rules: a steep hill, steep stairs, a steep drop, a steep ascent. The nineteenth century added the famous figurative slang — "a steep price", "that's a bit steep!" (exorbitant, unreasonable) — extending the image of an ascent that costs too much effort to a demand that costs too much money; the twentieth added "a steep learning curve", the modern image of knowledge that must be climbed at a punishing gradient. Through all this drift the word has stayed phonetically small and stable (stēap → steep, /stiːp/), a native word passed orally for over a thousand years, and in the compound steep-walled it contributes precisely the modern core: near-vertical angle.

PART TWO — WALLED: FROM ROMAN PALISADE TO ENCLOSED PLACE

The walled half is a Roman import dressed in English clothes. Its ultimate root is Proto-Indo-European *wel- "to turn, roll, wind" — one of the family's great "turning" roots, seen in Latin volvere "to roll" (English volume), valēre "to be strong", and the whole revolve/involve family. From *wel- Latin built vallus "a stake, post, palisade" — the image being a turned, rounded, sharpened pole — and from vallus the collective noun vallum "a rampart, a wall of stakes, a palisade fortification": the military earthwork whose outer edge was a line of sharpened stakes, the standard field-fortification of the Roman legions. As Roman influence and the Roman road-and-camp world spread through northern Europe, the Germanic peoples took the word in as a loan: Proto-Germanic *wallaz / *wallą "wall, rampart", which passed into Old English as weall "wall, rampart, earthwork, dam".

The Old English weall was at first a military and fortification word — the walls of burhs, the ramparts of Roman ruins that dotted the British landscape, the earthwork defences of towns and estates — and it is directly attested in the Anglo-Saxon chronicles, charters, and place-names (many English towns preserve weall in street-names along old walls). Middle English wall generalised the sense from fortification to any vertical enclosing structure: house walls, garden walls, city walls, the walls of the heart and of the womb in religious and medical prose. By Early Modern English, wall was the everyday word for any vertical partition or enclosure, and the past-participle adjective walled — "having walls, enclosed by walls" — was in regular use: walled cities, walled gardens, walled courtyards. In the compound steep-walled, the word is used metaphorically of nature: the rock faces are walls, and the little bay is nature's own walled garden, still and quiet.

THE COMPOUND IN CONTEXT

  • Phonetic: both halves are short and old; /stiːp-wɔːld/ shows the Old English long vowel (stēap) and the English development of wall from Latin vallum.
  • Semantic union: steep contributes the angle, walled contributes the enclosure — together they mean "shut in by near-vertical sides", the perfect description of a sheltered grassy cove.
  • Literary attestation: the compound thrives in geology, geography, and mountaineering prose and in adventure narrative; Tolkien's "little steep-walled bay, grassy-floored, still and quiet" is a model use — precise topography in a few words, and perfect shelter.
  • Register: descriptive, precise, slightly literary — the compound is built on demand from two fully productive, native-rooted words, which is why it feels both ancient and fresh.

SUMMARY

steep-walled unites an Old English adjective of towering height turned to steepness (stēap, from PIE *(s)teup- "to strike, stand up") with an English adjective of enclosure whose word goes back, through Old English weall and the Germanic loan-wall, to the Roman palisade (Latin vallum, from PIE *wel- "to turn, roll"). In two words it describes a place whose sides rise like stakes, walls, and ramparts — a little bay on the side of the Mountain, grassy-floored, still, and quiet.
