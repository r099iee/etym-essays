CHAIN: FROM PIE TO COPIOUSLY (7 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *h₃ep- 'to work, to produce, to be in abundance; wealth' (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; attested in Sanskrit ápas 'work, sacred act' and Hittite ḫappira- 'to sell, trade'; the root's vocalism is debated — older handbooks write *op-).
  2. Latin ops, genitive opis 'wealth, power, resources, help' (attested classical; plural opēs 'riches, resources'; from PIE *h₃ep- with the noun suffix -s).
  3. Latin copia 'abundance, plenty, ample supply, wealth' (attested classical; co- 'together' + ops, with the abstract ending -ia — literally 'resources gathered together').
  4. Latin copiosus 'abundant, plentiful, rich, copious' (attested classical; copia + adjectival -ōsus, 'full of plenty').
  5. Middle English copious 'abundant, plentiful' (14th century, borrowed from Latin copiosus, reinforced by Old French copieux).
  6. Middle English / Early Modern English copiously 'in great abundance' (adverb, 15th century, formed from copious + the adverbial suffix -ly; this is an English formation, not a loan of the Latin adverb copiōsē).
  7. English COPIOUSLY — the modern adverb, 'in large amounts, abundantly', standard since the 15th century and still in ordinary literary and formal use.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The PIE root is traditionally written *op- 'to work, produce' in older handbooks; with the laryngeal theory it is reconstructed as *h₃ep-. The detail of the reconstruction is debated, but the root's existence and its sense of production and wealth are well supported by Sanskrit ápas and Latin opus (work).
  • Latin ops and opus are related but separate formations; copia is built on ops, not on opus.
  • The English adverb is formed from the English adjective (copious + -ly); the Latin adverb copiōsē existed but is not the direct source.
