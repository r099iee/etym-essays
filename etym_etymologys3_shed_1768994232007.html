The biography of "shed" is the biography of the split. Its Proto-Indo-European root, *skei-, "to cut, split, divide," is one of the most widespread in the language family, and each of its branches specialized the act of cutting in a different direction.

  • Indo-Iranian. In the east the root produced Sanskrit chinátti, "he cuts," the root chid-, and the Hindi and Odia adjective chinn, "cut off."
  • Hellenic. In the south it gave Greek skhizein, "to split," from which English borrowed schism — the splitting of a church — and schist, the rock that splits into leaves.
  • Italic. In Italy it became Latin scindere, "to cut," the ancestor of rescind and scission, with the caution that scissors is a false friend from a different root (caedere).
  • Celtic. The same root named the scíath, the shield — literally a cut piece of wood.
  • Balto-Slavic. It survives in Lithuanian skíesti, "to separate, dilute," and Russian ščit, "shield," from the same "cut piece" formation.
  • Germanic. In the north the root became Proto-Germanic *skaidaną, "to separate, part," which produced Gothic skaidan, Old Norse skilja (whence English skill, the "distinction" or "discernment" that separates), German scheiden, and Dutch scheiden — and, in Old English, scēadan, "to divide, separate, discriminate."

Into English. From Old English scēadan the verb shed entered Middle English around 1200, and its senses branched along the old root's lines.

  • The "divide" sense fossilizes. The oldest meaning, "to divide, to part," survives in watershed (19th century) — the ridge that divides and sheds water to either side — and in the "shed" of the hair, the parting.
  • The "cast off" sense grows. From dividing came casting off: to shed tears, blood, skin, leaves, or light is to let them part from the body, a natural and effortless separation.
  • The "emit" sense. "To shed light" transfers casting-off to giving-out, illuminating literally and figuratively.
  • The participial fossil. Old English and Middle English used "shed" itself as the past participle ("has shed," "is shed"), and "to be shed of" something came to mean "to be separated from, rid of it." The American dialectal idiom "get shed of" preserves that construction intact.

In the passage. The frontier grammar of McCarthy's sentence — "you caint get shed of him" — is therefore a fossil, carrying a Middle English participial construction down to the nineteenth-century Texas road. The sentence means exactly what its ancient root says: you cannot split yourself from him. The whole family — schism, scission, skill, sheath, and shed — is one long history of things being cut apart, and the passage belongs to its oldest register. Each stage of the tree left a meaning behind: the Greek branch specializes the split as religious rupture, the Latin branch as legal and surgical cutting, the Celtic and Slavic branches as the shield that separates its bearer from harm, and the Germanic branch as the everyday parting of waters, skins, and burdens. In "get shed of," the ancient root is at its most domestic — the cutting-away of an encumbrance — and McCarthy's sentence uses it with absolute finality: the divine presence cannot be shed, cannot be split off, and the word's whole family history of division stands behind the impossibility.
