UNDERCUTS is the plural of the noun undercut, used here in its swordsmanship sense: cuts delivered upward, from beneath the opponent's guard or under the stroke. In the passage from The Hobbit, the dwarves debate "stabs and jabs and undercuts" among the arts of dragon-slaying — a catalogue of blade-strokes in which an undercut is the sneaky rising cut that slips in under a guard or a descending arm. But the word-family has grown remarkably: the same spelling now names commercial price-cutting, the weakening of arguments and authority, geological erosion, a boxing blow, a tennis backspin stroke, and even a fashionable hairstyle.

1. Swordsmanship and fighting — the literary sense. In fencing and old swordplay, an undercut is a cut or stroke that travels upward from below, aimed to pass under an opponent's blade or guard. It is a rising, half-unexpected blow — precisely the sort of trick a wily dragon-slayer might employ. In the nineteenth century the term also meant a rising blow in boxing, later largely displaced by the more famous uppercut.

2. The commercial sense — to undersell. As a verb, to undercut means to offer goods, services, or prices below a competitor's level, or to charge less than the going rate: "the chain store undercuts every local shop." The image is of cutting at the foundation of the rival's trade, stealing custom from beneath. This is now the dominant everyday sense in business, economics, and journalism (undercut prices, undercut wages, undercut a bid).

3. The figurative sense — to undermine or weaken. To undercut an argument, a claim, an authority, or a plan is to weaken or destroy it from within, often slyly: "the leaked memo undercuts the minister's credibility." It implies sapping the foundation so that the whole structure topples. This sense is close to undermine and subvert but keeps the cutting image of the original compound.

4. Geology, construction, and woodwork. In physical description, to undercut is to cut away the underside of something: a river or wave undercuts a cliff, leaving an overhang; a carpenter makes an undercut in a joint or dovetail; miners undercut a seam. The noun denotes the notch, recess, or overhang so produced.

5. Sport. In tennis, squash, and golf, an undercut (or undercut stroke) is a shot struck with the racket or club cutting beneath the ball, producing backspin and a lower, floating trajectory.

6. Fashion — the undercut hairstyle. Since the 2010s, the undercut is a popular men's haircut in which the sides and back are cut very short or shaved while the hair on top is left long. It is a striking modern repurposing of the word: the hair beneath is "cut under" the long top layer.

7. Register and tone.

  • Business/economic writing: "undercut prices," "undercut by foreign imports" — neutral, frequent, almost technical.
  • Politics and media: "undercuts her authority," "undercuts the whole argument" — critical, suggests sly or unintended damage.
  • Fighting and literary prose: the old blade sense, now chiefly historical or descriptive, as in Tolkien's adventure prose.
  • Colloquial: "don't undercut yourself" — do not cheapen or weaken your own position.

8. The semantic bridge. Every sense is a variation on the literal compound of under + cut: a cut delivered from beneath, a cut at the base, a weakening from below. Once the image of "cutting underneath" is grasped — under a guard, under a price, under a foundation, under a hairstyle — the entire family of meanings, from dragons to haircuts, falls into place as one movement: the strike that comes from below.
