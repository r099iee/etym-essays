- Middle English origin sense - a lout is an awkward, clumsy fellow, the word growing out of the verb meaning to bow, stoop or crouch; the sense of one who cringes or stoops low carries a built-in sneer at a base, creeping posture
- 16th-18th century settled insult - lout fixes as an insult for an uncouth, ill-bred countryman, and loutish names the coarseness of one without refinement; a plain, vigorous word of the popular vocabulary
- 19th-20th century press sense - the word is taken up for rowdy, aggressive young men, and British journalism builds its favourite 'lout menace' and 'lager lout'; the connotation shifts from rural boorishness to urban rowdiness
- Contemporary sense - an insult of medium strength for a boorish, clumsy, aggressive fellow, implying lack of breeding rather than criminality; equally at home in the playground and the editorial column
