- 16th century - 'bleak' first appears as a colour word: pale, wan, of a sickly complexion (from Old Norse 'bleikr', 'pale'); 'bleakly' inherits this bloodless whiteness.
- 17th-18th century - the colour sense fades and 'bleak' migrates to the land: cold, bare, windswept, stripped of shelter — a bleak moor, a bleak hilltop — so that exposure becomes weather.
- 19th-20th century - transferred from climate to mood: cheerless, comfortless, offering no hope (a bleak prospect, a bleak future); 'bleakly' hardens into the adverb of emotional desolation.
- Contemporary - the adverb fuses its two inheritances, the pale colour and the exposed ground: a bleak sigh is heatless, colourless, a piece of winter landscape exhaled through one man's chest — despair without a single warm shade.
