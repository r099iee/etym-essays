- [Old to Middle English (10th–14th c.)] — Inherited from Old English ongēan / ongeġn ("opposite, facing, back, against"); Middle English retained ayen / agein as the standard form before southern metropolitan scribes appended excrescent genitive -es and inorganic dental stop -t (yielding standard against) — [perception: core native preposition of spatial direction, physical contact, and reciprocal action].
- [15th–17th c. Early Modern English] — While standard London printing codified against, the original un-suffixed forms again / agane persisted vigorously across Northern English, Scots, and rural western dialects, both spatially ("up again the door") and adversatively — [perception: increasingly marked as provincial, regional, or unlettered compared to metropolitan court English].
- [18th–19th c. Dialect Literature & American Frontier] — Orthographically fixed as agin (or ag’in) in dialect poetry (Robert Burns), British rural fiction (Thomas Hardy, George Eliot), and American frontier humor (Mark Twain, Artemus Ward); famous satirical political trope: "agin the government" — [perception: celebrated in regional writing as a marker of earthy common sense, rustic obstinacy, and feisty grassroots defiance of urban authority].
- [20th c. Literary Dialect & Mid-Century Colloquialism] — Masterfully deployed by stylistically conscious authors like J.R.R. Tolkien (put into the rustic mouths of Shire hobbits like Daddy Twofoot and the Gaffer) and P.G. Wodehouse to evoke pastoral authenticity, rooted folk skepticism, and cozy tavern banter — [perception: beloved literary shorthand for rustic insularity, rural common sense, and instinctive suspicion of foreign novelties].
- [21st c. Contemporary Vernacular & Stylistic Archaisms] — Survived in living regional speech (Appalachian English, Ulster Scots, Northern English dialects) and humorously preserved in political commentary ("the party that is agin everything") — [perception: recognized either as authentic regional heritage speech or as an intentional folksy idiom expressing stubborn, reflexive opposition].
