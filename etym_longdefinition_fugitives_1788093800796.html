Fugitives — the plural of fugitive — are people who have fled, and are still fleeing, from danger, pursuit, capture, or justice, and who must therefore keep moving and stay hidden. In the Tolkien context the last goblins and wargs of the Battle of Five Armies are the fugitives: driven into the Running River, hunted into the marshes about the Forest River, they perish "while those that came hardly to the Wood-elves' realm were there slain, or drawn in to die deep in the trackless dark of Mirkwood". The word perfectly captures their state: still running, still hunted, with no safe haven.

Primary senses:

  • One who flees pursuit or capture — the core sense; the pursuit may be by enemies, police, slave-hunters, or a hostile army.
  • One who flees justice — the legal sense: a "fugitive from justice" is a person who leaves a jurisdiction (or escapes custody) to avoid arrest, trial, or punishment; extradition law exists precisely to retrieve such people.
  • One who flees danger — the humanitarian sense: civilians running from war, massacre, or oppression; in modern usage this shades into refugee and displaced person.

Adjective senses (the same word does double duty):

  • "Fleeing, in flight" — a fugitive army, fugitive troops (the sense used of hunted soldiers).
  • "Transient, fleeting, elusive" — the oldest metaphorical bridge: that which flees passes quickly, so "fugitive" came to mean anything ephemeral: "fugitive thoughts", "fugitive impressions", "fugitive hues" (in art criticism, transient light and colour effects).
  • "Unfixed, roving" — in technical registers: "fugitive emissions" (gases that leak and escape from industrial plant — pollutants that flee), "fugitive pigments" (unstable colours that fade quickly), "fugitive dust".

The semantic bridge at the root of everything: Latin fugere "to flee". Every sense — legal, military, humanitarian, metaphorical — is "that which flees", whether from a courtroom, an army, or the sun.

Historical and legal extensions worth knowing:

  • Fugitive slave laws: in the United States, the Fugitive Slave Acts of 1793 and 1850 obligated the return of escaped enslaved people across state lines; "fugitives" became one of the most charged words of the antebellum era, and the 1850 Act is a central grievance in abolitionist writing (Uncle Tom's Cabin, 1852, stages the flight of Eliza across the ice — the era's iconic fugitive).
  • Fugitive warrants: legal instruments for the return of accused persons fleeing between states or countries (in the US, the Fugitive Felon Act; internationally, extradition).
  • Fugue, the musical form, and the "fugue state" of psychology (a dissociative flight), share the same root: Latin fuga "flight".
  • Refuge and refugee come from the same verb: Latin refugium "a place to flee back to".

Register and connotation:

  • "Fugitive" is more literary and more dramatic than "escapee": it foregrounds the hunter and the hunt, not the escape itself.
  • It spans sympathy and suspicion: a fugitive from tyranny is heroic; a fugitive from justice is criminal — the word itself stays morally neutral, and context supplies the judgement (Tolkien's context supplies tragedy).
  • In the plural, "fugitives" often denotes a group in flight — a routed army, a fleeing population — rather than a single hunted person.

Summary: FUGITIVES = people in flight and still in danger of being caught — fleeing enemies, fleeing courts, or fleeing the passing of their own moments.
