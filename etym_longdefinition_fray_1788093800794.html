Fray (noun) — a noisy, disorderly, often confused fight, skirmish, or brawl between hostile parties; by extension, any heated contest, dispute, or competitive struggle in which opposing sides clash. In the reading context, Thorin has fallen in the Battle of Five Armies and Beorn carries him out of the fighting — out of the thick of the combat. The word does service in that sentence because a "fray" is precisely a battle seen not as strategy but as chaos, noise, and hazard.

Primary sense — armed combat seen as disorder:

  • Core meaning: an engagement between armed opponents, usually of modest scale and improvised character, with heavy connotations of confusion, uproar, and close-quarters violence.
  • Classic collocations: "in the thick of the fray", "the fray of battle", "to re-enter the fray".
  • Historical registers: in medieval and early-modern English, a "fray" could be a skirmish between retainers, a border raid, or a duel — the law distinguished an "affray", a public fight that terrified onlookers and breached the king's peace, from a premeditated assault.

Secondary sense — brawl and public disturbance:

  • "Fray" overlaps with "brawl": a disorderly, often drunken or spontaneous fight in a street, tavern, or fairground.
  • The ancestor noun "affray" survives as a technical crime in English law today: a person who fights in public so as to terrify others commits an affray, an offence now governed by the Public Order Act 1986.

Tertiary sense — metaphorical contest (the dominant modern use):

  • "The political fray", "to enter the fray", "to stay above the fray": any vigorous competition — elections, court cases, corporate rivalry, academic disputes, even video-game tournaments — is now a "fray".
  • The semantic bridge is transparent: a fray was a disorderly multiple-sided combat; any argument or contest in which many parties collide without clear rules is experienced, and therefore described, as the same kind of storm.
  • "Above the fray" adds a moral tint: the phrase praises (or envies) those who refuse to plunge into a squabble, implying the fray is degrading and engulfing.

Related and homonymous words (important for precision):

  • The noun fray is historically the aphetic short form of affray, and is therefore a cousin of afraid — all three descend from a Frankish word for "peace": to "affray" someone was to frighten them, literally to knock them out of their peace.
  • The verb to fray ("the rope frayed", "frayed nerves", "frayed tempers") is a different word, from Latin fricāre "to rub" — with the result that modern perception blends the two: frayed tempers sound like tempers worn thin in the fray.
  • No longer current: "fray" as mere "fright, alarm" (15th–16th centuries), now surviving only in archaic verse.

Register and style:

  • Spoken English today uses "fray" almost only in the fixed idioms above; a bald "there was a fray" sounds bookish or archaic.
  • Fantasy and historical fiction (including Tolkien) deliberately reach for it exactly because it is old-fashioned: it instantly signals medieval-style combat.
  • Some game and comic titles pun on the collision of "free(-for-all)" and "fray", recycling the core sense of unordered many-sided fighting.

Summary: FRAY = a fight so disorderly it reads as chaos — literal in the Tolkien passage (the last battle against the goblins and wargs), metaphorical in nearly all modern prose.
