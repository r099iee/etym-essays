- Early 13th c. — Penitential and moral entry: borrowed from Old French amendement into Middle English, signifying religious repentance, atonement for sins, and moral reformation.
- 14th–15th c. — Legal restitution and civil remedy: used in medieval English common law for compensation paid to rectify wrongs, repair property damage, or correct clerical errors in legal writs.
- 16th–17th c. — Liturgical and parliamentary codification: enshrined in liturgical language (the Book of Common Prayer’s prayer for ‘amendment of life’) alongside the emergence of English parliamentary procedure for inserting amending clauses into draft bills.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment moral satire: wielded by Swift, Pope, and Fielding as the supreme aim of satire—chastising human vice to achieve moral correction rather than mere amusement.
- 1787–1791 — American constitutional transformation: the drafting of Article V of the US Constitution and the ratification of the Bill of Rights (the First Ten Amendments) forever linked the word to fundamental constitutional law and human rights.
- 19th–21st c. — Institutional and agronomic specialization: technical adoption in soil science (soil amendments) and complex legislative parliamentary maneuvers (riders, amendments in the nature of a substitute), while maintaining its dignified moral register.
