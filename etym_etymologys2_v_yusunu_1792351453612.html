[Root Component 1: vāyu (wind / the wind god)]
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500 BCE)
*h₂wéh₁-
"to blow, exhale, breathe"
(source of Greek aēr, Latin ventus, English wind)
↓
Proto-Indo-Iranian (reconstructed; agentive noun with *-yu suffix)
*wā́-yu-
"the blower, wind, atmospheric divinity"
(cognate with Avestan vāyu- "wind, atmosphere")
↓
Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500 BCE; Ṛgveda)
vā́yu (वायु)
"the wind, the personified god of wind"

[Root Component 2: sūnu (son, male offspring)]
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500 BCE)
*sewh₁- / *suh₁-
"to give birth, bear, beget offspring"
(source of Sanskrit sūte, Greek hyios)
↓
Proto-Indo-European (thematic nominal derivative with *-nu suffix) (c. 3500 BCE)
*suHnús
"son, begotten male child, offspring"
(source of Proto-Germanic *sunuz, English son)
↓
Proto-Indo-Iranian (reconstructed)
*suHnúš
"son, offspring"
(cognate with Avestan hunuš "son")
↓
Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500 BCE)
sūnú (सूनु)
"son, child, male offspring"

[Synthesis into Tatpuruṣa Compound]
Epic & Classical Sanskrit (vāyu + sūnu, stem compounding) (c. 500 BCE; Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa)
Vāyusunu
"son of the wind god; title of Hanumān and Bhīma"

Note on Indo-European cognates and orthographic variation: Both components of Vāyusunu represent extraordinarily conservative retentions from Proto-Indo-European. The first stem, vāyu, derives from PIE *h₂wéh₁- ("to blow"), which also yielded English wind (via Proto-Germanic *windaz from the active participle *h₂wéh₁-nto-) and Latin ventus. The second stem, sūnu, stems from PIE *suHnús, which produced Old English sunu and Modern English son, German Sohn, Russian syn (сын), and Lithuanian sūnus. Thus, the compound Vāyusunu is etymologically equivalent to an English compound *"Wind-son". Classical Sanskrit grammarians record the stem with a long vowel ū (Vāyusūnu); the variant spelling with short u (Vāyusunu) in the raw prompt represents standard western Anglicized transliteration where vowel length macrons on final unstressed syllables were omitted.
