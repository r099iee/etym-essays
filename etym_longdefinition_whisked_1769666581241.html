whisked — past tense and past participle of the verb whisk; (1) moved, taken, or swept away quickly, suddenly, and lightly; (2) beaten or whipped rapidly with a whisk (in cooking). In the source context, Trumpkin "squinted and _____ his beard to and fro" — he moved his head so his beard swung rapidly from side to side as he tried to see his own shoulder: a quick, sweeping, back-and-forth motion exactly captured by the verb.

Primary sense — quick, light sweeping motion. To whisk is to move or brush something with a quick, light, sweeping motion — a flick. The core image is the brush or whisk itself sweeping across a surface; from this came (a) moving something out of the way quickly ("whisked the crumbs off the table"), (b) moving someone or something away with speed ("whisked her off to Paris"), and (c) moving one's own body parts rapidly ("whisked his beard to and fro").

  • Transitive sense: "she whisked the plates away" (removed quickly); "he whisked her off her feet" (carried away suddenly); "the wind whisked the leaves along" (swept rapidly).
  • Intransitive sense: "she whisked into the room" (moved quickly, lightly); "the bird whisked past" (passed in a flash).
  • Culinary sense: "whisked egg whites" — beaten rapidly with a whisk (or fork/electric beater) to incorporate air and produce foam; "whisked cream." This is now one of the word's two dominant everyday senses.
  • In context: "whisked his beard to and fro" describes a rapid, sweeping, side-to-side motion — the dwarf moving his head quickly so that his long beard swings from side to side while he squints and twists to examine his own shoulder.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The verb family includes the noun and related forms:

  • Noun — whisk: (1) a small hand-held brush (whisk broom) for sweeping; (2) a kitchen tool of wire loops for beating; (3) the motion itself ("a whisk of the tail").
  • Whisk broom: a short, stiff brush for brushing clothes or dust, named for the whisking motion of use.
  • Whisker: a hair of the beard/moustache or the long bristle of a cat/animal; from the "brushing, whisking" appearance of facial hair (a folk-etymological but historically plausible association).
  • Metaphoric use: "to whisk away/off" anything or anyone with swift, smooth efficiency — a chauffeur whisking a guest away, a waiter whisking plates, a breeze whisking leaves.
  • Adjective participle: "whisked" in cooking (whisked eggs, whisked cream) has become a standard recipe term.

Historical bridge & semantic reasoning. The verb comes from Middle English wisken/whisken (to move quickly, sweep), from Old Norse viska "to wipe, brush," from the Germanic noun wisk "a bundle of straw, a wisp" (a brush). The chain of meaning is literal-to-metaphor: a brush (bundle of twigs/straw) sweeps with a quick flick, so the action of sweeping/flicking is "whisking," and anything done with that quick light motion is "whisked." The culinary whisk came later (a wire loop tool that "beats" like a brush), cementing the modern cooking sense.
