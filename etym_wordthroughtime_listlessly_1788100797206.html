- c. 1300 — the adjective “listless” is coined from the dying noun “list” (“desire, appetite”) + “-less”; it first means heedless and careless, one who cares for nothing.
- 15th–16th c. — “listless” migrates from “caring for nothing” to “having no energy”: early medical prose treats will and body as one thing, so loss of desire becomes loss of vigour.
- 1727 — the adverb “listlessly” is first recorded, giving the condition a manner: characters now do things listlessly rather than merely being listless.
- 19th c. — Victorian fiction makes it a stock adverb of pathos and invalidism: the listless gesture signals grief, exhaustion, or slow decline, and carries pity with a touch of reproach.
- 20th–21st c. — journalistic and clinical use: a listless economy, a listless performance, a listless child; the word still keeps its quiet, sad, unmotivated flavour.
