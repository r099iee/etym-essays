The story of REPOSE begins not with the Latin of Rome but with a Greek verb of stopping: pauo (παύω), "to bring to a halt, to make cease, to stop." The deeper PIE ancestry of pauo is disputed — Robert Beekes marks the verb as of uncertain origin, possibly an expressive or reduplicative formation — so the honest tree begins with the attested Greek stem itself, which is already Homeric. From pauo was formed the abstract noun pausis (παῦσις), "a stopping, a cessation, a pause," and it is this noun that gave the Romans pausa, the source of our own word pause. From pausa a new Latin verb grew, pausare, "to halt, to rest, to cease," a word of the late and Christian centuries; and with the intensifying prefix re-, "again, thoroughly," it became repausare, "to cause to rest thoroughly, to give complete rest to." The shift is already visible here: what began as "to make something stop" becomes "to make something rest," and rest is a stopping that is also a release.

This Late Latin verb passed into the language of the Franks and their descendants as Old French reposer, "to rest," whose verbal noun repos named the rest itself. The French forms are instructive: alongside reposer the same stem produced poser, "to place, to put," which English borrowed separately as pose, and from the Latin pausa came the French and English pause — so that pause, pose, and repose are three modern English words from one ancient Greek family. English took over the rest-word in Middle English — the verb reposen and the noun repos — settling into the single spelling repose around 1400, meaning simply "to rest, to lie at rest." Chaucer already uses it, and the word has never since left the language.

By the sixteenth century the word had begun its climb in register. From physical rest it extended to the mind: "repose of mind," "repose of spirit" — the tranquillity that comes when agitation stops. It also took on the dignified, euphemistic sense of death: "final repose," "eternal repose," the resting of the dead, a usage that gives the word its gravity and ceremony in funeral and memorial language from the 16th-17th centuries onward. It is precisely this elevated register that distinguishes repose from the plain word rest: repose is rest with poise, stillness with dignity, almost sculptural immobility.

In the eighteenth century the word acquired an aesthetic career. Sir Joshua Reynolds, in his Discourses on Art (1769-1790), makes "repose" a formal principle of painting: the quality by which a composition settles, a calm balance that gives the eye rest after movement. Art critics, musicians, and architects use the word for harmony, for a cadence that brings a passage to stillness, for the composed balance of a facade. The bridge from body to mind to art is one of the classic metaphors of the language: to stop moving becomes to stop worrying, and a stopped, balanced composition becomes "reposeful." The German art-historical ideal of "noble simplicity and calm grandeur" (edle Einfalt und stille Grosse) that Winckelmann praised in Greek sculpture was regularly glossed in English through the vocabulary of repose.

One further complication must be cleared away. There is a second English verb repose, "to place, to lay, to put," as in "to repose trust in someone" or "to repose confidence in the government." This is a different word entirely: it descends from Latin reponere, "to place back" (re- + ponere, past participle repositus), not from pausare. In French the two verbs converged on the same spelling, reposer, and English inherited the ambiguity; but etymologically the rest-word and the place-word are unrelated, cousins only in the accident of their spelling. The careful lexicographer keeps them apart, and for the passage from which this card comes — books lying in undisturbed repose on the library shelves — only the rest-word, the child of Greek pauo, is in play.

The sound changes along the chain are regular and well attested: Greek pauo/pausis (with the diphthong au) to Latin pausa (au preserved) to pausare to re- + pausare, then Old French reposer, where the vowel of the stem was levelled as the verb was assimilated to the French conjugation classes and the stress fell on the infinitive ending; Middle English reposen and repos settled the spelling; and modern repose, /rɪˈpəʊz/, shows a stressed second syllable and a long French o. From a Greek "stop" to a French "rest" to an English "dignified stillness," the word has spent three thousand years slowing down.

Summary of the eras:
  • Greek (Homeric, c. 8th c. BCE): pauo "to make cease"; pausis "a stopping" (PIE ancestry uncertain; Beekes).
  • Latin (1st c. BCE - Late Latin): pausa "pause" (Greek loan); pausare "to halt, rest"; repausare "to give thorough rest."
  • Old French (c. 10th-12th c.): reposer "to rest"; repos "rest"; sibling poser gives English pose.
  • Middle English (c. 1400): reposen/repose "to rest, lie at rest."
  • 16th century: extension to mind ("repose of mind") and dignified death ("final repose").
  • 18th century: aesthetic principle — Reynolds' "repose" in painting; art, music, architecture.
  • Modern: elevated, literary register; homonym "repose (trust) in" < Latin reponere kept separate.
