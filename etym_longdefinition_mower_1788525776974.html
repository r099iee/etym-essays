A mower (noun) is defined in its primary historical and agricultural sense as a person who cuts down standing grass, hay, clover, or grain using a manual bladed implement—most characteristically a scythe or sickle. In modern technology, horticulture, and everyday parlance, the term has predominantly transitioned to denote a mechanical machine or motorized apparatus (such as a lawnmower, tractor-drawn disc mower, or autonomous robotic mower) designed specifically to cut, trim, and groom turf, meadows, lawns, or forage crops to an even, uniform height.

The word encompasses several distinct functional domains, historical evolutions, mechanical categories, and allegorical senses:

  • Historical Agrarian and Labor Sense: For over a millennium in pre-industrial Europe, the mower (Middle English mowere, Old English māwere) occupied a central, physically demanding role in the rural economy. Teams of skilled mowers worked in staggered lines across communal meadows during the midsummer hay-harvest (haymaking), swinging heavy two-handed scythes in rhythmic synchrony at dawn while the grass was still damp with dew, ensuring sufficient dried winter fodder for livestock.
  • Modern Mechanical and Turf-Care Applications: Following the industrial revolution, mower became the standard generic term for an extensive array of grass-cutting machinery:
    • Cylinder / Reel Mowers: Invented in 1830 by Edwin Beard Budding, utilizing a horizontal rotating helical cylinder against a bedknife to produce an immaculate, scissor-like cut on fine turf and golf greens.
    • Rotary Mowers: The standard modern domestic lawnmower, employing a high-speed horizontally spinning steel blade to cut grass through high-velocity impact.
    • Agricultural Mowers (Disc, Drum, and Flail Mowers): Heavy tractor-mounted machinery used in modern farming to fell extensive fields of alfalfa, silage, and hay.
    • Robotic Mowers: Modern autonomous battery-powered units programmed with GPS and perimeter sensors to continually maintain residential lawns.
  • Allegorical and Mythological Iconography (Death and Time): In classical and late medieval Western art, the mower serves as a profound metaphysical personification of Death (the Grim Reaper) and Father Time. Armed with his sweeping scythe, the allegorical Mower levels all human lives without distinction of wealth, youth, or social rank, reducing kings, maidens, and beggars alike like grass before the blade (drawing on biblical metaphors such as Psalm 103:15: "As for man, his days are as grass...").
  • Pastoral and Poetic Literature: In 17th-century English metaphysical poetry, preeminently in Andrew Marvell’s celebrated "Mower Poems" (The Mower Against Gardens, Damon the Mower, The Mower's Song), the mower is elevated into a complex pastoral archetype whose labor mirrors the violent beauty and unrequited passions of the human soul. In Oscar Wilde's fairy tale The Nightingale and the Rose, the yellow-rose tree describes its blossoms as "yellower than the daffodil that blooms in the meadow before the mower comes with his scythe," contrasting the fragile, fleeting perfection of natural beauty with the inevitable arrival of the blade.
  • Semantic Bridge: The historical bridge connecting the ancient agricultural laborer to the modern mechanical appliance is the agent suffix -er: when mechanized cutting machines replaced manual scythe-wielders in the 19th century, the existing agent noun mower was seamlessly transferred from the human laborer to the labor-saving apparatus.
