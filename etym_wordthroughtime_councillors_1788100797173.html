- c. 1200–1300 - 'counseillour' enters English from Anglo-French as a court adviser — the King's Councillors; perception: high office, closeness to the throne, personal wisdom.
- 14th–15th centuries - chartered towns, guilds and dioceses institutionalise councils; the word broadens from royal adviser to civic and corporate member; still prestigious, but now plural and corporate rather than personal.
- 1500s–1600s - the spelling is refashioned after 'council': 'councillor' splits from 'counsellor'; a Privy Councillor is a great office of state, a town councillor a civic dignity — the word becomes institutional.
- 1700s - municipal corporations grow self-perpetuating and complacent (closed corporations, patronage, borough-mongering); 'councillor' begins to pick up associations of petty power and place-seeking.
- 1835–1900s - the Municipal Corporations Act and later reforms create elected town councillors; the word acquires democratic, bureaucratic weight, and the novelists — Dickens first, then Wilde — satirise the type as pompous, image-conscious and useless.
- 20th–21st centuries - local-government reorganisation (Local Government Act 1972) fixes 'councillor' as the ordinary term for elected local representatives; modern associations: doorstep campaigns, ward surgeries, committee papers — respectable, democratic, unglamorous.
