- Bronze and Iron Age — lived-on waste: open heathy ground grazed, burned, and buried in, the everyday margin of farming life.
- Old English — hǣþ names both the tract and its shrubs: waste land, but useful waste, yielding fuel, grazing, and thatch.
- Medieval — the common waste: manorial heaths feed the village flock and the poor's turf-cutting, jealously guarded by custom.
- 1600s–1700s — the dangerous waste: highwaymen on Hounslow Heath and Bagshot make the word mean robbery and gibbets to travellers.
- Romantic and Victorian — the sublime waste: Lear's heath, Emily Brontë's moors, and Hardy's Egdon turn it into the landscape of the soul.
- Today — the protected waste: fenced, wardened nature reserves where nightjars still churr, and the suburb's proudest address.
