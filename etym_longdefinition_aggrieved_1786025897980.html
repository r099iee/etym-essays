AGGRIEVED describes the mental, emotional, and legal condition of a person who believes they have been unjustly wronged. It blends an internal state - resentment, sadness, wounded pride, indignation - with an external claim that a wrong has actually been committed by a person, institution, or authority. To be aggrieved is to feel not merely upset but entitled to redress: the aggrieved party regards their suffering as an injury that ought to be acknowledged, apologized for, or compensated.

The word operates across three broad provinces. In everyday emotional life, an aggrieved person is sulky, defensive, and full of self-justified complaint, convinced they have been treated with undue severity, passed over, insulted, or slighted. In socio-political discourse, "aggrieved groups" are communities that believe they have been systematically marginalized, disenfranchised, or victimized by law, policy, or institutions; the collective sense of grievance becomes the fuel of protest movements, identity politics, and what political scientists call "grievance politics." In law, "aggrieved" is a precise term of art: an "aggrieved party" is a person whose direct legal rights, property, or financial interests have been injured by a judgment or administrative decision, which confers the standing (locus standi) necessary to appeal or sue.

Primary Senses

  • Emotional / psychological: feeling resentment, distress, hurt, or injury as a result of perceived unfair treatment - "she felt deeply aggrieved by the promotion decision."
  • Legal / technical: having suffered a direct violation of one's rights, conferring legal standing to seek a remedy - "an aggrieved party may file an appeal within thirty days."
  • Social / political: describing groups or individuals who feel collectively wronged or disenfranchised by institutions - "aggrieved communities demanded reform."

Secondary and Nuanced Senses

  • Performative / self-righteous: "aggrieved tone," "aggrieved expression," "aggrieved silence" describe a posture of wounded virtue used to guilt or pressure others - a modern psychological extension of the core sense.
  • Fixed legal collocations: "party aggrieved," "person aggrieved," "aggrieved by the order" recur in statutes and judgments across all common-law jurisdictions as established terms of art.
  • Arbitration and international law: the "aggrieved party" is the claimant who must show a real, particular, and non-speculative injury to establish jurisdiction or standing.
  • Psychologized register: in relationship and self-help writing, "feeling aggrieved" names the resentment loop in which a person clings to a sense of injustice to protect wounded self-esteem.
  • Mild / ironic extension: in informal speech one can be aggrieved by trivia - "aggrieved that his coffee was cold" - deflating the strong legal-emotional word to comic disproportion.

Historical Semantic Bridges

AGGRIEVED descends from Latin aggravare, "to make heavy, to weigh down," built from the prefix ad- ("toward") and gravis ("heavy"). The chain of metaphors is instructive: first physical weight (a heavy load), then experiential burden (grief, hardship, affliction), then specifically unjust injury (a wrong that weighs upon one), and finally the precise legal idea that the wronged person bears a concrete injury that the law must relieve. Every modern sense - the sulking colleague, the outraged citizen, the party with standing to sue - is a descendant of that original image of being literally weighed down.
