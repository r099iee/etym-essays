The biography of ECLECTIC begins some six thousand years ago in the Proto-Indo-European root *leǵ-, whose concrete meaning was "to collect, to gather up". A speaker gathering sticks or berries was leǵ-ing. In Greek this root settled as légein, and a telling semantic shift occurred: because speech "gathers" words into ordered rows, légein came also to mean "to count, to recount, to say". Greek therefore used the same verb for gathering grain and for delivering a speech, and from it grew a whole family of words for speech and reason — lógos, "word, account, reason", and léxis, "speech", which gave English lexicon through the Greek lexikón, "a word-book". The Albanian verb mbledh, "to gather", survives as a modern cognate of the same root.

From légein the prefix ek-, "out", produced eklégein, "to pick out from a mass" — the act of a man choosing ripe fruit from a basket. Its verbal adjective eklektós, "chosen, select", was applied to livestock, to people, and to troops chosen for special service, and from it came eklektikós, "selective". In the second century AD the word acquired a philosophical career: a group of thinkers, chiefly in Alexandria, refused to bind themselves to a single master and began to pick out doctrines from Stoicism, Platonism, and Aristotelianism alike; they came to be known as the Eklektikoí, "the Eclectics". Diogenes Laertius applies the label to a philosopher who combined systems, and Galen and the medical writers of the period describe "eclectic" physicians who chose remedies from opposing schools on the evidence of their usefulness rather than on allegiance to a master.

For more than a millennium the word slept in Greek and Latin texts. It re-entered European life through French philosophy: in the eighteenth century French thinkers adopted éclectique, and the word crossed into English in 1683, first as a narrow label for the ancient philosophical sect. The decisive widening came with the nineteenth-century French eclectic school of Victor Cousin, who preached a philosophy of reasoned selection among systems; thereafter the word spread from philosophy to art, architecture, taste, and scholarship. In architecture the eclectic movement of the nineteenth century deliberately revived and combined Gothic, Classical, Renaissance, and Oriental forms; in criticism, an eclectic method was one that borrowed the best of competing theories without swearing allegiance to any.

The semantic development can be set out in stages:

  • 2nd c. AD: eklektikós names the philosophers who chose doctrines from many schools — sectarian and technical.
  • 1683: English eclectic, a learned label for the ancient sect.
  • 18th–19th c.: widens from philosophy to art, architecture, and taste — any principled borrowing across styles; approving in reformers, faintly reproachful in critics who saw it as lack of commitment.
  • 19th–20th c.: becomes a standard, neutral-to-approving descriptor of mixed but deliberate collections, music, reading, and methods.
  • Modern use: approving when self-applied ("eclectic taste"), faintly critical when it implies flitting without depth; always implies choice guided by taste, not randomness.

Meanwhile the same PIE root had produced, in Latin, a parallel family: legere, "to gather, to choose, to read", with its participle lectus, "gathered, chosen", gave English select, collect, elect, and intellect, and its "read" branch gave legend, lecture, legible, and lectern; legiō, "a legion" — literally a levied, selected body of troops — gave legion. The philosophical idea at the heart of eclecticism is thus the oldest meaning of the root itself: gathering, choosing, and picking out — the activity of the mind that does not take one basket but gathers from many, exactly as the author of this card gathers his examples from a hundred editions.
