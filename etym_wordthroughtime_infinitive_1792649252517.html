- [Late 14th–15th c. (c. 1380s–1450s)] — Borrowed into Middle English from Old French *infinitif* and Late Latin *infīnītīvus* (in John Trevisa’s translations and early grammatical treatises), translating Latin *modus infinitivus* to describe the unlimited, unconjugated mood of Latin verbs - [perception: understood as an erudite grammatical term of Latin scholastic syntax and classical translation]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English & Vernacular Grammars] — Applied systematically to vernacular English by early grammarians such as William Bullokar and Ben Jonson, identifying the two-word English construction (*to* + verb) as the functional equivalent of Latin single-word synthetic infinitives - [perception: regarded as a foundational grammatical category necessary for mastering English rhetoric and foreign languages]
- [18th–19th c. Prescriptive Era & The Split Infinitive Taboo] — Prescriptive grammarians (such as Robert Lowth, Lindley Murray, and Henry Alford) championed classical analogies, formulating the famous taboo against the "split infinitive" under the mistaken assumption that English *to* should cling indivisibly to its verb just like Latin synthetic suffixes (*amare*) - [perception: became a fiercely contested battleground of social propriety, stylistic purity, and pedantic etiquette]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Descriptive Linguistics] — Descriptive syntax and generative linguistics dismantled the 19th-century prejudice, analyzing the infinitive as a versatile non-finite clause head (CP/TP), while popular culture celebrated natural split infinitives (e.g., Star Trek’s "to boldly go") - [perception: appreciated as an indispensable, flexible structural feature of global English syntax]
