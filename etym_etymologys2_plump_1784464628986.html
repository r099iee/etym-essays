Chain for the drop-verb / adverb (the sense used on the card), followed by the related adjective branch. The word has no Indo-European root: it belongs to the North Sea Germanic sound-symbolic family, and that origin must be stated rather than forced into a PIE chain. Chain 1 — the drop-verb and adverb 'plump' (heavy, blunt falling) 1. Middle Dutch plompen 'to plunge, to fall flat, to plump into water' and Middle Low German plumpen 'to fall with a dull heavy sound' (attested from the 14th–15th centuries). These are imitative / expressive verbs of the North Sea coastal dialects; there is no PIE antecedent. 2. Middle English plumpe, adverbial 'with a heavy, sudden fall; bluntly, heavily' (attested from the 15th century, borrowed from the Low Countries trading dialects). 3. Early Modern English plump, verb, 'to fall or drop heavily; to set something down heavily and abruptly' (16th century, formed from the adverb). 4. Modern English plump, verb, extended figuratively: 'to plump for' = 'to come down heavily on one side; to vote or choose decisively' (attested from the 19th century). 5. Modern English PLUMP (the verb sense: to drop or set down heavily). Chain 2 — the adjective 'plump' (full, rounded) 1. Middle Dutch plomp / Middle Low German plump, adjective, 'blunt, thick, lumpy, dull, heavy' (attested from the 15th–16th centuries), again a sound-symbolic formation (thick soft heaviness), not a PIE word. 2. English plump, adjective, 'pleasingly full, rounded, well-fleshed' (attested from the 16th century). 3. Modern English PLUMP (the adjective sense: full and rounded). Notes on debate and nuance: Dictionaries treat the drop-verb (plump v.1) and the rounded adjective (plump adj.) as separate words of identical spelling, though both draw on the same North Sea Germanic expressive family; the exact relationship between them is not fully settled. Neither branch has any secure Indo-European ancestry: the pl- and -ump sound shape is imitative of a heavy soft mass, and scholars group these words with the sound-symbolic stratum of the Germanic vocabulary.
