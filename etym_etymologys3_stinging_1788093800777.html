1. A Germanic verb of the point. Stinging descends from a verb whose oldest recorded meaning in English is simply "to stab, thrust, prick": Old English stingan, a strong verb of the third class whose present-day forms (sting - stung - stung) still reflect its old ablaut pattern (stingan - stang - stungon - gestungen). The verb belongs to a small North and West Germanic family - Old Norse stinga, Old High German stingen - all meaning "to thrust, prick, pierce", pointing back to a Proto-Germanic *stinganą. Beyond Germanic the trail goes cold: the standard handbooks propose a PIE *steng- ("to thrust, push, jab"), but the cognates outside Germanic are not securely established, and suggested links to Greek stizō ("to prick, tattoo") or Latin īnstīgāre ("to goad, incite") are speculative. The honest statement is that this is a solidly Germanic word whose deeper ancestry is unknown - a fact worth teaching, since English verbs of sharp physical action (sting, pinch, punch, jab) are disproportionately of exactly this short, blunt, substrate character.

2. Old English: the point of the blade. In Old English, stingan was used primarily of weapons and sharp instruments - stabbing with a sword or spear, piercing a body - and early glosses render Latin pungere ("to prick") and stimulare ("to goad") with it. The noun sting ("a stab, thrust; a pointed tool") appears beside the verb. Nothing in the earliest record is specifically about bees or nettles: the word is a general verb of piercing whose victims are people and beasts and whose instruments are points. The transfer to insect-stings and nettle-burns, which seems so natural today, is a medieval development that followed the word when glossators and translators needed vernacular equivalents for the stinging plants and insects of the everyday world.

3. Middle English: bees, nettles, and the conscience. Middle English stingen completes the narrowing: to sting is now chiefly what bees, wasps, and nettles do - "the stinging nettle" becomes the standard English name of Urtica dioica, and later herbals (Gerard's Herbal of 1597 calls it "the common stinging nettle") keep the collocation alive into modern botany. Crucially, the same period discovers the moral sting: devotional and homiletic prose speaks of "the sting of conscience" - the pricking of the heart by guilt, imaged exactly as a bee's sting - and preachers exploit the paradox that the smallest of creatures can inflict the sharpest of pains. The figurative career of the word begins in the language of the pulpit, the most influential prose of the age.

4. The Reformation and the sacred sting (16th-17th centuries). The King James Bible (1611) fixed the word's greatest literary moment: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55), which made "the sting of death" a proverb of English mourning - death, the great enemy, reduced to a bee whose sting has been drawn. In the same period "stinging" enters the vocabulary of satire and controversy: 16th- and 17th-century polemicists call lampoons and replies "stinging" when they wound their targets precisely and personally, and critics of the next two centuries routinely praise or condemn "stinging satire". The physical and the moral senses now run in parallel, each borrowing from the other: a stinging wind and a stinging rebuke are felt to be alike, and the sensation-word has become a judgment-word.

5. The scientific century (18th-19th centuries). The age of Linnaeus made "stinging" precise without removing it from daily speech. Linnaeus's tenth edition of Systema Naturae (1758) established the order Hymenoptera for the bees, wasps, and ants - within it the aculeate (stinging) groups - so that "stinging insect" became a technical phrase; botany named the stinging hairs (trichomes) of Urtica and Loasa, and nineteenth-century cytology identified the stinging cells (cnidocytes with their explosive nematocysts) of jellyfish and sea anemones. The word now did double duty: a scientific classifier with a genus-and-function meaning, and still the street word for the sensation itself. Meanwhile the figurative sense solidified into the formulaic registers we inherit: "stinging criticism", "stinging defeat", "stinging cold" are all in place by the late nineteenth century, and the adjective has become one of English's default tools for pain that is sharp, small, and disproportionately powerful.

6. The twentieth century and the sting as trap. The modern era added a telling offshoot on the noun side: "sting" came to mean, in early twentieth-century American slang, a swindle or sharp trick, and then a police undercover trap - the "sting operation" of the 1970s, in which the target is enticed and then suddenly caught, i.e. "stung". The adjective "stinging" never took the criminal sense, but the family's logic is the same: a sting is sudden, sharp, and over before the victim understands what has happened. In journalism, "stinging" remains almost formulaic in sports and politics - "a stinging defeat", "a stinging rebuke", "stinging criticism" - and in the natural-history essay it still carries the full sensory charge it had in the herbals.

7. Phonetic outline. Old English /ˈstin.ɡɑn/ → Middle English /ˈstin.ɡən/ → modern /stɪŋ/: the final syllable is reduced and lost, the vowel stays short, and the strong-verb pattern persists in sting - stung (from OE stang - stungon). The modern -ing suffix is itself a historical merger: in Old English the present participle ended in -ende and the gerund in -ung/-ing, and Middle English collapsed both into -ing(e) - so "stinging" today carries two ancestries, the participial ("a stinging nettle" - one that stings, from OE -ende) and the gerundial ("the stinging of the cold" - the act, from OE -ing). The adjective has become so independent that in modern written English it now appears more often than the base verb.
