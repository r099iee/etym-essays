Introduction. Hastens is a modern English verb form, but its story begins more than a thousand years ago in the Germanic world, with a word for violent contention that somehow became a word for speed. The whole family — haste, hasty, hasten, hastily — descends from a Proto-Germanic root meaning “strife, contention”, and the semantic leap from “fighting” to “hurrying” is one of the most striking drifts in the history of English vocabulary.

1. The Germanic base. The Proto-Germanic noun *haifstiz meant “strife, contention, quarrel, violence”. It is securely attested in Gothic as haifsts (“strife, contention”), and its deeper Indo-European connections are uncertain; no convincing PIE etymon has been established. This is the crucial point: the earliest known meaning is adversarial, not fast. Speed is not present at the root at all.

2. Through the Franks into French. The Franks, the Germanic people who conquered Gaul, carried the word into their own Frankish dialect as *haifst (meaning “violence, hostility”). From Frankish the word entered the developing Romance speech of northern France — Old French haste — recorded from the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with the meaning “haste, hurry, urgency, speed”. Somewhere between the Frankish and Old French stages, the sense shifted: from the “violence, effort, contest” of the Germanic base, Old French haste took the sense of “eager effort, hurry, speed”. The bridge is metaphorical: a violent struggle requires urgent, quick, forceful effort, and that urgency was generalised to any quick action.

3. English borrowing. Middle English took haste from Old French in the thirteenth century (recorded c. 1300), meaning “speed, quickness, urgency”. Chaucer uses the word freely, and “in haste” and “with haste” become fixed phrases. The adjective hasty appears in the fourteenth century (meaning “speedy; also rash, precipitate”), and the adverb hastily follows. The family is at first purely nominal and adjectival in English; there is as yet no verb.

4. The late-blooming verb. The verb to hasten is a late, native formation: it is haste plus the English verbal suffix -en, first recorded around the 1560s. This is a distinctly modern English way of making verbs (cf. strengthen, lengthen, quicken), and it creates both the intransitive sense (“to make haste, to hurry”) and the transitive sense (“to cause to happen sooner”). From the beginning the verb carried the fuller semantic load of the noun, including the older implication of urgent effort. Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and Milton all use it; the Bible’s frequent “hasten” (e.g. “hasten unto me”) helped keep the word in elevated, urgent contexts.

5. Semantic specialisation in modern English.

  • 17th–18th c.: transitive uses flourish — to hasten an event, hasten a death, hasten the end; medical and legal prose pick these up.
  • 19th c.: the intransitive use is fully literary and narrative (“he hastened to the door”); it becomes a staple of Victorian fiction, where it coexists with “hurry”.
  • 20th c.: “hasten to add / say / emphasise” becomes a fixed rhetorical formula; in ordinary conversation hurry and rush displace hasten, leaving it formal, literary, or used for irony and elevation.
  • Present: hastens survives in written English, in science writing (“the drug hastens recovery”), in news prose, and in fantasy and epic narrative — precisely the register of the source sentence, where a river “hastens” to a Gate.

6. The semantic paradox preserved. Because the root meant “strife”, English hastens still faintly implies effort and eagerness, not merely mechanical speed: when something hastens, it does so with purpose and urgency, not casually. The word thus preserves, in its tone if not in its surface meaning, the violent effort of its Germanic ancestor.

Conclusion. Hastens is a short, plain-looking word with a deep and surprising history: Germanic “strife” → Frankish “violence” → Old French “haste, hurry” → Middle English “haste” → a sixteenth-century native verb “to hurry, to speed up”, now a formal and literary word whose urgency still carries the echo of a Germanic battle-word.
