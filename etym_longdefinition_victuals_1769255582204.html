victuals (plural noun, /ˈvɪtəlz/) — In this scene the word names exactly what the Doctor is stuffing into the wallet: food taken from the supper table of Caspian's Highness, provisions for the road. Victuals are not a single dish nor a snack but food regarded as stores — the practical, life-sustaining supplies that a traveler, soldier, or household lays in for a journey or a siege. The tone is homely and dignified at once: the word carries a faint old-fashioned courtesy (it is a word for a prince's provisions) and a sturdy, practical weight (it is also a word for what an army eats). And it hides a delicious trap: it is spelled with a c but pronounced "vittles," a fossilised echo of its Latin ancestry that trips up native speakers to this day — which is itself part of the word's charm and the reason a reader must be told how to say it.

Primary sense — food as provisions

  • Meaning: food and drink regarded as provisions, supplies, or stores — the food a household, army, ship's crew, or traveler lays in for consumption, especially for a journey, siege, or campaign.
  • Form: always plural in this sense; the singular victual is rare and chiefly used for a single item or in the verb form.
  • Register: homely and sturdy — dignified without being fine; the everyday provisioning word of armies, inns, ships, and kitchens.

Polysemy and related senses

  • Military and nautical sense: in military and naval usage, "victuals" are the food supplies of a force or ship — "the troops were short of victuals," "victualing the fleet." The word is strongly associated with logistics and supply.
  • Household/stores sense: the provisions a household keeps in its larder — "they stocked up on victuals for the winter."
  • Verb victual: "to supply with food" — "to victual a ship, an army, a garrison"; also reflexive, "the soldiers victualed themselves." The verb carries the supply-logistics sense into action.
  • Agent noun victualler (also victualer): one who supplies provisions — historically a licensed purveyor of food (a "licensed victualler" being a tavern or innkeeper licensed to sell food and drink), also a ship's provisioner.
  • Technical jargon victualling: the trade or practice of supplying provisions — "victualling yard," "victualling house," "victualling bill."
  • Dialectal/folksy vittles: the traditional spoken form, now folk-flavored — the food of campfires, frontiersmen, and simple folk; the spelling variant "vittles" reflects actual pronunciation and appears in dialect and informal writing.
  • Figurative/humorous uses: occasionally extended to anything that sustains — "intellectual victuals" — and in jocular archaic register used for a hearty meal; the word can also mean, loosely, "grub, food."

The spelling–pronunciation puzzle

  • Spelled victuals, spoken vittles: the c is silent, and the word rhymes with "bottles" /ˈvɪtəlz/. The pronunciation preserves the Old French form, while the spelling was re-Latinized in the 16th century to restore the etymological c.
  • Why it happens: English borrowed the word from French with the French pronunciation (no /k/ sound); Renaissance scholars then "corrected" the spelling toward Latin victualia, and the mismatch froze — the classic case of etymological spelling without etymological sound.
  • Pronunciation note: the two-syllable /ˈvɪtəlz/ (not /ˈvɪktʃuəlz/) is the only standard pronunciation; saying "victuals" as spelled is a shibboleth that instantly marks a reader as having never heard the word spoken.

Boundary comparisons with the vocabulary of food-as-supply

  • provisions: the broadest term — food and supplies laid in for a journey or need; victuals is narrower (food proper) and more old-fashioned and homely.
  • food: the general hypernym for anything edible; victuals specifically means food regarded as stored or supplied, with a sturdy, old-fashioned, plural register.
  • supplies: any provisions of any kind — food, fuel, ammunition; victuals are specifically the edible part of supplies.
  • rations: fixed, measured allowances of food, especially in the military — victuals are supplies generally, not necessarily portioned.
  • fare: the food available or served — "simple fare"; fare is about what is on the table, victuals about what is laid in.
  • sustenance / nourishment: what keeps one alive — abstract, biological, often formal; victuals are concrete, countable-in-plural, practical stores.
  • grub / chow / eats: informal, modern slang for food; victuals is old-fashioned and literary but shares the homely, hearty spirit.
  • viands: choice or delicate food, elegant and archaic; victuals are plain provisions, sturdy not delicate.
  • provender: dry fodder for livestock, extended humorously to human food; victuals is ordinary human provisions without the animal-feed flavor.

Connotation summary

  • Old-fashioned, homely, sturdy — the food of journeys, armies, and larders.
  • Dignified without being fine — a word for a prince's provisions and a soldier's rations alike.
  • Famous spelling trap — silent c, pronounced "vittles," the classic case of Latin-revival spelling clashing with living pronunciation.
