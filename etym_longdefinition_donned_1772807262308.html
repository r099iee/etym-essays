When Yu-cun 'hastily donned the hat and robe of office which he had temporarily laid aside', the verb is not a neutral fashion note. Donning official dress in a Qing bureaucratic world is a ritual of re-assuming public power: hat and robe are regulated signs of rank. The haste underscores urgency, for he must present himself as magistrate, not private citizen, before receiving a visitor. The tone is formal and slightly literary; donned makes the act of dressing feel deliberate and symbolic even when done quickly, converting cloth into authority. DEFINITION, PRIMARY SENSE. Donned is the past tense and past participle of don, a transitive verb meaning to put on an item of clothing or attire. To don a garment is to put it onto one's body, the act of dressing rather than the state of wearing. In the form on this card, donned is the narrative preterite: he put the hat and robe on, and the action is complete. The verb is standard but leans literary; everyday speech prefers put on. POLYSEMY AND HOMOGRAPHS. Don is a small word with a busy homograph family, and it is important to keep the true etymology distinct from the look-alikes:
  • don, verb 'put on clothing' (the sense of the card): from Middle English do on, a contraction of the phrase 'do on' meaning 'put on.' The paired antonym is doff, 'take off,' from 'do off.' Figurative extensions: to don a disguise, to don an air of authority, to don mourning.
  • don, noun 'a fellow of a college' (Oxford and Cambridge): a university teacher, a fellow or head of a college. This is a completely different word, borrowed in the 17th century from Spanish or Portuguese don, from Latin dominus, 'lord, master.'
  • Don, noun 'a Spanish or Italian title': Don Quixote, a courtesy title for a gentleman, also from Latin dominus. Unrelated to the verb don.
  • don, noun 'a Mafia boss' (modern slang): a gangland chief, from the same Spanish/Italian title tradition. Unrelated to the clothing verb.
  • don, verb, rare figurative 'to take on or assume': to don a role, to don responsibility, extending the clothing image to roles and burdens.
  • Related formations of the do + particle pattern: doff (do off), dialectal dout (do out, 'extinguish,' as in 'dout the candle'), and the modern phrasal verb do up ('fasten, renovate').
SEMANTIC BRIDGE. The bridge is a pattern of English phrasal verbs. Old English dōn meant not only 'perform an action' but also 'put, place, cause to be,' so 'do on' meant literally 'put (clothing) onto oneself,' and 'do off' meant 'take off.' In late Middle English the two syllables fused: do on became don, do off became doff. The verb's whole history is native Germanic, a contraction rather than a loanword, which is why it can sound both plain (in origin) and ceremonious (in collocation with armor, robes, and uniforms). COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER. Common collocations include donned his coat/hat/armor/robes, donned a disguise, donned mourning, and the formula don the robe of office. Register is literary, journalistic, or historical; everyday speech prefers put on. In historical fiction and translations of imperial Chinese or European court scenes, don plus robe or hat of office is a stock way to mark status change. The past form donned on this card is the exact narrative tense of the source; keep it as the study form rather than lemmatizing to don. As with all English weak verbs, the past tense adds the -ed suffix with no change to the stem: don, donned, donned.
