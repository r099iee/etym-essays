An avalanche is a large mass of snow, ice, and frequently rock or earth that suddenly detaches from a mountainside and descends rapidly under gravity, gathering material, debris, and speed as it moves. In its strictest sense the word denotes the catastrophic natural event itself; by powerful metaphorical extension it also names any sudden, overwhelming, and practically unstoppable onrush of things, people, or information.

Primary sense — snow and ice avalanches

  • A rapid flow or slide of accumulated snow down a steep slope, triggered when the force of gravity overcomes the cohesion and friction of the snowpack. Standard types include loose-snow avalanches, slab avalanches (a cohesive sheet breaking as one), powder (dust) avalanches, and wet-snow avalanches; they range from a thin surface slough to a kilometre-scale slide of millions of tonnes.
  • In snow science and ski safety the noun anchors a whole technical vocabulary: avalanche danger, avalanche forecasting, avalanche terrain, avalanche transceiver, avalanche control (deliberate triggering by explosives), avalanche paths, and avalanche cycles.
  • The dry, fast-moving powder avalanche can outrun a skier or even a car, and its air-blast alone can destroy buildings — the reason avalanche warnings close roads and pistes after heavy snowfall or thaw.

Secondary senses — rock, debris, and mixed avalanches

  • In geology and mountaineering the word extends to cascades of fractured rock, glacial ice, or mixed debris (rock avalanche, ice avalanche, debris avalanche), often set off by earthquakes, rapid thaw, or human activity, and behaving like a granular flow of matter.
  • In geomorphology it is classed among rapid mass-wasting events (gravitational downslope transport), contrasted with slow creep, solifluction, or movement as a coherent block.

Tertiary and figurative senses

  • An overwhelming influx: "an avalanche of criticism", "an avalanche of letters", "an avalanche of data", "an avalanche of accusations". The image is of something that begins small and grows unstoppably, sweeping aside resistance.
  • In physics and electronics, the avalanche effect is the self-amplifying cascade of charge carriers in dielectric breakdown or in photodiodes; in computing, "avalanche" names a sudden propagation of failures or changes through a network or system.
  • In sports journalism, "an avalanche of points/goals/runs" means an unstoppable scoring burst; the idiom is near-equivalent to a snowball effect but grander in scale and violence.

Polysemy and the historical bridge

English took the word in 1789 directly from French as a technical Alpine term for a snow-slide. The semantic bridge from snow to rock, earth, and finally to any overwhelming mass rests on one shared image: a body of material that gives way and pours downhill irresistibly, engulfing everything in its path. The figurative sense is attested in the 19th century, and twentieth-century media culture hardened it into the standard metaphor for a large, sudden, enveloping and escalating onrush. The word's staying power as a metaphor lies precisely in scale plus momentum: it is not a mere flood but a cascading collapse, which makes it the default image for anything that cannot be stopped once begun.

Register and usage

  • Neutral-to-formal register; ubiquitous in news, safety, scientific, and literary writing.
  • The verb ("to avalanche", "avalanching") is rarer and largely confined to technical and journalistic prose.
  • The plural "avalanches" may refer to repeated events in a region or to a sustained series of overwhelming misfortunes.
