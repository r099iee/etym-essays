thrust: Thrust is a word of pressure, born in the PIE root *treud-, “to press, push, squeeze,” a root that seems to have meant the force of a body bearing upon another. Latin carried it into trūdere, “to push, shove, thrust,” and from that verb Latin compounded a whole vocabulary of directed pushing that survives in English: intrude, “to push oneself in uninvited” (intrūdere); extrude, “to push out” (extrūdere), as of metal forced through a die; protrude, “to push forward”; obtrude, “to push oneself upon another against his will”; and abstruse, from abstrūdere, “to push away, to hide” — the abstruse is the pushed-out-of-sight, the hidden. The Germanic branch took the same root and built from it verbs and nouns of oppression: Proto-Germanic *þrautaną, “to press, to oppress,” gave Old English þrēat, “pressure, oppression; a crowd,” and from that noun grew English threat — the word for pressing someone with menace, later the menace itself. The direct ancestor of our verb, however, is northern: Old Norse þrysta, “to press, squeeze, thrust,” which Middle English borrowed as thrusten around 1300.

The borrowed verb then did something characteristically English: it fell in with the native strong-verb pattern, so that its past tense and past participle are identical to the present — thrust, thrust, thrust — a fact that suits its meaning, for a thrust is instantaneous, a force that is over in the same moment it begins. From the physical lunge, English extended the word in two directions: downward into the mechanical (the thrust of an engine, of a piston, of a screw, and in the 20th century the rocket thrust that launches spacecraft) and upward into the rhetorical (the thrust of an argument, the point of an attack, the thrust of a policy). In the 15th to 18th centuries the word was above all the weapon-word — the thrust of a sword or spear, the penetrating jab that stops dead on the -t — and it remained the standard noun for a piercing attack. The word has never lost that primitive pressure: even the modern rocket engine’s thrust is a pushing, a directed, concentrated force along a line. In Vance’s sentence the word retains all its primitive pressure: Turjan is thrust in four directions and then one — pushed along the lines of the compass like a coin on a board, each shunt a separate, exact, instantaneous act of force, the geometry of the journey dictated by the verb itself, a vector rather than a flight.

hurled: Hurl is a word of noise and speed, and its ancestry is strikingly shallow and unstable: no Indo-European root can be established for it. The verb rests on an imitative Germanic base *hurr-, “rapid motion; roaring sound,” probably onomatopoeic in origin — the word sounds like the rush of something tearing through the air. Low German hurreln, “to throw, to dash,” and East Frisian hurreln, “to roar, to bluster,” show the base in two faces, motion and noise, and Middle English hurlen entered the language in c. 1225 in the collision sense: “to run against,” to rush into contact. Within a century the meaning had been refined to what we know: by c. 1300 hurlen meant “to throw forcibly,” and by the late 14th century “to rush violently.” The verb’s career is a perfect example of semantic specialization from imitative noise to precise ballistic action.

From the start hurl was entangled with hurtle, the frequentative of hurt (from Old French hurter, “to strike”), and English writers confused them for centuries; the OED finally drew the classic boundary — in hurtle the notion is forcible collision, in hurl forcible projection. The two words have since parted company: hurl is the throw, hurtle the collision. The noun hurl (1520s) names the violent throw itself; hurling, the ancient Irish and Cornish stick-and-ball game, preserves the word’s bodily violence in sport; and hurly-burly (1530s) — the reduplicated commotion — gave Shakespeare his “hurlyburly of the battle.” Across the centuries the verb’s registers widened: to hurl a spear, a stone, or a blow, and to hurl words — abuse and insults shouted with ballistic force — so that hurl became the throw of giants and storms, of catapults and gods. In Vance’s sentence the word is used at its purest: a great blow hurled him from the cloud — not thrown by a hand but projected by a strike, the body in flight after one syllable of release, pure ballistic physics, the imitative roar of the Germanic base heard again in the movement of the magic.

sprawled: Sprawl is a word of scattering, and its biography runs from convulsion to posture to the shape of cities. The probable root is PIE *sper-, “to scatter, spread,” well attested in Latin spargere, “to scatter, sprinkle” — the ancestor of sparse, disperse, and aspersion — and in the Germanic family of spreading. Proto-Germanic *spreaw- gave Old English sprēawlian, “to move convulsively, to writhe,” and the earliest English sense of the word is not a posture at all but a motion: the convulsive jerking of a body out of control, limbs moving against the will. Norwegian sprala, “to sprawl, wriggle,” and Danish sprælde, “to sprawl, jerk,” are its close kin, and they confirm the writhing, twitching origin. From that convulsion the word turned, in Middle English spraulen and after, to the posture: by the 16th and 17th centuries to sprawl was to lie or fall with the limbs flung open — an undignified, unheroic scattering of the body, the shape of a man dropped like a puppet. Shakespeare’s age used the word of the fallen and the drunk, and the posture sense has dominated ever since: we sprawl on sofas, on beaches, on grass, always without grace.

In the 20th century the word made its great metaphorical leap from body to landscape: urban sprawl, recorded from the 1950s, names the unplanned, disorganized spread of a city across the countryside — the city as a fallen body, its limbs scattered every which way, growing without plan or direction. The noun sprawl (the spread itself) and the adjective sprawling (a sprawling estate, a sprawling novel) complete the family. The word has thus moved from writhing to lying to building: the same scattered, graceless, ungoverned spreading now describes bodies, cities, and even narratives. In Vance’s sentence the word is used at the boundary of its two oldest senses: the great blow hurled him from the cloud and sprawled him into Embelyon — the body arriving convulsively, limbs flung open, the convulsion and the posture fused in a single verb of arrival.
