The weapon mace descends from a popular Latin word for mallet. Classical Latin had mateola, a beetle or mallet for pounding, and Vulgar Latin generalized reduced forms *mattea, *mattia, club, hoe-handle. Old French took this as mace, masse, heavy club, sledgehammer, stock of a crossbow, mass of metal. Middle English borrowed it in the 13th century as mace, masse for a spiked club and for the staff of an official. The sense development within French already shows the double track: crushing tool and weighty lump, hence Modern French masse both for mass and for ceremonial mace, and English mass as learned doublet for quantity.

Deeper pre-Latin origin is obscure. Latin mateola has no secure Indo-European pedigree; handbooks suggest a borrowing from a Mediterranean substrate or a expressive formation for pounding tools, akin to mattock words. There is no agreed PIE root, so the responsible chain stops at Latin with an explicit unknown beyond, rather than inventing a root. This must be distinguished sharply from the spice mace, which comes from Latin macis, from Greek makir, an eastern aromatic bark, through Old French macis. The two English words converged in spelling by the 15th century but have wholly separate histories.

  • Latin (c. 1st c. BCE–5th c. CE): mateola, mallet, beetle; Vulgar *mattea / *mattia, club, lump; agricultural and artisanal tool vocabulary.
  • Old French (c. 11th–13th c.): mace, masse, club of war, hammer, heavy staff; massier, mace-bearer; Chanson de Roland and chronicles show maces against armor.
  • Middle English (c. 13th–15th c.): mace, masse, roughly 1225 onwards for weapon and for sergeant's staff; civic records of London and guilds list maces as both arms and insignia.
  • Early Modern specialization (15th–17th c.): military mace declines with firearms while civic mace elaborates in silver-gilt; universities and parliaments commission great maces; Crown jewellers standardize form with crown, orb, and cross.
  • Modern extensions (19th–20th c.): Indian gada translated as mace in physical culture; Smith & Wesson Chemical Company's Mace trademark 1960s for CN tear gas, genericized to verb to mace.

Phonetically the path is regular: Latin -tt- and -teola simplify in Gallo-Romance, Old French nasalized vowel denasalizes in Anglo-French borrowing, Middle English great vowel shift raises the vowel to modern diphthong. Spelling fluctuates mace / masse / mase until 18th-century standardization fixes mace for weapon and emblem, mass for quantity, mace also retained for spice by convergence.

Social history explains the ceremonial survival. When sergeants-at-arms ceased to guard with functional iron, cities and corporate bodies retained the form in precious metal to assert chartered legitimacy. The English Civil War struggle over the Commons Mace, its removal by Cromwell as a fool's bauble and restoration in 1660, and colonial assemblies claiming mace-rights, show the object as constitutional shorthand. University maces from St Andrews to Harvard copy parliamentary form to claim parallel learned authority. Hence your passage ends its catalogue with the mace: the stick fully politicized as the magistrate's or king's coercive guarantee.
