Primary sense — the insect. A locust is a short-horned grasshopper of the family Acrididae (notably the subfamilies Oedipodinae and Cyrtacanthacridinae) that, under conditions of crowding and good rainfall, undergoes a striking phase change from a solitary to a gregarious form and migrates in enormous swarms which strip vegetation over thousands of square kilometres. The best-known species are the desert locust (Schistocerca gregaria), the migratory locust (Locusta migratoria) and the red locust (Nomadacris septemfasciata). A single swarm can cover hundreds of square kilometres and contain tens of billions of individuals; the UN Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that a large swarm can consume in a single day the food of tens of thousands of people. Because the insect's devastating effect is inherently collective, the plural locusts is the form in which the word most often appears in literature and reportage — one locust is a grasshopper; a thousand million are a plague.

Secondary senses — the word's wanderings. “Locust” has travelled far from entomology:

  • Cicadas. In North American English “locust” is commonly applied to cicadas — the periodical cicadas (Magicicada spp.) are popularly called “seventeen-year locusts” — a transfer motivated by the loud buzzing and the vast synchronous mass emergences every 13 or 17 years, although cicadas are true bugs (order Hemiptera), not grasshoppers, and do not eat crops.
  • Trees and fruit. The “locust tree” is the black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia), whose compound leaves and pods reminded early settlers of the carob; the honey locust (Gleditsia triacanthos) bears long twisted pods. The carob tree itself (Ceratonia siliqua) is also called “locust”, because its sweet pods were long identified with the “locusts” eaten by John the Baptist in the wilderness (Matthew 3:4; Mark 1:6) — the “St John's bread” tradition, although modern scholarship holds that the Baptist almost certainly ate the actual insects, which Leviticus 11:22 lists as clean food.
  • Metaphorical multitudes. Since the plague of Egypt (Exodus 10), “locusts” has meant any devouring, destructive mass of people or things: plundering armies, rapacious landlords, speculators, parasites. The German politician Franz Müntefering's 2005 comparison of private-equity firms to Heuschrecken (“locusts”) was translated into headlines worldwide, and “swarms of locusts” remains stock imagery for anything that strips value and moves on.
  • Biblical and apocalyptic register. In the Bible locusts are the eighth plague of Egypt and the instrument of judgment in Joel (2:25, “the years that the locust hath eaten”) and Revelation (9:3–7, the locusts of the abyss led by Apollyon), so the word carries a permanent eschatological undertone: “a sea of locusts”, as in this card's context from The Hobbit, evokes an army as numberless, hungry and unstoppable as a swarm.

History and grammar. The word entered English in the 13th–14th century from Old French locuste, from Latin locusta, which the Romans used for both the insect and the lobster — the same Latin word split into English locust and (via Old English loppestre) lobster, a classic doublet. The singular/plural pair locust/locusts is regular; the plural predominates in agricultural, biblical and figurative writing because swarms are the salient reality. In this card's sentence — “the bats are above his army like a sea of locusts” — Tolkien uses the plural in its full ancient force: a simile of numberless, devouring, apocalyptic motion.
