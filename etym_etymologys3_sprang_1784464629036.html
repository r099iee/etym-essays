Sprang is the inherited strong past tense of one of English's oldest and most vital verbs of motion. Its history reaches from a reconstructed Indo-European root of rapid movement, through a Proto-Germanic verb that the northern peoples applied to leaping, bursting, and the springing of water, down to the comic-chaotic moment when a dozen dwarves leave their benches at once and knock over the table. Because the word is a native Germanic inheritance rather than a loan, its history includes genuine sound change, a living inflectional pattern, and an unusually rich radiation of figurative senses. 1. Indo-European foundations. The deepest recoverable layer is an Indo-European root of sudden, rapid motion, reconstructed in the handbooks in the neighborhood of *sprengʰ- or *spergʰ- (Pokorny's *sp(h)er(e)gh- "to move quickly, hasten"). The semantic kernel is sudden, vigorous movement — precisely what the English word still does. The Indo-European cognate set is thin, however, and the precise shape of the root is debated; what is securely reconstructible is the Germanic verb that descended from it. 2. Proto-Germanic and the strong-verb system. Proto-Germanic formed *springaną "to spring, jump, burst," a class III strong verb — that is, a verb that signals its past tenses by changing the root vowel (ablaut) rather than by adding a dental suffix. This is the crucial inheritance for understanding sprang: the past form is not a grammatical invention of English but a living relic of the old vowel-grading system of Indo-European and Germanic. West Germanic kept the verb with the same shape and meaning, and the separate branches carried it in parallel.
  • Sound form (ablaut series): the verb shows the classic i-a-u alternation — Modern English spring / sprang / sprung — inherited from the Germanic strong-verb ablaut.
  • Germanic cognates: Old Norse springa "to jump, also to burst, crack"; Old High German and German springen "to jump"; Dutch springen "to jump" — all from the same *springaną.
3. Old English: springan. Old English inherited the verb as springan, "to leap, fly up, burst forth," a class III strong verb with the past singular sprāng, the past plural sprungon, and the past participle sprungen. Old English prose and verse use it for leaping bodies, bursting springs, and the sudden emergence of things from within — the same semantic range of jump and burst that the modern word still commands. This is genuine inherited vocabulary, the kind of everyday motion-verb that English has kept unbroken for over a thousand years. 4. Middle English and the modern sound. Middle English continued the verb as springen with the same strong pattern, and the modern form spring continues it directly. The vowel of the past tense has shifted with the general English vowel changes: Old English sprāng with a long open a became modern sprang /spræŋ/, following the ordinary development of that sound in southern English. The spelling sprang is the direct written descendant of the old strong past. Meanwhile the participle branch produced sprung, and colloquial English in some dialects has begun to level sprung into the simple past slot — a continuing grammatical change that careful and literary usage, including Tolkien's, resists. 5. The great semantic radiation. The history of spring is also the history of a word that has never stopped generating new senses, all from the single image of sudden, elastic emergence.
  • Bodily leap: the original motion-sense — "sprang up," "spring to one's feet."
  • Water source: water "springs" from the ground; the noun spring for the source itself dates to Old English.
  • The season: Spring is the time of bursting-forth, when plants "spring up" — the seasonal name grew from the verb.
  • Mechanical spring: from the sixteenth century onward, the elastic coil that leaps back was named spring, and from it "to spring a trap," "to spring a lock."
  • Figurative emergence: "spring to mind," "spring a surprise," "spring into action" — all retain the idea of sudden release.
6. In the literature. The strong past sprang is therefore not a weak -ed invention but living ablaut — the old vowel change still signaling completed sudden motion. In the Bag End chaos, "All the dwarves sprang up, knocking over the table" is pure kinetic inheritance: one strong past for a dozen bodies leaving the benches at once. The card requires exactly that inflection — the narrative past of collective leap — not the present spring and not a weak substitute.
