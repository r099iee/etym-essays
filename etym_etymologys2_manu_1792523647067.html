Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*men-
"to think, possess active mind, remember"
(source of Greek ménos, Latin mens/memini, English mind)
↓
Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*mon-u-s / *man-w-
"the thinking one, human being, person, mortal"
(source of Proto-Germanic *mann- "human/man", Proto-Germanic *Mannus)
↓
Proto-Indo-Iranian (reconstructed)
*mánuš
"human, thinker, primal progenitor of mankind"
(source of Avestan manuš-)
↓
Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500–1000 BCE; Ṛgveda)
manu (मनु)
"the thinking one, human being, primal sacrificer"
↓
Late Vedic / Epic Sanskrit (c. 800–200 BCE; Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa & Rāmāyaṇa)
Manu (मनु) / Vaivasvata Manu
"the flood hero; progenitor of mankind; founder of Ayodhyā"
↓
Classical Sanskrit Dharmaśāstra (c. 200 BCE–present; Manusmṛti)
Manu
"the archetypal lawgiver; progenitor of humanity; founder of Ayodhyā"

Note on historical debate: Comparative Indo-European philologists (including Jacob Grimm, Georges Dumézil, and Manfred Mayrhofer) have highlighted the extraordinary cognate relationship between Sanskrit Manu and Proto-Germanic *Mannus. In Tacitus's Germania (chapter 2), the Germanic tribes celebrated an earth-born god Tuisto and his son Mannus as the founder and progenitor of their race. Both Sanskrit Manu and Germanic *Mannus descend directly from the PIE root *men- ("to think"), confirming that the Indo-European ancestors designated humanity specifically as "the thinking beings." While traditional Indian commentators within the Mimāṃsā school considered Manu an eternal divine archetype, modern historical linguistics demonstrates that the name is an ancient inherited Indo-European mythological figure representing the primal ancestor-priest who first established ritual sacrifice and legal order.
