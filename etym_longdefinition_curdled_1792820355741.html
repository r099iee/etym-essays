Curdled is the past tense and past participle of curdle: to turn milk, cream, or sauce from smooth liquid into clots and whey — the white lumps rising as the proteins seize and the liquid thins. Every reader knows the sight from a neglected carton: the pour that comes out in chunks, the smell faintly sharp, the tea ruined. Curdled names both the event and its queasy aftermath.

In the source sentence the curdling is done by a face, not by time: a countenance so sour, so disapproving, that it would have curdled new milk — milk so fresh it should be proof against anything. The hyperbole is an old country joke with a supposed physics behind it: thunder, it was said, turned milk, and a thunderous face could do the same. Tolkien gives the line as village gossip, the relish of the teller audible in the exaggeration: her glare soured the very air of the dairy.

Core meaning in detail: Chemically, curdling is protein denaturation: acids or enzymes (rennet in cheese-making, lemon in cooking, bacteria in souring) make casein clump into curds while the whey separates. Curdle is the frequentative of curd — literally to keep making curds — and curdled the completed state: the milk has curdled, the sauce is curdled. Cooks fear accidental curdling (split hollandaise, grainy custard) and exploit deliberate curdling (cheese, junket, buttermilk); the same chemistry ruins one dish and founds another.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Separated into curds and whey (primary dairy sense, from 1580s): curdled milk, curdled cream left in the sun. The bridge from curd is the frequentative -le: to keep forming curds until smoothness is gone.
  • Split or seized in cooking (culinary extended sense): the sauce curdled when the lemon hit it, curdled custard. The bridge is the same chemistry moved to the saucepan: heat plus acid does in seconds what days do to the carton.
  • Soured in temper or relations (figurative social sense): their friendship curdled, curdled affection. The bridge is taste: just as milk turns from sweet to sharp, so goodwill turns from warm to acid — the metaphor keeps the smell.
  • Frozen by fear or horror (the blood-curdling sense): to curdle the blood, a scream to curdle your marrow. The bridge imagines blood thickening like milk in the veins: terror as a cold acid poured into the body — first recorded in the 1600s and never out of fashion in ghost stories.
  • Chilled and stilled by a look or atmosphere (Tolkien's comic sense): a face to curdle milk, a glare that curdles conversation. The bridge is the old folk belief that sour faces, thunder, and witches could turn milk at a distance — malice imagined as weather.
  • Clotted or congealed generally (physical fringe): curdled paint, curdled fog, curdled clouds. The bridge is visual: anything white, lumpy, and parted from its liquid reads as curd, from bad emulsion to November skies.

Register and connotation: curdled is visceral and faintly disgusting — the stomach's word as much as the dictionary's. Dairy contexts make it domestic and comic (ruined puddings, suspicious cartons), horror contexts make it physiological (curdled blood, curdled screams), and social contexts make it sourly moral (curdled idealism, a curdled heart). It always implies a fall from smooth to lumpy, from sweet to sharp: nothing curdles upward. Tolkien's usage keeps the folk comedy — the sour face as dairy disaster — while letting the horror idiom curdle the blood echo faintly behind it.
