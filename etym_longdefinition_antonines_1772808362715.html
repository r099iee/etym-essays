The Antonines (plural) are the Roman emperors of the Antonine house — above all Antoninus Pius (r. 138–161) and Marcus Aurelius (r. 161–180) — and, by extension, the whole dynasty and era they define. The name derives from the Roman family name Antonius, which became the imperial cognomen of Antoninus Pius; when he adopted Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus into the Antonine name, the family label became an imperial brand that outlived the bloodline through the adoptive succession. In Gibbon's usage — 'THE EXTENT AND MILITARY FORCE OF THE EMPIRE IN THE AGE OF THE ANTONINES WHICH WAS IN THE YEARS 98–180 A.D.' — the label gathers the adoptive sequence from Nerva and Trajan through Hadrian to Pius and Marcus (and sometimes the shadow of Commodus), the stretch of the second century that Gibbon famously judged the period during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous. The word is thus already half-mythic: a proper-name derivative turned into a period concept, a genealogical label recast as a historiographical golden age. The tone is panegyric tempered by the tragic architecture of The Decline and Fall — the Antonine ceiling makes every later drop steeper. The reader is meant to feel not only chronology but a moral-political climate: absolute power guided, for a time, by virtue and wisdom. Boundary work among near-labels matters. Nerva-Antonine dynasty is the modern scholarly tag for the broader adoptive line (Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Pius, Marcus, and Commodus). Five Good Emperors is a later moralizing list (Nerva through Marcus) that overlaps Gibbon's ideal stretch but is not identical to 'Antonines' in every historian's mouth. Principate names the early imperial constitutional system rather than this dynasty. Silver Age can refer to Latin literature of a partly overlapping era, not the political brand. 'Antonine' as an adjective (Antonine Wall, Antonine Plague) is more technical and period-specific than Gibbon's capitalized AGE OF THE ANTONINES. Using 'Antonines' alone, especially in the plural-as-epoch, signals Gibbonian or classically educated prose rather than dry prosopography. Polysemy and modern usage:
  • Strict onomastic sense: emperors bearing the cognomen Antoninus — above all Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius (Marcus Aurelius Antoninus).
  • Extended dynastic sense: the adoptive dynasty from Nerva through Commodus, the 'Antonine house' and its succession by adoption rather than blood.
  • Period and moral sense: 'the age of the Antonines' as the Gibbonian golden age (98–180 CE), when the empire was, on Gibbon's famous claim, most happy and prosperous.
  • Adjective 'Antonine': technical, datable labels — the Antonine Wall in northern Britain (begun c. 142 under Antoninus Pius) and the Antonine Plague (the pandemic under Marcus Aurelius in the 160s).
  • Modern scholarly usage: 'Nerva-Antonine dynasty,' 'Antonine constitution,' 'Antonine era,' 'Antonine age' as a period label in Roman history.
  • Onomastic afterlife: the same gens name Antonius, via the Christian cult of St Anthony (d. 356), seeded the European name family Anthony, Antoine, Antonio, Anton — a separate branch descending from the family name, not from the dynastic label.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the word descends, by name only, from the Roman family name Antonius (of unknown, likely Etruscan or Italic-substrate origin). The derivative Antōnīnus ('of the Antonii') became the imperial cognomen of Antoninus Pius and his adopted heirs; the plural 'Antonines' then stretched from a set of named emperors to a whole era, converting a prosopographical label into a civilizational concept. Register and collocations: the word is historical-literary and high; it assumes knowledge of Roman imperial onomastics. Collocations: 'the age of the Antonines,' 'under the Antonines,' 'the Antonine emperors,' 'the Antonine Wall,' 'the Antonine Plague.'
