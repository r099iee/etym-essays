Introduction. Issued is the past tense of a verb that has spent its whole life saying "out." The word descends from the Latin verb for "go" fitted with the prefix for "out" — exīre, "to go out" — and every one of its modern senses, from a dragon leaving a gate to a bank issuing currency, is a variation on that single image of something coming forth. Its history is a long story of institutionalisation: a plain verb of physical emergence became, over five centuries of English, the standard official verb of publishing, distributing, and putting into circulation.

Phase 1 — the Indo-European root (before 500 BCE).

  • The reconstructed PIE root *h₁ey- meant "to go." It is among the best-attested roots in the family: Sanskrit éti "he goes," Greek eîmi "I go," Latin īre/eō, Lithuanian eĩti, Old Church Slavonic iti — the same root underlies English exit, itinerary, initial (what goes first), transit, and ambition (literally "a going around" for votes).
  • Latin īre "to go" was a suppletive, high-frequency verb, and its compounds were legion; the one that matters here is exīre — ex "out" + īre "to go" — "to go out, depart, escape." Roman usage already stretched it from the literal (leaving a city) to the final (exīre ē vītā, "to go out of life," to die) and the political (magistrates "going out" of office).

Phase 2 — Old French: issir and issue (9th–13th centuries).

  • In the development of Latin into French, exīre underwent regular sound changes to become Old French issir "to go out" — the cluster ex- + ī produced the doubled s of issir, and the verb remained common in medieval French for emerging and departing.
  • From issir, French took the feminine past participle issue — "gone out" — and made a noun of it: "the way out, the exit," and by extension "the outcome, the result" (what comes out of an affair) and "offspring" (what comes out of a family). This noun, not the verb, is what English borrowed first.

Phase 3 — Middle English: the noun issue (13th century).

  • English took the noun issue in the 13th century, chiefly through Anglo-French legal and administrative language: the "issue" of a case was its outcome; a man "without issue" had no children; the "issue" of a fortress was its exit or sally.
  • Chaucer uses the noun of outcomes and exits; the legal phrase "without issue" (without offspring) is still the standard phrase in wills and peerage records today. All the noun's later senses — result, offspring, exit, point in question — were in place by the 14th–15th centuries.

Phase 4 — the verb: from emergence to sending forth (16th–17th centuries).

  • The verb issue (16th c.) derived from the noun (or directly from French issu) with the meaning "to go out, come forth": troops issued from gates, rivers issued from mountains, words issued from mouths. This is the sense Tolkien uses.
  • Within a generation the transitive sense appeared: "to issue" a command or proclamation — to send it out from authority. By the 17th century kings and councils issued orders, books were issued (published), and the phrase "issue forth" was standard high prose.
  • The figurative "to result from" (issues from) attached to the noun's "outcome" sense: "what issues from a quarrel" is its consequence — a sense still alive in formal writing.

Phase 5 — the financial and military centuries (17th–19th).

  • Finance adopted the word for putting money into circulation: to issue banknotes, bonds, shares, stamps, coins — the emission of value "out" into the economy. The noun "an issue of currency" and the issuer (the bank or state) are 17th–18th-century institutions.
  • Military and colonial administration made issue the verb of supply: rations, uniforms, ammunition, and equipment are "issued" to troops — given out by authority to those entitled. The bureaucratic age generalised this to passports, warrants, licences, and refunds.
  • The noun simultaneously grew the sense "point in question" (what stands out between parties — "at issue," "the issue at hand"), and in the late 19th–20th centuries colloquial English flattened this into the modern "problem": "what's the issue?"

Phase 6 — the twentieth century and Tolkien.

  • By 1900 the dominant senses of issued were official: statements, visas, banknotes, rations, warnings. The literal emergence sense ("issued from") had retreated to literary and historical prose — alive, but marked as formal.
  • J.R.R. Tolkien, a philologist writing epic prose in 1937, used exactly that archaic-emergence sense for Smaug's death: "He issued from the Gate, the waters rose in fierce whistling steam." The old verb gives the dragon's last emergence its ceremonial weight — the exit of a king of beasts.

Phase 7 — today.

  • Issued is the everyday past tense of official sending-forth: a statement is issued, a passport is issued, shares are issued. The emergence sense survives in formal and literary prose, where it lends any coming-out a Roman dignity.
  • Five centuries after the verb was coined, and two and a half millennia after Latin speakers said exīre, the whole family still means exactly what it meant at the start: something — a man, a river, an order, a coin — going out.

Summary. From PIE *h₁ey- "to go," through Latin exīre "to go out," Old French issir and its participle issue, a 13th-century English noun of exits and outcomes, a 16th-century verb of emergence, and four centuries of official, financial, and military extension — issued now carries a dragon out of a gate and a passport out of a ministry with the same ancient word for going.
