Bacilli is the plural of bacillus, meaning rod-shaped bacteria, especially in medicine and microbiology. A bacillus is one slender cylindrical bacterial cell; bacilli are many. The term describes shape, not a single species: tuberculosis, tetanus, typhoid, anthrax, and many harmless soil organisms are all bacilli in the generic lowercase sense.

In strict taxonomy, Bacilli (capitalized) is a class of Firmicute bacteria and Bacillus (capitalized, italicized) is a genus of spore-forming rods including Bacillus anthracis (anthrax) and Bacillus subtilis (lab workhorse). In everyday and literary use, bacilli loosely means germs or microbes generally, even cocci and viruses, as in the prompt's insinuating bacilli that will devour man. Context tells whether the writer means precise rods or poetic pestilence.

  • Generic shape sense: any rod-like bacterium versus coccus (sphere) and spirillum / spirochete (spiral). Example: Gram-positive bacilli in the throat swab. Pure morphology under microscope.
  • Pathogen sense: disease-causing rods. Example: tubercle bacilli, typhoid bacilli, plague bacilli. Implies infection, contagion, and 19th-century dread. The prompt uses this apocalyptic charge.
  • Taxonomic sense: members of genus Bacillus or class Bacilli. Example: Bacillus thuringiensis as biopesticide; Bacilli include Listeria. Requires italics and capitals in formal writing.
  • Literary-metaphorical sense: invisible corruption, moral decay, creeping revenge of nature. Example: bacilli of discontent spreading through society. Exploits invisibility and insinuation.

Polysemy bridges stick to germ via shape metaphor: a bacillus looks like a tiny staff under the lens. Once named, the word absorbed all cultural meanings of germs - dirt, disease, contagion, biowarfare, antibiotic resistance. To call something bacilli is never neutral; even scientists hear danger, while lay readers hear plague.

  • Medicine: bacilli cultured, stained (Gram, acid-fast), tested for sensitivity. Doctors say acid-fast bacilli for TB, Koch's bacillus historically.
  • Public health: bacilli in water, milk, air. Implies need to boil, chlorinate, ventilate, vaccinate. Opposite: sterile, bacillus-free.
  • Lab work: bacilli grown on agar, counted as colony-forming units. Harmless model bacilli like E. coli (technically a bacillus-shaped enterobacterium) and B. subtilis are daily tools.
  • Warfare / fear: anthrax bacilli as bioweapon; insinuating bacilli as planetary revenge. Taps into germ panic from 1880s cholera to COVID era.

Grammar: irregular Latinate plural; singular bacillus, plural bacilli (pronounced -lai or -lee). Countable: one bacillus, many bacilli. Collocates with tubercle, typhoid, dysentery, anthrax, comma, acid-fast bacilli; kill, harbor, spread, culture bacilli. Register technical-formal; in conversation people say germs, bugs, bacteria. Using bacilli in ordinary speech sounds learned or deliberately archaic-literary, which is precisely the prompt's elevated misanthropic effect.

Understanding bacilli therefore requires seeing staff, shape, and scourge together: little rods visible only by microscope, named for walking sticks, feared as devourers of empires. The insinuating bacilli of the passage are at once biological fact and moral metaphor - nature's smallest rods becoming its mightiest sceptres.
