→ PIE *wer- (to perceive, watch out for, guard), reconstructed; the Italic and Greek reflexes confirm the core sense — Latin verērī (to fear, hold in awe, respect) hardened the "watch out for" sense into dread, while Greek horā́n (to see, perceive, from the o-grade *wor-) built "seeing" from the same root → Proto-Germanic *waraz (cautious, watchful, wary), late 2nd–1st millennium BCE → Old English wær (aware, wary, cautious), attested from the 8th century CE, and its intensified form gewær (same word with the perfective prefix ge-) → Middle English war / iwar / aware (watchful, conscious), with the negative unware / unwar (ignorant, off one's guard, formed with the negative prefix un-) — 12th–15th centuries CE → 16th century CE: unawares — the adverb formed from the negative adjective by adding the adverbial genitive -s (the same ending fixed in always, needs, towards), meaning "unexpectedly, without warning" → Modern English UNAWARES — the card's target form: the adverb meaning "in an unsuspecting manner," now fossilised chiefly in the idiom to catch / be taken unawares. NOTE on the debate: no direct Sanskrit reflex is securely attested for this particular *wer- sense, so none is given; the Germanic, Italic and Greek branches above are the firm cognates. The modern adjective unaware is a later back-formation from the adverb (18th century), not the direct ancestor of unawares.
