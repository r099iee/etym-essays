DYSPHEMISM is a 19th-century English coinage built from two Ancient Greek elements of separate and independent origin: the prefix dys- and the noun phēmē. The two strands are traced in isolation below, then joined at the compound. CHAIN 1 — the dys- element (the negative prefix): 1) PIE *dus- "bad, ill, hard, unlucky" (reconstructed; the negative prefix of the Hellenic and Indo-Iranian branches, essentially unknown elsewhere in the family) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Ancient Greek dus- "bad, ill, hard, unlucky, difficult" (prefix, attested from the 8th c. BCE in Homeric Greek; the productive negative prefix of Greek, as in duskolia "difficulty" and dusmeneia "hostility"). 3) English dys- "bad, abnormal, difficult" (learned prefix borrowed in Greek-derived medical and scientific vocabulary from the 17th c. onward, as in dyslexia, dysfunction, dyspepsia; the combining element used at the coinage of dysphemism). 4) English dysphemism — DYSPHEMISM (coined 1884; the full compound, "the deliberate substitution of a harsh or degrading term for a neutral one," formed on the model of euphemism). CHAIN 2 — the -phemism element (the noun phēmē): 1) PIE *bheh₂- "to shine, gleam, be bright" (reconstructed; in Greek the root developed the extended sense "to make manifest, to bring to light", and from that the sense "to speak, declare, utter" — speech conceived as making things known, as light makes them visible) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Hellenic *pʰā- "to shine; to make known, to say" (reconstructed; the Greek verbal base). 3) Ancient Greek phēmḗ "utterance, speech, report, reputation" (noun, attested from the 8th c. BCE; from phēmí "I say, declare"; also the base of phā́sis "statement" and, through the related phaínō "to bring to light", of phenomenon and epiphany). 4) Ancient Greek dysphēmía "ill-omened speech, profane speech, words of evil omen" (compound of dus- + phēmḗ, attested in Greek; the sacred opposite of euphēmía, the discipline of uttering nothing unlucky). 5) English euphemism (borrowed 16th c. from Greek euphēmismós, via Latin euphemismus) "the substitution of a mild or pleasant word for an offensive or painful one" — the model on which dysphemism was deliberately patterned. 6) English dysphemism — DYSPHEMISM (coined 1884 as the antonym of euphemism, from Greek dys- + phēmḗ + -ism, either directly on the Greek noun dysphēmía or by simple analogy with euphemism; both paths converge on the same compound). NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The reconstruction of PIE *dus- is secure for Hellenic and Indo-Iranian (Avestan duš-, Sanskrit duṣ-/dur-, with duḥkha "sorrow," literally "standing badly," from *dus-stha-), but the root has no generally accepted cognates in Italic, Germanic, or Balto-Slavic, and its own deeper pre-PIE origin is unknown; nothing here is asserted beyond that attested range. (2) The semantic link between PIE *bheh₂- "shine" and the Greek "speak" words is standard and widely accepted, but the precise bridging sense ("to bring to light" = "to make known") is a scholarly inference, not a directly attested meaning. (3) The 1884 coinage date of dysphemism is the standard first-attestation date given by the OED; the specific author of the coinage is not securely documented. (4) The word is correctly treated as a two-root compound: no single invented "master root" links *dus- and *bheh₂-.
