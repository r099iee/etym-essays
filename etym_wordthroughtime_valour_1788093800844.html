- c. 1300 – valour enters English meaning “worth, value, merit” as well as “courage” — the Old French inheritance in both senses.
- 14th–16th c. – under chivalric romance the courage sense tightens: a knight's worth is his valour in battle; the word becomes the epic virtue.
- 1597 – Shakespeare's Falstaff: “the better part of valour is discretion” — the proverb gives valour a moral nuance: courage tempered by judgement.
- 17th c. – value takes over the “worth” sense; valour specialises as elevated martial courage; the Latinising spelling valor appears and becomes standard in American English.
- 18th–20th c. – valour retreats into epic, ceremonial and eulogistic diction; everyday speech uses courage and bravery instead.
- Today – valour survives in honours (Cross of Valour, Medal of Valor), in the proverb, and in dignified literary prose — slightly archaic, deliberately grand.
