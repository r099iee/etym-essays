- Early 20th Century (c. 1908–1920s): Coined in German music criticism (*Atonalität*) as a polemical, hostile descriptor for the radical harmonic experiments of Arnold Schoenberg, Alban Berg, and Anton Webern (the Second Viennese School), who abandoned traditional major and minor key centers.
- 1930s to 1950s: Adopted into academic musicology and high modernist aesthetics, evolving from an insult into an official, prestigious theoretical category encompassing free atonality and twelve-tone serialism.
- Mid-20th Century (1960s–1980s): Expanded into cinematic horror and avant-garde film scoring (e.g., Bernard Herrmann, György Ligeti in Kubrick’s *2001: A Space Odyssey*), where atonality became the universal sonic signifier of cosmic dread, psychological fragmentation, and existential terror.
- Late 20th to 21st Century: Broadened metaphorically into literary criticism, architecture, and cultural theory to describe any art form, social state, or philosophical system characterized by the deliberate absence of a unifying center, traditional resolution, or comforting anchor.
