BAR-TAILED is a hyphenated ornithological compound joining BAR and TAILED, and its evolution must therefore be tracked as two independent histories converging in modern bird nomenclature. Neither element began as a colour term or a bird term; both were reshaped by medieval technology, early modern science, and nineteenth-century field ornithology into a precise diagnostic adjective.

BAR begins with an etymological puzzle. Vulgar Latin *barra, meaning bar or barrier, is securely attested through its Romance descendants but has no secure Latin or Indo-European ancestor. Old French barre in the twelfth century meant beam, bar, gate and barrier — a stout rod used to fasten doors, close passages, or divide space. Middle English borrowed it by the late twelfth century as barre, first for an iron rod fastening a door or gate. From this concrete fastening sense the word radiated outward for eight centuries. By the 1530s it meant anything that obstructs or impedes. By the 1580s it meant a sandbank across a harbour mouth that obstructs navigation. By 1833 it meant a rectangular cake of soap and by 1906 a stick of candy, in both cases from resemblance of shape. Parallel to this, bar developed a visual-pattern sense: a straight transverse stripe or band contrasting with its ground, as in heraldry, textiles, and natural history. It is this stripe sense that feeds bar-tailed. The disputed Gaulish hypothesis deserves explicit mention. Gamillscheg and others proposed Gaulish *barros, bushy end, as the source of *barra, but the OED rejects this as discredited because bushy end in no way suits the sense of straight rod or barrier, and because the Celtic forms in Welsh and Irish are demonstrably loans from English and Romance rather than sources. Responsible lexicography therefore lists Vulgar Latin *barra as the earliest recoverable stage and marks everything before it as unknown rather than inventing a Celtic or PIE root.

  • Beam and fastening: Old French barre as a physical beam and gate-bar, borrowed into Middle English for iron door-bars and prison bars.
  • Obstruction generalised: anything that blocks, from legal bars to sandbars, creating the abstract idea of a bar as a transverse interruption.
  • Stripe visualised: heraldic bars, barred plumage, barred rock and barred owl, where bar means a contrasting transverse band — the direct bridge to bar-tailed.

TAILED begins far earlier, in deep Germanic prehistory. Comparative evidence points to Proto-Germanic *tagla-, meaning hair, fibre, tuft or hairy tail, attested in Old High German zagal, German Zagel, Old Norse tagl meaning horse tail, and Gothic tagl meaning hair. Watkins derives this from PIE *dek- (2), meaning something long and thin, with reference to fringe, lock of hair and horsetail, citing Old Irish dual meaning lock of hair and Sanskrit dasah meaning fringe or wick. The OED is more cautious, stressing that the primary sense at least in Germanic seems to have been hairy tail or just tuft of hair, already extended in Old English to the hairless tails of worms and bees. Old English tægl or tægel thus already covered the posterior extremity generally, not only hairy appendages. Middle English tail consolidated this, and by the late fourteenth century the word extended to any hinder, bottom or concluding part in space or time — tail of a text, tail of a storm — and later to tail-coats, coin tails, and letter descenders. The suffixed adjective tailed is attested from around 1300 as having a tail or tail-like appendage, productive in compounds: long-tailed, short-tailed, fork-tailed, fan-tailed, pintail and cottontail. It can mean both furnished with a tail and, in compounds describing pattern, characterised by a tail of a specified kind.

  • Hairy tuft to animal extremity: Proto-Germanic hair-tuft becoming Old English general tail, including insects and worms.
  • Positional metaphor: tail as rear, end, conclusion, follower — to turn tail, to tail off, to tail a suspect.
  • Compounding productivity: tailed becoming a second element for length, shape and pattern, enabling nineteenth-century ornithologists to coin bar-tailed alongside black-tailed, white-tailed and red-tailed.

The compound BAR-TAILED itself belongs to Linnaean and post-Linnaean descriptive ornithology. As global exploration brought closely related waders into museums, naturalists needed stable English names distinguishing Limosa lapponica, the bar-tailed godwit with barred rather than solid tail patterning, from Limosa limosa, the black-tailed godwit. The hyphenated form follows standard English compounding for attributive adjectives before nouns and is retained predicatively in field guides. Its triumph came with twentieth-century field guides, where barred versus black tail, barred rump and barred under-tail coverts became key field marks. Socio-culturally the term migrated from museum label to conservation banner as satellite tracking revealed the bar-tailed godwit as the longest non-stop migrant on Earth, Alaska to New Zealand, making a once-technical plumage adjective globally familiar.
