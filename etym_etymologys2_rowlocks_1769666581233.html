ROOT 1 — "row" - Proto-Indo-European *h₁erh₁- / *er- "to row, propel with oars" (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE) - Proto-Germanic *rōaną "to row" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) - Old English rōwan "to row" (attested 9th c.) - Middle English rowen "to row" (12th–15th c.) - Modern English row "to propel a boat with oars" (16th c.–present) ROOT 2 — "lock" - Proto-Indo-European *leug- "to bend, turn, twist" (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE) - Proto-Germanic *luką "fastening, bolt, enclosure" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) - Old English loc "fastening, lock" (attested 8th c.) - Middle English lok "lock, fastening" (12th–15th c.) - Modern English lock "a fastening that shuts" (16th c.–present) COMPOUND STAGE - Middle English rowlok "oar-holding fitting on a boat's gunwale" (attested c. 1450) - Modern English rowlock (spelling from 16th c.; pronounced /ˈrɒlɪk/) → rowlocks (plural, the target root word) Debate / nuance note: The first element is reduced in speech (hence variant spellings rollock, rullock), and some scholars treat the compound as row + lock where "lock" keeps its old "fastening" sense. American English independently re-coined oarlock in the 19th c. The PIE shape of the rowing root (*h₁erh₁-) is reconstructed from Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, and Germanic evidence; the exact vowel grade is debated but the lineage to English row is secure.
