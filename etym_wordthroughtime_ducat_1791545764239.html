- Late 14th c. — Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman and Italian; perceived as the quintessential prestige gold coin of Mediterranean trade and Venetian wealth.
- 15th–16th c. — Firmly established across Europe as the gold benchmark of commerce; in English imagination, associated with Renaissance Italian opulence, banking houses, and the Levant trade.
- Late 16th – 17th c. — Immortalized in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (most notably Shakespeare's Shylock in The Merchant of Venice); perceived as the archetype of hard, unyielding metallic treasure and mercenary greed.
- 18th–19th c. — Regarded as a standard continental bullion coin (especially the Austrian and Dutch issues); increasingly archaic in daily British commerce, evoking historical romance and numismatic precision.
- Late 19th – mid-20th c. — Slang evolution in American English (theatrical, carnival, and sporting parlance); shifted colloquially to denote admission tickets, passes, or greenback cash ("pass over the ducats").
- Late 20th–21st c. — Largely retired from everyday speech; celebrated in numismatics, economic history, and Shakespearean drama, with lingering echoes in American vintage hip-hop and underworld slang for money.
