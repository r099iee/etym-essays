Proto-Indo-European Roots of Law and Speech:
The deep etymological roots of injudicious unite two fundamental Proto-Indo-European concepts: sacred law and authoritative speech. The first element derives from PIE *yew- (or *yoh₁-), conveying binding religious oath, ritual law, or right (the source of Latin iūs, "law, right", which produced jury, justice, and jurisdiction). The second element stems from PIE *deyk-, meaning "to point out, show, pronounce, or proclaim" (the source of Greek deiknynai, "to show", and Latin dīcere, "to say").

Latin Morphological Fusion: The Judge and Judicial Discernment:
In archaic and classical Rome, these two elements fused into the foundational compound noun iūdex (genitive iūdicis), literally signifying "one who pronounces the law"—a judge or magistrate. From iūdex, Latin generated the noun iūdicium ("judgment, judicial trial, mental discernment, critical capacity"). In Late Latin, the adjective iūdiciōsus was formed to describe a person or decision endowed with exceptional discernment, wisdom, and soundness of mind. This passed into Middle French as judicieux, which English borrowed in the late 16th century as judicious.

The Formation of the Negative Antonym in 17th-Century English:
During the early 17th century (circa 1630), English scholars and legal theorists prefixed the Latin negative particle in- ("not, un-", from PIE *ne / *n̥-) to judicious to create the formal antonym injudicious. Its earliest attestations occurred in philosophical treatises, ecclesiastical debates, and parliamentary speeches. It served as a precise, dignified rhetorical instrument to critique the unsound arguments, rash policies, or poor governance of adversaries without stooping to crude personal insults like foolish or mad.

Enlightenment Jurisprudence and Victorian Social Nuance:
Throughout the 18th century, Enlightenment thinkers such as John Locke, Edmund Burke, and Edward Gibbon routinely employed injudicious to analyze historical crises, such as the fall of empires or royal mismanagement, where rulers failed to weigh the long-term repercussions of their decrees. In the 19th century, the word flourished across Victorian prose. Novelists like George Eliot, Jane Austen, and Anthony Trollope utilized injudicious to dissect the delicate psychological mechanics of social blunders, ill-advised marriages, and indiscreet dinner-party gossip.

Comedic Irony and Contemporary Precision:
In the comedic literature of the 20th century—especially the works of P.G. Wodehouse—injudicious became an indispensable weapon of mock-heroic irony. Bertie Wooster frequently reflects upon his own harebrained impulses, concluding with immense scholarly dignity that executing them would be singularly injudicious. Today, injudicious remains an essential formal adjective across jurisprudence, corporate governance, investigative journalism, and literary writing, precisely evaluating actions that lack foresight and discernment.
