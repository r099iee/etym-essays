- (Old English, hebban “to lift up, raise; exalt”) - A strong verb for concrete raising: shields, swords, corpses onto pyres; also “to exalt, praise” in religious verse — tone neutral to solemn, weighty.
- (Middle English, heven, c. 1200) - Broadens to “to throw, toss”, “to raise the voice or a cry”, “to raise from the dead” in devotional texts; the verb loses most of its strong conjugation and becomes weak (heaved) — tone energetic, physical.
- (14th–15th c.) - The nautical expansion: “heue ho” (c. 1300) becomes the rhythmic cry of sailors hauling together; “heave” attaches to anchors, yards, and cables — the archetypal sailor’s word for strenuous collective pulling.
- (16th–17th c.) - “To heave a sigh” grammaticalises the verb onto breath and emotion; “the earth heaves” and “the sea heaves” name slow, massy motion; “to heave in sight” = a ship rising over the horizon — tone broadens from labour to natural rhythm.
- (late 18th c.) - Colloquial “to heave” = to retch, vomit: the internal spasm read as an upward lifting of the gut — informal, bodily register.
- (19th c.) - Geology borrows the noun: “heave” = horizontal displacement of rock along a fault; “upheaval” enters politics and society — technical jargon.
- (20th c.) - “To give someone the heave-ho” (mid-20th c.) = to dismiss or reject: the old sailor’s cry becomes comic idiom of ejection; weightlifters heave barbells; “frost heave” enters civil engineering.
- (today) - Chests heave with sobs or exertion, seas heave in a swell, stomachs heave in nausea, and “heave-ho” survives as playful dismissal — the word still carries its muscular, effortful force in every sense.
