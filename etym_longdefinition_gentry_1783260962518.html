In Macaulay's sentence “alienated a loyal __________ and priesthood from the House of Stuart,” the noun gentry denotes the landed class of England below the peerage — the county families, the squires, knights, and gentlemen of good estate who dominated local government as justices of the peace, sheriffs, and members of parliament. The historical referent is exact: James II's errors — his Declaration of Indulgence, his attacks on the Test Act and the Church of England, his remodelling of the borough charters and the judiciary, and his Catholicizing of the Oxford colleges — turned the most loyal and conservative class in the kingdom against him within a few months. Macaulay pairs gentry with priesthood to name the two great pillars of the old regime, the landowning and the clerical estates, whose defection made the Stuart cause untenable. The word is corporate and social-historical: it names a class, not individuals, and carries with it the associations of landed wealth, local influence, traditional loyalty, and social respectability that defined the English squirearchy.

Sense 1 — The untitled landed class below the nobility (the core historical sense, used on this card). In the precise English social vocabulary, the gentry are the class of gentlemen of good estate: baronets, knights, esquires, and plain gentlemen who held land and enjoyed the social status of gentle birth but carried no hereditary title that seated them in the House of Lords. From the sixteenth through the eighteenth centuries this class governed England at the local level — as justices of the peace, sheriffs, lords lieutenant, and members of the House of Commons — so that the gentry, not the peerage, were the daily rulers of the counties. This is the sense of “The local gentry gathered at the manor to discuss the new land enclosure acts.”

Sense 2 — People of good social position generally. More loosely, the word is used of anyone of comfortable, respectable, “gentlemanly” status — the better sort of a town, the families of standing in a district — without the strict landed criterion. The short definition on this card (“people of good social position, specifically the class of landowners just below the nobility”) captures both the loose and the strict senses at once; a rising merchant or professional who married into the gentry acquired, by association, its social prestige.

Sense 3 — The gentry as a political estate (the sense Macaulay uses). In seventeenth- and eighteenth-century constitutional prose, “the gentry” stands beside “the clergy” (or “the priesthood”) and “the nobility” as one of the estates of the realm; the loyalty of the gentry decided the fate of regimes, and their alienation from the Crown in 1687–88 was one of the decisive causes of the Glorious Revolution. The word in this register is corporate and political, naming a class whose interests were bound up with Parliament, property, and Protestantism — a national constituency, not a set of individuals.

Sense 4 — Modern and derived uses.

  • Gentrify / gentrification (20th century): a new verb-family built directly on the noun, meaning to renovate or “improve” a working-class or run-down urban neighbourhood so that middle-class professionals move in, often displacing poorer residents. The semantic bridge is the old ideal of the gentry as the respectable, improving class — but the term is now usually used critically, as the respectable class colonizes the neighbourhood.
  • “The gentry” as a sociologist's term: in historical sociology of early modern England, “the gentry” is a technical label for the whole stratum of landowners below the peerage, subdivided into the greater gentry and the lesser gentry by the size of their estates.
  • Pejorative or jocular uses: in Britain, “the landed gentry” or simply “the gentry” can carry a shade of deference or of mockery toward an upper class; in American usage the word often means simply the comfortable upper-middle class (e.g. “the coastal gentry”).

Semantic architecture. The whole range grows out of a single chain of ideas: Latin gens, “clan, family,” gave gentilis, “of the family, of good family, noble,” which became Old French gentil, “noble, well-born, courteous” — the source of English gentle and gentleman — and the Old French abstract genterie, “gentle birth, nobility,” gave English gentry. The word is thus, at root, about birth and lineage: the gentry are the people of gentle (that is, gentle-born, well-born) stock. The later extensions — gentrification, the sociological sense — all presuppose that core meaning of a well-born, respectable, landowning class. In the vocabulary of social rank the discriminations are crucial: the nobility, peerage, and aristocracy denote the titled class who sat in the House of Lords; gentry is specifically the class below them, the untitled gentlemen who stood for the Commons and governed the counties; squirearchy is a more particular and slightly mocking word for the rule of the squires; and bourgeoisie names the urban, commercial middle class, the gentry's social opposite in the classic schema.
