superseded (v. past tense / past participle; also adj.) — replaced, displaced, or set aside by something newer, superior, or more authoritative. On this card the word appears in the sentence "In our island the Latin appears never to have superseded the old Gaelic speech, and could not stand its ground against the German": here the verb means that Latin, despite its imperial and ecclesiastical prestige, never managed to displace the native Gaelic tongue or to hold its own against the incoming Germanic speech. The tone is sober and historiographical — an orderly judgement about linguistic priority, not a violent conquest.

Sense 1 — The core meaning: to take the place of something so that the older thing is set aside or rendered obsolete. A new law supersedes an old law; a new edition supersedes the previous one; a new technology supersedes the outdated machine. The emphasis is on the newcomer occupying the higher or later position and thereby voiding the authority of what came before.

Sense 2 — Legal and statutory use. In law a "superseding cause" is an intervening act that breaks the chain of causation and replaces the original cause of harm; a "superseded statute" is a law that has been legally nullified or replaced by a later enactment. The noun supersedeas (from the Latin verb's subjunctive, "you shall desist") names the writ that stays legal proceedings — the oldest English legal echo of the word, dating to the fifteenth century.

Sense 3 — Technology, manufacturing, and standards. "Superseded parts" are components officially retired and replaced by updated part numbers; a "superseded standard" is a specification no longer current, replaced by a newer revision. Here the word acquires a bureaucratic, catalogue flavour: the old item is not just beaten but formally withdrawn from service and substituted by its successor.

Sense 4 — Metaphorical and general use. A theory, fashion, practice, or institution is "superseded" when it is overtaken by newer thought or conditions; in computing, a superseded version of software is one replaced by a later release. The common thread in every sense is the image inherited from Latin: the new thing sits above the old and takes its seat, so that the old thing is left below, no longer operative.

  • Register and tone: formal, calm, almost judicial; unlike ousted or supplanted it carries no violence or treachery — things are superseded by progress, not by force.
  • Collocations: supersede a law, superseded by a new constitution, superseded part/standard/version, superseding cause, a superseded theory, supersedeas writ.
  • Distinction from near-synonyms: replace is neutral and everyday; supplant implies scheming or force; supersede alone implies that the newcomer is superior or more authoritative and thereby voids the old.
  • Historical bridge: Latin supersedēre meant literally "to sit above." One who sat in the higher seat either took precedence over the matter below or, from that height, chose to refrain from acting on it. Both senses — "take precedence" and "forbear, desist" — flowed into English, and by the 17th–18th centuries the "occupy the superior position and set the older thing aside" sense prevailed, giving the modern word its force.
