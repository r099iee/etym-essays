Metz has two names, and the older one is not the source of the modern word. The Gaulish town was Divodurum, “divine fortress”, from divo- “divine, sacred” and duro- “fort, walled place”, a compound type familiar from Durocortorum (Reims) and Augustodurum. The inhabitants were the Mediomatrici, a people of the middle Moselle whose name was to outlive both the town and the tribe.

The tribal name

  • Elements. Medio- “middle” continues Indo-European *medʰ-yo-, the same root as Latin medius and English middle; matri- “mother” continues *méh₂tēr, the root of Latin māter, Greek mētēr and English mother.
  • Sense. The compound is usually rendered “those of the middle mother” or “the mid-mothers' people”. The reference may be to the Matres or Matronae, the mother-goddesses widely worshipped in the Rhineland and eastern Gaul, or to a mother-land conceived as lying between two rivers; the sources do not decide.
  • Suffix. The ending -ici is the Latinised form of a Gaulish tribal suffix, not a separate root; it marks a people, like the -es of so many ethnic names.
  • Caution. Some scholars prefer a topographical reading (“those of the middle plain”) and treat matri- as a river or place element; the mother-goddess interpretation, though attractive, is not proved.

From Divodurum to Mettis

The Roman city flourished under the name Divodurum, but from the late empire the sources increasingly use Mettis, a form that looks like a drastic reduction of the tribal name. The change is not documented step by step. The most economical explanation is that the unwieldy Mediomatrici was shortened in everyday speech, with syncope of the medial syllables and assimilation of the dental cluster, producing something like *Mettis; the city then took the name of its people, as often happened in Gaul. Ammianus Marcellinus and the Notitia Dignitatum use Mettis, and the form is secure by the fourth century. French preserves the final -s, as it does in Reims, Arras and Chartres, while dropping it in ordinary nouns; the tz spelling represents the affricate that later simplified to /s/ in French but remains /ts/ in English pronunciation.

Medieval and early modern city

Metz became a Merovingian and Austrasian centre, then a free imperial city governed by a patriciate and a prince-bishop. Its Roman walls, its amphitheatre and its legends — above all the Graoully, the dragon supposedly slain by Saint Clement — gave it a distinctive civic identity. The Reformation found many adherents, and the city's position on the road between the Rhine and Paris made it a permanent strategic prize. Henry II's occupation of 1552 and the successful defence against Charles V in the winter of 1552–53 attached Metz to France; the Peace of Westphalia recognised the fact in 1648, and Vauban's fortifications made it a cornerstone of the eastern frontier.

Modern history and the name

The nineteenth century turned Metz into a symbol. In the Franco-Prussian War the Army of the Rhine under Bazaine shut itself in the fortress and capitulated on 27 October 1870 with some 180,000 men, a surrender that helped decide the war and was later bitterly blamed on the commander. The city became German until 1918, French again until 1940, German again under the Nazi annexation, and was liberated after hard fighting in late 1944. Each change of sovereignty left its mark on the street names, the garrison architecture and the local memory.

Contemporary reference and caution

Today Metz is known for its cathedral, its stained glass, the Centre Pompidou-Metz and its position at the heart of the Grande Région between France, Luxembourg and Germany. In English the name is a straightforward borrowing, pronounced with /ts/ and never translated; in French it is /mɛs/. The etymology of the tribe is secure in its elements but uncertain in its sense, and the passage from Mediomatrici to Mettis is a plausible reconstruction rather than a recorded sequence — both points are flagged rather than smoothed over.
