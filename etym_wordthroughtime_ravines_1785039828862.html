- 17th century - English borrows 'ravine' from French, where it meant a rushing torrent or violent flood (from Latin 'rapina,' a seizure; root 'rapere,' to snatch); the earliest English sense is water in violent motion, a scouring flood.
- 18th century - the sense shifts from the water to the wound it makes: a ravine becomes the deep, narrow gorge cut by a torrent, the steep-sided gap itself, and the word settles into topography.
- 19th century - geological writing fixes the ravine as a distinct landform — steep-walled, water-carved, wide enough to bar crossing; it enters Romantic landscape vocabulary as an image of sublime, forbidding terrain.
- Contemporary - 'ravine' is standard for a deep, sheer-sided gorge, darker and more final than a gully and less grand than a canyon; its older watery sense survives only in the word's memory, and the name still hints at torrential violence.
