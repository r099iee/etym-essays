quays is the plural of quay (pronounced "key", /kiː/), a noun meaning a stone or wooden landing stage built along, or projecting into, the edge of a body of water — a harbour, river, or canal — for the loading and unloading of ships, boats, and barges.

Primary meaning: engineered waterfront landing stages.

  • A quay is a solid, permanent structure: a masonry wall, a pile-built platform, or a paved apron against which vessels berth.
  • Quays are distinguished from natural banks and beaches by being constructed: they offer deep enough water alongside and a level surface for handling cargo.
  • The word commonly denotes the working face of a port — the strip where ships tie up, cranes work, and goods move between vessel and shore.
  • Quays may run along the shore (linear) or, less commonly, project into the water; the plural quays naturally refers to several such landing places in a port or along a waterfront.

Related but distinct structures.

  • Wharf / wharves: very close in meaning and often interchangeable; historically a wharf could also project out from the shore, while a quay typically runs along the bank.
  • Pier: usually a structure projecting into the water (often on piles), commonly used for mooring, ferry landings, or pleasure promenading.
  • Dock: technically the enclosed water area (basin) between quays, or the whole dock facility; ships lie "in dock," though usage overlaps with "quay."
  • Jetty: a projecting structure, often small, used for ferries or as a breakwater.
  • Landing stage / landing: a simpler, often temporary wooden platform or steps for passengers and light goods.
  • Berth: the specific place where a vessel ties up, of which a quay is one kind.
  • Mooring: the point where a vessel is secured, not necessarily a built structure at all.

Secondary and transferred senses.

  • Quayside: the land area adjoining a quay, with warehouses, cranes, and customs sheds — a frequent formation in British English.
  • Canal quays: the word applies to inland canal and river loading banks, not only seaports.
  • Urban-redevelopment sense: modern waterfront "quays" are often converted into leisure districts — promenades, restaurants, galleries, marinas — so the word has drifted from industrial working waterfronts toward the picturesque.
  • Figurative: "the quays" can stand metonymically for the port community, its trade, and its crowds, as in Tolkien's Lake-town scene.

Etymological bridge (why "quay" is spelled oddly).

  • The word entered English in the late 14th century as keye/key "sandbank, wharf," from Old North French cai; for about two centuries it was written key, surviving in old London street-names and the surname Kay.
  • From the 17th century the French-style spelling quay was adopted while the pronunciation stayed "key," producing one of English's famous spelling–pronunciation mismatches.
  • The plural quays simply adds regular -s to that respelled singular, still pronounced /kiːz/ (homophonous with "keys").

Grammar and usage notes.

  • Countable noun: a quay / two quays; plural quays.
  • Common collocations: "along the quays," "on the quay," "quayside," "wooden quays," "stone quays."
  • Pronunciation trap: the qu + ay spelling looks like "way" but rhymes with "key."
  • Register: neutral-to-formal; maritime and literary flavour; in British English increasingly seen as slightly old-fashioned or evocative beside "dock" and "wharf."

The word in The Hobbit.

  • Tolkien's "long wooden quays ... with many steps and ladders going down to the surface of the lake" describes exactly the pile-built landing platforms of a medieval lake-town.
  • The "tall piles" on which Lake-town's houses stand visually echo the staked riverbank "enclosures" of the word's Celtic origin — a happy coincidence of etymology and imagery.
