1. A weapon-word of the Germanic north. The family of dart is Germanic, and its deepest reconstructible member is Proto-Germanic *darōþaz, "a light javelin or dart" — a soldier's and hunter's word. Its reflexes are attested across the old Germanic world: Old English daroþ, "javelin, dart", in the poetry and glossaries; Old Norse darraðr, a poetic word for "spear" (the famous Darraðarljóð, the "Song of the Spear", is the battle-song woven by the Norns around the battle of Clontarf in 1014); and Old High German forms in tart-. No secure Indo-European etymon has ever been agreed for this family: suggested connections to roots of "splitting" and "tearing" remain speculative, and the dictionaries record the ultimate origin as uncertain — possibly an ancient substrate or early loanword in Germanic. For the practical history of the word, the reconstructed Germanic base is the earliest honest starting point.

2. Through Frankish into French. The West Germanic form travelled southwest with the Franks: Frankish *daroth, "dart, javelin", entered Old French as dart, attested from the eleventh and twelfth centuries in the age of the chansons de geste, where knights and sergeants cast darts and javelins among the first flights of missile weapons. In English, two streams met: the native Old English daroþ had been in the island since the Anglo-Saxon settlements — the word appears in Old English verse as a rank-and-file missile — and the Anglo-Norman dart arrived with the Conquest and the court. Middle English dart, recorded from about 1300, is thus the product of a double inheritance, the native and the French forms coalescing into one word.

3. The verb: from throwing to moving. The verb is Middle English too, appearing by the fourteenth century in the transitive sense "to throw or shoot a dart". The decisive shift of the word's life occurs in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: to dart internalises the missile — the dart's sudden, straight, deadly flight becomes the pattern for any swift movement, and the verb goes intransitive: animals dart among the reeds, fish dart in clear water, eyes dart about a room, rays of light dart through clouds, and abstract thoughts dart through the mind. Shakespeare's age sets the pattern; the King James Bible has Moses "darting" nothing, but the Elizabethan stage and prose are full of darting looks and darted glances, and the participle darted enters the stock vocabulary of vivid narration. From the 17th century the transitive figurative construction "to dart a glance" (to shoot one out suddenly) is fully established and remains the most elegant surviving use of the transitive verb.

4. The word's three other lives. (1) Dressmaking: in the nineteenth century the needle trades took the pointed missile's name for the pointed, V-shaped tuck that shapes cloth at the waist and bust — the tailoring dart — and "darted" gained its quiet domestic sense (a darted bodice). (2) Zoology: malacologists use "love dart" for the sharp calcareous spike that land snails fire into their mates during courtship — a genuinely projectile structure, and one of the most quoted curiosities of invertebrate behaviour; the verb "darting" describes its discharge. (3) Sport: round about the turn of the twentieth century the pub game of darts codified the old missile sense into rules, boards, flights and steel points; the game grew from inn parlours to televised professional championships and Olympic ambitions, and the noun now belongs to sport as much as history. Figurative literary use — "darts of pain", "darts of grief", "darts of light" — has kept the missile sharp in poetry.

5. The inflected form darted. The past tense and past participle is regular and transparent: dart + -ed, pronounced /ˈdɑːtɪd/ in Received Pronunciation. It is the form Wilde needs for swift, single narrative action — the Swallow darts through an opening, and in one verb the reader sees the flight: sudden, straight, and gone. From a prehistoric Germanic spear-name of uncertain descent, through the Frankish frontier and the Norman court, to tailoring, snail-sex and the dartboard, the word has kept its one bright image: a thing that moves in a straight, sudden line.

  • Eras: Proto-Germanic *darōþaz (reconstructed) → Frankish *daroth → Old French dart → Middle English (c. 1300) → the intransitive sense-shift (16th–17th c.) → tailoring dart (19th c.) → the pub game (c. 1900) → modern literary and sporting use.
  • Semantic drift: missile → act of throwing the missile → any sudden straight motion → glance and light and thought → tuck, snail-spike and sport.
  • Key attestations: Old English verse (daroþ); Old Norse Darraðarljóð; Middle English (c. 1300); Elizabethan prose (darted glances); Wilde, The Happy Prince (1888).
