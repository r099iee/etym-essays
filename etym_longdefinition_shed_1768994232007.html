shed — In the American dialectal idiom "get shed of," the word "shed" means to cast off, part with, or get rid of. The Reverend's hearer has asked how he can take the son of God into a hellhole; the answer is that he "caint get shed of him" — he cannot divest himself of the divine presence, cannot shake it off. The verb treats God as a burden, a clinging thing to be discarded like a worn skin, and the homely grammar of the idiom — "get shed of," not "get rid of" — belongs to frontier speech, blunt and final. The tone is admonitory and practical: the divine is not a proposition to be believed but a presence to be carried, and carrying is not optional. The word's physical root — separating, dividing, casting off — is exactly the sense in play: to get shed of something is to split your path from it, decisively and forever.

Polysemy of "shed."

  • Core verbal sense: to cast off naturally (c. 1200). To let something part from the body or surface in a natural, effortless way: "shed tears," "shed blood," "shed skin," "shed leaves," "shed hair," "shed its load."
  • To discard or get rid of (modern). To divest oneself of something burdensome — "shed employees," "shed weight," "shed inhibitions" — an extension of the natural casting-off to deliberate disposal.
  • To emit or radiate. "Shed light on" (to illuminate, literally and figuratively), "shed a little warmth," "shed a glow" — a transfer from casting off to giving out.
  • The participial idiom "get shed of." Chiefly American dialectal, "to be or become rid of" — a fossil of the older participial construction, exactly as in the passage.
  • Noun: a shelter (a different word). "Shed," meaning a small lean-to or storage hut, is a separate word, probably related to "shade" — a caution for etymology, not the verb's family.

Boundary comparisons. To discard is to throw away as useless, an act of disposal that judges the thing; to cast off is to release or let go, especially of lines, moorings, or garments, with a nautical flavor; to divest is formal and legal, to strip of rights, goods, or office; to slough is the biological word for casting off dead tissue or a snake's outgrown skin, the closest parallel to "shed" in its naturalness; to moult refers to the loss of feathers, hair, or horns in renewal; to jettison is to throw cargo overboard, deliberate and sudden; to abandon is to give up control or connection, often with moral weight; to rid (as in "get rid of") is the standard modern idiom, slightly more analytic than "shed." "Shed" alone implies an easy, natural, almost effortless separation — a snake sheds its skin, a tree sheds its leaves, a man sheds tears — and in the idiom it makes the parting with God as natural and as impossible as the body's parting with its own integument.

Collocations and register. The verb is richly collocational: "shed tears," "shed blood," "shed light on," "shed its leaves," "shed skin," "shed hair," and, in the idiom of the American South and West, "get shed of." Register is everyday and physical, and the verb is often transitive with the object the thing cast off. The idiomatic phrase "watershed" (a ridge dividing waters, 19th century) preserves the older "divide, part" sense. In the passage the verb form is the archaic-sounding past-participial adjective, a fossil of the older participial use that survives precisely in the folk idiom "get shed of."
