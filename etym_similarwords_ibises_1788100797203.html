• Herons: Similar because herons are long-legged wading birds of marshes and riverbanks like ibises, with long bills and necks; differs because herons (Ardeidae) have straight dagger-like bills and stalk fish with spearing strikes, while ibises have down-curved bills and probe the mud — and herons carry none of the ibis’s sacred Egyptian pedigree.
  • Egrets: Similar because egrets are white heron-type waders that stand in long rows along waters exactly like Wilde’s ibises; differs because an egret is a heron (straight bill, S-curved neck in flight) and its name refers to white fishing herons, not to the curved-bill family.
  • Storks: Similar because storks are large long-legged, long-necked wading birds that nest colonially in towers and rows; differs because storks are heavier, have straight stout bills, fly with neck outstretched and in Europe are the roof-nesting “baby-bringers” of folklore, while ibises are slender curved-bill probers of mud.
  • Spoonbills: Similar because spoonbills are the ibises’ closest relatives within Threskiornithidae, sharing habitat and colonial nesting; differs because the spoonbill feeds with a flat spatulate bill swept sideways through water, whereas the ibis probes with a curved point — the fork and spoon of the wading-bird world.
  • Cranes: Similar because cranes are tall elegant waders that gather in long rows and fly in formations; differs because cranes belong to the Gruidae — loud bugling calls, dancing displays, straight bills, epic migrations — while ibises are quiet probers with curved bills and no dance.
  • Flamingos: Similar because flamingos are long-legged pink water-birds that feed in vast orderly rows like ibises; differs because flamingos are filter-feeders of a separate order (Phoenicopteriformes) whose bills work upside-down, and their pink is dietary through krill and algae, whereas the ibis’s scarlet is also dietary but its bill probes rather than filters.
  • Bitterns: Similar because bitterns are secretive marsh waders of the heron family sharing the reedbed world; differs because bitterns are solitary, cryptic, booming, short-necked ambushers, not communal curved-bill waders.
  • Curlews: Similar because curlews are shoreline birds with long down-curved bills that probe mud exactly like ibises; differs because curlews are smaller sandpiper-kin (Scolopacidae) of coasts and moors, without ibises’ colonial nesting or ancient sacred role.
