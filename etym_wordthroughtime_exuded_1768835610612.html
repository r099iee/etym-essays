- A strictly physical verb (1570s) - borrowed from Latin exsūdāre, 'to sweat out'; for three centuries exude is about matter - sap, gum, resin, moisture oozing from stems, pores, and rocks - with no figurative life
- The medical offshoot (17th-19th c.) - the derivatives exudation and exudate enter medicine for fluid that seeps through vessel walls into tissue or a wound, keeping the word's scientific register alive
- The figurative turn (19th-20th c.) - the verb is released onto the invisible: to exude confidence, charm, authority, menace; the metaphor keeps the old physiology - what is exuded is not said but perspired, passed involuntarily through the surface
- Contemporary - the figurative sense dominates ('she exuded confidence'), while the physical sense survives in botany and medicine; the word sits formal but not stiff, more organic than emit, less violent than discharge
