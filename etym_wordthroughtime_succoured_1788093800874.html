- 13th c. - socouren (from Old French socorre) is plain, ordinary English for 'to help' — knights succour castles, kings succour the poor in chronicle and romance; perception: practical, unmarked.
- 16th–17th c. - the re-Latinised spelling succour appears, and the Authorized Version gives the word a sacred glow (2 Corinthians 6:2: 'have I succoured thee'); perception: military and divine at once.
- 18th–19th c. - succour becomes the great verb of organised mercy — succouring the poor, the sick, the distressed in charity discourse and the Victorian novel; perception: philanthropic, earnest.
- 20th–21st c. - succoured retreats to literary and ceremonial English; American spelling sheds the -u (succored); Tolkien's use in The Hobbit makes it sound chivalric and antique — perception: elevated, archaic, noble.
