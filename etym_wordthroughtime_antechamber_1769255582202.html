- 17th-century court protocol - the word enters English from French court usage as the architectural term for the room before a monarch's or noble's great chamber, where courtiers wait to be admitted; it is inseparable from ceremony and hierarchy.
- 18th-19th century institutional spread - it moves from palaces into government offices, grand houses, and public buildings as the formal waiting and reception zone; it keeps a dignified, protocol-laden tone while hosting hurried business alongside ceremony.
- 19th-20th century drift toward the dated - as domestic and civic architecture simplifies, the word yields everyday ground to "hallway," "lobby," and "waiting room"; "antechamber" begins to sound stately and literary, at home in historical novels and palace descriptions.
- Contemporary figurative use - modern usage keeps it chiefly for palatial or ceremonial settings, and extends it metaphorically - "an antechamber to power" - as the preliminary, waiting stage before something significant begins.
