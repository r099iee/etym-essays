- Late 15th to Early 16th c. - Entered English during the Renaissance alongside classical translations of Greek political treatises, functioning primarily as a neutral descriptive adjective for governance centered in a single sovereign (*monarchia*).
- 17th c. (English Civil War & Glorious Revolution) - Became a fiercely contested political keyword; royalists exalted the divine right of monarchical order as divinely ordained, while parliamentarians and republicans used it with suspicion to critique royal prerogative and tyranny.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment & Montesquieu) - Systematized in political theory (e.g., Montesquieu, Hume, Adam Smith) to contrast with republican and despotic forms, distinguishing between "free" or constitutional monarchical regimes and arbitrary absolutism.
- 19th c. (Victorian Constitutionalism) - Evolved alongside the rise of constitutionalism and democracy; the term shifted from denoting active personal rulers to characterizing ceremonial, limited institutions serving as stabilizing, non-partisan national symbols.
- 20th to 21st c. - Primarily used in historical and comparative political science to analyze dynastic regimes, and figuratively in sociology and corporate governance to describe centralized, hierarchical command structures.
