Chalk is in its first sense a soft white limestone: a porous sedimentary rock of nearly pure calcium carbonate, formed from the microscopic shells of Cretaceous plankton and laid down in warm shallow seas some seventy to a hundred million years ago.

In the source sentence the word is pure geology underfoot: the travellers stand upon a bed of chalk, and the proof of the rock is in the farming above it — the downs always afford good sheep food. Chalk soils drain fast and stay sweet and lime-rich, growing the short springy turf that has fattened sheep on southern English hills since before history, so the single word chalk quietly explains the whole pastoral economy of the scene.

Core meaning in detail: English chalk forms the great white ribs of the southern landscape — the North and South Downs, Salisbury Plain, the Chilterns — and breaks at the coast into the white cliffs of Dover, Beachy Head, and the Needles. Soft enough to cut with a spade and to crumble between the fingers, it has been quarried for building stone, burned in kilns for lime and mortar, spread crushed on acid fields to sweeten them, and sawn into sticks for writing. Blackboard chalk in the modern classroom is often technically gypsum rather than true chalk, but the name, the whiteness, and the dusty squeak preserve the older stone in daily use.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The white limestone rock (primary sense, from Old English): the Cretaceous stone of cliffs, downs, and pits; the ground beneath the source sentence's sheep pasture. The bridge to every other sense is this stone's whiteness, softness, and willingness to mark.
  • A stick for writing or marking (from the 16th c.): classroom chalk, tailor's chalk, carpenter's chalk, gymnasts' chalk for the grip. The bridge is manufacture: the soft rock sawn or the powder pressed into a convenient crayon, keeping the colour and the crumble of the cliff.
  • A mark or line made with chalk (from the noun): the chalk line of the carpenter snapped blue on timber, the hopscotch grid on the playground. The bridge is use: the tool lends its name to its trace.
  • The verb to chalk — to write, record, or credit (from the 17th c.): to chalk up a score on the slate, to chalk up a victory, to chalk it up to experience. The bridge is the tavern slate: debts and drinks were literally chalked on boards, so every modern credit — runs, points, favours — is still notched in imaginary chalk.
  • Idiomatic chalk — difference and distance: chalk and cheese for two things utterly unlike (white crumbly chalk against rich yellow cheese); not by a long chalk for falling far short, from the long chalk lines of sailing reckoning or the tailor's board; the chalk outline of crime scenes for the stark white silhouette of the fallen. Each bridge runs through whiteness made conspicuous — chalk shows up, so it marks extremes.

Register and connotation: chalk is a warm, concrete, countryside word — cliffs, classrooms, and sheep bells rather than laboratories, though chemists know it as calcium carbonate all the same. It carries nostalgia in handfuls: the squeak on the blackboard, the hopscotch square, the white horse cut into a hillside. Even its idioms are affectionate, chalk and cheese among the cosiest of all contrasts. The source sentence catches it at its most fundamental — not the stick, not the score, but the stone itself, feeding sheep through the grass it sweetens.
