The history of coppices is the history of a blow: every stage of the word descends from the moment steel meets wood, and from the Latin and Greek words for a struck blow that slowly narrowed into the French verb for cutting.

The deepest visible link is Greek kolaphos, a cuff or box on the ear — a <> word, probably imitative in feel, borrowed into late Latin as colaphus with the same meaning. In the spoken Latin of Gaul the form was shortened to *colpus, a blow or stroke, and Old French inherited it as colp, later spelled coup — the ancestor of modern French coup and of English coup, a master-stroke. From the noun came the verb couper, to cut: literally to deal a cutting blow, as the billhook deals it to the hazel stool.

The past participle of that verb, copeiz — that which has been cut — became a forestry noun: a copeiz was a panel of wood cut over for regrowth, with its own legal fencing-off time while the shoots recovered. Norman administrators and wood-reeves carried the term across the Channel after 1066, and Middle English records spell it copys or copies: compartments of underwood, coppice panels, each with its cutting year, its fence-month, and its appointed woodward.

Sound and spelling shifts:

  • Greek to Latin borrowing: kolaphos enters late Latin as colaphus with little change beyond orthography, keeping its meaning of a struck blow.
  • Syncope in Vulgar Latin: the unstressed middle syllable drops, giving *colpus, so that a three-syllable cuff becomes a one-syllable stroke — the seed of French coup.
  • Old French palatalisation and spelling: colp softens toward coup, and the verb couper with its participle copeiz shows the characteristic -eiz ending that Anglo-Norman scribes rendered -is, -ys, hence copys.
  • Middle English to modern doubling: copies acquires the doubled p and the -ice spelling under the influence of words like notice and practice, settling as coppice by the early modern period.

Socio-cultural context: the coppice was the medieval fuel station, ironworks, and building merchant in one. Charcoal for smelting, faggots for ovens, poles for houses and hedges — all came from the stool, and manorial courts fined trespassers who broke the coppice fence before the spring growth was safe. When coal and imported timber undercut the trade in the nineteenth century, thousands of acres fell silent, and the word drifted from account-books into poetry. Modern conservation has reversed the valuation without changing the vocabulary: the dormouse surveys of today walk the same panels the woodwards walked, billhook in hand, six centuries on, the stool outliving every fashion that declared it obsolete, and each spring the cut panels greening first of all the wood.
