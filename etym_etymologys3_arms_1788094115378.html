Few English words display the double descent of arms. The form we write today is the meeting point of two separate histories — a Germanic word for the limb and a Latin word for weapons — which nonetheless turn out, at the deepest level, to be cousins. The story is a textbook demonstration of how homonymy can conceal kinship.

The root. Both lines begin with the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂er-, “to fit, to join, to put together”. From this root Indo-European built a remarkable family: Greek ararískō “to fit together”, harmós “joint”, harmonía “agreement, harmony”; Latin ars (gen. artis) “skill, art” — the ability to join things into a crafted whole — armus “shoulder”, and arma “equipment, weapons”; Vedic Sanskrit īrmá- “foreleg, arm”; Old Church Slavonic ramo “shoulder”. The mental image is of a limb that is fitted to the body — the arm as the great joiner, the member by which we grasp and hold what we have joined ourselves to.

The Germanic line: the limb. From *h₂er- with the suffix *-mo- came *h₂ermos, “arm, shoulder”. In Proto-Germanic this became *armaz, and in Old English earm — the form shows the Old English “breaking” of *a to ea before r plus a consonant. Old English earm meant “arm (of the body)” and already shows the extended sense “arm of the sea”, an inlet; the word is frequent in Old English prose and poetry. Middle English levels the diphthong to arm, and the plural arms is the everyday word for the pair of limbs. The extended “projecting part” sense grows naturally from this: anything that reaches out from a body — a chair, a crane, a mountain, a law — can be its arm. The Germanic cognates are wide: German Arm, Dutch arm, Old Norse armr, Gothic arms.

The Latin line: the weapons. Meanwhile, from the same root, Latin formed arma, a neuter plural meaning “tools, equipment, implements of war”. The semantic logic is that weapons are the “fitted equipment” of the warrior — the things he is fitted out with for the fight. Latin literature gives the word its most famous attestation in the very first line of Virgil's Aeneid: “Arma virumque cano” — “I sing of arms and the man”. The derived verb armāre “to equip with weapons”, the adjective inermis “unarmed” (literally “without equipment”), and armātūra, source of English “armature”, all belong to the same family.

The convergence in English. Latin arma passed into Old French as armes, and into English after the Norman Conquest (recorded from around 1300) as armes, “weapons, armour”. English spelling later dropped the final -e, producing the identical form “arms”. The heraldic sense follows in the 14th century: the knight's identifying device was painted on his shield and armour, so his “arms” became his bearings — hence “coat of arms” and “the College of Arms”. The verb “to arm” (from Latin armāre via French armer) was already present in Middle English, and “armament”, “army” (from French armée, “armed force”), “armistice” (from Latin arma + sistere “to stand still”), “armour” and “armadillo” (the “little armoured one”) all radiate from the same root.

Modern developments. The 17th century sees “small arms” for portable weapons; the 19th century generalizes “arm” in machine terminology (the arm of a lever, of a balance, of a crane); the 20th century coins “arms race” — a phrase that became a defining term of the Cold War — together with “arms control” and “nuclear arms”. In the same period the limb-sense continues its metaphorical career: “the long arm of the law”, “the arm of the organisation”, and, in the topographic register of the source text, “the arms of the Mountain” — a phrase that personifies the landscape as a body embracing the valley. The two strands, Germanic limb and Latin weapon, remain distinct in origin but indistinguishable in form, and English speakers use both without ever noticing the seam.
