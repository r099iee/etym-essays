1. Proto-Indo-European *mer- "to rub away, crush, harm, wear down" (reconstructed root) — the ultimate base of Latin mordere "to bite"; some scholars reconstruct an extended root *merd- "to bite" for the Latin branch (the debate is noted below). 2. Latin mordēre "to bite" (attested from the 3rd century BCE) — a root verb; its past participle is morsus, morsa, morsum "bitten." 3. Latin morsus, -ūs (noun, fourth declension) "a biting, the act of biting; a bite; a bite-wound" (attested from the 1st century BCE, e.g. in Lucretius) — nominalized from the past participle. 4. Vulgar Latin *morsellum "a little bite, a small piece of food" — a diminutive of morsus formed with the suffix -ellum (reconstructed, not directly attested, but the regular ancestor of the Romance forms: Italian morsello, Old French morsel, Spanish morsello). 5. Old French morsel "a bite, a small portion of food" (attested 11th–12th centuries; continued in Modern French morceau). 6. Middle English morsel / morselle "a mouthful, a small piece of food" (attested c. 1300). 7. Modern English MORSEL — present day, with the figurative extension to any small valued quantity (a morsel of truth). Debated / nuanced note: (a) the PIE root for Latin mordere is usually given as *mer- "to rub, crush" (de Vaan), with a possible *merd- "to bite" extension; the distinction is scholarly and does not affect the attested Latin chain. (b) The Vulgar Latin intermediate *morsellum is reconstructed rather than directly attested, but its existence is guaranteed by the uniform Romance outcome (morsello, morsel, morceau). Everything from Latin morsus onward is securely attested.
