The hyphen in landing-stage joins a Germanic shore to a Latin standing-place, and its story is the story of nineteenth-century rivers learning to receive steam.

Landing: from soil to step. Behind landing lies Proto-Germanic *landą, open ground as opposed to sea, which gives Old English land in all its senses: soil, country, and, crucially, the shore seen from the water. To land was originally to reach that shore — inherently a sailor’s verb looking landward — and by Middle English landen meant to come ashore or bring ashore. The gerund landing then did what English gerunds endlessly do: it slid from action to place. First it meant the act of disembarking, then the spot where one disembarks, then, with Victorian precision, the structure built for the purpose. Harbour language is full of such slides — anchorage, mooring, berth — but landing made the longest voyage, from the solid earth itself to a few planks over the tide.

Stage: the Latin that learned to float. The second half descends from PIE *steh₂-, to stand, one of the busiest roots in Europe, yielding Greek histemi, Latin stare, and English stand itself. Late Latin built on it *staticum, a standing-place, which Old French received as estage: a dwelling, a storey of a building, a floor to stand on. Borrowed into Middle English as stage in the fourteenth century, it first meant a raised floor for readings and plays, then any raised platform — scaffolds, pulpits, public platforms for orators. The theatrical boards and the builder’s boards are one word because they are one thing: planks that lift feet above the ground for a purpose.

  • Steam creates the compound (early 19th c.): river steamers and ferry companies need timetabled, insured, advertised places of call. Newspaper notices and company Acts begin to specify the landing-stage at each town, distinguishing the maintained structure from any muddy landing-place.
  • Timber zenith (Victorian): creosoted pine and oak piles multiply along every navigable reach — excursion stages, coal stages, ferry stages — each broad enough for crowds and crates, each smelling of tar in summer.
  • Floating ingenuity (late 19th c.): on tidal reaches engineers moor old hulks and pontoons as stages that ride the water, linked to the bank by brows that hinge with the tide — the same Latin standing-place taught to swim.
  • Decline and affection (20th c.): buses and cars starve the river services; stages rot, burn, or are replaced by concrete. Survivors become club pontoons, camera points, and novelists’ props — the broad wooden stage of memory.

Sound and hyphen: the compound keeps both stresses half-alive — LANDing-STAGE — with the hyphen recording its working origin in notices and timetables rather than in poetry. Say it and you hear function: the purposeful landing settling onto the upright stage.

Why the parts fit: landing supplies the motion arrested — the boat’s voyage ending — and stage supplies the boards that arrest it. Together they name civilisation’s simplest harbour: not a dock, not a port, but enough dry standing to step from water to land without wetting a shoe.
