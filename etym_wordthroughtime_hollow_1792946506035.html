- Old English — a hole or den: holh names hollow places, holes, and lairs, earthy and animal, a badger earth or river cutting.
- Middle English — the cupped landform: holowe becomes noun and adjective for dips, dells, and empty vessels, the farmer word for ground that holds water and shadow.
- 14th–15th c. — sound joins shape: hollow vessels boom when struck, so the word learns to mean echoing and reverberant, the ear hearing the shape.
- 16th–17th c. — morals hollow out: preachers and poets speak of hollow hearts, hollow promises, and hollow men, the landscape emptiness turned into insincerity.
- 19th c. — rural romance: novelists and poets cherish the wooded hollow as refuge and idyll, the secret dip where lovers meet and outlaws hide.
- Today — land, body, and boast: walkers still follow the lip of a hollow, doctors still chart hollow cheeks, and headlines still mock hollow victories, one cupped word for terrain, flesh, and talk.
