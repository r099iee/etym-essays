1. A name and its shadow. The word Hellenism rests on the Greeks’ own name for themselves — Ἕλληνες, Hellēnes — a name whose origin is lost. The Greeks themselves told that Hellen was the son of Deucalion and Pyrrha, the couple who survived the flood, and that from his sons Aeolus, Dorus and Xuthus descended the three great dialect-branches of the nation (Aeolic, Doric, and the Ionians through Xuthus’s son Ion); the genealogy is a national aetiology in the shape of a family tree. Yet the name resists etymology: no convincing Indo-European derivation exists, scholars suspect a pre-Greek (Aegean or “Pelasgic”) substrate, and the ancient tradition that connected the Hellenes with the Σελλοί (Selloi), the dream-interpreting priests of Zeus at Dodona, has been revived and disputed in modern form. What is certain is that Homer’s world does not yet use the name generally — his Achaeans, Danaans and Argives are the Greeks of the poems — and that by the fifth century BCE “Hellenes” is the standing self-designation of all who speak Greek, set off against barbaroi, the speakers of unintelligible tongues.

2. From idiom to culture. The abstract noun ἑλληνισμός, hellēnismós, appears in the age after Alexander with a grammatical and cultural meaning: using the Greek language correctly, thinking and writing as a Greek. The grammarians oppose hellēnismós to barbarismós as correctness to corruption — an opposition that reaches English in the very late Greek test of correct speech. In the same period the word begins to stretch beyond language: to “hellenize” (hellēnízein) is to adopt Greek ways, and the whole eastern Mediterranean after Alexander — Egypt under the Ptolemies, Syria under the Seleucids, western Asia under the Attalids — becomes a zone of continuous Hellenization, in which Greek became the language of government, commerce, science and literature, and native cultures translated themselves into Greek idiom, as Judaism did in the Septuagint. It is this doubled sense — the classical culture and its post-classical spread — that later historians divide with the adjective “Hellenistic”, a word Droysen coined in 1836 to name the fusion-era world and rescue it from the condescension of earlier scholarship.

3. Religion and resistance. In the third and fourth centuries CE the word took a religious colouring: Julian the Apostate and the pagans of his circle called their polytheism hellēnismós, the “Greek religion”, and Christian writers took up the word to name the pagan enemy. In this phase Hellenism is no longer a matter of birth or language but of commitment — one could be a Greek-speaking Christian and still reject hellēnismós, or a Syrian worshipper of Greek gods and embrace it. The word had become an -ism in the modern sense: an allegiance with content, boundaries and polemic.

4. The Renaissance recovery. The humanists of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries did not normally use the English word “Hellenism” (they spoke of studia humanitatis, of Graeca), but they performed its meaning: the flight of Greek scholars to Italy after 1453, the printing of the Greek New Testament and of Plato, the lectures of Chrysoloras in Florence and of the New College circle in Oxford — all of it was the love of “the things of the intellect and the imagination for their own sake”, the recovery of the Greek spirit as a way of life. Pater’s sentence on the humanists of the earlier century is the pivot on which his Winckelmann essay turns: Winckelmann is a Renaissance humanist born late, a man of the eighteenth century who “belongs in spirit to an earlier age” — because he, too, made the pursuit of the Greek spirit his life-long struggle.

5. Arnold, sweetness and light. The word’s modern critical career is dominated by Matthew Arnold. In Culture and Anarchy (1869) Arnold set Hellenism against Hebraism as two poles of the human spirit: Hellenism is “spontaneity of consciousness”, the desire that things be seen as they are — the intellectual virtues of clear sight, measure, proportion, “sweetness and light”; Hebraism is “strictness of conscience”, the moral virtues of duty, obedience, earnestness. The pairing was enormously influential: it gave Victorian culture a vocabulary for the quarrel between knowledge and conduct, and it coloured everything after — Pater’s aesthetic Hellenism, the “Greek spirit” of the late-Victorian Hellenists (the Shelley Society, the Cambridge Apostles), and even the periodic revivals of “the Greek way” as a slogan for the intellectual life. Modern scholarship has corrected both the ideal and its history — the Greeks were not uniformly serene, and “Hellenism” names as much projection as fact — but the word retains its double life: a period and a spirit, a civilization and a longing for it.
