Derivation chain for brooches (base: brooch) — root core of non-PIE, uncertain origin; a single linear chain from the first attested Latin form to the present English plural: 1. Latin broccus "with projecting, jutting teeth" (attested in Pliny the Elder, 1st c. CE; ultimate origin unknown — a Gaulish / Celtic-substrate source being the most plausible guess; no secure Proto-Indo-European ancestry is attested). 2. Vulgar Latin *brocca "a pointed thing, a spike, a spit" (feminine substantive of broccus; reconstructed, Romance period). 3. Old French broche "a spit for roasting, a pointed rod, a needle; an ornamental pin worn on the breast" (attested from the 12th c.). 4. Anglo-Norman / Middle English broche "a spit; a pointed pin; an ornamental pin" (attested c. 1300). 5. English brooch "an ornamental fastener pinned to clothing" (re-spelling of the 16th–17th c., introduced to distinguish the jewellery from the roasting spit "broach"). 6. English brooches (plural; the note's exact inflected form). Note on debate and nuance: Latin broccus has no accepted Indo-European etymology; OED, Etymonline, and de Vaan treat its origin as uncertain, with a Gaulish or otherwise Celtic-substrate source the most plausible guess. No PIE root should be forced onto it. The related English "broach" (a spit; the verb "to broach") is the same word, un-re-spelled, and French "brochure" (a stitched booklet) is a distant cousin via French brocher "to stitch".
