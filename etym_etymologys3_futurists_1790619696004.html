Futurists looks transparent — future + -ist + -s — but each morpheme carries two millennia of philosophy of time, and the capital F marks a violent 1909 rupture when a common adjective became a party name.

The root is PIE *bʰuH-, to be, become, grow, source of English be, been, bond, boom and Latin esse. Latin forms its future participle as futurus, -a, -um, literally about to be, from old stem fu- seen in fui (I have been) and fore (to be about to be). Futurum substantivized means what will be. Through Old French futur and Middle English future (14th century, Chaucer), the word enters English as adjective and noun for time to come, with theological overtones of prophecy and eschatology.

  • Pre-history of -ist: Greek -istes via Latin -ista and French -iste forms agent nouns of doctrine: artist, socialist, realist. By the 1830s French political language already has futuriste for one who concerns himself with the future, recorded in Saint-Simonian circles, but without program.
  • 1909 coinage: Marinetti, Italian poet writing in French, deliberately chooses Le Futurisme for Le Figaro manifesto to outbid Cubism, Expressionism and Symbolism with sheer temporality. Italian Futurismo and Futuristi are calqued instantly; English newspapers by March 1909 report Futurists. Manifestos multiply: Painting (1910), Music (1910 Russolo), Literature (1912), Architecture (1914 Sant'Elia).
  • Phonetics: Latin u /uː/ fronts via French to English /juː/ in future /ˈfjuːtʃə/, with palatalization t + yod to /tʃ/. Plural adds /s/ devoiced after /t/ to /s/: /ˈfjuːtʃərɪsts/. Italian retains /futuˈristi/.
  • Semantic narrowing then broadening: from generic futurological to sectarian (1909-1920s member), to stigmatized (post-1945 fascist taint), to art-historical neutral (1960s revivals, MoMA exhibitions), to genericized lowercase (1980s business futurist). Capitalization now disambiguates.
  • Typographic legacy: Marinetti's parole in libertà demands destruction of syntax, abolition of adjectives and punctuation, mathematical signs, onomatopoeia, varied typefaces on one page. This is the sense activated in the source sentence: Futurists as readers of alphabet as 26 sculptural-phonetic objects.

Russian parallel history enriches the term: Kruchenykh and Khlebnikov declare independence from Marinetti in 1913-14, accusing Italians of mere industrial imitation while Russians invent zaum. English history adds Vorticism's 1914 BLAST both imitating and cursing Futurism. Thus Futurists in Anglophone media theory almost always means Italians first, with Russians as critical counterpoint.

Attestations: futur in Roman de la Rose; English future c. 1380 in Chaucer; futurist 1842 in Carlylean sense of millenarian; Futurist capitalized Feb 1909 Le Figaro, March 1909 Daily Mail, 1910 Boccioni manifesto, 1912 Slap in the Face, 1914 Zang Tumb Tumb. Each attestation moves the word from grammar of time to politics of the page.
