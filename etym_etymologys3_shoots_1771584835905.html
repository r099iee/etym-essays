A root about hurling The biography of shoots runs from a root about hurling a missile to a word about the newest green growth of a plant, and every stage of that journey is a small lesson in how language keeps its oldest images alive. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *skeud-, “to throw, shoot, hurl, impel”, a root whose whole genius was sudden, sharp outward propulsion. In the Indo-Iranian branch it became the Sanskrit verb chodati, “he impels, urges, drives forward”, and the same root appears in Avestan in formations meaning “to push”. In the Balto-Slavic branch it produced *kydati, “to throw, cast” — Old Church Slavonic kydati, modern Russian kidát', “to throw, fling”, Czech kydat — so that one branch kept the root forever in the sphere of casting and shovelling, while the Germanic branch steered it towards missiles and, remarkably, towards vegetation. The core image, across every branch, is the same: something darting outward with force — a hurled spear, a goaded ox, a growing stem. The Germanic strong verb In Germanic the root became the strong verb *skeutaną, “to shoot, project, push out”, which in Old English appeared as scēotan, “to shoot, dart, rush; to push, thrust”. Old English scēotan was a verb of immense range: it shot arrows (the word of the bowman and the huntsman), it described water rushing or spurting out, it described things projecting suddenly — and, already in the oldest English poetry, it described the darting motion of warriors and of light. The derived noun scēot meant “a shot, a missile”, the thing shot. It is important to notice that the English strong verb shoot (shoot – shot – shot) is a direct continuation of this Old English verb, with the characteristic sound change by which the Old English cluster sc before a front vowel had already become [ʃ], the sound of modern English sh. From missile to meadow The decisive semantic step came when the verb's sense “to grow rapidly” (attested in Middle English from c. 1300) met the noun-making habit of the language: a stem that grows with sudden speed is a stem that has been “shot out” of the bud. By c. 1500 the noun shoot was in use for “a young branch”, and the botanical sense was firmly established: a shoot is etymologically a small piece of the root's ancient “hurling” lodged inside a tender green stem. The plant world and the weapon world have lived side by side in this one word ever since: we still say that a plant shoots up, that a star shoots across the sky, that a pain shoots up the arm, that a boat shoots the rapids, and that a film crew shoots a scene — five centuries of metaphorical travel from the bowstring. Sound and form
  • Old English scēotan [ʃeːo.tɑn] → Middle English shoten [ʃoːtən] → modern shoot [ʃuːt]: the OE diphthong ēo monophthongised and raised during Middle English, and the long vowel was later raised again in the Great Vowel Shift, giving the modern long [uː].
  • The strong-verb pattern (shoot – shot – shot) descends from the OE class II ablaut; the -t forms preserve the old -t of the past participle.
  • The verb shut is a sister formation from the same root via OE scyttan, “to bolt, to fasten” — a door is shut when the bolt is “shot” into place; shout, sheet, scud, and the noun shot are further members of the same family.
Literary and social life The word's darting image has been a favourite of English poetry and journalism. Shakespeare exploits it for sudden motion, and the traditional image of the bud giving birth to the shoot kept the botanical sense alive and tender through the centuries. In the twentieth century the idiom “green shoots” was borrowed from agriculture into economics: journalists writing after the financial crash of 2008 spoke of “green shoots of recovery”, meaning the first fragile, positive signs — a perfect modern instance of the old image of new growth darting out of barren ground. And the photographic sense — a photo shoot, to shoot a film — has made the word a noun of the modern image industry, so that the same syllable that once described the loosing of an arrow now describes the releasing of a camera shutter. Socio-cultural context The history of shoot is tied to the history of English archery. In medieval England the longbow made the shooter — Old English sceotend, the “shotter” — a figure of national legend, from the yeoman at Agincourt to the outlaw Robin Hood, whose fame rests on shooting. That martial prestige is why “to shoot straight” became, in time, an idiom for honesty itself. As firearms replaced bows, the verb transferred its allegiance, and then, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, it transferred to the camera, the football field, the basketball court, and the video game — each new technology taking over the oldest word of projectile motion. The card's tender willow shoots, in turn, show the same word at its gentlest: the missile's energy, spent and softened, turning into spring growth. The plural in the novel Finally, the form in the card is the plural shoots, used by David Hawkes to render the willow imagery of the Chinese couplet on Dai-yu's grace. The plural is unremarkable grammatically — simply the plural of the botanical noun — but it matters: shoots are plural because they grow in masses, because the beauty of spring is a crowd of small tender things, and because the image of “tender willow shoots caressed by the wind” needs the many, not the one, to convey yielding softness. Every sense of the word, from the arrow to the willow, is still powered by the same root's ancient image of things darting outward into the world.
