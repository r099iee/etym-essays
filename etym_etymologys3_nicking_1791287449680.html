The history of nicking is the history of the base verb and noun nick, a small word with an obscure birth and a surprisingly large afterlife in British English. Unlike many core verbs that can be traced cleanly to Latin or to a well-attested Proto-Indo-European root, nick appears suddenly in late Middle English and early modern records as a term for a small, precise mark, and lexicographers from Skeat to the OED have had to admit uncertainty about where it came from. That uncertainty itself is instructive: it shows how much of everyday vocabulary grows out of workshop talk, gamblers cant, and thieves slang rather than literary borrowing.

The earliest secure ancestor is the noun nick in the sense a small notch or groove cut into wood, tally-sticks, or bone, attested in the late fifteenth century. Tally-sticks were central to medieval English accounting and law: debts and payments were recorded by cutting notches of different sizes into hazel sticks, then splitting the stick. To nick in this world was to record, to count, to make legally binding. From this concrete craft sense came the verb to nick, first recorded in the 1580s meaning to make a notch in something. Shakespeare and his contemporaries already use nick in extended senses: the precise moment (in the nick of time, from the notch that marked the exact point), to cheat or to win narrowly at dice or cards by hitting the mark, and to wound slightly.

Three semantic branches then diverge and all survive in the present participle nicking:
  • Nicking as cutting or chipping: to make a small cut, to chip a blade, to nick ones chin shaving. This is the most literal survival of the tally-stick sense and remains standard in craftsmanship, surgery, and engineering.
  • Nicking as catching, arresting, and stealing: British slang from the mid-nineteenth century, to nick something means to steal it, and to get nicked means to get caught or arrested. This likely blends the idea of snatching quickly with the gamblers sense of winning by a narrow nick, reinforced by thieves cant where short, sharp monosyllables were prized.
  • Nicking as flicking or whisking away: as in Wodehouses nicking a speck of dust from lace, meaning to flick lightly with fingertip or nail. This bridges the cutting sense and the snatching sense: a tiny, deft, almost instantaneous movement of the hand.
Phonetically the form is stable. There is no great vowel shift drama here: nik / nick has kept its short /ɪ/ and final /k/ for five centuries, with only the regular addition of the velar nasal in the inflected form nicking /ˈnɪkɪŋ/. The spelling with ck is the normal Early Modern English device for marking a short vowel before k, replacing earlier nik, nyke, and nocke variants. The -ing ending is the ordinary Old English -ung / -ing participial suffix, unbroken from Old English through Middle English to today.

Socio-culturally, nick belongs to two opposite registers at once, which is why Wodehouse can use it for comic upper-class effect. On one side it is respectable and technical: nicks in arrows, nicks in blades, nicks in time. On the other it is low and slangy: nicking a wallet, nicking a car, getting nicked by the police. By the late Victorian and Edwardian period both layers were fully active, so a writer like P. G. Wodehouse could exploit the word for bathos — Bertie Wooster nicking dust from Mechlin lace sounds at once foppishly delicate and faintly roguish, as if grooming were a tiny theft. Modern British corpora still show this split: nicked as stolen or arrested dominates informal speech, while nicked as slightly cut dominates manuals and medical notes.

Literary attestations track this double life. Early dice manuals use nick as a winning throw. Seventeenth-century plays use in the nick for exactly on time. Dickens uses nicked for caught and for stolen. Police procedural slang of the twentieth century makes to be nicked a standard euphemism for arrest. Sport keeps nicked as narrowly won. Wodehouses usage — nicking a speck — belongs to a quieter, domestic line where nick means a light, dismissive flick, close to flicking or brushing, and it shows how a word born in wood-shops and gambling dens could end up on the cuff of a gentleman's dress shirt.
