Slanting is the present-participial adjective (and gerund form) of the verb to slant, describing something that lies, moves, or falls at an angle other than the vertical or horizontal — leaning obliquely rather than standing upright or lying level. When a mast is slanting, it is tilted out of true perpendicular; when rain is slanting, it drives diagonally across the wind rather than falling straight down. The word implies a deviation from a straight, upright, or level baseline, and it is almost always perceived relative to an expected norm of straightness.

The word is highly concrete and spatial in its core sense, and in literary prose it is frequently applied to light, terrain, structures, and movement. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the Company's path runs slanting away from the River Running, a usage that captures the sense of a course that gradually veers off at an angle from a reference line. The adjective frequently modifies nouns such as roofs, rays, shadows, lines, letters, and eyes, and it describes both static inclination (a leaning tree) and dynamic oblique motion (a bird slanting down toward the water).

Primary senses

  • Physical inclination (static): lying at an angle to the vertical or horizontal; leaning or sloping without being exactly upright or level. E.g. a slanting roof, a slanting hill, a slanting posture. This is the most central sense, preserved since the sixteenth century.
  • Oblique direction of motion (dynamic): moving, falling, or striking at a diagonal rather than straight; e.g. slanting rain, a slanting blow, a slanting flight path. Here the word emphasises the angle of a trajectory.
  • Diagonal line or writing: applied to handwriting, letters, or strokes that slope forward or backward; a slanting hand was a common description in nineteenth-century handwriting manuals.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Figurative angle of view (from the noun slant): a particular point of view, interpretation, or bias — a slant on the story. The participial adjective shades into this in phrases such as a slanting perspective, where the sense of deviation from the straight and true is transferred from space to judgement.
  • Bias and distortion (chiefly US journalism): in the verb sense, to slant a report means to colour or distort information, presenting facts from a prejudiced angle rather than straight-on; the participial form shares this loaded tone in phrases like slanting coverage.
  • Derogatory racial usage: note carefully that the noun slant and related terms have been used offensively of East Asian people, from the perceived shape of the eyes; this usage is a slur, is not descriptive English, and must never be reproduced.

Grammar and register

  • As an adjective, slanting is gradable (a slightly slanting floor, a sharply slanting roof) and can be used predicatively (the post was slanting) or attributively (a slanting beam of light).
  • It belongs to the participial adjectives that retain a strong verbal feel; unlike slanted, which in modern usage often connotes bias, slanting remains overwhelmingly physical and spatial.
  • Register is neutral to literary; it is comfortable in narrative prose, technical description of geometry, and everyday speech alike, though formal mathematical writing prefers inclined or oblique.

Collocations and fixed phrases

  • slanting light / slanting rays — light arriving at a low angle, common in landscape and photography writing.
  • slanting rain — rain driven sideways by wind.
  • slanting roof / slanting ceiling — architecture for a pitched or raked surface.
  • slanting handwriting — cursive sloping to the right (or left).
  • a slanting glance / slanting eyes — a look or gaze directed at an angle; in older texts this could also describe a sidelong or suspicious look.

Semantic relationships

  • It is closely allied with sloping, leaning, inclined, tilted, and oblique, all of which denote deviation from the straight, but slanting stresses a clean diagonal line and is the most purely descriptive of a physical angle.
  • Its antonyms are upright, vertical, level, and perpendicular; something that is slanting has lost, or never possessed, perfect alignment with gravity or the horizontal.
  • The word belongs to a family of Germanic-inherited slope words (with slope, slip, slide) that all encode a sideways or downward gliding motion.
