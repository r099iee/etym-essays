corporal — Corporal is a word with two parents and one spelling. The rank-name descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *kaput- "head". In Latin this became caput, and in the Romance dialects the head-word produced a whole family of leaders: Old French chief gave English chief and chef; capitālis gave capital; and in Italy caput, reduced to capo, became the ordinary word for "head, chief, leader". From capo the Italians formed caporale, "one who leads a squad" — literally "pertaining to a head", the head-man of a small body of men. French borrowed the word as caporal in the sixteenth century, and English took it over as corporal in the late sixteenth century, about 1570–1600. At this stage the word meant exactly what it does today: the lowest non-commissioned officer, the head of a few soldiers. The second parent is the root *kwrep- "body", which in Latin produced corpus, corporis "body" and its adjective corporālis "of the body". English already had the adjective corporal — "of the body" — since the fourteenth century, best known today in corporal punishment and the corporal works of mercy. Because the rank word named the head of a body of men, and because it sounded and looked like the existing adjective, English remodelled the spelling of the Italian caporal / French caporal toward corpus: the general shape came to be written as if the word were "of the body". The result is a perfect etymological accident — a rank that truly means "head" but is spelled as if it meant "body". Both trees must therefore be kept separate, and etymologists do: the head-chain runs through Latin caput, the body-chain through Latin corpus, and the two converge only in the modern English spelling. The same corpus family supplies corps, corpse, corporate, corporation, and corporeal, all genuinely "body" words, while the rank noun alone is a "head" word wearing a "body" spelling. Once established, the rank settled into a fixed place in the military hierarchy: below the sergeant, above the private, commanding a squad or small body of men. English developed lance corporal (an acting or temporary rank, literally a corporal who carries a lance — the oldest corporals were armed foot-soldiers, and the title preserves the sense of the lowest grade of leader). The rank spread with the professional armies of the early modern period and became institutionalised in the national armies of the nineteenth century, where it took on its flat, administrative, bureaucratic flavour — a rank, not a character. In C. S. Lewis's "Prince Caspian", the chilling effect of "Corporal" as a title of address depends on exactly this institutional flatness: the soldiers are interchangeable carriers of rank, and the rank itself has replaced the person. The ecclesiastical noun — a corporal, the linen cloth that holds the Body of Christ — descends from the body-root through corpus Christi, and its existence shows how wide the corpus family spread: the same Latin root that gave the army its rank also gave the church its altar-cloth. The word's three lives — military rank, bodily adjective, and liturgical cloth — are a small museum of English lexical history, where a single spelling gathers three distinct etymologies.
  • Sound change and form: *kaput- → Latin caput → Romance capo shows the weakening of the final -t; Italian caporale and French caporal show the derivational suffix -ale/-al "pertaining to".
  • Morphology: the rank is capo + -ale; the adjective is corpus + -alis; the two suffixes happen to have produced identical-looking English forms.
  • Chronology: the adjective corporal is English's first (14th c.); the rank word arrives in the late 16th c.; the spelling merger is complete by the 17th c.
  • Literary attestation: the rank word appears throughout Shakespeare's and the Civil War period's military writing, and in modern narrative its vocative use — "Corporal!" — carries the institutional chill Lewis exploits.
  • Socio-cultural context: the professionalisation of armies made the corporal a fixed grade; in fiction, the lowliness of the rank makes its casual cruelty more human and more terrible — ordinary men, not lords, discussing the disposal of a prisoner as routine.
