aftermath (noun) — in its original and literal sense, a second growth of grass (or clover) in a meadow after the first crop has been mown, often grazed or cut late in the season; in modern general use, far more commonly, the consequences, results, or after-effects of an event, especially an unpleasant or destructive one. Walter Pater's sentence uses the word at the hinge of the two senses: the Renaissance "putting forth in France an aftermath, a wonderful later growth" — an image drawn straight from the meadow, of a second flowering after the first harvest, and thereby a metaphor for the later, subtler phase of a cultural movement.

Primary literal sense — the second mowing. In British rural usage an aftermath (also called aftergrass or the dialect eamath — the "after-mowing" from Old English ǣfemǣþ?) is the grass that springs again in a field after the hay has been cut, furnishing a late crop of pasturage or a second cut. Agricultural writers from the sixteenth century onward use the word soberly: "the aftermath or second crop of grass". It is a plain term of husbandry, and its internal anatomy is transparent: after + math, where math is the old word for "a mowing" (Old English mǣþ), seen also in eamath and surviving in dialect verb-forms of mow.

Secondary sense — figurative "later growth". From the second crop the word naturally spread to any later or secondary product or phase: the aftermath of a season, of a harvest, of a generation. This is the sense in which Victorian literary prose could speak of a cultural aftermath — a late, second blossoming after a great age, often finer or more refined than the first. Pater's "wonderful later growth" is the exact avatar of this usage and shows the word still tasting of grass and hay even at its most aesthetic.

Tertiary sense — consequences and after-effects (dominant today). In the twentieth century the word was decisively pulled toward its modern meaning: the state of affairs that follows an event, particularly a destructive one — the aftermath of a war, of a storm, of a bankruptcy, of an injury. The shift is a natural metaphor: as the aftermath-grass follows the mowing, so consequences follow deeds. Modern collocations are almost automatic — "in the aftermath of", "the immediate aftermath", "dealing with the aftermath". The connotation is normally negative: aftermaths are cleared up, lived through, endured.

Related and derived usages. The compound survives only as this one noun; there is no verb "to aftermath". In agronomy the word is still current for late-grass crops. In journalism it functions almost as a prepositional phrase (post-event). The adjective "aftermathal" is a learned rarity; the idiom does the work instead.

Register. Literary-husbandry origin; today's standard usage is journalistic and neutral-reporting, with a persistent undertone of disaster. The word is mildly formal: "the aftermath" says what "what came after" says, but with an acquired gravity.

Polysemy summary.

  • second crop of grass after mowing — the literal rural sense (16th c. – now agricultural);
  • any later growth, later period, or second flowering — Victorian literary metaphor;
  • the consequences / after-effects of an event — the dominant modern sense, usually of misfortune;
  • "in the aftermath of" — a fixed locution functioning like a prepositional phrase.

The semantic bridge. The whole career of the word is one extended agricultural metaphor. Because the second grass-crop comes after the cutting, "aftermath" could be extended to anything that grows after an event — and once the event was a war or a storm, the "growth" was the tangle of consequences the event left behind. The meadow is still faintly visible at the root of every modern headline.
