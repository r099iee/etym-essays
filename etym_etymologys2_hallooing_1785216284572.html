The word is of imitative (echoic) origin and has no Indo-European root; it begins as a raw cry of the hunting field rather than as a derived lexical formation.

  • Proto-form — an echoic attention- or chase-cry of the raised voice, freely varied for sonorous effect ("halloo", "halow", "hollo"); no written attestation, by its nature a spoken interjection, but the reconstructed base behind all the Old French and Middle English forms. Stage count note: the interjection is inherently pre-literary, and scholars treat the word's "earliest known stage" as this reconstructed expressive cry rather than an attested PIE root.
  • Old French haloer / halloer "to cry out, to incite hounds" (attested from the 13th century; itself formed from the echoic hunting cry, possibly reinforced by the verb haler "to haul, pull" in the sense of drawing the hounds on). Some scholars also see a conflation in Middle English with the native Old English interjection ēalā "O!, oh!, lo!" (recorded in Old English, c. 9th–11th centuries), which could have given the cry a second, home-grown lease on life; this is the principal debated stage.
  • Middle English halow / hallow "to pursue with shouts, to shout in the chase" (late 14th century); by the mid-15th century the same cry, halow, is recorded as a shipman's call to incite effort at sea.
  • English halloo, the form stabilizing in the 16th–17th centuries: as an interjection "halloo!" (recorded by the OED from 1671) and as a verb "to call or shout to, to hail, to urge on hounds" (recorded 1602 in the writing of John Marston).
  • Modern English present participle HALLOOING "the ongoing, repeated act of shouting 'halloo!'" — the form in the Tolkien sentence, derived regularly from the verb halloo + -ing.

Debate / nuance note: (1) The word's ultimate origin is onomatopoeic by nature and cannot be traced to an Indo-European root; the "PIE stage" simply does not exist for this word. (2) The Old French haloer origin is the standard account, but the possible blending with Old English ēalā is debated — some etymologists prefer a purely Franco-Latin ancestry, others a native English reinforcement; the two are not mutually exclusive and the medieval evidence cannot fully separate them. (3) The variant family — hallo, halloa, halloo, hollo, holla — shows that the word was a free-floating interjection long before it froze into any single spelling; holla (Old French ho + là "ho there!") is a separate but converging formation whose descendants holler and hello complete the family tree.
