- Old English 'floterian' - a frequentative of the verb 'to float,' meaning to float or move about lightly and unsteadily; its early senses belong to water and air alike, of things borne along without fixed direction.
- Middle English - 'floteren/flutter' develops the sense of quick, trembling, irregular motion — of banners, of leaves, and then of wings; it keeps its lightness and its loss of steady purpose.
- 16th-19th century - the wing-sense fixes: a bird or moth flutters; poets seize on the word for the nervous, delicate motion of small creatures and of the heart, and for anything agitated but unsteady.
- Contemporary - 'fluttering' is the precise word for rapid, light, irregular beating within roughly the same space — moths at dusk, a flag, a nervous pulse; it sits between flit (directed darting) and flap (heavy, slow beating), and retains its Old English gentleness.
