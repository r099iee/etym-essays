The quickening family holds a mirror to one of the best-known semantic shifts in English: quick, whose oldest meaning is “alive, living”, now means “fast”. The gerund quickening lived on both sides of that shift — first as “making alive”, then as “making fast” — and it preserves the older sense in religion, medicine, and poetry long after the adjective itself moved on.

The Indo-European root: *gʷeyh₃- “to live”

The family begins with the Indo-European verb *gʷeyh₃- “to live” (older notation *gʷeiə-), the source also of Latin vīvus / vīvere “alive / to live”, Greek bíos “life”, Old Irish béo “living”, and Sanskrit jīvati “he lives”. From this root Indo-European formed an adjective *gʷih₃-wó- “alive”, which in the Germanic branch yielded Proto-Germanic *kwikwaz “alive, living” — the initial *gʷ hardening to *kw by the regular Germanic consonant changes (visible also in *gʷou “cow” > *kū). Modern fossils of *kwikwaz in English include quick itself, “quicksand” (originally “living”, i.e. shifting, treacherous sand), “quicksilver” (mercury, the “living metal”), and “the quick” = the living flesh under the nails, as in “cut to the quick”.

Old and Middle English

Old English had cwic “alive, living” — the word of the Creed's “the quick and the dead” (the living and the dead, a phrase still current with an archaic flavour) — and the denominative verb cwician “to come to life, revive, animate”. Middle English formed quiken / quyken with the senses “to make or become alive; to cause to grow; to revive in spirit”, and the -ing noun appears in late Middle English religious prose: Wycliffe's Bible renders 1 Corinthians 15:45 “the quykenynge spirit”, which the King James Version (1611) gives as “the quickening Spirit” — for the translators of that age, “to quicken” was still the plain word for “to give life”.

Obstetrics and law: quickening as a public milepost

From the 16th century, English medical and legal writing uses “quickening” for the first perceptible movements of the fetus — the moment at which the unborn child was regarded as alive, traditionally felt by the mother between the fourth and fifth months of pregnancy. The word acquired real legal weight: early modern common law treated abortion after quickening as a graver offence, and the distinction was written into English statute in 1803 (Lord Ellenborough's Act), while several American states followed in the 19th century. For roughly three centuries “quickening” operated as a workable public line between “life” and “pre-life”; the term and its history became a standing reference point in 20th-century debates over abortion law and remain glossed in medical-historical dictionaries.

The great shift: from “living” to “fast”

Meanwhile quick itself migrated: “alive” → “vigorous, lively” → “hasty” → “fast”. Already in the 16th century “quick” meant “speedy” (“quick march”, “quick return”); by the 18th–19th centuries the speed sense dominated everyday use, and the “living” sense survived mainly in fixed phrases (the quick and the dead, cut to the quick, quicksilver, quicksand). The gerund followed the adjective: from the 19th century onward, quickening in general prose ordinarily means “acceleration, growing more rapid” — “the quickening pace of life”, “a quickening in trade”. Wordsworth's source passage (the breeze that “gently moved / With quickening virtue”) still uses the older, life-giving sense; the two meanings of the word sit side by side within a few lines of each other in the verse — the whole history of the shift in miniature.

Modern state

Today quickening has three faces: (1) everyday — acceleration (“the quickening of the process”); (2) religious and biblical — the quickening of the spirit, of faith, of the year; (3) historical-medical — the fetal quickening, still cited in law and medical history. Popular culture adds a fourth: the “Quickening” of the Highlander franchise, a transfer of life-force between immortals, in which the old “living” sense returns as high melodrama. Few English words show more neatly how a culture kept its old word for “life” alive inside its new word for “speed”.
