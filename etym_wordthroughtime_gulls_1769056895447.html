- 15th-century settlement - 'gull' enters English as the plain, neutral name for grey-and-white seabirds, carrying no particular emotion; the word simply names the bird.
- 17th-18th century - a homographic verb sense ('to gull', to trick or dupe, hence 'gullible') shadows the form in public speech, so 'gull' begins to be heard with a sly, dupe-able undertone even though the bird and the cheat are distinct words.
- 19th century - the colloquial double 'seagull' rises in everyday speech, and 'gull' settles into the literary and natural-historical register - the word of books and bird-lore rather than the street.
- 20th-21st century - field naturalists reclaim 'gull' as the technically correct term, insisting the birds also live far inland, and 'seagull' is demoted to informal usage; in its possessive plural form ('gulls''), the word does quiet domestic work, turning wild seabirds into countable pantry items like 'gulls' eggs'.
