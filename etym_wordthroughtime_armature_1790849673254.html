- Classical Rome (1st c. BCE–4th c. CE): Latin armatura denoted military armor, weapons, the defensive equipment of soldiers, and specialized military weapon drill routines.
- 1400s–1600s: Borrowed into Middle French and Early Modern English to denote defensive harnesses, suits of armor, and protective military gear.
- 1680s–1780s: Broadened in natural history and comparative anatomy to denote the natural protective armor (horns, scales, shells, quills) of animals and plants.
- 1830s–1890s: Adopted in fine arts for the internal wire or metal skeleton supporting clay sculpture, and in electrical engineering for the iron keeper of a magnet and the rotating coil of dynamos and electric motors.
- 1950s–Present: Re-conceptualized in structural anthropology (Lévi-Strauss) and poststructuralist literary theory (Barthes) as the fundamental grammatical or symbolic scaffolding organizing meaning in myths and texts.
