Brunswick is first and most properly the English form of German Braunschweig, a historic city in Lower Saxony in northern Germany. Founded in the early 11th century on the River Oker, it became under Henry the Lion in the 12th century a great princely capital, seat of the Welf dukes, famed for its Dankwarderode Castle, its bronze lion, its churches and its Hanseatic trade. In the source fragment — he was able to take a house in Hunter Street, Brunswick Square — the word is not the German city itself but its London namesake: Brunswick Square in Bloomsbury, laid out in the 1790s and named for Caroline of Brunswick, the estranged wife of the Prince Regent. John Ruskin's father, a prosperous wine-merchant, took No. 54 (later numbered 28) there, and Ruskin was born and spent his childhood in that tall, plain, respectable London house. To know Brunswick here is thus to know respectable early-19th-century Bloomsbury: professional, evangelical, orderly, within walking distance of the British Museum.

Behind the London square stands a whole dynastic world. Brunswick names the former Duchy of Brunswick (Herzogtum Braunschweig), later Brunswick-Lüneburg and Hanover, whose Protestant princes supplied Britain with its Hanoverian kings from George I in 1714. The House of Brunswick (House of Welf, Guelph) is therefore a standard synonym in English histories for the Hanoverian dynasty and its German possessions. A Victorian reader meeting Brunswick heard loyalty, Protestant succession, German kinship, and the Napoleonic wars — the Black Brunswickers, the Duke of Brunswick killed at Quatre Bras in 1815, Caroline's scandalous trial in 1820. The name carried constitutional and sentimental weight far beyond a street address.

  • Core toponymic sense: the German city Braunschweig: e.g. Brunswick lion, Brunswick cathedral, Technical University of Brunswick. Always capitalised. English histories, Baedekers, and war narratives use Brunswick where Germans write Braunschweig. The bridge to other senses is naming by origin or honour: things from the city, regiments raised there, emigrants carrying the name, or places christened to flatter its princess.
  • London square sense (source sense): Brunswick Square, Bloomsbury: e.g. to lodge in Brunswick Square, Brunswick Square Gardens. Built by the Foundling Hospital estate, with a central garden, terraced houses, later the School of Pharmacy and historic associations with Virginia Woolf, E. M. Forster, and Ruskin. Here Brunswick means genteel, professional, slightly severe London domesticity, not Germany at all except by commemorative naming.
  • Dynastic and territorial sense: Duchy, House, and troops of Brunswick: e.g. Duke of Brunswick, Princess of Brunswick, Brunswick contingent at Waterloo, Brunswick green (the livery colour). In this use Brunswick is shorthand for a small north-German Protestant state and its soldiers, diplomats, and marriages. The semantic extension is metonymy: city for rulers for territory for army.
  • Transferred global toponyms: Brunswick in Georgia and Ohio in the USA, New Brunswick in Canada and New Jersey, Brunswick in Australia and New Zealand. Most derive from the British-Hanoverian connection or directly from the German city via emigrants. E.g. New Brunswick, Brunswick County, East Brunswick. The bridge is colonial commemoration: to name a new settlement Brunswick was to declare Hanoverian loyalty.
  • Product and recipe senses: Brunswick stew, Brunswick sausage, Brunswick black, Brunswick green: an American slow-cooked meat-and-vegetable stew of the US South (Virginia, Georgia, North Carolina all claim it); a smoked pork-beef sausage; a Japan-black varnish for iron; a deep green pigment (copper oxychloride). Each is Brunswick by supposed origin or by maker's brand, not by essence. The stew, for instance, is said to have been invented by a cook in Brunswick County, Virginia — local pride turned into menu English.
  • Figurative loyalist sense (historical): a Brunswicker: in 1790s–1820s Britain a staunch supporter of the Brunswick dynasty against radicals and against Caroline's partisans ironically both claimed Brunswick. E.g. Brunswick clubs, Brunswick mob. The bridge is dynastic partisanship: to be for Brunswick was to be for King, Church, and Protestant succession.

Literary nuance matters. When Ruskin writes Hunter Street, Brunswick Square, he is not showing off geography but placing his family socially: not Mayfair fashionable, not City mercantile, but sober Bloomsbury professional. The square's very newness in the 1820s — stucco, railings, Foundling Hospital walks, water-carts and postchaises outside — frames his childhood sensibility: order, observation, propriety, and the panoramic travel that follows. A modern reader who hears only Germany misses the domestic point; a reader who hears only London misses the Hanoverian echo that made such naming natural.
