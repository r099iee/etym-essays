- 1850s–1918 — Jugoslavija circulates among South Slav intellectuals as a romantic, unrealised programme of liberation and union; it sounds idealistic, progressive and slightly conspiratorial.
- 1918–1928 — The new state officially calls itself the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes; “Yugoslavia” remains a popular synonym carrying hopes of a shared nation.
- 1929–1941 — King Alexander I imposes the name officially as part of a unitarist dictatorship; it acquires an authoritarian, centralist flavour and is resented by federalists.
- 1941–1945 — The name splits in two: the royal government-in-exile claims it, while Tito’s Partisans promise a new, federal Yugoslavia; it becomes a slogan of antifascist resistance.
- 1945–1980 — Socialist Yugoslavia becomes a byword for workers’ self-management, non-alignment, relative prosperity and the freedom to travel; abroad it is admired as “the good communist country.”
- 1980–1991 — Economic crisis and nationalist mobilisation hollow out the name; to Serbian centralists it means unity, to Slovenes and Croats it increasingly means domination.
- 1991–2003 — The name is reduced to Serbia and Montenegro and becomes a diplomatic fiction; “former Yugoslavia” turns into a synonym for war, sanctions and the tribunal at The Hague.
- 2003–present — The state name is retired; “Yugoslavia” survives as nostalgia (“Yugonostalgia”), as a marketing label for shared pop culture, and as a warning example in debates about multi-ethnic federations.
