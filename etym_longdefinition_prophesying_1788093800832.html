Prophesying is the present participle and gerund of the verb prophesy: to speak or declare as a prophet — that is, to utter what is believed to be divinely inspired truth about the future, or more loosely, to predict an outcome with confident conviction. The word has a sacred core and a secular halo, and both are needed to understand it.

1. The religious sense (primary).

  • To foretell future events by divine inspiration: the prophets of the Bible prophesy coming judgement, exile, restoration and the Messiah.
  • To speak or preach under prophetic inspiration, which in biblical usage includes far more than prediction: in 1 Corinthians 14, "he that prophesieth speaketh unto men to edification, and exhortation, and comfort" — prophesying is inspired preaching, teaching and encouragement, the Spirit speaking through a human voice.

2. The secular and extended senses.

  • Confident prediction: to prophesy an outcome without any claim to divine inspiration — "he prophesied the market crash"; the word lends solemnity and drama to ordinary prediction.
  • Ironic and jocular: "prophesying doom", "prophesying disaster", "prophesying the end of the world" — predicting bad outcomes, often mock-solemnly; in this use the sacred register is knowingly borrowed for effect.
  • Of texts and omens: loosely, to foretell — events "prophesy" later developments in retrospect ("the early novels prophesied the later style").

3. The gerund in use. As a verbal noun, prophesying names the activity itself: "his prophesying annoyed the council". It takes direct objects ("prophesying floods and poisoned fish" — the context sentence, where Bard is mockingly accused of predicting disasters) and prepositional complements ("prophesying of the future"). The form always keeps the verb's spelling with -s-.

4. Spelling: prophesy vs prophecy. The verb is prophesy (rhyming with "sigh") and the noun is prophecy (rhyming with "see"): one "prophesies a prophecy". The -ing form is therefore always prophesying, never "prophecying". The modern distinction is a 16th–17th century regularization of forms that were freely mixed in Middle English.

5. Register and nuance. Prophesying is grander and more solemn than predicting: it implies conviction, urgency and — in religious use — a mandate from beyond. Secular uses borrow that grandeur, which is why "prophesying doom" sounds archaic-dramatic rather than neutral. In contemporary religious communities the word remains fully literal and active; outside them it is usually ironic or literary.

6. Summary. To be prophesying is to speak with prophetic authority — foretelling the future under divine inspiration in the religious sense, or predicting with confident, often solemn conviction in the secular and ironic senses; the word descends from the Greek for "speaking before/for (a god)" and carries that sacred weight into every modern use.
