AFFECTATION (noun): behavior, speech, writing, manner, or style that is artificial, studied, and designed to impress - a deliberate adoption of qualities, feelings, accents, tastes, or refinements that one does not genuinely possess. Pronounced /ˌæfɛkˈteɪʃən/; adjective affected; abstract noun affectedness. Sense 1 - Artificial behavior and mannerism (core sense). In ordinary usage, affectation is the visible, performed layer of the self: a posh accent that is not one's own, a limp handshake, exaggerated refinement, an assumed air of boredom or sophistication. It is behavior put on to impress - "his British accent was a complete affectation", "she dropped the affectation of indifference". The key element is strain: the display costs effort and is not natural. Affectedness is the quality of being affected. Sense 2 - The Austen sense: the affectation of a virtue. In Pride and Prejudice (1813, Chapter 4) Elizabeth tells Jane: "Affectation of candour is common enough - one meets with it everywhere. But to be candid without affectation or design... belongs to you alone." Here affectation is the false performance of a moral virtue - pretending to judge people charitably, pretending to admire everyone, performing sweetness - in order to appear good. The affectation of candour is the counterfeit of Jane's genuine generosity of judgement. The historical bridge: affectare in Latin meant "to strive after, pursue eagerly"; to affect candour is to strive after candour so as to be seen as candid. Sense 3 - Pretentiousness in taste and style. Affectation covers pretentious tastes and mannerisms of taste: "an affectation for obscure wines", "the affectation of preferring vinyl", "a writer's affectations of learning". The word suggests showing off a refinement that is chosen for status rather than organic. Sense 4 - Technical and specialist uses.
  • Rhetoric and acting (historical): in classical and Renaissance rhetoric, "affectation" (Latin affectatio) named the deliberate cultivation of a stylistic manner, usually condemned as straining after ornament at the cost of naturalness - the failing of the "Asiatic" style against the "Attic".
  • Linguistics and sociolinguistics: "affected pronunciation" or "affected speech" designates socially motivated pronunciation that departs from a speaker's natural dialect (e.g., hypercorrection).
  • Medicine (historical): in 16th-18th century medical writing, "affectation" could refer to a disposition or condition of the body (an affectation of the nerves), overlapping with affection in the old sense of "state, condition". This sense is now obsolete except in historical contexts.
  • Philosophy of mind: "affect" (the feeling state) is distinct from "affectation" (the performed display of feeling); modern psychology speaks of "affect display" rather than affectation.
Sense 5 - Fondness, inclination (archaic). In 16th-17th century English, affectation sometimes meant "fondness for, inclination toward" ("he had an affectation of learning" = a leaning toward learning), a direct trace of Latin affectatio "a striving after, an eager pursuit". This positive or neutral sense is now obsolete or literary. Grammar and formation. affectation is a noun; affected the adjective; affectedly the adverb; affect the verb. The verb affect belongs to three distinct families: (1) "to influence" (Latin afficere), (2) "to pretend, assume" (Latin affectare, whence affectation), and (3) "to like, incline toward" (archaic). The double ff reflects Latin ad- + facere. Register and nuance. Affectation is critical but usually mild - it mocks show rather than condemning deep hypocrisy. It differs from hypocrisy (concealing vice behind claimed virtue), from ostentation (flaunting wealth and magnificence), and from pretension (the claim or ambition itself). Affectation is the manner, the costume, the performance. Common collocations. affectation of candour/learning/grandeur/indifference; without affectation; a mere affectation; literary affectation; the affectations of the court; an affectation of humility. Metaphoric logic. The word's deep image is the striving performer: Latin affectare is the frequentative of afficere, "to do something to someone", so to affect a quality is literally "to keep acting it out toward an audience" - the eternally repeated gesture of the actor.
