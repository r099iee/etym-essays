The past tense sped began life not in swiftness but in success. Its deepest root is PIE *speh₁-, to thrive or prosper, which gave Proto-Germanic *spōdiz — success, prosperity — the ancestor also of Dutch spoed and German Sputen kin. Old English spedan meant to succeed: a king sped when his campaign prospered, a petitioner sped when his suit was granted, and wealth itself was sped, the thriving abundance of flocks and fields.

The road changed the meaning. To wish a traveller God speed you was originally to wish him a prosperous journey — but prosperous journeys are quick ones, and by the later Middle Ages the wish had slid from fortune to velocity. Middle English speden already hurries as well as thrives; by Shakespeare's day swiftness owns the verb and success survives only in the fossilised farewell. The past form sped rode the same slide: from he prospered to he hastened to he flew.

Early modern literature fixed the flying sense in amber. Ballads sped arrows, messengers, and errands; the Prayer Book prayed that proceedings might be sped; admirals wrote of packets sped to distant stations. The railway age then gave the old word iron lungs: sped trains, sped telegrams, sped orders — Victorian urgency poured through an Anglo-Saxon pipe.

The twentieth century split the past tense in two. Motoring law needed a verb for exceeding limits, and fined drivers were said to have speeded, with regular -ed, while sped kept the ancient irregular stronghold of noble flight — arrows, seasons, and errands. Usage guides now bless both, assigning each its territory like gamekeepers.

  • Sound shifts: PIE *speh₁- lengthened to Germanic *spō- by regular laryngeal colouring; Old English spedan lost its infinitive ending in Middle English; the vowel shortened in the past form sped exactly as in led and fed.
  • Semantic drift: prosperity to prosperous journey to quick journey to sheer velocity — success narrowed to the one success travellers crave, arrival, until arrival's speed swallowed the fortune whole.
  • Socio-cultural context: pilgrimage, post-horses, railways, and motorways each redefined what haste meant, and each left sped marking the completed dash of its age.
  • Literary attestations: Malory's knights speed their quests; our ballad line seals an errand with sped; modern fantasy from Tolkien onward uses the form to toll archaic completion over finished journeys.
  • The motor-age fork: when speed limits and speedometers arrived, the law needed a tidy regular past, and speeded was conscripted for summonses — leaving sped free to keep its commission in poetry, prayer, and story.

So in sped sleeps a forgotten blessing: every swift thing that ever sped was once a wish that it might prosper and arrive safely home.
