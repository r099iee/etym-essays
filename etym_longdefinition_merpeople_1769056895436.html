merpeople (noun, plural; singular merperson) — mythical beings who are half human and half fish, living in the sea; the collective, gender-neutral name for the race of mermaids and mermen. In this passage Susan is remembering the happy times in Narnia—"the merpeople singing in the sea" among a catalogue of vanished delights that also includes chess with fauns, good giants, and her beautiful horse. The specific nuance of the word here is collective and gentle: merpeople are the sea-dwelling folk, half human and half fish, who sing in the ocean, and they are presented not as a threat or a wonder of shock value but as part of the ordinary loveliness of a world where even the sea was alive, musical, and peopled. The word's plural, people, is decisive: it makes them a society, a race with songs and customs, rather than the lone, romantic figures of the traditional mermaid. Within the context the tone is wistful and nostalgic; Susan is overwhelmed by the return of these memories, and "merpeople singing in the sea" is the image that most purely conveys a lost world of innocent enchantment—the ocean made conscious and harmonious. 1. The primary sense
  • Definition: a merperson is a legendary sea-dwelling being with the head, arms, and torso of a human and the tail of a fish in place of legs; merpeople (and the older merfolk) are the whole race of such beings, male and female, as a society living under the waves.
  • Composition of the word: merpeople is transparently "sea-people"—the old English sea-element mer- (from mere, "sea, lake") combined with the word people. Unlike mermaid or merman, which name single figures of a fixed gender, merpeople is deliberately plural and inclusive, the collective noun for the singing nation beneath the waves.
  • Origin of the coinage: the word is a modern fantasy-term, crystallised in the 20th century as a back-formation and generalization from mermaid and merman (via the neutral singular merperson), so that the folkloric pair became a whole society—exactly what Susan's memory requires.
2. Secondary senses and modern usage
  • Fantasy literature and folklore writing: merpeople appears in children's literature and fantasy (including C.S. Lewis's Narnia, where sea-people attend the great feast) as the standard collective name for the sea-folk; writers use it to make the mermaid's world a culture rather than a curiosity.
  • Gender-neutral usage: since the late 20th century, merperson and merpeople have been the inclusive terms, replacing the gendered mermaid/merman in contexts where the sex of the being is unknown or where a whole community is meant; the word also appears in LGBTQ+ and queer-folklore writing as a symbol of hybrid identity and transformation.
  • The mer- prefix generally: the combining form mer- (sea) now generates many words—merfolk, mersong, mermaid, merman, merswine (the old medieval "sea-pig," a porpoise), and the fantasy mer-kingdom, mer-culture, mer-court—so merpeople is one member of a productive family built on the ancient sea-root.
  • Register: the word is literary, fantastic, and childlike, at home in fairy tale and myth; it deliberately sounds modern and transparent beside the ancient, opaque mermaid, and a reader needs no gloss to hear "sea-people."
3. Distinctions from the neighbouring sea-beings
  • Mermaid / merman: single, gender-specific figures; merpeople is their collective, neutral plural—a whole people, not one maiden on a rock.
  • Sirens: in Greek myth, the dangerous singers who lure sailors to death; Narnian merpeople sing for joy—sirens destroy, merpeople delight.
  • Tritons: male Greek sea-gods, attendants of Poseidon with conch-trumpets—deities, not a people.
  • Nereids: Greek sea-nymphs, daughters of Nereus, fully divine and not half-fish at all.
  • Selkies: in Celtic tradition, seals that shed their skins to become human—shapeshifters, not hybrids.
4. Semantic bridge
  • The historical bridge is the generalisation of the singular into the social: for a thousand years English had only the lone mermaid (Old English mere + mægden, "sea-maiden"); the 20th century added the plural people and turned a curiosity into a civilisation. The modern word preserves the ancient sea-root mere while grafting onto it the modern sense of a people—a society with songs, customs, and a culture, the ocean made conscious and harmonious.
