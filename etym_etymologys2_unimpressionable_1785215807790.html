PIE *per- 'to strike, hit' (traditional Pokorny account of Latin premere; modern scholars are cautious — the connection is not universally accepted; see note) → Latin premere 'to press, press down, bear upon' (attested from Plautus, 3rd–2nd c. BCE; supine pressum) → Latin imprimere 'to press in, stamp, imprint' (in- 'into, upon' + premere; attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Latin impressiō 'a pressing into, stamp, mark, impression' (from imprimere; also used in its printing sense) → Old French impression 'mark made by pressure' → Middle English impression (14th c.) 'a mark made by stamping' → English impression 'mark made by a stamp', extended figuratively to 'effect produced on the mind' (the Lockean metaphor of the seal in wax, 17th–18th c.) → English impressionable (1836; cf. French impressionnable) 'susceptible to impressions, easily affected' → un- + impressionable → English unimpressionable (early 19th c.) 'not susceptible to impressions; emotionally impervious'. Note: the traditional derivation of Latin premere from PIE *per- 'to strike' goes back to Pokorny, but modern etymologists (e.g. de Vaan) are cautious and treat the Indo-European connection as uncertain; the Latin family itself (premere, pressus, pressiō) is secure, so the chain from Latin onward is solidly attested.
