SPED is the standard past tense and past participle of the verb speed, meaning to move or travel quickly, to hasten, or to cause something to move quickly. The form is a single short syllable, and it is that very brevity that gives it its force in narrative: sped is faster than "hastened" and more elegant than "zoomed". In the source sentence — "...he sped from his deep lair through its great door, out into the huge passages of the mountain-palace and up towards the Front Gate" — the dragon, having coiled his length, hurtles out of his lair in one continuous dash; sped captures both speed and directionality, the urgent, unimpeded rush through door, passages, and gate.

Primary senses

  • To move quickly (intransitive): to go fast, to rush, to hasten: "the car sped down the highway".
  • To make something go fast (transitive): to cause to move or proceed quickly: "he sped the arrow from his bow"; "the drug sped his recovery".
  • To go at excessive speed: in traffic-law usage, to drive above the legal limit: "he was caught speeding".
  • As past participle: used with have/had: "the messenger had sped across the plain before dawn".

Secondary and peripheral senses

  • Archaic-blessing sense: "God speed you!" — from the older meaning "to prosper, succeed, further"; the formula survives as Godspeed, a wish of success on a journey. Related participle: "well sped" (well furthered).
  • Rapid progress (abstract): time or events "speed by"; work "speeds along"; the years sped past — quickness applied to time, process, and change.
  • Technical usage note: for the transitive sense "to exceed the speed limit", the past is usually speeded ("he speeded up the process"; "the car was speeded"); sped dominates for simple motion.
  • The noun link: the verb's history is inseparable from the noun speed ("rate of motion"), which began life meaning "success and prosperity" and only later acquired the mechanical sense of velocity.

Semantic bridge between senses: the Old English core meaning was "success, prosperity, advancement" — to speed was to thrive, to make progress, to get on. Since successful progress came to be measured by how fast one moved, the sense narrowed and shifted from "thriving" to "going quickly". The blessing "Godspeed" preserves the older, nobler sense in amber.

Connotations and register

  • Sped is a literary, somewhat elevated past tense — favoured by narrative prose, news writing, and fantasy for its one-syllable urgency; it feels fleet and elegant.
  • In traffic and engineering contexts the word is dry and technical ("speed limit", "speeding fine").
  • Caution: in some schoolyard slang sped is an offensive shortening of "special ed" student — a modern, derogatory branch entirely unrelated to the verb; it is best avoided in neutral usage.

In the source text the past tense sped is chosen over "rushed" or "dashed" because it sustains the whole journey in one breath — lair, door, passages, gate — with no slackening: the dragon's motion is a single swift unbroken line, and the word's brevity makes the reading itself fast.
