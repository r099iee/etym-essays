The historical trajectory of drivel exemplifies the visceral genius of the Germanic lexicon, demonstrating how a concrete, earthy term for physical slime and turbid dregs was transformed through centuries of spoken English into an intellectual and comedic insult of devastating precision.

  • The Proto-Indo-European and Germanic background (*dʰerebʰ-): The deepest ancestry of drivel connects to the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰerebʰ-, signifying “to curdle, coagulate, make turbid, or thicken.” This root represents an extension of the primary base *dʰer- (to make muddy or dark). In the Proto-Germanic daughter branch, this root generated nominal and verbal formations centered around wet, thick refuse, dregs, and sediment: Proto-Germanic *drab- gave rise to Middle Dutch draf, Old Norse draf (malt dregs, waste), and English draff (the refuse of malt after brewing, or hog-swill).
  • Old English dreflian and bodily secretions: From this nominal stem arose the frequentative/iterative verb *drablijaną, which developed into late Old English dreflian. In the medical and descriptive glosses of the Anglo-Saxon period (around 1000 CE), dreflian possessed a purely physical, physiological meaning: “to slaver, slobber, drool, or run at the nose.” It was recorded alongside words describing phlegm, nasal catarrh, and the uncontrollable salivation of domestic beasts or diseased individuals. It was entirely devoid of any metaphorical or intellectual application.
  • Middle English evolution (14th century): Following the phonetic shifts of early Middle English, Old English dreflian became drivelen or drevelen (with the characteristic softening and centering of the vowels). It is during the fourteenth century that the word embarked upon its profound semantic expansion. In the alliterative masterpiece Piers Plowman (c. 1370–1390), William Langland uses dravelen to describe old age and drunkenness: the drunkard or the doddering old man not only lets saliva escape from his slack lips, but his mind loses its grip on rational thought, causing him to babble incoherently. The conceptual connection was instantaneous: physical drool and mental twaddle were both signs of an uncoordinated, undisciplined organism.
  • Early Modern English and Shakespearean insult: By the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, the spelling had settled on drivel, and the metaphorical sense of “talking nonsense or acting like an imbecile” had outstripped its literal ancestor in polite literature. In Romeo and Juliet (Act II, Scene 4), Mercutio mocks Romeo’s lovesick verses: “for this drivelling love is like a great natural [fool], that runs lolling up and down to hide his bauble in a hole.” Here drivelling perfectly bridges slobbering idiocy and romantic claptrap. The agent noun driveller emerged as a stinging put-down for an incompetent, rambling speaker.
  • The 19th-century nominalisation: Although the verb had been active for centuries, the use of drivel as an independent mass noun meaning “inane speech or writing” blossomed fully during the nineteenth century. Victorian book reviewers, essayists (such as Thomas Carlyle and Thomas Babington Macaulay), and political pamphleteers found in drivel the ideal critical scalpel. To label an opponent’s treatise or a romantic poem “pure drivel” condemned it not merely as mistaken, but as mentally incontinent, childish, and unworthy of serious rebuttal.
  • Comedic perfection in P. G. Wodehouse: In the twentieth century, British humorists adopted the word as a staple of refined conversational combat. P. G. Wodehouse made “Must you drivel, Bertie?” one of the signature catchphrases of the Wooster and Jeeves canon. Put in the mouths of domineering aunts, the phrase weaponizes the word’s ancient history of slobbering infancy to deflate Bertie’s aristocratic dignity with instant comic perfection.

From ancient Germanic brewing dregs and Anglo-Saxon running noses to Shakespearean wit and the drawing rooms of Mayfair, drivel proves that language never truly forgets the physical body from which its metaphors are born.
