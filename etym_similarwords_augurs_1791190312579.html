• Soothsayer: Similar because it predicts the future from signs; differs because it is generic and archaic-folkloric, without Roman collegial or legal authority.
  • Seer: Similar because it claims vision of hidden things; differs because it emphasizes inspired sight rather than technical observation of birds and formal announcement.
  • Prophet: Similar because it speaks for divine will; differs because it is typically Hebrew or religious-revelatory, moral and absolute, not procedural Roman auspices.
  • Oracle: Similar because it delivers divine answers; differs because it can mean the shrine, utterance, or person, and suggests ecstatic mediumship rather than systematic sky-reading.
  • Diviner: Similar because it practices divination; differs because it is the superordinate term covering all techniques, while augur specifies Roman bird-auspices.
  • Harbinger: Similar because it foreshadows coming events; differs because it is the sign itself or forerunner, not the priest who interprets.
  • Forecaster: Similar because modern augurs forecast markets or elections; differs because it is secular, statistical, and neutral, without religious sanction.
  • Prognosticator: Similar because it makes formal predictions; differs because it is pompous-modern and often medical or economic, not priestly.
  • Auspices (priests): Similar because Roman auspices were taken by augurs; differs because auspice strictly is the rite or patronage, augur the person.
  • Haruspex (pl. haruspices): Similar because it was the fellow Roman diviner; differs because haruspices read entrails, especially livers, often Etruscan, distinct from bird-augurs.
  • Pontiff: Similar because it was a major Roman priestly college member; differs because pontiffs managed sacred law and calendar, not bird-omens.
  • Flamen: Similar because it was another Roman priest; differs because flamines served individual deities with taboos, not divination.
  • Sibyl: Similar because she uttered Roman-approved prophecy; differs because she was an ecstatic female figure of books and oracles, not a male college magistrate-priest.
  • Pundit: Similar because journalistic augurs are pundits reading signs; differs because pundit is secular expertise, often ironic, with no ritual act.
