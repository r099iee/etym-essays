libertine (noun, also adjective) — a person who behaves without moral principles or a sense of responsibility, especially one who rejects conventional restraints on conduct, particularly sexual conduct; a free-thinker grown dissolute, one who carries liberty to the point of license. In the literary context given — "Sir Jia Zheng is mistaken in treating the boy as a future libertine" — the word is used as a grave moral warning: the speaker judges it an error to suppose the boy will become a dissolute, morally unrestrained man. The tone is cautionary and reproachful, and the word carries the full weight of its history — the image of a person who has so thoroughly abused freedom that it has become a licence for depravity. The future libertine is feared as one who will flout moral and social law without remorse.

Core senses

  • Moral sense (primary modern sense): a libertine is a man (the word is normally applied to men) of dissolute, unrestrained habits, especially in sexual matters, who disregards conventional morality, religion, and responsibility. It implies aristocratic or leisured corruption — the rake who treats pleasure as a right and conscience as a weakness.
  • Historical sense (religious free-thinker): in the 16th and 17th centuries, a libertine was first a freethinker — one who rejected religious dogma and clerical authority, who claimed freedom of thought in matters of faith. This intellectual sense preceded the moral one and explains the word's construction from liberty.
  • Historical sense (freedman): at the root of the word, in Latin libertinus, is the freedman — a former slave granted liberty. This social sense survives in ancient history and in the biblical term "Libertines" (Acts 6:9, a synagogue of freedmen in Jerusalem).
  • Philosophical sense (libertinism): in French and continental thought, libertinage or libertinism names a philosophical stance that questions moral and religious authority in the name of intellectual freedom — the radical scepticism of the 17th-century French libertins érudits.
  • Adjective use: libertine also functions as an adjective meaning "dissolute, unrestrained, licentious" — "libertine philosophy," "libertine excess," "libertine habits" — describing behaviour or doctrine that flouts restraint.
  • Literary and historical sense: in eighteenth-century literature — the novels of Richardson, Fielding, Laclos, and the figure of Don Juan — the libertine is the stock villain-hero of aristocratic dissipation, a man of wit and rank undone by appetite. This is the sense closest to the given context.

Semantic structure and historical bridge

The word descends from Latin libertinus, "pertaining to a freedman; a freedman," itself from libertus, "freedman," built on liber, "free." The deep semantic history is the migration of the idea of freedom from social status to thought to conduct. In Rome, a libertinus was simply a man who had been freed from slavery — the word was neutral, social, legal. When the word passed into French and English in the sixteenth century, it was first applied to religious freethinkers — those who claimed liberty of conscience against the church. But freedom of thought, in the suspicious eyes of the age, readily became freedom of conduct: the freethinker who questioned God was assumed to question morals too. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, libertine had slid decisively from "freethinker" to "dissolute man," the semantic bridge being the ancient fear that liberty and license are the same thing. The modern word thus preserves, compressed, a whole debate about the danger of freedom.

Grammar and collocations

Libertine is a countable noun ("a notorious libertine," "the libertines of the court") and also an attributive adjective ("libertine philosophy"). It is masculine in association and register; the feminine is rare. Common collocations: "notorious libertine," "libertine philosophy," "life of a libertine," "libertine excess," "a confirmed libertine," "libertine habits." The derived nouns libertinism and (French) libertinage name the doctrine or practice of the libertine. The register is literary and historical, with a distinctly condemnatory colour: unlike "rake" or "rogue," which may carry a trace of charm or affection, libertine is grave, final, and moralistic. In the given context, "a future libertine" is the judgment that the boy, left unguided, will become a man who has converted his liberty into a licence for depravity — the word uniting social fear, moral reproach, and the ancient question of what freedom is for.
