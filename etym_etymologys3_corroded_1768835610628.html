corroded - the biography of a word of gnawing. The story begins with teeth. The PIE root *red- signified the oral violence of small animals: "to gnaw, to scrape, to chew" - the verb of the rodent at the beam, the tooth at the gristle. The Indo-Iranian branch preserved it with remarkable fidelity: Sanskrit radati, "he bites, he gnaws", with the noun rada, "tooth", the very instrument of gnawing, and radana, "the act of gnawing". Latin received the root as rōdere, "to gnaw", and built from it a family of compounds that would come to dominate European scientific vocabulary: corrōdere, "to gnaw away entirely" (with the intensive prefix com-, "wholly, together"), ērōdere, "to gnaw out, away", and the present participle rōdēns, "gnawing". From corrōdere the path runs through Old French corroder into Middle English corroden in the fourteenth century. The earliest English senses are strictly literal and organic: of worms gnawing wood, ulcers eating flesh, sharp substances wearing away material. The original object of corrosion was a living or once-living body - the corrosion of the flesh by disease, the corrosion of timber by worm and rot - and the word carried the small, patient, creeping quality of living gnawers. This is the semantic seed of everything that follows: corrosion is damage done slowly, from the surface inward, by an agent with the persistence of teeth. With the chemical revolution of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word's centre of gravity shifted decisively from teeth to acids. Corrosion became the technical name for the slow chemical eating of metals - rust and verdigris its visible signatures - and the word gained a scientific, quasi-forensic authority. Robert Boyle and the early chemists speak of corrosives, corrosive liquors, the corrosion of iron by acid, and the sense hardened into the modern metallurgical core: the oxidative or acidic destruction of a material by its environment. The metaphor of the gnawer did not disappear; it was re-theorized. Chemistry explained what the metaphor had always claimed: acids and rust "eat" metal exactly as a rodent eats a beam, slowly, from the surface inward, consuming mass and leaving the object pitted, darkened, and smaller. The moral metaphor accompanied the chemical sense throughout. Latin poetry had imagined curae rodunt - "cares gnaw" - the anxieties that eat the heart from within, and English preserved the image: "corroding cares", "corroding envy", the passions that gnaw the mind as rust gnaws iron. The seventeenth-century translators and divines loved the word for exactly this double force: corrosion was at once the rusting of metal and the wasting of the soul, an image that fused chemistry and conscience. Modern English keeps the metaphor alive in "corroded trust", "corroded hopes" - institutions and feelings eaten away from within by slow, invisible agents. Two collateral branches deserve notice. Erosion, from Latin ērōdere ("to gnaw out"), was taken up in the nineteenth century as the geological term for the gnawing of wind and water - the landscape eaten as a rat eats a beam, though the geological sense split off from the chemical one and never carries the same dark intensity. And rodent, from rōdēns, "gnawing", became in the 1820s the zoological name of the gnawing order itself - the Latin participle, Latinized as Rodentia, the order of beavers, rats, and mice, the very animals whose teeth gave the whole family its meaning. To these must be added the probable Germanic member: rat (Old English ræt) has traditionally been explained as "the gnawer", from the same root; the etymology is still widely cited, though modern scholarship regards the origin of rat as uncertain - a probable but disputed branch, and an instructive example of how even a confident family-tree has its loose threads. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries corroded had settled into its modern shape: a darkly entropic word, naming the slow consumption of matter by acid, rust, or time itself. In gothic literary use it carries full mineral devastation - Peake's skull "dark and small like a corroded musket bullet" - an object smaller, blacker, and rougher than it was, the invisible teeth of chemistry working from the surface inward across decades. The word has completed its journey from the rodent's mouth to the chemist's flask to the gothic imagination, and at every step it has meant the same thing: the patient, tooth-like destruction that works from the outside in and leaves its victim diminished.
