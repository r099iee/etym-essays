The verb ANOINTS preserves an ancient Indo-European ritual and cosmetic heritage, evolving from a Proto-Indo-European root for unctuous fat into the supreme liturgical act of sacred kingship and the everyday ritual of personal grooming.

Proto-Indo-European Roots of Oil and Unguent:

  • The deepest ancestor is reconstructed as Proto-Indo-European *h₃engʷ- (or *h₃n̥gʷ-), meaning 'to smear, rub with fat, oil, or salve.'
  • This root produced cognates across the Indo-European spectrum associated with butter, oil, and unction, including Sanskrit anakti ('smears, anoints'), Old Irish imb ('butter'), Old High German ancho ('butter'), and Latin unguen ('fat, grease, ointment').

Latin Development: *Unguere* to *Inungere*:

  • In classical Latin, the root crystallized into the third-conjugation verb unguere (also written ungere), meaning 'to smear, rub with oil, or perfume.' Roman culture made lavish use of unctions in the public baths (thermae), athletic palaestras, and funeral rites.
  • Prefixing the locative preposition in- ('in, upon, onto') yielded inungere, meaning 'to rub ointment or salve into or upon a specific surface or body part.'
  • In Christian Late Latin (4th–5th centuries CE), the Vulgate translation of the Bible extensively deployed ungere and inungere to translate Hebrew *mashach* and Greek *chriein*, embedding the verb at the core of sacramental theology (the anointing of priests, prophets, and kings, as well as the sacrament of Extreme Unction for the dying).

Old French and the Formation of *Enoindre / Enoint*:

  • During the transition into Old French, Latin inungere developed regularly into the verb enoindre (with regular nasalization and vowel shift).
  • The past participle form was enoint ('anointed'). Because the participle was so ubiquitously repeated in liturgy, homilies, and coronation ceremonies, speakers reanalyzed the participle stem as a new primary verbal base.

Middle English Adoption:

  • Middle English borrowed the Old French past participle around 1300 as anointen (re-prefixing with Anglo-Norman a- / en-). Early Middle English texts (such as the *Cursor Mundi* and Wicliffe Bible) used anoynt to describe Christ, David, and holy vessels.
  • The term rapidly replaced native Old English smierwan (the ancestor of modern English smear) for high-register sacred unctions, leaving smear to occupy the low-register, messy physical sense.

Early Modern English to Victorian Grooming:

  • By the 16th and 17th centuries, the inflected third-person form anoints was thoroughly integrated into the King James Bible (e.g., Psalm 23:5, 'Thou anointest my head with oil; my cup runneth over') and Elizabethan drama.
  • In the 19th century, the rise of commercial perfumery and barbershop culture saw anoint applied with elegant, slightly elevated domestic phrasing to gentlemen's hair tonics. In Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle (1892), Sherlock Holmes notes that Henry Baker 'anoints with lime-cream' his grizzled hair. The choice of anoints rather than simply 'puts' or 'rubs' highlights the deliberate, ritualistic daily care that a Victorian gentleman bestowed upon his appearance.
  • In contemporary usage, the third-person present anoints thrives both in religious ceremonies and as the dominant journalistic metaphor for political and corporate succession.
