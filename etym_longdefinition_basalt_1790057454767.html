basalt is a dark, dense, fine-grained volcanic rock formed when low-silica lava cools quickly at or near the Earth’s surface — the flooring of oceans, the stuff of lava plains, columnar cliffs, and volcanic islands: basalt columns, basalt cliffs, basaltic lava, the basalts of the Deccan. In your sentence — “an island raised basalt bulwarks against the ocean” — the word is used attributively and scenically: the island’s sea-walls are not built masonry but basalt itself, black volcanic ramparts shouldering back the surf, with all the connotations the rock carries: hardness, darkness, vertical jointing, salt-battered endurance. Pronounced /bæsɔːlt/ (RP) or /bəˈsɔːlt/, often attributive: basalt island, basalt shore, basalt black.

Geologically basalt is the most abundant volcanic rock on Earth and the defining rock of oceanic crust. Chemically it is mafic — rich in magnesium and iron, poor in silica (roughly 45–52% SiO2) — built of microscopic plagioclase feldspar, pyroxene, and olivine that crystallise too fast to grow large, giving the rock its dark grey-to-black colour and its toughness. Erupted fluidly at mid-ocean ridges, hotspots (Hawaii, Iceland, the Deccan and Siberian Traps), and rift valleys, it spreads as pahoehoe and aa flows, pillow lavas underwater, and vast flood plateaus; contracted on cooling, it famously splits into columnar jointing — the organ-pipe hexagons of the Giant’s Causeway, Fingal’s Cave, Devil’s Tower’s phonolite cousin, and Reynisfjara’s black cliffs. The semantic bridge to every secondary sense is this visual signature: black + hard + hexagonal + sea-cliff.

  • Petrological core sense (primary): extrusive mafic igneous rock; fine-grained equivalent of intrusive gabbro; divisions include tholeiitic basalt (mid-ocean ridges), alkali basalt (ocean islands), olivine basalt, flood basalt. Collocations: basalt flow, basalt plateau, basaltic magma, vesicular basalt (pocked with gas bubbles), pillow basalt (underwater lobes). Opposite in the volcanic spectrum: pale silica-rich rhyolite. Register scientific-technical; field geologists test it with hammer ring, colour, and density.
  • Landform and scenic sense (your context): basalt cliffs, basalt columns, basalt stacks, basalt bulwarks, a basalt island. Here the rock names the architecture it weathers into: vertical jointed walls that read as fortifications. Travel and adventure writing loves it — black basalt ramparts, basalt cathedrals, basalt organ-pipes — because columnar basalt genuinely looks built: nature’s masonry. Your island’s basalt bulwarks belong here: cliffs doing a fortress’s work against the ocean.
  • Material and building-stone sense: crushed basalt aggregate for roads and railway ballast; cut basalt setts and kerbs; ancient Egyptian and Aztec basalt sculpture; modern basalt fibre reinforcement as an alternative to glass fibre. Implies hardness, durability, and dark good looks. Bluestone in Australia and whinstone in northern England are quarrymen’s names for the same family. Bridge: abundance plus toughness makes it the workhorse stone wherever volcanoes have passed.
  • Colour and attributive sense: basalt black, basalt grey in design, fashion, and car paint — a blue-black darkness with stony matte connotations. Wedgwood basalt ware (basaltes), the black vitrified stoneware Josiah Wedgwood perfected in the 1760s, borrows the name for prestige hardness. Adjective basaltic does most scientific attributive work (basaltic plain); basalt itself stays noun-modifier in popular prose (basalt shore).
  • Planetary and figurative extensions: (a) Lunar maria, Martian basalts, Venusian basalt plains — the dark flat seas of the Moon and the plains of Mars are basaltic lavas, so basalt becomes the default rock of other worlds. (b) Figurative hardness: a basalt resolve, basalt convictions, a face of basalt — rare literary metaphor for obduracy, built on hardness plus darkness. (c) Archaeology: basalt grinding stones, basalt axe-heads, the basalt stele (Hammurabi’s code is diorite; many Levantine monuments are basalt) — the stone that kept edges and inscriptions.
  • Usage notes: countable in geology (the basalts of Iceland = basaltic formations) but usually mass noun (built of basalt). Keep apart: basalt (fine-grained lava) vs gabbro/dolerite (same magma cooled slowly underground, coarse-grained) vs obsidian (same family cooled instantly to glass). Your sentence uses it exactly as 19th-century voyage literature does: the exotic island announced by its geology before its people.

In short, basalt is the ocean’s own stone — black lava frozen fast — and your island raises it the way a fortress raises walls: dark, jointed, and indifferent to the sea that made it visible.
