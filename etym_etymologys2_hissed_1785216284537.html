Imitative (echoic) root; no recoverable PIE etymon — the word is onomatopoeic from birth.
1. Prehistoric echoic sound-image — forced breath through nearly closed teeth, imitating the serpent's noise; no attested form survives.
2. Proto-Germanic *hissōną (reconstructed, imitative) "to hiss" — 1st millennium CE; cf. Old High German hissōn "to hiss", Dutch hissen.
3. Middle English hissen "to hiss" (of serpents and of sibilant speech) — late 14th century.
4. Early Modern English hiss / hisse "to hiss; to deride by hissing" — 16th–17th centuries (the theatre's hiss of contempt).
5. Modern English hiss "to emit a sharp sibilant sound" + past-tense suffix -ed.
6. hissed "made a sharp, sibilant sound" — Modern English.
Note on debate: hiss is classed as onomatopoeic/echoic in origin; no Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be securely reconstructed, and speculative links to PIE roots for "to gape" or "to yell" are not accepted by the OED.
