Avignon is a historic city in southeastern France, standing on the left bank of the Rhône near its junction with the Durance, and known since the fourteenth century as the Cité des Papes, the City of the Popes. In the biographical context that anchors this card — John Stuart Mill's Autobiography, recounting the death of his wife Harriet Taylor Mill in 1858 "after seven years of happy companionship" — Avignon names the place of her death and burial: the St. Véran cemetery where she lies, and the city where Mill bought a house overlooking her grave so that he might spend the remainder of his life near her resting place, returning every year on the anniversary of her death. The word on this card is thus not a common noun but a proper noun — a place-name with a rich cultural, historical, and personal weight. Core senses and associations of the name:
  • The city itself: an ancient settlement on the Rhône in the Vaucluse department of Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur, today a city of some 90,000 inhabitants, famous for its medieval walls, its Palais des Papes, and its bridges.
  • The Avignon Papacy (1309–1377): the period when seven successive popes, beginning with Clement V, held their court at Avignon rather than Rome — the era of the "Babylonian captivity of the Church," which the name evokes in papal and church history.
  • The bridge and the song: the Pont Saint-Bénézet, the "Pont d'Avignon" of the famous children's song "Sur le pont d'Avignon," of which only four arches remain standing; the name is inseparable in popular memory from the tune.
  • The biographical association (this card): the place where Harriet Taylor Mill died (November 3, 1858) and was buried, and where John Stuart Mill lived out his remaining years beside her grave — the melancholy, personal Avignon of the Mill biography.
  • Adjectival and derived forms: Avignonese and Avignonnais (inhabitant or adjective), and the derived French form used in phrases such as "the Avignonese papacy."
Historical development:
  • The site is ancient: a settlement of the Cavares, a Celto-Ligurian people, stood here on a marshy reach of the Rhône before the Roman conquest, under the name Avennio.
  • Roman administration Latinized the name as Avenniō and made the town a flourishing river port and later a civitas (self-governing city).
  • In the Middle Ages Avignon became a papal possession, and between 1309 and 1377 it was the seat of the papacy; the Palais des Papes built in those years still dominates the city.
  • It remained a papal enclave within France until the French Revolution (annexed in 1791), after which it became a French city.
  • In the nineteenth century the city entered English literary awareness through Mill's Autobiography and other Victorian writings as the place of Harriet Taylor Mill's death and burial.
Register and usage notes:
  • As a proper noun, Avignon has no synonyms, but it is commonly grouped in histories and travel writing with its Rhône and Provençal neighbours — Arles, Nîmes, Aix-en-Provence, Marseille.
  • In English the stress falls on the first syllable (AV-in-yon), unlike the French stress on the last (a-vee-NYON); the pronunciation given in this card follows the anglicized reading commonly used in Victorian and modern English prose.
  • The name functions in discourse as a cultural signifier: "Avignon papacy" is a fixed historical term; "the Avignon" alone is never used — the definite article is not part of the English name.
Etymological note: the name preserves a pre-Roman hydronym built on the ancient water-root *ab-/*avan- ("river, water, marsh"), the same element that appears in Welsh afon and English river-name Avon. It thus means, in effect, "the town at the water.
