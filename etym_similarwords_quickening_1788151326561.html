• Acceleration: similar in the modern sense (a process speeding up); differs in being precise and technical-neutral, while quickening is warmer and still carries its life-giving twin meaning.
  • Hastening: similar (making something happen sooner or faster); differs in implying urgency and human effort, while quickening suggests a natural surge.
  • Revival: similar in the “coming back to life” cluster; differs in normally following decline or death and in applying to events, movements, or practices; quickening is a process of new life or increased activity, not a restoration.
  • Animation: similar (giving life or liveliness); differs because modern animation primarily means cartoon film or general liveliness, while quickening keeps a directional, physiological force.
  • Enlivening: similar (making lively); differs in being deliberately applied to moods and spirits, while quickening may be literal (fetal) and accelerative as well.
  • Stimulation: similar (rousing into activity); differs in being often external and technical (economics, physiology), while quickening suggests an inner surge or quick motion.
  • Arousal: similar (waking responses); differs in being strongly psychosexual or physiological, while quickening is gentler, broader, and not specialised.
  • Speed-up / speeding-up: similar (colloquial acceleration); differs in being informal and mechanical, entirely without the “living” overtone.
  • Quickening of the fetus (historical): not a synonym of the others but the sense that anchors the word's legal-medical history — first felt movements of the unborn child.
