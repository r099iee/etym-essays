The stumpy harbour guardian bollard grows, fittingly, out of a tree-trunk.

Bole: the trunk that stayed put. Middle English bole, the stem or trunk of a tree, descends from Old Norse bolr or its close Germanic kin, all naming the solid cylindrical stock left when branches are lopped. It belonged to carpenters and shipwrights, for whom a bole was raw material at its most honest: thick, round, and immovable. To call a deck-post a bolard in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries was therefore to praise its trunk-like virtue — contemptuous -ard and all. The suffix, familiar from drunkard, dullard, and sluggard, here does not insult but intensifies: a bol-ard is emphatically a chunk of bole, squat and strong, the nautical equivalent of calling a man a Briton of oak.

Aboard ship (16th–18th c.). The earliest sea-uses name upright timbers on deck to which sheets and mooring hawsers are belayed — the ancestors of modern bitts. A ship built of oak naturally furnished itself in oak: spare trunk-sections, knee-timbers, and Samson-posts became rope-holders by the simple virtue of standing fast while tons of canvas pulled. Sailors’ speech kept the word aboard for two centuries before the shore borrowed it back.

  • The quay adopts it (1840s): iron replaces timber as ports rebuild for steam and screw. Foundries cast mushroom-headed iron bollards by the thousand; engineers’ specifications and dock Acts fix bollard as the official name. The first dictionary trail in the harbour sense dates to 1844, exactly the railway-and-steamer moment.
  • Paint and light (Victorian): whitewash makes iron visible on black nights and wet stone — the source sentence’s glimmering white row under twin lamps is period-perfect. Gas, then electric lamps on high posts turn working edges into legible ones.
  • Cannon-bollard lore (19th–20th c.): old cannon, buried muzzle-down, serve worldwide as ready-made bollards, lending folk support to the trunk-theory: any stout cylinder, gun or bole, will hold a ship if planted deep.
  • Streets and security (20th–21st c.): the motor car gives the bollard a second career. Traffic bollards save pavements; illuminated bollards guide; crash-rated steel cores, tested against lorries, guard crowds. The rope has gone, but the virtue — rooted mass — is unchanged.

Sound and spelling: the double ll and blunt -ard thump like the thing itself: short, thick, and final. The plural -s almost always accompanies it, because bollards work in rows — one bollard is an accident, a row is a berth.

A debated tail: scholars agree on bole but argue over -ard, weighing the native intensifying suffix against possible Low German or Scandinavian parallels. The debate changes nothing aboard or ashore: whatever its tail, the bollard holds fast, white in lamplight, while water moves and ropes strain.
