The etymological biography of smacking offers one of the most intriguing demonstrations in Germanic lexicography of how two distinct etymological rivers—one rooted in ancient gustatory perception and the other in acoustic onomatopoeia—flowed together to create a singularly dynamic, polysemic Modern English word.

The primary semantic branch traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)magʰ- or *(s)mak-, which was sound-symbolic of oral smacking, tasting, and savoring. In the Proto-Germanic period (c. 500 BCE), this root developed into the noun *smakkuz ("taste, flavour, scent") and the causative-factitive verb *smakkijaną ("to taste, try the flavour of"). This ancient lineage spread widely across the Germanic languages: Old High German produced smac and smacken (whence Modern German Schmack and schmecken, "to taste"), Middle Dutch had smaec / smaken, and Old English developed the noun smæc ("taste, flavour, savor, scent") and the weak Class I verb smæccan ("to taste, savour").

In Anglo-Saxon England, smæc was an exclusively gustatory and olfactory term. In theological and medical Old English texts, to have a smæc of something meant to carry its lingering flavor or scent. This usage endured continuously into Middle English, where the construction "to smacken of" acquired its lasting figurative meaning: to exhibit the characteristic flavor, quality, or ideological taint of something (e.g., a statement smacking of heresy or pride).

Simultaneously, during the 13th and 14th centuries, expanding maritime trade between English ports and the Low Countries (the Hanseatic League and Flemish merchants) introduced a vibrant Low German and Middle Dutch verb: smacken. This word was an expressive, imitative formation created to mimic the sharp, acoustic report of lips suddenly parting, an open hand slapping down, or a body hitting a surface. Unlike the old native Old English verb smæccan, this North Sea coastal loanword was purely acoustic and kinetic.

By the late 14th and 15th centuries in Middle English, these two distinct lexical streams merged seamlessly due to their identical phonetic shape. English speakers began using smacken to signify both the taste-based metaphorical nuance and the visceral physical blow. In the works of Geoffrey Chaucer and medieval mystery plays, the word was used for both savoring wine and planting a loud, affectionate kiss upon someone's cheek.

During the Elizabethan and Early Modern English eras, William Shakespeare and his contemporaries exploited both dimensions of the word with brilliant theatrical versatility. In Love's Labour's Lost and Measure for Measure, Shakespeare used smack to describe both the subtle flavor of wit and the physical slap of chastisement. By the 18th century, nautical English adopted the participial adjective smacking to describe a stiff, invigorating wind that slapped the canvas sails ("a smacking breeze"), which rapidly broadened into general idiom to mean vigorous, brisk, and lively ("a smacking pace").

In modern literature, including the vivid descriptive prose of J.R.R. Tolkien, smacking retains all of its kinetic vigor. When Tolkien describes the barrels carrying the dwarves "smacking into the water," the word evokes both the sharp acoustic crack against the river's surface tension and the dynamic, splashing velocity of their escape, uniting centuries of physical and auditory evolution in a single stroke.
