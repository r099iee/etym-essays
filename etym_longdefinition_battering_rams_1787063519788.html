battering-rams (noun, plural) — massive siege engines: long, heavy beams, often tipped with an iron, bronze, or stone head carved like a ram's head, that were swung or pushed repeatedly against a city gate or wall to smash a breach. In the Hobbit passage, a blow against the Mountain arrives 'like the crash of battering-rams made of forest oaks and swung by giants' — the image fuses the historical weapon with a superhuman, mythic scale.

  • 1. The siege engine (primary, historical sense): a beam of timber — frequently an entire oak trunk — sometimes slung in a frame on rollers or ropes and swung or rammed end-first into gates, doors, and masonry. The Greek krios and Roman aries both mean 'ram', because the engine's head was shaped or carved like a butting ram; the animal's habit of hammering with its skull is the model for the whole device.
  • 2. Figurative sense: any force or thing that batters down resistance — an argument, a demand, a wave, a law, or an army 'used as a battering-ram' to break through opposition. This is common in journalism and metaphor (e.g. 'use the courts as a battering-ram against the industry').
  • 3. Morphology: a compound noun, battering (present participle of batter, 'to strike repeatedly') + ram (the animal and the engine). The hyphen is standard British usage; the plural marks the plural of the head noun ram.
  • 4. Register: primarily historical and military vocabulary, vivid and concrete; in figurative use, emphatic and forceful. The plural in the source sentence is hyperbolic — not a literal army of engines but the imagined sound of their crashes.

Historical note: battering rams appear in the ancient Near East (Assyrian reliefs of the 9th-8th centuries BCE), in Greek siegecraft, and throughout Roman warfare, where aries was the standard term; they remained central to medieval sieges. They could be armoured, roofed, and hung on suspensions to gain momentum, and they declined only with gunpowder artillery in the 15th-16th centuries.

How the machine worked: the classic ram was a single massive tree trunk, sometimes thirty feet or more in length, with a reinforced metal or stone head shaped like a ram's brow. It was either carried and swung by teams of men or, more effectively, suspended from a frame on ropes or chains so that it could be pulled back and let fly at the target in a pendulum stroke; a roofed gallery (testudo, or 'tortoise') sheltered the crew from missiles and fire. Its power came from mass and repetition rather than speed: each swing concentrated the trunk's whole weight into one small point of the wall, and repeated blows would crack masonry, split timbers, and finally knock a breach through which infantry could pour. Against mud-brick or wooden gates the effect was devastating, which is why the ram was both the most feared and the most carefully counter-attacked of siege engines.

Why 'ram'?: the engine is named for the male sheep, the one animal that attacks by lowering its head and smashing its skull into a barrier. Ancient engineers saw the same motion in the beam, and the Greek krios and Latin aries — both meaning 'ram' — show the metaphor was already fixed in classical antiquity; the same image spread through every later European language, so that the English compound battering-ram is a literal translation of a two-thousand-year-old idea.

Modern and figurative life: outside history books, the plural is now most often metaphorical. A 'battering-ram' may be a legal judgment, a press campaign, a technological innovation, or a sports team's forward line — anything that steadily and irresistibly breaks through resistance. In that use the word keeps its physical core: it is never a gentle instrument, and to call something a battering-ram is to say it wins not by surprise or finesse but by sheer accumulated force against a solid obstacle.
