Part of speech and core sense. Historiography is a mass noun formed from historio- (history) and -graphy (writing). Its core sense is “the writing of history,” and from that flow two specialised senses that dominate modern academic use: the study of how history is written, and the body of historical writing on a given subject.

In the source sentence the word appears in the phrase “highly unorthodox within historiography.” The reference is to the professional discipline and its conventions—detached third-person narration, past tense, documented sources, a measured tone. Carlyle's present-tense, first-person-plural history violates those conventions, and the criticism is therefore made in the name of historiography as a craft with rules.

Primary modern senses

  • The writing of history as an activity. The oldest English sense: the composition of historical narrative, considered as a literary and scholarly practice. “The historiography of the Middle Ages” can mean the way medieval writers wrote history.
  • The study of historical writing. The modern academic sense, established in the nineteenth century: the branch of study concerned with the methods, assumptions, sources, institutions and ideologies of historians. A course in historiography examines how histories have been produced rather than what happened.
  • A body of historical scholarship. The accumulated literature on a topic, together with its schools and disputes: “the historiography of the French Revolution” means the successive interpretations from Michelet and Carlyle through Lefebvre and Furet, not the Revolution itself.
  • Official or ideologically directed history. Court historiography, imperial historiography, Soviet historiography, nationalist historiography: history written under the patronage or censorship of a state, and shaped by its legitimating needs.
  • The style or narrative method of a particular historian. A rarer, evaluative use: “Carlyle's historiography” denotes the distinctive manner in which he constructs and voices his history.

Related terms and distinctions. A historiographer was formerly any historian; since the seventeenth century the title has usually meant an official historian attached to a court, city or institution (the office of Historiographer Royal still exists). Historiographical is the adjective, common in phrases such as historiographical debate and historiographical tradition. Historiology is a rare synonym for the science of history. Metahistory, popularised by Hayden White's book of 1973, denotes the study of the deep narrative structures underlying historical writing.

The crucial distinction is between history and historiography. History is the past and, by extension, the study of it; historiography is writing about the past and, reflexively, the study of that writing. A historian asks what happened and why; a historiographer asks how historians have answered those questions, with what evidence, assumptions and rhetorical strategies. Historical method is the practical technique of source criticism; philosophy of history is speculation about pattern, meaning and direction in the past. Historiography sits between them, empirical in material and theoretical in orientation.

Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge. Greek historia began as “inquiry” or “the finding out” of facts by a witness; only later did it mean the written account, and later still the events themselves. Graphia supplied the writing. The compound therefore literally means “the writing of inquiry,” and its oldest sense keeps that emphasis on research and narration rather than on the past as such. When history became a university discipline in the nineteenth century, a word was needed for the critical study of the discipline's own output; historiography was available and was re-tooled for the purpose. The modern sense is thus a Victorian specialisation of a Renaissance borrowing.

Register, connotation and collocation. The word is academic and formal. It rarely appears in speech outside departments, and it signals a meta-level: to discuss historiography is to discuss historians rather than events. Standard collocations include historiographical debate, historiographical tradition, Whig historiography, Marxist historiography, revisionist historiography, Annales historiography, postcolonial historiography, the historiography of the Holocaust, and a historiographical essay. In looser use it can mean simply “the literature on a subject,” especially in introductions to dissertations.

In this reading. The charge that Carlyle is “unorthodox within historiography” presumes a norm: neutral, detached, evidence-led prose in the tradition of Gibbon. Carlyle's participatory present tense is unorthodox precisely because it refuses the historian's conventional invisibility, and the word historiography allows the critic to make that refusal a professional matter rather than a mere matter of taste.
