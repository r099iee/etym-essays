This note has two root words, mer- and people, so each is given its own complete linear chain. === CHAIN 1 OF 2: the element mer- (sea) === 1. Proto-Indo-European *móri- (3rd–4th millennium BCE) — "a body of water; sea, lake." A well-attested root shared by the Italic, Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic branches (Latin mare "sea"; Old Irish muir; Old Church Slavonic morje; Lithuanian marios). It did not survive in Greek or Indo-Iranian. 2. Proto-Germanic *mari- (c. 500 BCE–1st century CE) — "sea, lake." The Germanic outcome of *móri-. 3. Old English mere (attested before the 9th century) — "sea, lake, pool of water." The word covered both the open sea and standing water. 4. Middle English mere (12th–15th centuries) — "lake, pool, fen." As the word sea took over the "ocean" senses, mere narrowed to the domestic "lake, pool," surviving in the modern poetic archaism and in place-names such as Windermere, Grasmere, and Buttermere. 5. Old English compound mere + mægden, "sea-maiden" → mermaid, and mere + man → merman (Old English period; mermaid is attested from the Old English glosses, c. 11th century). From these compounds the reduced combining form mer- ("sea") developed. 6. Modern English coinage mer- + people (20th century; the neutral singular merperson is a back-formation from mermaid/merman, and merpeople is its plural) — "the sea-people, the race of mermaids and mermen" — the exact rootWord of this card: merpeople. === CHAIN 2 OF 2: the element people === 1. Latin populus, populum (attested from the Republican period, 3rd century BCE) — "a people, a nation, the body of citizens; the populace." Origin uncertain: no secure Proto-Indo-European etymology is established. Some scholars have compared it with the PIE root *pleh₁-, "to fill" (cf. Latin plēnus, "full"), reading populus as "the full one, the throng"; others have proposed an Etruscan or substrate origin. The etymology is genuinely debated, and the chain therefore begins with Latin as the earliest securely attested stage. 2. Vulgar Latin / early Romance populum (late 1st millennium CE) — the accusative form underwent regular sound change to give Old French pueple / pople. 3. Old French pueple / people (9th–12th centuries) — "a people, persons, folk." By regular French sound change, Latin populum > pueple, later people. 4. Anglo-Norman / Middle English people / peple (12th century; Middle English peple is recorded from c. 1200) — "a people, persons, inhabitants." 5. Early Modern English people (16th century onward) — "people, persons, a nation, the public." In English the word never took a regular plural (persons is a separate formation); people is itself both singular collective and plural. 6. Modern English compound: mer- + people → merpeople (20th century) — the exact rootWord of this card. Note on nuance/debate: the element mer- has an entirely secure PIE chain (*móri- > *mari- > mere > mer-). The element people is secure from Latin populus onward, but the origin of populus itself is unknown and debated—the *pleh₁- "to fill" comparison and the Etruscan hypothesis are both reported as possibilities, and the stage before Latin must be stated as uncertain rather than invented.
