PIE *h₁ey- "to go" (the ancestral root of motion; also in Greek iénai "to go", Latin īre "to go", and Sanskrit éti "he goes") → Proto-Hellenic *iyā-/*iā- "to go" → Mycenaean Greek (Linear B, c. 1400–1200 BC) i-ja-wo-ne "Iāwones, the Ionians", the earliest attested Greek name of the group, already an ethnic plural meaning "the going ones, the wanderers" → Ancient Greek Iāones (Ἰάονες), the poetic and Ionic form found in Homer (Iliad 13.685), alongside the contracted Īōnes (Ἴωνες) "the Ionians"; singular Iāōn (Ἰάων) and later Iōn (Ἴων); the personal name Iōn was then back-formed as the eponymous ancestor of the tribe (son of Apollo and Creusa), although the hero is a secondary invention built on the tribal name → Latin Iōnēs "the Ionians" (also Iōnii), adjective Iōnicus "Ionic" (the dialect and the architectural order), 1st c. BC onward → English Ionians (plural), from the 16th century, naming the ancient Greek tribe and, by extension, the people of Ionia and its intellectual and architectural legacy. Note on the debate: the traditional derivation of the ethnonym from the Indo-European root *h₁ey- "to go" (hence "the travellers, the roamers") is the standard account, but because the name is extremely old (attested already in Mycenaean), some scholars allow an Anatolian or substrate influence in the specific suffix. The core chain — a motion root yielding an ethnic name meaning "those who go/wander" — remains secure, while the later connection to the hero Ion and to the folk-etymology from íon "violet" is rejected by modern scholarship as legend.
