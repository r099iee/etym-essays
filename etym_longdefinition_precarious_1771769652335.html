Closing the long labour of the Decline and Fall, Gibbon feels pride give way to "sober melancholy": whatever the future fame of the History, "the life of the historian must be short and precarious." Here precarious is existential rather than merely physical. It names a life held as if on sufferance — dependent on health, time, and forces no author commands. The word's legal ancestry (tenure by another's will) deepens the pathos: the historian's remaining years are not freehold property but a grant that may be revoked. The tone is chastened and lucid, not melodramatic; Gibbon lowers the triumph of completion into an awareness of mortal contingency.

Core meaning: not securely held or in position; dangerously likely to fall, collapse, or be lost; dependent on chance, circumstances, or another's will; uncertain and at risk.

Primary senses:
  • Physical insecurity — not securely held or balanced: "a precarious position," "a precarious ladder," "a precarious foothold on the cliff."
  • Existential or material insecurity — dependent on circumstances beyond one's control: "a precarious living," "precarious health," "precarious peace."
  • Uncertainty and risk — likely to fail or collapse: "a precarious ceasefire," "the precarious stability of the regime."
  • Legal-historical sense — held by favour or at the will of another, revocable: "precarious tenure" (tenancy granted at the pleasure of the lord or grantor).

Secondary and extended uses:
  • Precarious employment / work / workers (modern jargon) — a technical term in labour sociology for jobs lacking security, protection, and predictability; the related noun "precarity" describes the condition of contingent, flexible, or gig labour. Here the older legal sense of revocability is reactivated: such work is held at the pleasure of an employer or platform.
  • International relations — "precarious peace," "precarious balance of power," "precarious truce": situations maintained only by fragile equilibrium, always liable to break.
  • Medicine and biography — "precarious health" describes a condition hanging in the balance, capable of sudden deterioration.
  • Figurative literary use — any state "held on sufferance": reputations, fortunes, alliances, and lives may all be precarious, as in Gibbon's "the life of the historian must be short and precarious."

Historical semantic bridge: the word comes from Latin precārius, "obtained by entreaty or prayer," from precārī "to pray, entreat." What one holds by asking, one holds by the goodwill of another — hence the Roman legal sense of property or privilege held precario, at the pleasure of the grantor, revocable without the security of ownership. From "held by favour" the meaning widened to "dependent on chance or will," and then to "dangerously unsteady." The bridge is therefore: prayer → favour → revocability → insecurity → physical fragility. Gibbon's "short and precarious" couples mortality's brevity with life's hold-by-permission quality — closer to precarious tenure than to mere danger.

Collocations and register: precarious position / balance / living / livelihood / health / peace / hold / existence / employment. Register is standard educated English, at home in historiography, journalism, labour sociology, and everyday cautionary speech. In legal-historical writing the older sense (held by entreaty or at will) still surfaces; in modern use the prayerful origin is usually forgotten, but "precarious employment" retains the idea of conditions that can be withdrawn. Antonymic foils include secure, stable, safe, certain, and firm.
