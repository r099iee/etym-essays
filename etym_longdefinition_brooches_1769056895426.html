brooches (noun, plural) — ornamental fasteners: pieces of jewellery pinned to clothing by means of a pointed pin and a catch, usually of precious metal and often set with gems. In the treasure chamber of Cair Paravel in The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, the brooches sit on the dust-covered shelves "in between the suits of armor," listed between "long tusks of ivory" and "coronets and chains of gold." Of all the regalia in the list, brooches are the most intimate. They are not symbols of state like coronets, nor bulk treasure like golden bowls — they are the small, personal ornaments by which a queen or noblewoman fastened her cloak or gown at the shoulder, the breast, or the throat. The word therefore strikes a note of private, domestic magnificence among the public grandeur: these are the objects that once touched the wearer, that held a royal garment in place, that someone's fingers fumbled with every morning. In context their loneliness is poignant — the same sense of "forsaken and long ago" that pervades the chamber, but scaled down to the human: jewellery made to be worn, not to be shelved, now gathering dust beside piles of unset stones. Setting brooch against its near-neighbours sharpens its meaning. A pin is the purely functional ancestor — a pointed piece of metal (or a modern safety pin) that fastens cloth without ornament; a brooch is a pin that has been promoted into jewellery. A clasp or clasp fastener is likewise functional, closing a box, bag, or necklace by interlocking parts, with no connotation of being worn decoratively on the garment's surface. A buckle fastens straps and belts through a frame and tongue, serving dress or harness rather than ornament worn for display. A pendant hangs on a chain or cord about the neck — it dangles rather than pierces the cloth, so it occupies a different part of the jewellery anatomy. A medallion is a round, coin-like ornament, often commemorative or award-bearing, worn on a chain or ribbon. A badge signals membership, office, or rank rather than personal adornment. A fibula is the ancient Roman predecessor — a safety-pin brooch that fastened the toga — and a standard archaeological term. The brooch alone combines three features the others lack in combination: it is a clasping mechanism, it is worn visibly on the clothing as an ornament, and it is personal rather than institutional. That personal quality is what makes "brooches" the right word for the queens' former private finery in the Narnian chamber. Collocationally, brooch pairs with metals, gems, and verbs of fastening: "a diamond/silver/pearl brooch," "a cameo brooch," "a Celtic/Scottish brooch," "a brooch clasp," "to pin a brooch (to a dress)," "to fasten/fix one's shawl with a brooch," and, in period style, "a breastpin." The word is pronounced /brəʊtʃ/ (rhyming with "coach") and forms the plural "brooches" /ˈbrəʊtʃɪz/ — a plural that still surprises because the spelling and the sound diverge, a legacy of the word's split from "broach." Its register is descriptive and slightly old-fashioned, at home in costume catalogues, antique-shop windows, Victorian novels, and historical fantasy such as this one. It sits comfortably in an inventory of royal treasure precisely because it denotes the jewellery of the wardrobe rather than of the throne — a reminder that a royal court's richness included the small, personal things as well as the emblems of power. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings — the word family fans out into several distinct modern senses:
  • Ornamental clothing pin — the primary sense: a decorative fastening of metal and gems worn visibly on the breast, lapel, collar, or shoulder.
  • Homonym broach (the spit and the verb) — the once-identical word re-spelled in the 16th–17th centuries: "broach" is a roasting spit or a tool for tapping casks, and "to broach" means to pierce or tap a cask and, figuratively, to open up a subject for discussion; the jewellery and the piercing instrument are now separated on the page but share one ancestry.
  • Engineering / machining: a broach — in modern technical jargon, a cutting tool with a series of teeth used to shape or enlarge holes; the "pointed instrument" sense of the same word, fully modernised.
  • Related French-family words — French brochure (a "stitched" booklet) and brocher ("to stitch, to sew a pamphlet") are distant cousins that descend from the same Old French broche "a spit, a needle."
  • Historical / archaeological usage — "brooch" (and its Latin cousin "fibula") is the standard term for ancient, Celtic, Anglo-Saxon, and Viking dress-fasteners recovered in excavation, where a brooch may be the only object that identifies a burial's date or rank.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge — the word descends from Old French broche "a spit, a pointed rod, a needle," from Vulgar Latin *brocca "a pointed thing," from Latin broccus "projecting, pointed (of teeth)," of uncertain, plausibly Gaulish origin. The bridge from "spit" to "jewellery" is the pin: a brooch is, after all, a small pointed thing with an honourable job — the same point that pierces a roast pierces a dress, and once the pointed instrument was set with gems and worn on the breast, the word for the utensil quietly became the word for the ornament. Every modern sense of the family — the roasting spit, the machinist's broach, the stitched brochure — is a different branch of that single pointed thing.
