The chain for the deepest stages is conjectural and is stated as such. Stage 1 (proposed donor, uncertain): Old Norse / Icelandic flaustra 'to hurry, to bustle' (Icelandic flaustra, noun flaustr 'a flurry, bustle') — a widely cited but not securely proven Scandinavian source; the connection is plausible because the word first appears in English in the 15th century with no Old English ancestor, but the exact Norse form that would have been borrowed is not documented in the surviving Old Norse corpus, and the semantic fit ('hurry, bustle' → 'agitate') requires an inferred shift. Alternative hypotheses: (a) an independent imitative/echoic formation in English; (b) a blend with flutter. No stage before Middle English is securely attested. Stage 2 (attested): Middle English flustren 'to agitate, disturb, make heated, confused, or excited' — first recorded c. 1420, with the early senses strongly coloured by heat and drink ('flustered with drink'). Stage 3: Early Modern English fluster 'to agitate, to make nervously excited; to become agitated' — established by the 16th century, with the noun fluster 'a state of agitation' appearing in the 17th century. Stage 4: past participle / adjective flustered 'agitated, confused, or nervous, typically from hurry or surprise' — recorded from the 17th century onward. Final node: FLUSTERED. Debate note: the OED and most authorities classify the origin of fluster as 'uncertain'; the Scandinavian etymology (Icelandic flaustra) is widely repeated but remains conjectural, and there is no attested English form earlier than c. 1420. The mnemonic folk-analysis 'flutter + bluster' is a memory aid, not an etymology, and must not be presented as the word's history.
