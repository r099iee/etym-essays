CHAIN — single root to English vain: - PIE *h₁weh₂- "to leave, abandon, be empty, forsake" (verbal root). - Proto-Italic *wanos "empty, void, hollow" (adjective; reconstructed). - Latin vānus "empty, void, idle, false, deceptive, ostentatious" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; Plautus, Cicero, Lucretius). - Old French vain / vein "empty, futile, worthless, idle" (attested from the 9th–12th c. CE; also Anglo-Norman vain). - Middle English vein / veyn / vain "futile, worthless; proud" (attested from the 13th c. CE). - Modern English vain "producing no result; futile; excessively proud of one's appearance or worth." NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: The PIE root is reconstructed as *h₁weh₂- (with laryngeal; some handbooks write *weh₂-), and its reflexes include Sanskrit ūná "deficient, wanting," Germanic *wanaz "lacking, deficient" (whence English wane and German Wahn "delusion"), and Greek εὖνις (eûnis) "bereft." The shift from "empty" to "conceited" is internal to Latin (vānus already meant "ostentatious, boastful") and passed unchanged into Old French. Vain must not be confused with vein (from Latin vēna, a blood vessel) or vane (from Old English fana, a flag or blade); the spellings converge but the etymologies are separate.
