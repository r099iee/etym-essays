- c. 1590–1650: Coined in Early Modern English within military warfare and siegecraft (*untenable*), describing a fortress, outpost, or defensive fortification that could not be held or defended against enemy assault.
- c. 1650–1750: Shifted metaphorically from military fortifications to intellectual debates, philosophical disputes, and theological doctrines that could not be defended against logical refutation.
- c. 1750–1900: Solidified across parliamentary politics, diplomacy, and academia to describe unsustainable political positions, untenable ministries, or unmaintainable legal claims.
- c. 1900–Present: Universally used in political journalism, literary criticism (e.g., Roland Barthes describing fleeting, impossible psychological instants), business management, and scientific discourse for any unsustainable, indefensible, or precarious situation.
