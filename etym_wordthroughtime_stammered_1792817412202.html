- Old English — a named affliction: stamerian already describes faltering speech, and stamur names the stammering person — the impediment recognised a thousand years ago.
- Middle English — Chaucer's folk stammer: the Pardoner and the Miller's world know stammering as village reality, comic and pitiable by turns.
- 16th–18th c. — elocution and shame: speech manuals promise cures (pebbles, verses, breathing drills); the stammerer becomes the stock shy lover and the butt of stage comedy alike.
- 19th c. — the sympathetic turn: Dickens and later novelists give stammerers interior lives; medicine begins describing rather than mocking, though quack cures flourish.
- 20th c. — the King's speech: therapy, support groups, and eventually royal example (George VI) remake stammering as a respected condition to be managed, not hidden.
- Today — Bilbo's tag and the clinic side by side: stammered in fiction still signals endearing nerves, while clinicians and advocates defend fluent respect for every disfluent voice.
