- 17th century - Formed in English from “hesitate” (to stick fast, falter) with the negative prefix; early uses describe swift, decisive actions and speech — an answer or blow given without pause.
- 18th–19th century - Used in moral and religious discourse for firm, immediate conviction; Mill's “unhesitating belief” in his wife's intellectual influence is an avowal delivered instantly and completely, with no tremor of reservation.
- 20th century - Broadens in everyday use: unhesitating support, an unhesitating reply — stressing instantaneity of response rather than merely firm resolve.
- Contemporary - Common in journalism and narrative prose; it contrasts with resolute and steadfast (constancy over time) by denying the initial falter itself.
