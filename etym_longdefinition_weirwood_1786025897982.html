WEIRWOOD refers to a legendary species of sacred tree at the heart of George R.R. Martin's fantasy world of A Song of Ice and Fire and its television adaptations (Game of Thrones, House of the Dragon). Anatomically, weirwoods are distinguished by a spectral appearance: bone-white bark, five-pointed blood-red leaves, and deep red sap that weeps like blood. They are virtually immune to rot and survive for thousands of years, and their petrified wood is as hard as stone, making it an extraordinary material for weapons, armor, bows, and sacred architecture.

Linguistically, WEIRWOOD is a compound of two inherited English words: weir (a dam, fence, or sacred barrier) and wood (forest, timber). The compound was coined by Martin in 1996 for A Game of Thrones, yet it grafts perfectly onto ancient Germanic vocabulary of protective enclosures and sacred groves. In the lore of Westeros, the Children of the Forest carved human faces into central weirwoods - the "heart trees" - in sacred groves called godswoods. Through these carved faces, the Old Gods are believed to watch the world, and greenseers and skinchangers can cast their consciousness through the weirwood network across space and time, viewing history through the trees' eyes. The weirwood is thus not a mere botanical specimen but a living historical record, a spiritual sanctuary, and a vessel for collective ancestral memory - as when the trees are described as "imbued with the spirits" of the slain.

Primary Senses

  • Literary / worldbuilding sense: the sacred white-barked tree of Westeros with red sap and carved faces, center of the Old Gods religion.
  • Narrative / magic sense: the connective network of weirwoods through which greenseers see the past, present, and possible futures.
  • Etymological sense: a compound of weir (barrier, fence, protection) + wood (forest), evoking a guarded, sacred grove.

Secondary and Nuanced Senses

  • Historical-mythic resonance: the name deliberately echoes real-world Germanic and Celtic traditions of sacred groves (Greek nemeton, Norse Yggdrasil) where trees were never felled and where blood was offered.
  • Material / craft sense: "weirwood" as a substance - petrified, rot-proof timber used for bows, armor, and the thrones and doors of the North.
  • Metonymic / spiritual sense: "the weirwoods" as the eyes of the Old Gods; the trees themselves are the gods' watchers and memory banks.
  • Subversive / ominous sense: weirwood groves also mark sites of sacrifice and blood magic ("Black Harren felled the grove of weirwood trees"), giving the word a double valence of sanctity and horror.

Historical Semantic Bridges

Modern English weir descends from the Old English word for a dam or fish-fence, a barrier across water built to protect a fishery or control a river - the same root as ward, guard, and wary. Modern English wood descends from Old English wudu, the word for both timber and forest, whose root reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European word for "tree." Martin's coinage binds these two strands - the protective barrier and the sacred forest - into a single mythic object: a tree that is itself a boundary between the living and the dead, a guardian, and a place of worship. The word thus carries, without inventing a new etymology, the full weight of the Germanic landscape's oldest vocabulary of enclosure and grove.
