Mortar is one of English's great homonym collisions: three heavy things sharing one shell of sound — the grinding bowl of the kitchen and pharmacy, the binding paste of the bricklayer, and the squat cannon of the battlefield — each arrived at by a different road from Latin, and each still fully alive today.

In the source sentence the word is the witch's vehicle: she at once sprang into her iron mortar — Baba Yaga's famous conveyance from Russian folktale, the giant iron grinding-bowl in which she flies through the forest, steering with the pestle as with an oar or rudder. Here mortar is the apothecary's vessel grown monstrous, a domestic grinder scaled up into a flying chariot.

Core vessel sense in detail: The grinding mortar is a thick-walled cup — stone, marble, brass, iron, or porcelain — in which substances are crushed with the club-like pestle. From Roman kitchens to medieval apothecaries to modern laboratories and Mexican molcajetes, its form has barely changed in two millennia: weight, hardness, and a hollow that holds the material while the pestle pounds it.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Grinding bowl used with a pestle (from Old English, mortere): the pharmacy and kitchen vessel for pounding spices, pills, and pigments. This is the oldest English sense and the folktale sense.
  • Building paste of lime or cement binding bricks and stones (from Middle English): the bricklayer's mortar, spread with a trowel, hardening into the wall's hidden skeleton. The bridge is crushing: the paste was ground in a mortar-vessel, and the vessel lent its name to the mixture made inside it.
  • Short wide cannon firing shells in a high arc (from 17th c.): the infantry mortar lobbing bombs over walls and into trenches. The bridge is shape: the squat muzzle-loading piece resembled an apothecary's mortar in stubby profile, and gunners borrowed the kitchen word for the engine of siege.
  • Firework mortar tube (modern extension): the launch tube from which display shells are fired skyward. The bridge runs through the military piece: same squat tube, same high arc, festive rather than lethal.
  • Figurative crushing or pounding attack (literary): troops mortared in their positions, a debate where one side is ground down. The bridge carries the cannon's pounding back toward the grinding bowl: to be mortared is to be crushed as grain is crushed.
  • Mortar-board cap and other compounds (cultural offshoots): the academic cap named for its flat square board, which resembles the bricklayer's hawk holding mortar. The bridge is visual resemblance carried into academic dress.

Register and connotation: mortar is solidly workmanlike in every sense — the kitchen, the building site, the battery — yet the folktale sense lifts it into wild comedy, the witch clattering overhead in her kitchenware. The grinding and building senses feel ancient and honest, smelling of herbs and lime; the military sense adds thunder. Few English words unite the pestle, the trowel, and the shell so compactly, and each keeps the others faintly audible whenever the word sounds.
