Single root word: imperative — from Latin imperātīvus, "of commanding," formed on imperāre, "to command." Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *per-, "to produce, procure, bring forth; to go through, carry through" (extended stem *perə- / *par-) — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives include Ancient Greek póros "way, passage" and peirā́ō "to try, attempt," and Latin parāre. NOTE: the derivation of Latin parāre from *per- is the traditional account but is debated; de Vaan treats the formation as uncertain in detail. 2. Latin parāre, "to make ready, prepare, procure, furnish, provide" — attested from Plautus, c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE; the stem of praeparāre "prepare," comparāre "compare," separāre "separate," apparāre "apparel/apparatus." 3. Latin imperāre, compound of the prefix in- ("upon, toward, in") + parāre, "to command, order, dictate, impose one's will" — literally "to prepare upon / set in order over (someone)" — attested from Plautus and Cato, c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE. From the same stem: imperium "command, authority" (source of English empire) and imperātor "commander, emperor." 4. Latin imperātīvus, "relating to a command; (in grammar) the imperative mood," an adjective formed on the past participle imperātus + the suffix -īvus — attested in the Latin grammarians (e.g., the ars grammatica tradition of the 4th–6th c. CE; modus imperātīvus, "mood of commanding"). 5. Old French imperatif (feminine imperative), "pertaining to command; the imperative mood" — attested from c. 13th c. CE. 6. Middle English / early Modern English imperative, "relating to command; authoritative; (grammar) the imperative mood" — attested from c. 15th c. (grammatical use) and c. 16th c. (authoritative sense); the noun sense "a command, a duty" from the 17th c.; the modern "absolutely necessary" sense develops from the 19th c., reinforced by Kant's "categorical imperative" (1785). 7. Modern English imperative, "of vital importance, crucial; an authoritative command or binding duty; the imperative mood" — final node. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Two nuances. (1) The precise PIE shape behind Latin parāre is uncertain: *per- "to produce" is the standard reconstruction, but de Vaan notes the formation is not completely clear, and the older popular account conflates parāre with parere, "to give birth," a different verb. (2) A minority view derives imperāre from the sense "to prepare an expedition against"; the mainstream reading is simply "to set in order over, to command." Neither nuance affects the securely attested Latin-to-English chain, which is documented at every stage.
