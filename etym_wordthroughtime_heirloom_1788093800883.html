- 15th century: born in law — "heirlome", a chattel that descends with the freehold; dry, technical, the property of lawyers and manors.
- 16th–18th centuries: fixed as a legal-technical term in statutes and law dictionaries; the perception stays forensic — the word belongs to wills, debts and inheritance disputes.
- 19th century: migrates from the courtroom to the parlour; Victorian sentiment remakes heirlooms as beloved domestic keepsakes (jewels, plate, clocks, Bibles) — warm, nostalgic, genealogical.
- Early 20th century: a romantic and slightly aristocratic glow in fiction, weddings and family albums; the object of legend and lineage.
- Late 20th–21st centuries: horticultural takeover — heirloom tomatoes, seeds and breeds — plus "heirloom-quality" marketing and digital heirlooms; the word now signals authenticity, craft and continuity rather than property law.
