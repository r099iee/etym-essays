The idiomatic phrase dropped his ears represents an expressive synthesis of three ancient Germanic lexical roots, combining a causative verb of lowering, a masculine possessive pronoun, and the primordial anatomical term for the organ of hearing.

1. Evolution of 'Drop / Dropped': The verb drop originates from the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰrewb- or *dʰreb- ('to fall, crumble, or drip'). In Proto-Germanic, this developed into the geminated weak verb *druppōną and the strong verb *drupaną, which gave Old English dropian and droppian ('to fall in drops, trickle'). By the 14th century in Middle English, the causative transitive sense ('to cause something to fall, to lower or let down vertically') emerged alongside the intransitive liquid sense. In the context of equine posture, 'dropping' became the standard descriptive verb for lowering anatomical extremities—such as dropping the head, haunches, or ears—under physical or emotional pressure.

2. Evolution of 'His': The possessive pronoun his traces to the Proto-Indo-European demonstrative pronominal base *ḱe- / *ḱi- ('this, here'). In Proto-Germanic, this base supplied the third-person masculine/neuter nominative *hiz, with its genitive form reconstructed as *hisa ('of him, of this one'). In Old English, his functioned as the regular masculine and neuter genitive singular of the third-person pronoun hē. Unlike languages that employ invariable possessive particles, English retained the gendered genitive his, which in equestrian literature was consistently applied to male working animals—such as stallions and geldings—bestowing individualized personality upon the creature.

3. Evolution of 'Ear / Ears': The noun ear is among the most ancient anatomical terms in the Indo-European lexicon, deriving from Proto-Indo-European *h₂ows- / *h₂ous- ('ear'), the ancestor of Latin auris, Greek ous (οὖς), Old Church Slavonic uxo (ухо), and Sanskrit uṣi. In Proto-Germanic, it shifted regular phonetic contours via rhotacism in West Germanic to yield *auzô. In Old English, it was recorded as the neuter n-stem ēare (plural ēaran). Through the Middle English reduction of weak inflections, ēare became ere, adopting the northern plural marker -es to produce modern ears.

  • Equine Ethology and Literary Realism: The integration of these three terms into the fixed behavioral phrase dropped his ears highlights the profound intimacy between humans and working equines in agrarian society. Horses communicate predominantly through subtle changes in ear posture, facial grimaces, and tail swishing. In 19th-century realist literature, depicting a horse that 'dropped his ears' provided authors like Leo Tolstoy with an authentic, unvarnished means of conveying the animal's silent consciousness, weariness, and resistance to servitude without resorting to cartoonish exaggeration.
  • Historical Bridges of the Idiom: From Anglo-Saxon stable hands observing draft oxen and warhorses to Victorian coachmen managing carriage teams in St. Petersburg and London, the act of an animal lowering its ears remained a universally understood indicator of mood. The phrase encapsulates centuries of empirical animal observation, bridging ancient Germanic anatomy with the psychological depth of modern narrative literature.
