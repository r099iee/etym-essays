gulls' is the possessive (genitive) plural of the noun gull, the grey-and-white seabird of the family Laridae, and it means "belonging to gulls" or "of gulls". In the passage from C.S. Lewis's Prince Caspian, the children have just eaten the last of the sandwiches and are planning their next meal on the island where the magic has left them. Edmund "said they must gather gulls' eggs from the rocks." The little apostrophe is doing grammatical and narrative work at the same time. Grammatically, gulls' is a plural possessive: because the plural already ends in -s, English adds only the apostrophe after the s, with no extra letter. One bird owns a nest — gull's; the whole flock owns the whole harvest — gulls'. This is the so-called "s-possessive" of modern English, and getting it right is a classic learner's trap. Narratively, the form quietly turns wild seabirds into a pantry: "gulls' eggs" were a real, traditional food of British coasts, gathered from ledges and shingle, and the children's casual mention of them shows how thoroughly magic has carried them out of polite, shop-fed England into a foraging world. PRIMARY MEANING AND THE BIRD ITSELF:
  • The bird. A gull is any member of the family Laridae, medium-to-large seabirds with webbed feet, strong wings, typically grey-and-white plumage, and a raucous, mewing cry. They live almost everywhere along coasts, rivers and lakes, and, increasingly, inland towns. The word in this possessive form is the bird's grammar put to practical use: gulls' eggs, gulls' nests, gulls' cries.
  • The possessive plural in context. "Gulls' eggs" are eggs laid by gulls, gathered from rocky ledges. In the novel the detail is both practical and slightly comic — the children can remember the idea but not the actual sight of any eggs, and they cannot imagine cooking them even if they found them.
POLYSEMY AND THE SOUND-ALIKE VERB:
  • The homograph verb to gull. A separate word, of separate (and uncertain) origin, to gull means "to trick, cheat, or dupe", and a gull can be the dupe himself. From this verb comes the common adjective gullible ("easily deceived"). This is NOT a sense of the seabird word — the bird and the cheat are distinct etymons that happen to share a spelling and pronunciation — but because they sound identical, the bird name has absorbed a sly, "dupe-able" undertone in popular perception.
  • The doublet seagull. In everyday speech the birds are usually called seagulls; field naturalists prefer plain gull, since gulls also live far inland. "Seagull" is the colloquial, emotive form, "gull" the technical and literary one.
  • Modern technical and literary uses. In bird-lore and ornithology, gull is the standard genus-level name (herring gull, black-headed gull, lesser black-backed gull); in poetry and metaphor, gulls stand for the sea itself, for freedom, flight and the cry of the coast — "gulls' wings", "the gulls' lament".
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE: The possessive plural is not a new meaning but a grammatical extension of the noun, and its "development" is really the history of English inflections. Old English marked possession with case endings (gullan 's would be the old genitive plural); after the Norman period and the loss of most inflections, English reshaped possession around the clitic -'s (from the old genitive singular -es), and for plural nouns already ending in -s it kept just the apostrophe. So gulls' encodes a whole lost grammatical system: a thousand years of sound change, case loss and spelling reform compressed into one punctuation mark. In the novel it also encodes class and period: a child of the 1940s would have met "gulls' eggs" as a normal, slightly exotic food, and the word helps date the story's England even while the island is timeless. The modern learner therefore meets the word twice: once as a grammar rule about apostrophes, and once as a window onto coastal foraging, seabird biology, and the sea-stained vocabulary of British island life. Exhaustively, then: gulls' = (1) the plural possessive of the seabird gull, "belonging to gulls"; (2) in context, the eggs of gulls gathered as food; (3) by association, the entire register of coast, colony, cry and forage; (4) grammatically, the model case of the plural-s possessive that learners must place correctly; and (5) a homophone magnet for the unrelated verb to gull and its adjective gullible, which every careful reader must keep distinct. No other English word carries quite this load of grammar, gastronomy, ornithology and false-friend confusion in three letters and an apostrophe.
