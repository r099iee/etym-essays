- 12th Century — Anglo-Norman Breton Romance: Emerged in the courtly literature of England and northern France, spearheaded by Marie de France, designating short, elegant narrative poems in rhyming octosyllables based on ancient Celtic musical tales sung by Breton harpers.
- 14th to 15th Century — Middle English Balladry & Lyricism: Adopted into Middle English by writers like Geoffrey Chaucer (e.g. The Franklin's Tale) to describe courtly Breton narratives, while simultaneously broadening in popular usage to mean any song, melody, or vocal tune.
- 16th to 18th Century — Poetic Birdsong & Musical Lyric: Widely employed across Elizabethan and Augustan poetry as an evocative, pastoral synonym for melodic birdsong (the "lays of birds") or rustic songs sung by shepherds and lovers.
- 19th Century — Romantic Heroic Antiquarianism: Revitalized by Sir Walter Scott (The Lay of the Last Minstrel) and Thomas Babington Macaulay (Lays of Ancient Rome) as a powerful, archaizing vehicle for dramatic, chivalric, and martial balladry.
- 20th to 21st Century — Scholarly Term of Oral Poetics: Extensively used in folklore, comparative mythology, and medieval studies to categorize compact oral narrative poems across cultures (such as Old Germanic heroic lays like the Hildebrandslied and Indic oral narratives).
