HARK means to listen attentively, to strain the ear toward a sound, or to heed a call — in practice it survives almost entirely as a commanding imperative, 'listen!' In the elvish lyric sung in the trees at Rivendell in The Hobbit, ch. 3, the wanderers are urged to 'listen and HARK till the end of the dark': the two verbs of audition are twinned into a single incantatory summons. The archaic, hunting-field tone of the word turns a simple invitation into a spell-like ritual — the weary company, hunted over the Misty Mountains, is asked to lay down its fear and surrender to the healing nonsense-music of the wood-elves. Its nuance is ceremonial, playful and elevated: HARK commands the whole body to attend, not merely the ears, and in Tolkien's verse it functions as a metrical ornament as much as a verb.

Set beside its near-synonyms

  • Plain listen descends from a different Germanic stem (Old English hlysnan, from PIE *kleu- 'to hear') and is the neutral, modern word.
  • Hearken is HARK's longer doublet, now chiefly biblical or rhetorical ("hearken unto me").
  • Attend implies deliberate, disciplined focus; heed carries a warning of consequence; overhear implies accidental hearing.
  • Only HARK preserves the imperious urgency of the medieval hunting cry — the word that once set hounds and huntsmen in motion, and which, in hark back, still evokes a pack retracing a lost scent.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings

  • The commanding imperative: "Hark!" — the archaic summons to silence and attention, beloved of poetry, hymn, and drama ("Hark! the herald angels sing," 1739; Shakespeare's "Hark!" imperatives).
  • Hark back (1817): literally, of hounds, to turn back along the track when the scent is lost; metaphorically, to return to an earlier topic, era, or practice — "this design harks back to the Gothic Revival."
  • Hark at him/her (dialectal, 19th c.): "listen to him (in a scoffing tone)" — "Hark at him, pretending he knew all along!"
  • Hunting cries: "hark away," "hark forrard" — commands urging hounds to follow a scent, fossilising the word's origin in the field.
  • The doublet hearken: a longer form of the same intensive stem, surviving in elevated or archaic prose; both hark and hearken descend from the same Old English base.
  • Plain verb use in dialect: in northern English and Scots dialects to hark survived as the ordinary verb "to listen, give ear to," so the word was not always a fossil — it was a living dialect verb until recent centuries.
  • As a noun: the rare noun a hark or hearkening meant an act of listening or a summons to listen, echoing the verb's commanding force.

The word's modern range is a set of archaic survivals: a poetic imperative, a hunting idiom, a dialect scoff, and a fossil in compounds. Every sense keeps the intensity of "listen hard," which is precisely why Tolkien pairs it with the neutral listen — the two voices of the English ear, everyday and ritual, joined in one line. To HARK is never to hear passively but to lean into the sound, whole body and attention committed, exactly as the Elves demand of the travellers before their song casts its healing spell.
