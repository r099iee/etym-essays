The evolution of abjures leads from Roman courts to Reformation scaffolds to modern op-eds, always preserving the double gesture of speaking and turning away. Its deepest root is PIE *yewes-, denoting sacred law, right order, and the oath that guarantees it, whence Latin ius, law, right, oath, with genitive iuris seen in jurisdiction and jury. From ius Latin formed jurare, to swear, to take an oath by the gods, with compounds conjurare, to swear together, whence conspire, and perjurare, to swear falsely, whence perjury. With prefix ab-, away, from, Latin created abjurare, to deny or disavow upon oath, to swear that a debt is not owed, a charge false, or a claim abandoned. Classical usage is forensic and financial rather than theological, a defendant abjurat, he denies on oath, and is released.

Phonologically the journey is learned and stable. Late Latin abjurare passed into Old French abjurer with palatal j as zh, preserving ab- and -urer infinitive. Middle English borrowed abjuren around 1500 in legal-religious contexts of heresy trials, keeping Latin spelling with j for dzh, giving RP /əbˈdʒʊə/ for abjure and /əbˈdʒʊəz/ for abjures, with reduced initial a to schwa and stress on second syllable. Spelling never anglicized to abjure with -dge, signalling learned prestige. The third-person -s in abjures is the regular Modern English present ending, but its gnomic force, literacy abjures, gives timeless doctrinal weight.

  • Medieval heresy stage: to abjure heresy de levi or de vehementi before inquisitors, sworn recantation with candle, penance, and threat of relapse as capital, also to abjure the realm, sworn exile on pain of outlawry, as in sanctuary procedure.
  • Confessional state stage 1500s-1700s: oaths abjuring the Pope, the Pretender, transubstantiation, required for office, with abjuration as loyalty test, connotation of coercion and conscience, Quakers imprisoned for refusing to swear at all.
  • Secularized ethical stage 1800s-present: writers abjure romanticism, politicians abjure violence, media abjure sensationalism, oath weakened to solemn promise, but archaism retains gravity that renounce lacks, and personified abstractions like literacy can abjure by nature.

To say literacy by its nature abjures oral powers, as here, thus deliberately revives inquisitorial solemnity for media history. Writing is cast as a convert who swore off breath and gesture to enter the church of the alphabet, and prose stylists as later reformers trying to smuggle the abjured spirits back inside without breaking the oath. The verb's survival depends on that theatre, in an age of casual unfollows and deletes, abjure still demands a raised hand, a witness, and a past that will hold you to your words.

Linguistically the persistence of abjures with its Latin prefix intact shows how English preserves oath-magic in morphology long after courts stopped demanding oaths for office. The ab- still points away, the -jure still points to law, and speakers feel both even without Latin, hearing in abjure a backing-away while swearing. That transparency keeps the verb alive for personified systems, where literacy can abjure by nature because readers supply the raised hand imaginatively. In media theory such personification is analytic, not ornament, naming the trade by which permanence is bought with loss, and preparing the plea that style must buy back what structure swore away.
