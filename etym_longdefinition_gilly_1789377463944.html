Gilly is a fascinating botanical, historical, and dialectal noun and combining form operating across horticulture, folklore, Highland sporting traditions, and circus vernacular. In its primary botanical context—manifested in Oscar Wilde's fairy tale The Devoted Friend within the compound Gillyflowers—gilly designates several species of beloved, highly fragrant, clove-scented European garden flowers, most notably the clove pink or carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus), the garden stock (Matthiola incana), and the wallflower (Erysimum cheiri). Cherished since antiquity for their intense, sweet, spicy fragrance reminiscent of dried cloves, these blossoms formed an indispensable element of medieval monastic herb gardens and traditional Elizabethan parterres.

In Wilde's story, the author lists "Gillyflowers" alongside Sweet-william, Shepherds’-purses, and Fair-maids of France in Little Hans's radiant cottage garden. Hans tends these aromatic flowers with meticulous tenderness throughout spring and summer. The spicy, invigorating perfume of the gillyflowers symbolizes Hans's unblemished purity, industrious craftsmanship, and selfless love of beauty—qualities that stand in tragic opposition to the cold, transactional cynicism of the Miller, who routinely demands armfuls of Hans's prized blossoms without ever offering compensation or genuine kindness in return.

The term displays extraordinary polysemy, regional diversity, and specialized technical applications across varied cultural spheres:

  • Horticultural Botany and Folk Nomenclature ("Gillyflower"): Historically, gilly served as a standalone noun or combining form for three distinct floral families renowned for spicy fragrance:
    • The Clove Gillyflower (Dianthus caryophyllus): The true clove pink or carnation, whose petals were steeped in wine, ale, and vinegar to impart a warming clove spice;
    • The Wall Gillyflower (Erysimum cheiri): The cheerful yellow, orange, and bronze wallflower, celebrated for flourishing in the lime mortar of medieval stone walls and castle ruins;
    • The Stock Gillyflower (Matthiola incana): The fragrant ten-week stock, prized for dense spires of purple, white, and crimson blossoms.
  • Feudal Law and Symbolic Quit-Rents ("The Gillyflower Rent"): In medieval English and Norman property law, a gillyflower rent was a celebrated form of peppercorn rent or nominal feudal quit-rent. A tenant held tenure of an estate or manor by the ceremonial annual payment of a single red gillyflower (or clove pink) to the overlord on a designated feast day (often Midsummer or Michaelmas). This purely token payment legally acknowledged the lord's ultimate title while allowing the tenant to enjoy the land virtually rent-free.
  • Scottish Highland Sporting Culture ("Gillie / Ghillie"): Through a separate etymological stream originating in Scottish Gaelic (gille, meaning "lad, boy, or servant"), the word—frequently spelled gillie or ghillie—denotes a trusted attendant, guide, or gamekeeper who assists fishermen in salmon rivers or deer stalkers in the Scottish Highlands. A skilled gillie possesses encyclopedic knowledge of local river currents, salmon pools, weather patterns, and deer movements.
  • Military Camouflage and Tactical Warfare ("Ghillie Suit"): Derived directly from the Scottish Highland gamekeepers who crafted camouflaged cloaks to conceal themselves from poachers, the ghillie suit was formally developed during the Second Boer War by the British Army's Lovat Scouts (a Scottish Highland regiment). Composed of netting covered in loose strips of burlap, jute, and natural vegetation, the ghillie suit breaks up the human silhouette and is utilized globally by military snipers and wildlife photographers.
  • Traditional Highland Footwear and Dance ("Ghillie Brogues"): In Scottish Highland dress and competitive Highland dance, ghillies are soft, pliable leather shoes characterized by crisscrossing laces that tie around the ankles, designed without a tongue to facilitate rapid water drainage when traversing sodden heather and peat bogs.
  • American Circus, Carnival, and Theatrical Slang ("Gilly Show"): In late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American circus and itinerant carnival slang, a gilly was an outsider, local townsperson, or non-circus individual. Furthermore, a gilly show denoted a small, traveling carnival or tent show that owned no private railway cars, instead transporting its tents, performers, and menagerie via ordinary passenger trains and hiring local farm wagons (known as gilly wagons) to haul equipment from the station to the fairgrounds.

The botanical lineage of gilly represents one of the most celebrated examples of folk etymology in the English language. Originating in Ancient Greek karyóphyllon ("clove"), the word passed through Latin into Old French giroflé and Middle English gilofre. Hearing the unfamiliar French termination -fre, English gardeners creatively reanalyzed the sound as -flower, yielding gillyflower, from which the affectionate root gilly broke off as an independent floral name.
