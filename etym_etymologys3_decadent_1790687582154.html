Decadent falls into English, appropriately enough, by falling: from Latin cadere, to fall, with the downward prefix dē-, giving dēcadere, to fall away or sink, whose present-participial stem dēcadent- passes through French décadent into English. The physical image — fruit dropping, buildings subsiding, bodies sinking — carries the whole later moral and aesthetic career of the word, because every decadence imagines health as height and corruption as descent.

English first meets the word in the sixteenth century as a noun naming persons or ages in decline, a straightforward borrowing of the French adjective-turned-label for late Rome and its analogues. For two centuries it remains an historian’s and moralist’s term: decadent empires, decadent churches, decadent taste — always a falling-off from a nobler prior standard, always tinged with pulpit disapproval. The great transformation comes in nineteenth-century France, where poets and novelists — Baudelaire, Verlaine, Huysmans, and the self-styled Décadents — seize the insult and wear it as a badge. To be décadent becomes a programme: exquisite artifice over coarse nature, twilight over noon, the hothouse over the field. Verlaine’s ‘Je suis l’Empire à la fin de la décadence’ makes decline itself a style, and English Aestheticism imports both the word’s glamour and its notoriety in the 1880s and 1890s.

  • Roman prototype: from Gibbon’s Decline and Fall onward, late imperial Rome supplies the master image — luxury, effeminacy (in the period’s prejudiced idiom), and artistic over-ripeness — against which all later decadences are measured.
  • Moral-theological use: preachers and reformers deploy decadent for backsliding churches and licentious manners; the fall is from grace, and the tone is condemnatory.
  • Phonetic naturalisation: English keeps the French spelling but anglicises the stress to the first syllable (/ˈdɛkədənt/), with the final -ent reduced; the noun (‘a decadent’) and adjective (‘decadent art’) share the form, a flexibility inherited from French.
  • Aesthetic reversal: the Decadent movement inverts the value scheme — decline becomes ripeness, artifice becomes honesty — so that by the 1890s calling a poem decadent in London literary circles could be praise, provocation, or prosecution evidence, sometimes all three at once.
  • Twentieth-century broadening: after the Wilde trials the glamour curdles and the word spreads downward into general use: decadent desserts, decadent weekends, decadent chocolate — luxurious self-indulgence with a wink, the fall now only from the diet.
  • Critical oscillation: historians still debate every application — was late Rome decadent, was Weimar decadent, is contemporary culture decadent? — because the word smuggles a golden age into every diagnosis; to allege decadence is always to posit a height fallen from, which is why the source sentence’s dry denial (‘nothing especially decadent about this’) deflates precisely that grand narrative reflex.

Modern usage thus holds two incompatible charges at once: condemnation and relish. The moralist hears rot; the gourmand hears richness. When the author remarks that there is nothing especially decadent about some natural behaviour, the word’s full weight is ironically present — empires fell for less, the sermon practically writes itself — only to be dismissed as inapplicable. Falling, the word suggests, need not mean fallen.
