- [Late Middle English: adhesion] - clammy (15th century) first describes what is soft, sticky, and adhesive to the touch — clammy hands, clammy earth — a wetness that clings like clay; the word is bodily, earthy, and still morally neutral.
- [16th–17th century: coldness enters] - the sense concentrates on cold, unpleasantly moist contact: clammy air, clammy walls, and above all clammy skin in fear, fever, and nausea; the word becomes the signature of illness and dread rather than mere dampness.
- [19th century: Gothic & physiological formula] - in Gothic and sensation fiction “cold clammy dread” hardens into a stock phrase; physiologically, the clammy palm and clammy forehead come to mark anxiety, faintness, and the sweat of terror — the moisture of the body under stress.
- [Contemporary sense] - clammy still fuses coldness with clinging dampness — the skin of fear, the fog-cold wall, the hand that recoils — connoting revulsion and unease; in Tolkien's riddle (1937) Bilbo's “cold and clammy” hand is at once the guessed answer and the physical shudder that guesses it.
