The word enumerate tells the story of how counting became official. Its two Latin halves — ex- "out" and numerus "number" — fuse into a verb meaning "to number out," to call items out of the count one by one; and every extension of the word in English has been an extension of that original gesture of calling things forth, one at a time, into an ordered list. From Roman rhetoric to the modern census, from geometry to the novel, enumerate has been the word of the ledger — and, precisely because it is the word of the ledger, it is also the word used to refuse the ledger, as the narrator of the passage does with urbane irony.

1. The Indo-European root. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *nem-, "to allot, distribute, take one's share." This is not, at first glance, a root about numbers at all: its oldest sense is social and concrete — the sharing out of portions of meat, land, or plunder. The root is attested across the family in Greek némō "to distribute, allot, pasture," in Germanic in Old English niman and German nehmen "to take," and in Sanskrit námas, literally "a portion given," hence "homage, reverence." The semantic bridge from "share" to "number" runs through the practical arithmetic of distribution: to count is to allot shares, to give each thing its portion, to see a quantity as a set of separate shares. Latin numerus carried this heritage: from its earliest attestations it means both "a number, a quantity" and "a set, a rank, a part," and it even retains a musical sense — "rhythm, measure" — because counting was inseparable from measuring out time. (The debated point is only the Italic intermediate: the connection of numerus to *nem- is standard and accepted by de Vaan, but no Proto-Italic form is directly attested.)

2. The Latin formation. From numerus, Latin built the denominal verb numerāre "to count," an everyday word for counting money and reckoning sums. Adding the prefix ex- ("out of, out from") produced ēnumerāre, "to count out, to enumerate," attested in the first century BCE in Cicero, who used it both for counting money and for the rhetorical procedure of listing points one by one. The noun ēnumerātiō — "enumeration" — became a technical term of Roman rhetoric: the figure by which an orator piles up his proofs or the charges against his opponent, item after item, to overwhelming effect. Here we find already the double life the English word will inherit: enumeration is at once a mathematical act (counting out) and a verbal act (naming each item), and the Latin rhetoricians knew that the second could be a weapon. When the narrator of the passage declines to enumerate, he is declining the weapon of the orator — refusing to run through the list that would prove his point by sheer accumulation.

3. Entry into English (16th century). English borrowed enumerate in the early sixteenth century, in the great wave of Latin loans that rebuilt the English vocabulary of learning. The word arrived already loaded: it was a bookish verb of law, mathematics, and scholarship, used where a plain Englishman might say "number" or "reckon." Its first English contexts are legal and learned — enumerating the clauses of a statute, the articles of a charge, the reasons of an argument. The word's Latin dress (the e- prefix, the -ate ending) marked it as educated property, and it kept the elevated register it has never entirely lost.

4. The bureaucratic century (18th–19th). The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the age of the census, the inventory, and the statistical state, made enumerate the official verb of complete counting. To enumerate became the precise word for the state's act of numbering its people, its goods, its parishes, its households: the census enumerates the population; the survey enumerates the land. The word absorbed the values of the new administration — systematicity, completeness, item-by-item thoroughness — and its connotation became bureaucratic and impersonal. Yet even here the older rhetorical life flickered: parliamentary and journalistic prose used "enumerate" for the exhaustive listing of grievances and claims, the enumeratio of the nineteenth-century orator.

5. The modern register and the ironic decline. In modern English, enumerate remains formal and slightly elevated — the verb of the lawyer, the statistician, the careful essayist — but its most characteristic modern use is, paradoxically, the refusal to enumerate. "It would be superfluous to enumerate" is the urbane formula for vastness: the speaker proves abundance by declining to list it, and the formality of the verb makes the refusal a courtesy rather than an omission. This is exactly the register of the passage, where the narrator has been cataloguing the room's opulent furnishings — narrow side-tables, tea things, vases of flowers — and draws the line with amused gravity: "besides other furnishings which it would be superfluous to enumerate." The word does double work at once: it signals opulence (there are so many beautiful objects) and refinement (only a social climber would feel obliged to tick them all off). Enumerate, the verb of the ledger, is here turned against the ledger — proof that a word born of counting can end by serving the eloquence of the uncounted.
