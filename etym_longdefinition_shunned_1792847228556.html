Shunned is the past tense and past participle of shun: deliberately and pointedly avoided, kept at arm's length, refused company, notice, or dealings. In the source sentence a man grown hateful is shunned when visible by all his relations, cut off by his own kin, left to walk through the world unmet and unspoken to.

Core meaning in detail: to shun is not merely to overlook but to avoid with purpose and often with public meaning. The shunned person sees backs turned, invitations that never come, greetings withheld; the shunned book, pub, or opinion is passed by on principle. The word implies a chooser and a reason: fear, disgust, moral judgement, or tribal loyalty. Grammar marks it plainly: one shuns company, shuns publicity, shuns meat or alcohol, and the result is the state named here, being shunned.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Deliberately avoided as a person (primary modern sense): to keep clear of someone socially, to cut them dead, to cross the street rather than meet them. The bridge is the verb's oldest root of shrinking back in fear or loathing, stretched from flinching at a thing to organising a whole community's cold shoulder.
  • Formally ostracised or excommunicated (institutional): as when a sect practises shunning, forbidding members to eat, speak, or trade with the offender. The bridge runs from private avoidance to codified punishment: the community turns instinct into law, and a Mennonite or monastic shunning carries the weight of exile.
  • Steered clear of as a thing, place, or habit (general): to shun the limelight, to shun processed food, to shun a dangerous street after dark. The bridge widens the target from persons to anything distasteful: the same deliberate swerve serves for cameras, cigarettes, and quarrels.
  • Evaded as a duty or topic (figurative): to shun responsibility, to shun comparison, to shun the question. The bridge is metaphorical dodging: one treats an obligation the way one treats an unwelcome acquaintance, slipping past it with averted eyes.
  • Archaic — feared or shrunk from (Old English root sense): the earliest recorded shade, to recoil in dread. Modern readers meet it in old texts where heroes shun dragons, and the bridge to today's social sense runs through centuries of softening: terror cooled into disapproval, flinching into frostiness.

Register and connotation: shunned is stern and sorrowful, heavier than avoided and colder than ignored. It suggests judgement rather than accident: nobody is shunned by mistake. Journalists use it for disgraced figures, historians for heretics, novelists for misers and uncles who have spent their welcome. Yet the word can also carry quiet sympathy, for the sentence lingers on the shunned one, and readers feel the chill of the empty room around him. To be shunned is to be present yet treated as absent, the cruellest arithmetic of company.
