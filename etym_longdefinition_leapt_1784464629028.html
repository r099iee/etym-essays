Leapt is the past tense of the verb to leap: to spring, to bound, to surge upward or forward with sudden force — and, in one common British spelling, it is also the past participle. As a verb form it reports a completed, instantaneous movement of violent or eager springing: a flame that leapt up, a cat that leapt onto a sill, a runner who leapt a fence. The strong past keeps a slightly older, more literary feel than its regular twin leaped, and in Britain the -pt spelling is standard. The word's core is suddenness and force: leaping is not walking, not climbing — it is a single explosive bound that clears, rises, or springs in one motion. In Bilbo's quiet observation — “Suddenly in the wood beyond The Water a flame leapt up” — the verb does precise narrative work. The fire is almost certainly a mundane wood-fire, someone lighting a camp-fire, but the verb's kinetic violence is enough to trigger catastrophe in Bilbo's imagination: he immediately thinks of plundering dragons settling on his quiet Hill and kindling it all to flames. The past tense marks a completed, instantaneous surge — flames do not stroll, they leap — and the suddenness of the verb (opening with “Suddenly”) becomes the spark of hobbit anxiety. Tone: narrative alertness shading into comic-neurotic fear; the grammar of sudden motion feeds the panic that follows. Primary senses of leapt / leap
  • To spring or jump upward, forward, or over something — “flames leapt up”; “the cat leapt onto the windowsill”; “he leapt the brook.”
  • To move, rise, or increase suddenly and rapidly — “flames leapt”; “the share price leapt”; “her heart leapt with joy.”
  • To spring into action or conclusion — “to leap to one’s feet”; “to leap to conclusions.”
  • In fixed phrases — “a leap of faith,” a sudden trusting jump into uncertainty; “by leaps and bounds,” rapid progress; “leap year,” the year with an extra day that makes the calendar “leap.”
Polysemy and secondary modern meanings
  • Finance and economics: “to leap” describes sudden sharp rises — prices, shares, and profits leap; “a quantum leap” is a sudden enormous advance, borrowed from physics’ quantum jump and applied to progress.
  • Physics and chemistry: a “quantum leap” (or quantum jump) is an electron’s abrupt transition between energy states — the modern scientific sense of a discontinuous, instantaneous change, coined early in the 20th century.
  • Finance, acronym: LEAPS — Long-term Equity AnticiPation Securities — long-dated stock options, a back-formation that makes the ordinary verb leap into a financial acronym.
  • Gaming and sport: leaping as an athletic and video-game mechanic — jumping, vaulting, and bounding as standard verbs of movement and action.
  • Emotion and metaphor: the heart leaps (with joy, fear, or excitement); one leaps at an opportunity, leaps to defend, or takes a leap of faith — the physical bound transferred to emotion and decision.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge of the senses The word descends from the Old English strong verb hlēapan, “to leap, spring, run,” a native Germanic word with a long strong conjugation. From the start it names a sudden, forceful, airborne motion — the spring of a runner, the bound of a dancer, the rise of a flame. The modern polysemy is a series of transfers of that single bound: physical springing became emotional springing (the heart leaps), economic springing (prices leap), intellectual springing (leaping to conclusions, quantum leaps of thought), and even the calendar’s spring (leap year, whose extra day makes the dates “spring forward” past the weekday). The spelling leapt with -pt reflects the older strong pronunciation; the alternative leaped regularized the spelling to match the weak pronunciation, and both remain in use, leapt dominating in British English. How leapt differs from its near-synonyms
  • Jumped — the general, neutral word for leaving the ground; broader and more everyday than leap, which adds suddenness, force, and often upward reach.
  • Sprang / sprung — close to leap, but often stresses elasticity or a source of stored energy (a spring); leapt stresses the sudden bound itself.
  • Bounded — springing with vigorous steps or a series of leaps; more repeated and loping than a single sudden leap.
  • Vaulted — leaping over something, often with the aid of hands or a pole; stresses the clearance of an obstacle.
  • Flared — of fire, to spread or brighten suddenly; emphasizes light and heat expanding rather than the upward surge of leaping flame.
  • Shot up — rose extremely fast; colloquial, where leapt is the formal, slightly literary word for the same sudden rise.
In the card's context The line keeps the strong past LEAPT, not the base leap and not the regular leaped: the past form marks the action as already done, feeding the next anxious thought — what that leaping flame might become. A flame that leapt up in the wood is fire and panic rising in a single bound, and the word’s whole history of sudden springing — from Old English hlēapan to modern idiom — stands behind the image.
