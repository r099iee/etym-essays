- [15th-century borrowing] - 'Ambiguity' enters English from Latin ambiguitas via French, built on ambigere ('ambi-' both/around + 'agere' to drive); in early use it names the state of being uncertain or of double meaning, usually treated as a defect of language.
- [Renaissance to 18th century] - The word is largely pejorative: ambiguity is obscurity, equivocation, a flaw in rhetoric and law to be resolved, and a weapon in theological controversy — something a good writer or judge should remove.
- [1930 rehabilitation] - William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity transforms its public reputation: double meaning is recast not as an accident of language but as the very engine of poetic power, and the term becomes a prized literary-critical concept.
- [Modern usage] - The word keeps both faces: in science and law it is still an unwanted uncertainty to be disambiguated, while in literature and criticism it is celebrated as the simultaneous availability of two or more genuinely defensible interpretations — a richness rather than a flaw.
