The compound foxhounds binds together two of the oldest and most consequential words in the Indo-European vocabulary — the word for the red predator and the word for the dog — into a term that perfectly encodes the rise of a sport, a breed, and a social class. Its history reaches back over six thousand years and then, in the last four centuries, becomes the story of English country life itself.

Part 1 — The fox lineage: The first element descends from a Proto-Indo-European root *puḱ-/*peuḱ-, meaning "thick-haired, bushy-tailed, tufted." The ancient name for the fox in many branches is a description rather than a true inherited word — the animal was dangerous to livestock, so speakers avoided naming it directly and instead called it "the bushy-tailed one." This explains why the words for fox differ so widely across Indo-European languages (Greek alōpēx, Latin vulpes, Gothic faúhō), and why the Germanic branch has its own formation. Under Grimm's Law, PIE *p became Germanic *f and PIE *ḱ became Germanic *h, turning the reconstructed base into Proto-Germanic *fuhsaz. From this came Old English fox (with its feminine fyxen, our vixen), German Fuchs, and Dutch vos. In Old English the fox was a familiar, wily character of the fables — the trickster whose cunning became proverbial.

Part 2 — The hound lineage: The second element goes back to one of the most widespread PIE roots of all: *ḱwōn- (genitive *ḱun-), "dog." It produced Sanskrit śvan, Greek kyōn (source of cynic, after the dog-like Diogenes), Latin canis (source of canine), and — in the Germanic branch, again under Grimm's Law — Proto-Germanic *hundaz. In Old English, hund was simply the ordinary word for dog. But after the Middle Ages, the word dog (of obscure origin, perhaps from a pet-name or a specific breed) took over as the general term, and hound narrowed to mean a dog kept for hunting — a scenthound. The semantic specialization is complete: "hounds" no longer means all dogs, but hunting dogs.

Part 3 — The compound and its birth: English joined fox and hound to form foxhound in the early 17th century (first attested c. 1610s–1620s). The coinage was no accident of vocabulary: it accompanied a real historical change. Through the 16th and 17th centuries, the English gentry gradually abandoned the great staghound hunts of the medieval forest and took up the pursuit of the fox, whose cunning offered a faster, more demanding chase over the open fields of the new enclosed landscape. The breed itself — the English Foxhound — was developed by systematic crossing of various scenthounds, and the word and the animal grew up together.

Part 4 — Semantic and social history:
  • 17th century: "foxhound" is a plain working word for the new breed; hunts are local and practical.
  • 18th–19th centuries: The great masters of foxhounds (the MFHs) of Leicestershire, the Shires, and the provincial shires organized enormous kennels; keeping "a pack of foxhounds" became the supreme badge of landed wealth and aristocratic leisure, as the card's Pride and Prejudice context shows. The word acquired overtones of rank, hospitality, and country-house society.
  • Colonial America: English and French hounds shipped to Virginia bred the American Foxhound — lighter, faster, bred for long, solitary night chases over broken ground — so the word now covers two distinct breeds.
  • 20th century: As motorized society, animal-welfare concern, and urbanization grew, foxhounds became a symbol of tradition and privilege, celebrated and criticized in equal measure.
  • 2004 onward: After the Hunting Act 2004, traditional mounted fox hunting with dogs was banned in England and Wales; foxhounds survive in drag hunts, kennel-club shows, and country life, and the word now carries nostalgia, controversy, and heritage in one bundle.

Part 5 — Literary attestations: Foxhounds appear throughout English literature as markers of the rural gentry: in Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice (the card's context — Elizabeth's fantasy of keeping "a pack of foxhounds" if she were as rich as Mr. Darcy), in Surtees's Handley Cross and the comic hunting novels that made the foxhound and its master famous, and in countless country-house novels and memoirs. The sound of a foxhound pack in full cry — the "music" of the hounds — became a set piece of English sporting prose.

Summary of the drift: from PIE "bushy-tailed one" and "dog" → Germanic fuhsaz and hundaz → Old English fox and hund → a 17th-century English compound naming the fox-hunting breed → a word that now names two recognized breeds, an aristocratic sporting institution, and a contested emblem of rural England. Its two halves — the wily trickster and the loyal hunter — still carry the whole drama of the chase.
