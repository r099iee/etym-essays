fastidious (adjective) — being very attentive to and concerned about accuracy and detail; hard to please; excessively particular. The word descends from Latin fastīdium, "loathing, disgust, squeamishness", and its modern meaning is the refined, almost aristocratic form of choosiness: the fastidious person is not merely fussy but is possessed of standards so exacting that anything below them provokes a kind of revulsion. 1. Very attentive to accuracy and detail (primary modern sense)
  • Describes a person who demands a high standard of accuracy, cleanliness, correctness, or refinement in what they do, wear, eat, or approve: "a fastidious editor", "fastidious about his appearance".
  • In this sense it is nearly synonymous with meticulous and scrupulous, but with an added flavour of difficulty — the fastidious person is not merely careful but exacting, and their standards are hard to meet.
  • Applied to habits and tastes rather than persons: "fastidious table manners", "fastidious attention to detail".
2. Hard to please; excessively particular; squeamish (primary modern sense)
  • Describes a person who is difficult to satisfy because their tastes and standards are so refined or so fixed: "a fastidious eater", "fastidious about food".
  • The word carries a double edge: it can admire refined discrimination or censure impossible choosiness, depending on context — "so particular his choosiness borders on disgust".
  • Squeamishness — the readiness to feel revulsion — is the oldest surviving shade of this sense, inherited from the Latin "loathing".
3. Technical modern sense: fastidious organisms (microbiology)
  • In microbiology, a fastidious organism is one with complex nutritional requirements that will grow only under specific conditions and refuses to thrive on ordinary media.
  • The metaphor is exact: the organism is "hard to please" about its food, just as a fastidious diner is hard to please about his.
  • Familiar fastidious organisms include Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Haemophilus influenzae, which demand enriched media for laboratory culture.
4. Obsolete sense: causing disgust, loathsome (historical)
  • When first borrowed into English in the fifteenth century, fastidious meant "causing disgust, repulsive, loathsome" — the passive sense, describing a thing that makes one squeamish, so that a "fastidious disease" was a disgusting one.
  • In the seventeenth century the sense rotated 180 degrees to the active meaning: the word came to describe the person who feels the disgust — filled with distaste for the imperfect.
  • The obsolete sense is the historical key to the modern word: it preserves the Latin core of "disgust, loathing" that modern fastidiousness still quietly contains.
5. The semantic bridge
  • Latin fastīdium "loathing, disgust" (from fastus "haughtiness, disdain", shaped by taedium "weariness") named the revulsion of the proud toward anything beneath their standard.
  • Refined choosiness and revulsion are the same emotion at different intensities: the fastidious person's exacting standard is a low-grade disgust at the imperfect, the unclean, the wrong.
  • The modern word keeps the whole Latin complex alive — pride, weariness, disgust, and fussy discrimination folded into one adjective of exacting taste.
Register and usage notes
  • Fastidious is formal and literary; in informal speech "picky", "fussy", and "choosy" often replace it.
  • It differs from meticulous (positive thoroughness), scrupulous (moral exactness), and punctilious (formal etiquette) by adding the flavour of disgust and difficulty — the fastidious person finds it hard to be pleased.
  • Common collocations: "fastidious about", "fastidious in", "a fastidious palate", "fastidious readers".
