- [16th c. (c. 1520–1590)] — Borrowed into Early Modern English from Middle Dutch/Low German wagen to designate heavy four-wheeled continental freight conveyances — [perception: advanced mercantile shipping technology superior to rustic native two-wheeled carts]
- [17th c. (c. 1600–1700)] — Spellings wagon and waggon proliferated as regular cross-country carrier services connected London with provincial trading centers — [perception: indispensable commercial lifeline of national trade, heavy transport, and budget passenger transit]
- [18th c. (c. 1700–1800)] — The double-g spelling waggon was codified by Dr. Samuel Johnson in his 1755 Dictionary, reflecting standard British orthographic practice after short vowels — [perception: formal, prestigious orthography for freight transport throughout the British Empire]
- [19th c. Industrial & Victorian Era] — Adopted into the expanding British railway industry for open and covered freight rolling stock (goods waggon), while American English standardized on single-g wagon — [perception: industrial and agricultural staple across Britain, contrasting with American frontier covered wagons]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Usage] — British standard writing shifted predominantly to wagon, leaving waggon as a deliberate historical, antiquarian, military, or literary variant (such as Tolkien's Shire prose) — [perception: evoking nostalgic rural heritage, classic literature, and historical craftsmanship]
