The deep ancestry of the Indo-European root. The verb wane and its participial form waning descend from a very old Indo-European family expressing lack, emptiness, and deficiency. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root is usually given as *h₁wenh₁- "to lack, be deficient, be empty," a root that spread across the ancient languages: in Sanskrit it yields ūnā "deficient, incomplete"; in Latin it yields vānus "empty, vain, idle" (source of English vain and vanity) and vānēscere "to disappear, vanish" (source of English vanish and evanescent); and in the Germanic branch it produced the adjective *wanaz "lacking, deficient, wanting," the direct ancestor of English wan (pale, sickly — literally "deficient in color") and want (a lack, and hence desire). The same root underlies Old Norse vant "wanting" and modern German Wahn (in its older sense of "emptiness, futility").

From adjective to verb: the Germanic step. From the Proto-Germanic adjective *wanaz was built a denominative verb, *wanōną, meaning "to make or become deficient, to diminish, to grow less." This is the verb that the Germanic daughter languages all inherit: Old High German wanōn "to diminish," Old Norse vana "to lack," Old Frisian wania, and Old English wanian, attested from the earliest Anglo-Saxon texts (the 8th century onward). Old English wanian belongs to the second weak conjugation, a class that characteristically denotes a change of state, and its meaning is broad but stable: "to lessen, diminish, decline, decrease, fade." The moon was already the archetypal subject in Old English literature and learning; the verb is used of the moon's shrinking phase in the Old English glosses, in Anglo-Saxon computus texts that calculated the date of Easter, and in poetry where the transience of earthly things is compared to the waning of the moon.

The moon as the fixed image. Throughout the medieval period the pairing wax and wane (Old English weaxan and wanian) was a fixed collocation, inherited directly into Middle English as waxen and wanen. The image dominated calendars, almanacs, and medical treatises, where the moon's phases governed blood-letting, planting, and weather-lore. Middle English preserved the form wanen (12th–15th centuries) with the same meaning, and the participle waning (Middle English waninge) is recorded from the early 13th century. By the late medieval period the moon is so thoroughly the standard subject that the verbal and participial senses of "decrease" generalize outward: the day wanes at dusk, the tide wanes with the moon, a wound wanes as it heals, and a person's strength wanes with age.

The Renaissance and literary extension. In the 16th and 17th centuries the participle waning becomes a favorite instrument of literary and rhetorical pathos. Shakespeare exploits it repeatedly — "my powers are waning," "the waning age," and the melancholy progressions of the sonnets in which beauty and life are described as waning toward death. Because the word still carries its lunar frame, a "waning" thing is not simply decreasing; it is retreating from a former fullness, a former brightness, and the word therefore acquires its modern elegiac overtone: it names the visible approach of an end. This is exactly the tone Tolkien draws on when he writes that the company "were come at the waning of the year": the season itself is a moon-phase, light and life receding into winter.

Scientific consolidation and modern shifts. From the 17th century onward astronomy formalized the sense: the technical terms waxing and waning crescent and gibbous entered textbooks, and "waning" became the precise word for the decreasing illuminated fraction of the lunar disc. In the 18th and 19th centuries the figurative uses broadened into such set phrases as "waning years," "waning strength," and the fixed idiom "on the wane," while geology and geography borrowed the participle for retreating glaciers and falling waters. In the 20th and 21st centuries the word has quietly re-entered science in a new guise: immunology and epidemiology describe "waning immunity" and "waning antibody levels," the gradual loss of protection that became a household phrase during the COVID-19 pandemic. The sound shape of the word has been remarkably stable — Old English wanian → Middle English wanen → Modern wane, with the participle waning (/ˈweɪnɪŋ/) unchanged in its final form for over seven hundred years. Only the semantic frame has widened, carrying the old lunar image of gradual, inevitable, and quietly mournful decrease from the night sky into almost every sphere of human decline.
