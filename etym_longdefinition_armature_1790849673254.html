The noun armature denotes any protective covering, structural framework, internal skeleton, or functional apparatus that provides support, defense, or operational integrity to an organism, work of art, mechanical system, or conceptual structure. Originating as a term for military armor and weaponry, the word has undergone extensive metaphorical, artistic, and technological expansion, becoming an indispensable concept across visual arts, engineering, biology, and literary semiotics.

An exhaustive survey of the polysemic domains of armature reveals its diverse functional and conceptual applications:

  • Sculpture and Visual Arts: In sculpture and plastic arts, an armature is the internal framework or skeletal support—typically constructed of pliable wire, wood, metal rods, or piping—around which a sculptor molds clay, plaster, wax, or papier-mâché. The armature prevents the soft modeling material from sagging, cracking, or collapsing under its own weight before curing or casting. By extension in architecture, it refers to iron or timber bracing that reinforces structural stonework or stained-glass windows.
  • Electrical and Mechanical Engineering: In electrical engineering and electromagnetism, an armature is the power-producing component of an electric motor or generator. It consists of a coil of wire wound around an iron core that rotates within a magnetic field (in a rotor) or remains stationary (in a stator), interacting with the magnetic flux to convert mechanical energy into electrical current, or vice versa. In relays and solenoids, the armature is the movable iron piece attracted by the electromagnet to make or break an electrical circuit.
  • Biology and Zoology (Anatomical Defense): In comparative anatomy and zoology, armature designates the natural defensive armor, weaponry, or protective structures of animals and plants. This includes the bony plates of an armadillo, the shell of a tortoise, the quills of a porcupine, the horns of a stag, the stinging tentacles of cnidarians, or the thorns and spines of protective flora.
  • Linguistics, Syntax, and Structuralism: In structural linguistics, literary theory, and semiotics (as employed by Roland Barthes in describing Philippe Sollers's experimental prose), armature metaphorically designates the rigid foundational architecture, grammatical skeleton, or syntactic framework that underpins human language (such as the sacred subject-predicate nexus). When experimental literature attacks the "armature of syntax," it shatters the structural scaffolding that organizes words into conventional, normative meaning.
  • Anthropology and Mythological Analysis: In structural anthropology (notably in the structural mythology of Claude Lévi-Strauss), the armature of a myth denotes its invariant combinatory properties—the formal structural scaffolding and relational network of elements that remain constant even when the specific characters or thematic details (the "code" and "message") transform across different cultures.

The underlying semantic bridge across all these disciplines is the concept of an integral structural foundation or protective harness (Latin armatura): whether holding up a clay figure, conducting electromagnetic current, protecting a living creature, or structuring a grammatical sentence, an armature is the indispensable core that gives form, strength, and operational coherence to the whole.
