Shrank is the simple past tense of the strong verb shrink, and in the literary context — Gollum peering into the black passage beyond the goblins’ back door in J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit — it means “drew back, recoiled, withdrew abruptly in fear or dread”: “He peered in, and shrank back.” The word is doubly apt, because Gollum’s recoil is not merely a step backward but a physical contracting — the whole body drawing together and diminishing, as if the creature tried to make itself small enough to escape whatever stirred in the passage before him. The root sense of shrink is “to shrivel, contract, draw together,” and that ancient physical meaning is still alive in the verb’s figurative use: fear shrinks the body as frost shrinks fruit, and shrank therefore conveys both the motion of withdrawal and the emotional cringing behind it — a reflexive, serpent-quick pulling-in, the very opposite of a deliberate retreat. The tone is instinctive rather than rational: Gollum does not decide to withdraw; his body contracts at the sight, the way a snail’s flesh draws back into its shell. Because shrink is a strong verb, its past tense is irregular: shrink — shrank — shrunk, with shrunken as the participial adjective. Modern usage shows a well-documented tendency to pull shrunk into the past-tense slot (“he shrunk back”) and even shrunken in some dialects, but standard literary English keeps shrank as the preterite and shrunk as the past participle — the form Tolkien uses is the grammatically conservative one, which suits his deliberately archaic, storybook register. Core senses and usages:
  • Physical contraction (the root sense): to become smaller, to contract, to shrivel or draw together — cloth shrinking when washed, flesh shrinking from cold, fruit shrivelling; in the past tense, shrank: “the sweater shrank in the wash.”
  • Reflexive recoil (the figurative sense in the passage): to draw back from something frightening, distasteful, or repellent — to “shrink from” danger, duty, or the touch of the unclean; shrank back fuses the physical contraction with the emotional withdrawal.
  • To avoid or refuse from aversion: “she did not shrink from the challenge” — the verb takes a “from” complement and denotes reluctance born of distaste rather than of incapacity.
  • Transitive: to cause to contract: “to shrink wool,” “to shrink-wrap” (a product in tight plastic film), “the cold shrank the metal” — the participle shrunk/shrunken is common here.
  • Economics and business jargon: a shrinking economy, shrinking margins, shrinkflation (a coinage for products getting smaller while prices stay the same); retail “shrinkage” is inventory lost to theft and damage.
  • Slang noun “shrink” (psychiatrist): twentieth-century American slang, clipped from “head-shrinker,” itself from the practice of head-shrinking trophies; the psychiatrist “compresses” the troubled mind. The noun is unrelated in origin to the verb’s grammar but is a genuine modern sense of the same word.
  • Idioms and set phrases: “shrinking violet” (an excessively timid person), “shrink from nothing,” “shrank into himself” — the body-metaphor of drawing inward runs through them all.
Polysemy and historical bridge: every modern sense grows from the same physical image — the drawing-together of a body or substance into smaller compass. The thirteenth-century English idiom “to shrink from” danger or the unclean transfers the flesh’s reflex of contracting (as from cold, wet, or disgust) to the mind’s reflex of withdrawal: the body that shrivels when dried is the same body that draws itself small in fear. That ancient bridge between physical contraction and emotional cowering is exactly what makes Tolkien’s shrank back so potent — Gollum does not step away so much as compress, making himself smaller at the threshold, and the reader feels both the fear and the physical diminution in the single word. The slang “shrink” (1966) extends the same root image to the mind itself — the head-shrinker who reduces mental distress — while the modern economic uses (“shrinkflation,” “shrinkage”) keep the contraction sense squarely in the material world. Among its near-synonyms, recoiled is the closest, but it implies a springing-back jerk, as from a snake or a blow, with more violence and less cowering; flinched is a small, momentary start of the body without actual retreat; winced is the response to pain, chiefly facial; drew back is measured and deliberate, the motion of a careful person; withdrew is formal, controlled, and unemotional; and shied describes a horse’s sudden, swerving aversion. Only shrank back fuses withdrawal with diminution — the sense of the body compressing itself in fear, becoming smaller as it retreats — which is precisely the cowering, self-effacing motion Tolkien assigns to Gollum at the threshold of the goblin-guarded passage.
