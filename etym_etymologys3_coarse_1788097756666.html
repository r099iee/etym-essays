I. The PIE root and the Latin verb

The story of coarse is unusual: the word is not a native Germanic adjective at all, but the fossilised adjective use of the French and Latin noun course. The chain begins with the PIE root *ḱers- “to run”, the source of Latin currere “to run” and its vast family — cursus, cursor, curriculum, current, currency, corridor, courier, corsair — and, by a debated s-mobile variant, of Germanic *hursa- “horse”. From the verbal idea “to run” comes the abstract noun cursus “a running, a course, an order, a sequence”, attested in the prose of Cicero and Caesar; the cursus honorum of Roman politics, the curriculum, and the cursus of legal procedure all grow from this root. Latin then passes cursus down the Romance ladder: Old French cors, then cours, “a course, a running, an order of proceeding”, which Middle English borrows in the 13th century as cours/course — a course of a meal, a course of action, a racecourse, a course of study.

II. The adjectival turn in Middle English

In Middle English the noun course begins to be used attributively in phrases of the pattern “of course”, “in course”: goods, cloth, fare, or work of the ordinary course — the everyday, unexceptional run of things. The pivotal shift occurs when “ordinary” begins to mean “common, not choice”: by the 15th century course/cors as an adjective means “ordinary, base, of inferior quality” — coarse cloth, coarse bread, coarse fare. The grammatical engine is conversion: noun → attributive use → adjective. The old neutral sense survives, significantly, in the phrase “of course” itself, which has entirely lost its connection to the adjective it once bred.

III. The spelling split and sound change

From the 16th century the adjective acquires its distinctive spelling coarse, separating it visually from the noun course; the -a- spelling takes over during the 16th century and is standard by 1600, and the Oxford English Dictionary treats “coarse, adj.” as a word in its own right, distinct from “course, n.”. The pronunciation meanwhile follows the general English development of Middle English short u before r + consonant: ME cours /kurs/ lengthens and lowers (the same pre-r change seen in court), giving the modern /ɔː/ — identical, of course, to course. The Great Vowel Shift era thus produced a fine trap: two words, one sound, two spellings, and two careers.

IV. Semantic drift in Early Modern and Victorian English

  • Quality: “coarse fare”, “coarse cloth” — of low grade, rough, not fine; the primary sense of the 16th–17th centuries and the bridge to everything else.
  • Texture: coarse sand, coarse salt, coarse hair, coarse skin — physical roughness, which from the 17th century rivals and then surpasses the quality sense in everyday use.
  • Morals and manners: coarse behaviour, coarse language, coarse wit — the moral sense, from the 17th century, links physical roughness with social and ethical unrefinement; “coarse” becomes the polite English word for indecent or obscene talk (“coarse humour”, “coarse jesting”).
  • Class colouring: in 18th–19th-century usage “coarse” carries covert class judgement — the coarse hands, coarse food, and coarse speech of working people as seen from the drawing-room; Dickens and Gaskell use the word with a mixture of sympathy and condescension, and novelists of the 19th century made “coarse” a touchstone of the respectable vocabulary of taste.
  • Technical branching: coarse fish and coarse fishing (Britain; a legal and sporting category for non-salmonid freshwater fish, from the 19th century); coarse adjustment (microscopy); coarse thread (engineering); coarse aggregate (concrete); coarse-grained (geology, photography, and figuratively a blunt manner); coarse grades in cookery (coarse salt, coarse-ground pepper).
  • Archaic: “coarse weather” (rough, stormy — paralleling the sea sense of “rough”) is attested but obsolete.

V. Modern register

Today coarse is unmarked for texture, mildly technical for fishing and science, and gently euphemistic for indecency — “coarse language” is still the school report's word. Its moral force has softened since the Victorians: where “coarse” once implied a whole class of brutishness, modern English prefers vulgar, crude, or rough, leaving coarse with a slightly old-fashioned, literary colour. The word's history is a perfect miniature of semantic descent: from “of the ordinary run” (neutral) to “common” (social) to “rough and rude” (moral and tactile) — the whole ladder climbed on the back of a noun that never stopped meaning “a running”.
