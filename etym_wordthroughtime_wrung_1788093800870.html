- Old English (9th–11th c.): wringan "to press, squeeze, twist" — a strong verb of physical torsion, used of pressing and twisting, without special emotional colour
- Middle English: narrows toward moisture — to wring clothes and sponges and, in medical texts, to wring out humours; the old strong-verb past wrang/wrungen settles as wrung
- 15th–16th c.: "to wring one's hands" appears as the fixed gesture of grief and helplessness — the vocabulary of miracle plays, tragedy and devotion; the word takes on emotional weight
- 16th–17th c.: extortion senses flourish — to wring money, a confession, an answer out of someone; the twist becomes an instrument of pressure
- 18th–19th c.: "wringing wet" becomes a common intensifier for drenched clothes; domestic "wringers" mechanize the laundry image; Proverbs 30:33 (KJV) had already fixed the physical sense — "the wringing of the nose bringeth forth blood"
- 19th–20th c.: "heart-wringing" and "wrung with grief" fix the emotional metaphor in melodrama, poetry and journalism
- 20th–21st c.: "hand-wringing" turns the old gesture of grief into a noun of mockery — anxious, ineffectual fretting in editorials; to "wring your neck" is the colloquial threat
- Today: literal daily use for cloth and necks; formal or literary use for extraction and affliction; the word still sounds like the twist it describes
