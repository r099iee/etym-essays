oar — noun; a long pole with a flat blade at one end, used for rowing or steering a boat. Unlike a paddle, which is held freely in the hands, an oar is worked against a fixed point on the boat — the rowlock, thole, or oar-port — so that the boat itself provides the fulcrum and the rower's pull is converted into forward thrust. In the source context, Peter says "Ed and I will take an oar each" — i.e., each boy will take one of the two rowing poles and pull it as part of the crew rowing the boat.

Primary sense — the rowing implement. An oar consists of a shaft (loom) ending in a flattened blade; the rower grips the loom, the blade is submerged, and the oar pivots in its rowlock, sweeping the blade through the water to drive the boat forward. Oars are traditionally made of ash or spruce and are distinguished from paddles by their mechanical use of a fulcrum.

  • Parts: blade (the flat end that pushes water), shaft/loom (the long middle), handle/grip (the inboard end), and sometimes a leather collar or button where the shaft meets the rowlock.
  • Mechanics: the rowlock is the fulcrum; the oar is a lever — pulling the handle inboard swings the blade through the water, and each stroke is a lever action with the load (water resistance) at the blade.
  • Rowing vocabulary: a single rower with two oars sculls; a crew with one oar each sweeps; "to feather" an oar is to turn the blade flat on the recovery so it slices back without catching air.
  • In context: in the passage, two boys each take an oar while the D.L.F. steers — a small boat crew in the classic division of labour: two oarsmen, one steersman, and the girls in the bows shouting directions.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The noun is remarkably stable, but it anchors a set of idioms and extended uses:

  • Idiom — "to put/stick one's oar in": to interfere, to meddle in another's affairs — from the image of grabbing an oar and thrusting it into someone else's rowing. This is the commonest figurative use of the word.
  • Idiom — "to rest on one's oars": to stop working for a while, to pause after effort — from a rower stopping to rest, holding the oars idle.
  • Idiom — "to feather one's oars": to turn the blade flat on the recovery stroke; also (by confusion with "feather one's nest") sometimes wrongly used for self-enrichment.
  • Technical uses: in heraldry, an oar appears as a charge; in biology, "oar" appears in names like oarfish (a long ribbon-like fish); in sailing history, oars were the motive power of galleys, longboats, and galleons' boats.
  • Steering sense: a steering oar or "scull" over the stern is an oar used to steer, showing the word's secondary navigational function.

Historical bridge & semantic reasoning. The word has changed remarkably little in 4,000 years. It descends from Proto-Indo-European *h₁erh₁- "to row, propel" — the root of Greek eretmós (oar), Sanskrit aritra- (oar), and Latin rēmus (oar) — via Proto-Germanic *airō to Old English ār (attested 7th c.). The identity of the implement across cultures made the word a stable carrier of its meaning: an oar is an oar whether on a Viking drakkar, a Venetian gondola, or a modern rowing shell.
