CHAIN 1 (the Germanic core — the direct ancestor of the English word): Proto-Germanic *sendra- 'slag, dross, scoria' (reconstructed; no secure PIE etymon has been established — the occasional comparison with PIE *sendʰ- 'solid, coagulated, curdled' remains speculative, so the stage beyond Germanic is genuinely unknown) → Old English sinder 'iron dross, slag, scoria' (attested; a forge-word of the smithy) → Middle English sinder 'cinder, slag' (attested; survives today in northern English dialects as sinder/sunder, meaning an ember or cinder) → English cinder — the form reshaped from the 13th–14th c. onward under the influence of Anglo-Norman/Old French cendre 'ashes' (from Latin cinis, see CHAIN 2); the initial s- shifted to c- in respellings of the 16th c., and the meaning migrated from metallic dross toward the fire-remnants of the hearth → English cinders (plural; the idiomatic mass-form for the burnt, brittle fragments of coal, wood, or bone; source of compounds cinder block, cinder track, cinder cone) → CINDERS CHAIN 2 (the Latin contaminating source — a neighbour, not a second root of the compound): PIE *kenis- 'ashes, dust, powder' (reconstructed; cognate with Greek κόνις konis 'dust') → Latin cinis (genitive cineris) 'ashes; the ashes of the dead; the dead themselves' (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) → Vulgar Latin *cinerem (accusative) > Old French cendre 'ashes' (attested 9th–12th c.); also Cendrillon 'little ash-girl' → English Cinderella → Anglo-Norman cendre, borrowed into Middle English in the 13th–14th c., where it blended with the native sinder to produce the hybrid cinder (see CHAIN 1) NOTE ON THE BLEND: English cinder is not descended from Latin cinis. It is the native Germanic word sinder, rewritten and reshaped under heavy French influence — the spelling shifted to c-, and the meaning widened from metallic dross to all burnt remnants. The two chains meet in the Middle English scribes' hands and fuse into the modern word.
