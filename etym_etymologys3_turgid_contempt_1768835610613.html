Turgid: a career of swelling

The biography of turgid is short in time but rich in pressure. Unlike most of its Latin neighbours, turgidus has no secure Proto-Indo-European pedigree: scholars have floated connections with roots meaning 'to twist' or 'to contract,' but none commands general assent, and the verb turgēre, 'to be swollen,' appears in Latin as an isolated formation without clear cognates elsewhere in the family. The word's history therefore begins in Latin itself, with an unknown origin, and its whole career is a career of swelling.

  • The physical birth: from turgēre Latin derived the adjective turgidus, 'swollen, distended,' and English borrowed it in the early 17th century as turgid, at first strictly physical: a turgid vein, a turgid limb, a body part engorged with fluid, stretched taut by internal pressure. From the same verb came turgescere, 'to begin to swell' (English turgescent, of something in the very act of swelling), and turgor, 'a swelling, a distension.'
  • The scientific retention: turgor found a technical home in botany, where the turgor of plant cells — their internal fluid pressure — is still measured today; a cell 'turgid' with water stands firm, and wilting is the loss of turgor. The physical sense thus never died, though it retreated to the laboratory.
  • The rhetorical turn: the metaphorical transfer came early and proved permanent. From the body's swelling the word moved to anything puffed up beyond natural measure — swollen language, bombastic and inflated ('turgid prose'), swollen rhetoric, a style taut with words and short of content. The 18th century's critics of ornateness, and the 19th century's reviewers, made turgid a standard term of abuse for over-written prose: style as a limb engorged with fluid, at the point of strain.
A caution is essential. Latin also possessed tumēre, 'to swell,' from PIE *teuh₂-, 'to swell': from it descend tumid, tumor, tumescent, and — through the Germanic 'swollen finger,' *thūmōn — English thumb. The two families, turgēre and tumēre, rhyme in sense but not in blood; they are near-twins in meaning and distinct in origin, a standing warning against assuming that Latin's two great swelling-verbs must be one.

  • In the passage: 'the air between them was turgid with contempt and jealousy.' The metaphor is almost tactile: the air is not merely heavy or tense but swollen, physically thickened, distended by the mass of suppressed emotion pressing outward against the atmosphere like fluid against a membrane. The craftsmen 'stood about like beggars, their families clustered in silent groups,' and the turgidity is the accumulated pressure of that silent, resentful crowd — an air so full of unspoken hatred that it has become a substance, a taut body on the verge of bursting. The word's physical history does the work: we feel the pressure, the strain, the near-rupture.
  • Sound and sense: the initial tur- of the English word (from Latin turg-) is itself a small onomatopoeia of fullness — the syllable bulges in the mouth; and the modern dominant sense, of swollen style, keeps the whole career of the word alive in a single utterance: turgid prose is prose swollen, and the passage's atmosphere is prose made flesh.


Contempt: the word that cuts

The story of contempt is a story of cutting. Its deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *temh₁- (also written *tem-), 'to cut' — a root of wide and sharp progeny: it gave Greek τέμνω (témnō), 'to cut,' and from the Greek cutting-words came a whole English family — tome (a 'slice,' a cut-off volume), atom ('uncuttable,' the indivisible unit of matter), anatomy (a 'cutting up,' dissection), epitome (a 'cutting short,' a summary), appendectomy, tonsillectomy.

  • From cutting to despising: Latin temnere, 'to slight, to despise,' is traditionally connected with this cutting-root — to despise is to cut a thing off from consideration, to sever its worth — though the semantic bridge is not fully explained and some scholars treat temnere as of uncertain origin. The ancient Romans themselves linked the words, and the connection is still repeated in modern dictionaries with caution. What is not in doubt is the formation: con-, the intensive prefix, joined to temnere produced contemnere, 'to scorn utterly, to despise completely.'
  • The Roman verdict: from the participle contemptus — 'scorned, despised' — Latin made a noun, contemptus, 'scorn, contempt.' The word belonged to the formal speech of Roman law, politics, and morality: contempt was a judgement passed, an estimate of worthlessness recorded against a person or thing. The verb's family grew: contemptibilis, 'deserving contempt' (English contemptible, 14th c.), contumelia, 'an insult, an affront' (English contumely, 14th c.), and the adjective contemptuous (17th c.).
English took contempt in the 14th century, through Old French, as the formal attitude of scorning worthlessness. Its subsequent career is a slow institutionalisation.

  • The legal hardening (16th century onward): 'contempt of court' gave the word its coldest, most judicial weight. Contempt became not only private feeling but public judgement — conduct defying the authority of a court, punishable by the court itself. The Roman formality of contemptus found its true heir: a recorded verdict, with sanctions.
  • The moral and social spread (17th–19th centuries): the word became the vocabulary of class and refinement — to be beneath contempt was the floor of worthlessness; to treat something with contempt was the settled, superior disregard of the cultivated for the rude, the powerful for the weak. Its near-synonyms split by temperature: scorn is openly expressive, theatrical disgust; disdain is haughty, aristocratic aloofness; contempt is the quiet, final judgement that cuts the other down to nothing.
  • In the passage: 'the air between them was turgid with contempt and jealousy.' Contempt is the cold element in that swollen air — the craftsmen's contempt for their judges (and perhaps the judges' for them), a silent, settled, cutting hatred. Where turgid gives the feeling a body — a taut, straining mass — contempt gives it its edge: the emotion that severs worth, the judgement passed and held in the silence of the crowd. The word's long history of legal finality makes that silence feel like a sentence.
The sound changes are minimal: PIE *temh₁- → Greek témnō (with the laryngeal vanishing) and → Latin tem-n- (zero grade, *-mh₁- → -m-); the intensive prefix con- plus temnere yields contemnere; the participle contemptus gives Old French contempt and English contempt (14th c.), where the final -t, silent in French, is fully pronounced in English, giving the word its hard, cutting ending.
