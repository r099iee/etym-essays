The word AFOOT is transparently a compound — the preposition "on" (reduced to the unstressed prefix a-) plus the noun "foot" — and its whole history is the history of a simple phrase turning into a fixed adverb and then leaping from the physical world into the language of plots, plans, and foreboding. Tracing it is tracing a small masterpiece of everyday grammatical change.
  • The Indo-European and Germanic foundations: the noun foot reaches back to Proto-Indo-European *pṓds, the great "foot" word of the family, seen in Greek poús, Latin pēs, Sanskrit pád-, and Lithuanian pėda. In Proto-Germanic it becomes *fōts, and in Old English fōt, with the long rounded vowel that the word still shows in its modern pronunciation and its uninflected plural feet (a relic of an ancient umlaut plural). The preposition on goes back, through Proto-Germanic *ana, to PIE *h₁én "in, on". These are among the most ancient and stable words in English, and it is from two of the plainest of them that afoot is built.
  • The Old English phrase on fōte: in Old English the way to say someone was walking was to use the set phrase on fōte, literally "on foot", as opposed to riding. The phrase is purely physical: a messenger arrives on fote, a traveller journeys on fote, and the phrase is common in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and in homiletic prose. At this stage there is no trace of the modern figurative sense — nothing "is on foot" in the abstract; people simply walk.
  • The Middle English contraction: as Old English shades into Middle English, the unstressed preposition on is worn down to a single vowel and the phrase fuses into the adverb a fote (later afoot), in exactly the same pattern that gives aboard (on board), asleep (on sleep), afloat (on float), aground, abed, and astir. The contraction is phonological, driven by the unstressed position of the preposition before the stressed noun; it is not a change of meaning. For centuries afoot and on foot live side by side as interchangeable ways of saying "walking", and afoot also develops the extended physical sense "moving, in motion" — an army afoot, a wandering crew afoot — because what walks is obviously in motion.
  • The sixteenth-century figurative leap: the decisive change comes in the sixteenth century, when the physical sense "in motion" is transferred to events. From c. 1560 the word begins to mean "in progress, in preparation, astir", said of undertakings: a conspiracy is afoot, a war is afoot, changes are afoot. The bridge is the old physical idea: what is on foot is moving, and what is moving is happening — the motion of a walker becomes the motion of events. Notably, the transferred sense is from the start used of things that are stirring below the surface, and the question "what is afoot?" becomes a standard way of asking what hidden plans are being made. The physical walking sense does not vanish at once, but it recedes: by the end of the seventeenth century afoot is predominantly figurative.
  • Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries: the word now belongs to the language of politics, detection, and adventure. Shakespeare uses it of plots and alarms; the drama of the seventeenth century is full of "something is afoot" at moments of intrigue. The register is impersonal and slightly ominous from the beginning — one says that something is afoot, never that someone is afoot in the modern sense, and the agent is always left unnamed. In the nineteenth century the word is common in the sensational novel and the historical novel, and it carries a faintly archaic, storyteller's flavour even then.
  • Tolkien and the modern literary register: that archaic, storyteller's flavour is exactly what Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit. The Lord of the Eagles is "filled with curiosity to know what was afoot" — and the word does three things at once: it tells the reader that significant events are stirring in the world below, it keeps the instigators deliberately anonymous (the goblins and wolves are not named in the sentence), and it plants the mystery that the eagles themselves must investigate. The word is the hinge of the paragraph: curiosity about what is afoot drives the whole rescue flight. Tolkien, as a philologist, would have known perfectly well that the word meant literally "on foot" and derived its force from the shift to "in motion, in progress"; the physical pun is quietly present beneath the figurative use.
  • Modern distribution: in contemporary English afoot survives chiefly in literary and journalistic use. It is the stock word of mystery and suspense ("something is afoot in the old house"), of political reporting ("major changes are afoot"), and of fantasy fiction, where it helps create an archaic, high-register narrative voice. The literal sense "on foot, walking" is now essentially obsolete except in deliberately archaic writing; the word's modern home is entirely in the world of hidden plans and stirring events.
  • Linguistic interest: afoot is a compact illustration of two processes working together: phonological reduction (on foot → a-fote → afoot, with the prefix a- still recognizable in its dozen siblings) and semantic transfer (walking → in motion → in progress → secretly stirring). Because the compound is transparent, English speakers can re-derive it at need, which is why the word has survived at all as a fixed adverb while other on-phrases have drifted apart.
The career of afoot is thus a walk from the concrete to the conspiratorial: an ordinary phrase for walking, contracted by sound change, generalized to "in motion", transferred to events in the sixteenth century, fixed in the language of plots by Shakespeare and the dramatists, kept alive in literary and fantastic prose as an archaic token of mystery, and still doing its quiet, ominous work whenever a storyteller wants to say that something is stirring — without saying whose feet are moving.
