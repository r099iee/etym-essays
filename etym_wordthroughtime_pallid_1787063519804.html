- Latin inheritance - Latin "pallidus" ("pale, wan") grows from "pallēre", "to be pale" — the colour of fear, illness and grief in Roman literature.
- Renaissance borrowing - English takes "pallid" directly from Latin in the 1580s as the literary doublet of the French-derived "pale".
- Faces first - For its first two centuries in English, pallid chiefly describes bloodless, fearful, sickly or grieving faces.
- Light and colour - In the 18th–19th centuries the word extends to weak light and washed-out colour: "a pallid sun", "pallid grey".
- A critical weapon - By the 19th century critics apply it to art and writing: a "pallid style" means feeble, bland, uninspired — paleness of quality, not just of skin.
- Today - Pallid has three senses in parallel (unhealthy face, dim light, weak art), always suggesting something that should have life and colour but has lost it — as in the eerie "pallid light" of the hobbit's globe.
