CE: Classical Roman Empire] - originated as the colloquial administrative label servus a manu (slave at hand / slave for handwriting), developing into the imperial title amanuensis for skilled enslaved or freed scribes who managed confidential Caesar correspondence and legal decrees - [perception: respected for technical literacy and trusted discretion, yet legally regarded as household property or subordinate freedmen]
- [4th–15th c.: Late Antiquity & Medieval Europe] - survived in ecclesiastical Latin and monastic administration, designating private secretaries to bishops, popes, and royal chancellors who took dictation of theological treatises and papal bulls - [perception: highly esteemed as pious, learned guardians of sacred orthodoxy and Latin prose]
- [16th–17th c.: Renaissance Humanism & Early Modern English] - borrowed directly into English (c. 1600) during the classical revival, used by Francis Bacon, John Milton, and royal diplomats to denote a cultured humanist secretary or transcriber - [perception: associated with elite intellectual companionship, scholarly diligence, and humanist erudition]
- [18th–19th c.: Georgian & Victorian Literary Era] - became the standard literary designation for the indispensable assistants of prolific or infirm authors (such as Dr. Samuel Johnson, Sir Walter Scott, and Henry James) - [perception: romanticized as the devoted, silent witness to literary genius and creative labor]
- [20th–21st c.: Modern & Contemporary Era] - largely displaced in commerce by secretary, stenographer, and digital technology, but officially preserved in British/Commonwealth university disability accommodation (exam scribes) and used stylistically in literary prose - [perception: viewed either as a precise academic term for a physical scribe or as a delightful, evocative archaism signaling literary refinement]
