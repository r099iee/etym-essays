The root that made trees and truth. No English word carries its Indo-European root more openly than Dryad. Its ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European word *dóru, meaning "tree" or "wood," a noun that in the reconstructed language had three forms by vowel-grading: *dóru, *dréw-, *drú-. The root was at once material and moral: since wood is strong and stiff, the same syllable came to mean "firm, solid, true," so that across the family one finds the tree-words and the truth-words growing from the same stem. The Vedic poets said dāru for wood; the speakers of Old Church Slavonic said drěvo; the Germanic tribes said *trewą, the word that became English tree; the Latins said dūrus, "hard," from *dru-ró-, "firm as wood" — the ancestor of English durable, endure, and duration. From the same *dru- the Greeks of the Homeric age said drŷs, "oak" — and, in the oldest stratum, simply "tree," because for a Greek the oak was the tree, the sacred wood of Zeus whose rustling leaves spoke oracles at Dodona, where priestesses listened to the god in the motion of the leaves. The root thus carried a whole philosophy in a syllable: the tree was the image of firmness, endurance, and truth.

From oak to oak-nymph. In classical Greek, drŷs "oak" gave birth to a family of derivatives. The compound déndron "tree" (a secondary -n- stem built on *dréw-n-) built the scientific vocabulary of dendrites, dendrology, and rhododendron; the woodland word drymós meant oak-forest; and the noun dryás, formed from drŷs with the feminine suffix -άς, named a tree-nymph — literally "she of the oak." The Greeks conceived the dryas as the tree's own life made person: the nymph was born with the tree, shared its growth, its beauty, and its fate. Some dryads were long-lived forest spirits ranging through the woods, but the hamadryás (hamadryad, from háma "together with" and dryás) was born and died with her particular tree — the myth of Erysichthon, who cut down a sacred oak and heard the dying nymph cry out from within it, is the darkest dramatization of the bond. This is a religion of trees, not merely a decoration of them: to wound the oak was to wound a divine being.

The loan into Latin and the modern languages. Latin borrowed the word as dryas, genitive dryadis, and the Greeks' tree-soul passed into the Latin world of Ovid and the Roman poets, where dryads, hamadryads, naiads, and oreads populated the literary landscape of the Metamorphoses. English took the word through French — dryade — in the sixteenth century, in the great wave of classical learning that brought the whole vocabulary of Greek and Roman mythology into the language; the plural dryádes passed directly into English Dryads. From the Elizabethans onward the word has been a fixture of English poetry: Shakespeare and the pastoral poets people their woods with dryads; Milton and the neoclassical writers use the word as a standing emblem of the classical countryside; and the Romantics, especially Keats, revived the full mythology of the dryad and the hamadryad as part of their turn back to ancient Greece. The sound-shift along the way is modest — Greek /dryˈas/ to Latin /dryˈas/ to French dryade to English /ˈdɹaɪ.ad/ — but the semantic centre never moved: the word has always meant a tree, made divine.

Cultural diffusion and the oak. The same root travelled outside Greece with Greek culture. The Gaulish druids were read by the Romans as druidēs, "oak-knowers" (dru- "oak" + wid- "to know"), their sacred groves were oak-groves, and the Welsh derwen and Old Irish daur kept the oak-sense alive in the Celtic west. In modern English the oak-connection is faint but real: a Dryad is still, at root, "she of the oak," and the word carries a whisper of the sacred oak grove even in casual modern fantasy usage. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries secularized and broadened the word: in modern fantasy literature and gaming, dryads are simply tree-spirits of any enchanted forest, guardians of the woods; in zoology the "dryad" is a butterfly of oak-wood habitats; in botany Dryas is a genus of mountain avens and "dryad's saddle" a bracket fungus — every modern extension keeps the tree, and often the oak, in view.

Why the word works in Narnia. In the Narnia passage, "Naiads and Dryads they were called," the word does its full historical work. It certifies the reality of a pre-human enchanted world — a world where trees were alive and persons, exactly as the Greeks once believed — and it signals, by its classical form, that this world belongs to a vanished Golden Age, as distant from the modern child as Athens. The older Narnia is "quite different"; the Dryads, like the Fauns and the talking animals, are the fauna of that unrecoverable time. The word is thus not decoration but doctrine: it states the ancient religion of the tree, roots it in the oak, and mourns it, all in a single name. From the PIE syllable for wood and firmness, through the Greek oak-goddess and the Roman poet's wood, to the sixteenth-century loan and the fantasy worlds of today, Dryad has never once stopped meaning the soul of the tree.
