CHAIN: FROM PIE TO BRUISED (6 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *bhreus- "to break, shatter, crack" (reconstructed; accepted in outline by Pokorny, its exact shape debated).
  2. Proto-Germanic *brusjanan "to crush, pound, bruise" (reconstructed iterative verb built with the *-jan- suffix).
  3. Old English brȳsan "to crush, pound, bruise" (attested in Old English, c. 8th–11th centuries; a pounder crushed herbs in a mortar, a smith pounded metal).
  4. Middle English brusen "to bruise, crush" (recorded c. 1300), reinforced at the same period by Old French bruisier "to break, shatter" — itself a loan from a Frankish (Germanic) source, so the same ancient root re-entered English by a Romance door and helped fix the spelling and the sense.
  5. English bruise (verb) "to injure by a blunt blow that ruptures the vessels beneath the skin"; the noun bruise, "the discoloured mark itself", appears in the 17th century.
  6. BRUISED (weak past tense and past participle, regular -ed — the exact form of this card).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The exact shape of the PIE root is not certain: Pokorny gives *bhreus- "to break, shatter", but the Germanic brū-/brus- and the Romance (Frankish) bruk- may reflect variant extensions of the same base.
  • The Old French bruisier (and its descendant débris "broken fragments", from debrisier "to break apart") is a Germanic loan in Romance, not a native Latin development; it interwove with the native English verb in Middle English, and the two histories cannot always be cleanly separated.
  • Latin frūstum "a piece broken off" is possibly related (Pokorny connects it), giving English frustum and frustule, but most dictionaries keep it separate.
  • No secure Indo-Aryan (Sanskrit) cognate of *bhreus- is attested; the family is western Indo-European, Germanic and, via loan, Romance.
