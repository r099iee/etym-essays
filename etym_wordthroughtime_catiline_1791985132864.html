- 63 BCE — the name of a living senator under attack; Cicero's speeches fasten it to the conspiracy within a single year.
- c. 42–40 BCE — Sallust's monograph turns Catiline into a moral exemplum of aristocratic decadence.
- 1st c. CE–Middle Ages — the name survives mainly in the schoolroom, attached to Cicero's In Catilinam and to Sallust.
- 16th c. — English humanists naturalise “Catiline”; it becomes a stock example of sedition in grammar-school Latin.
- 1611 — Ben Jonson's tragedy Catiline His Conspiracy gives the name a stage life as the archetypal aristocratic plotter.
- 17th–18th c. — “a Catiline” is used in pamphlets and parliamentary rhetoric for any suspected traitor; the name becomes a partisan weapon.
- 19th c. — Ibsen's Catilina (1850) and the school curriculum keep it current, now as a romantic rebel as much as a villain.
- 20th–21st c. — chiefly historical and literary; “Catilinarian” survives in the phrase “Catilinarian orations”, and the name is occasionally revived for home-grown insurgents.
