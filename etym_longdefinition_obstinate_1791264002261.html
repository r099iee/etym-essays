obstinate is an adjective describing a person, attitude, action, or even a thing that stubbornly refuses to change, yield, be persuaded, or be removed, persisting in its course despite argument, evidence, entreaty, difficulty, or force. At its core it combines two ideas: firmness of hold and unreasonableness of holding. To call someone obstinate is not merely to say they are firm, which could be praise, but to say their firmness has become a fault — they stand where they should move. In the source sentence, obstinate criminals are those habitual or hardened offenders who will not reform, and who therefore, in that ancient system, could be condemned to slavery as incorrigibles alongside defaulting debtors.

The word applies across human character, debate, behaviour, and physical persistence. We speak of an obstinate child refusing to eat, an obstinate negotiator refusing compromise, an obstinate belief surviving refutation, an obstinate cough, fever, or stain that resists treatment or cleaning. In each case the metaphor is the same: something stands its ground and will not be dislodged. Register is standard and slightly formal; it is stronger and more judgemental than stubborn, more general than intransigent, and less literary than obdurate. It can be used attributively (an obstinate man, an obstinate refusal) and predicatively (he remained obstinate to the end).

Grammatically it grades normally (more obstinate, most obstinate, obstinately, obstinacy). It governs prepositions such as in (obstinate in error), about (obstinate about details), and against older use. Collocations include obstinate refusal, obstinate denial, obstinate silence, obstinate resistance, obstinate problem, obstinate constipation in medicine. Tone is almost always critical or exasperated, though context can flip it toward grudging respect when persistence succeeds.

  • Core character sense: unreasonably stubborn in will. The dominant modern meaning is a moral-psychological judgement: refusing to alter a decision, belief, or behaviour although reason, evidence, or compassion urges change. The semantic bridge from neutral firmness to fault is the idea of standing against (Latin ob-) what ought to move one. Thus obstinate preserves its etymological image of standing in the way.
  • Argumentative and political sense: refusing compromise or persuasion. In debate, diplomacy, and business an obstinate party will not concede points, split differences, or update views. Here the bridge is social: firmness that blocks collective movement becomes obstruction. This is close to intransigent but less technical.
  • Behavioural and disciplinary sense: refractory, hard to manage or reform. As in the slavery passage, obstinate offenders, pupils, soldiers, or animals resist discipline and correction. The bridge is institutional: incorrigibility justifies harsher control. This older legal-moral use survives in phrases like obstinate deserter or obstinate absentee.
  • Medical and physical sense: resisting treatment or removal. Doctors describe obstinate constipation, obstinate eczema, obstinate fever; cleaners curse obstinate stains, engineers curse obstinate bolts. The bridge is metaphorical personification: the body or object is imagined as wilfully holding out. This sense is fully standard and not merely figurative flourish.
  • Abstract and intellectual sense: clinging to ideas. Obstinate prejudice, obstinate illusion, obstinate ignorance, obstinate doubt. Here persistence is cognitive rather than volitional. The historical bridge is theological controversy, where heresy or schism was framed as obstinate adherence to error after correction.
  • Positive-tinged edge: dogged tenacity. Rarely, especially with adverbs like heroically or nobly, obstinate can shade toward praise for endurance, as in obstinate defence of a town or obstinate courage. The bridge is perspective: the same immovability condemned by opponents is celebrated by allies. Dictionaries still mark the core as disapproving, but usage allows this flip.

In short, obstinate moves from people to positions to pathologies while keeping its image of standing-against. Learning it means hearing both firmness and fault: to stand firm can be virtue, to stand merely because one has stood is obstinacy. Modern psychological language might speak of rigidity, reactance, or confirmation bias; obstinate packs that science into a single moral portrait of someone who will not be moved.
