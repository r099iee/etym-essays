Linear derivation chain for FORAYS (single root word):

*peh₂- "to protect, to graze, to pasture, to feed" (Proto-Indo-European; attested across the family in Latin pāscere, Sanskrit pāti) → Proto-Germanic *fōdrą "food for animals, fodder" (o-grade *poh₂- with the suffix *-tro- / *-dr-; the family also gives *fōdaz "food" and *fōdijaną "to feed") → Frankish *fōdar "fodder" (Germanic word adopted into the Gallo-Romance speech of Gaul) → Old French forre / fuerre "fodder, straw, hay" (loanword from Frankish) → Old French forrer "to forage, to provide with fodder, to plunder for provisions" and Anglo-French forrayer "to plunder, to raid" → Middle English forrayen "to plunder, to raid" (verb, 14th c.) → noun foray "a raid" (formed from the verb, 14th–15th c.) → FORAYS (plural noun — the exact form of this card).

Note on the debate: the ultimate root is secure, since the Germanic fodder-words clearly descend from *peh₂- "to protect, graze, feed", but the intermediate spelling history is tangled. English foray shows contamination between the Anglo-French verb forrayer and the French noun form forai "a raid", and the modern spelling with -ay- reflects French usage rather than the older English forray. The semantic bridge from "fodder" to "raid" runs through foraging: the purpose of the raid was to collect forage.
