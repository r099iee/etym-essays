cord is a string or small rope composed of several strands twisted, braided, or woven together, thicker than thread but thinner than cable or hawser. Prototypically it is made of cotton, hemp, jute, sisal, nylon, or gut, and serves to tie, bind, hang, pull, or conduct — from sash cord, blind cord, and apron strings to umbilical cord and spinal cord by anatomical metaphor. In the source passage, bark and fibres are woven into cord, evoking the earliest textile technology: retting bast, rolling fibres on the thigh, and plying them into continuous length.

Technical and figurative senses proliferate while retaining the image of a LONG FLEXIBLE BINDER. Wood is measured in cords, cloth shows corduroy ribs, music strikes a chord, and emotions strike a cord of sympathy.

  • Core textile sense — string/rope: twisted strands for tying parcels, hanging curtains, lacing, bricklaying lines, blind pulls; differs from rope only in diameter and from thread in strength.
  • Clothing and furnishing sense: cord as trimming, piping, drawstring, or corduroy rib — corded velvet with raised ribs; bridge is visual likeness to tiny ropes on fabric surface.
  • Anatomical sense — tendon, cord: umbilical cord linking foetus to placenta, spinal cord within vertebrae, spermatic cord, vocal cords; bridge is long flexible structure conveying matter or vibration; differs in being body tissue, not manufactured.
  • Electrical sense — flex: power cord, extension cord, MIDI cord; insulated copper inside textile or plastic sheath; retains tying/connecting function for energy.
  • Figurative bond sense: cords of love, family ties, to cut the cord, cord of remembrance; anything that binds or restrains; from late 14th century, e.g. cords of sin in proverbial language.
  • Measurement sense — cord of wood: stack of firewood 8 ft long, 4 ft high, 4 ft wide totalling 128 cubic feet, first recorded 1610s, so called because it was measured with a cord of rope; wholly conventionalised, no longer visibly rope-like.
  • Homophone confusion — chord: musical chord of simultaneous notes was originally cord, aphetic from accord, respelled chord in 17th century under influence of Latin chorda string; and geometric chord of a circle is Latin chorda by metaphor; learners must separate heart-accord from gut-string despite shared spelling history.
  • Verb sense — to cord: to bind with cord, to cord wood into stacks, to rib fabric; regular verb corded, cording.

Polysemy is held together by TWISTED LENGTH. Whether plant fibre, gut string, nerve tissue, electrical flex, emotional tie, or timber stack, a cord is what can be wound, knotted, severed, or struck. The passage uses the oldest sense — hand-woven plant cord — from which all anatomical and figurative metaphors later grew by seeing bodies and societies as woven things held together by lines.

In everyday handling cord is chosen by diameter, fibre, and lay: soft cotton sash for windows, braided nylon for blinds, tarred hemp for masonry lines, and elastic shock cord for luggage, each balancing grip, stretch, and knot-holding for its task.
