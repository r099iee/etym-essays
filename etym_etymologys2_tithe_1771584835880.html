PIE *déḱm̥ “ten”, with the ordinal *déḱm̥tos “tenth” (reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *tehun “ten” and the ordinal *tegunþō “tenth” (reconstructed; Grimm’s Law: PIE *ḱ → PGmc *h, PIE *d → PGmc *t) → Old English tēoþa “tenth; the tenth part” (attested from the Anglo-Saxon laws, 8th–11th c.) — alongside Old English tīen “ten” → Middle English tithe, teothe “a tenth; the tenth of produce paid to the church” (12th–15th c.) → English TITHE “a tenth part, especially of produce or income paid to the church” (modern) NOTE ON DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The English ordinal split into two descendants of the same Old English word: tēoþa gave both tithe (the specialized church tenth, preserving the older spirant) and tenth (re-formed by analogy with ten and the suffix -th); the doublet is a classic case of one ancestor yielding two forms. (2) The exact phonetic path of þ > ð in tithe is shared with other ordinal forms (e.g. fifþa > fifth) and is not in dispute; the vowel shows the regular Middle English raising and shortening. (3) The same PIE decimal root produced Latin decem / decima (whence English decimal, decimate, and dime via Old French disme) and Greek deka (whence decade, decagon) — these are cognates, not ancestors of tithe, which comes down the Germanic line.
