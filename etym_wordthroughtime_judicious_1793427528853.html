- Late 16th c. — borrowed from French humanism: enters Elizabethan English via Middle French judicieux (modeled on Latin iudicium), signifying a mind endowed with the power of sound intellectual discernment.
- Early 17th c. — scholarly and theological authority: famously applied to theologian Richard Hooker ('the judicious Hooker') in ecclesiastical and philosophical debates, establishing the epithet for sober, authoritative moderation.
- Late 17th–18th c. — Augustan critical ideal: Swift, Dryden, Pope, and Johnson adopt judicious as the highest compliment for a discerning reader, editor, or critic who judges art without pedantry or malice.
- 19th c. — Victorian political economy: extended from intellectual judgment to practical administration, governance, and financial stewardship ('judicious investments', 'judicious reforms').
- 20th c. — medical, managerial, and clinical precision: adopted in scientific and clinical literature to describe measured, evidence-based practices ('judicious use of sedatives', 'judicious diplomacy').
- Today — mark of sober discernment: widely used across legal, corporate, artistic, and academic registers to praise balanced decisions that reconcile complex competing variables with wisdom.
