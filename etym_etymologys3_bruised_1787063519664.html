Bruised is a word of two converging parentages, one Germanic and one Romance, which met in the melting-pot of Middle English and fused into a single verb. Its history is a classic demonstration of how English vocabulary was remade between the Norman Conquest and the Renaissance — and a case in which the earliest links are genuinely uncertain, so that any account must distinguish what is attested from what is reconstructed.

The Germanic line. Old English had the verb brȳsan 'to crush, bruise', attested from the earliest period, which descends from a Proto-Germanic base *brūsijaną 'to crush, shatter, break to pieces', itself built on the ancient root *bhreus- 'to break, crush, shatter' found across the Indo-European family. Old English brȳsan is the direct ancestor of the Modern English verb and noun bruise: its descendant brisen is recorded in Middle English from around 1200 with the meaning 'to crush, bruise, injure by pressure'.

The Romance line. Independently, the Old French verb bruisier (also bruiser, from Old North French) meant 'to break, shatter, smash into pieces', and it was borrowed into Middle English in the thirteenth century as bruisen. The Romance verb itself is of debated origin: most historical linguists derive it from a Gallo-Romance or Frankish base meaning 'to break' — commonly traced to a Gaulish root *brūs- cognate with the same Indo-European family as break itself — though its precise immediate source is not documented and remains a matter of careful reconstruction rather than direct attestation.

Convergence in Middle English. In Middle English the two incoming forms, brisen (from the Old English line) and bruisen (from the French line), were near-synonyms meaning 'to crush, injure by a blow'. They were fused by fourteenth- and fifteenth-century scribes into a single verb, brise(n) / bruise(n), whose modern spelling and pronunciation descend from the blend. The merger is characteristic of the period: two words of identical meaning, one native and one borrowed, collapsed into one.

The sense narrows: from 'crush' to 'contuse'. In Old and Middle English the verb could mean simply 'to crush, pound, or shatter' — you could bruise corn or bruise herbs, and this culinary sense survives today ('bruised mint'). But as crush and break themselves stabilised in their modern shapes, to bruise specialised in the meaning 'to injure by a blow without breaking the skin', the sense of the modern contusion. By the early modern period bruise was the ordinary word for the black-and-blue mark left by a knock, and bruised the ordinary adjective for the person who bore it.

Fruit and produce. From the later seventeenth century the verb and adjective extended naturally to fruit and soft goods damaged by rough handling — a 'bruised apple', a 'bruised berry' — where the mark beneath the skin resembles a bruise on flesh. This remains a fully living sense in shops, kitchens, and orchards.

The figurative leap: bruised feelings. The decisive semantic drift came in the modern period, when the physical bruise became the master metaphor for invisible emotional damage. 'Bruised pride', 'bruised feelings', 'a bruised ego', and 'a bruised spirit' are all standard modern idiom: the blow is an insult or rejection, the swelling is hurt, and the slow fading of the mark is the recovery of wounded dignity. The metaphor is so natural that few speakers notice it — but it rests entirely on the physical sense built up over a millennium.

Literary use. Bruised appears throughout English literature in all its registers. In The Water-Babies (1863), Charles Kingsley describes Tom washed ashore 'so sore and stiff, so bruised and buffeted he could hardly stand' — a compact, physical use that shows the word doing its oldest job. In religious prose the phrase 'a bruised reed' (from the book of Isaiah, via the Authorised Version) uses the adjective for fragility and vulnerability, a striking early figurative employment. And in modern sport, medicine, and fiction the word is everywhere: the bruised boxer, the bruised child, the bruised apple, the bruised heart.

Summary of the chain:

  • *bhreus- (Indo-European, 'to break, crush') — reconstructed base of both lines
  • *brūsijaną (Proto-Germanic, 'to crush, shatter')
  • brȳsan (Old English, 'to crush, bruise')
  • brisen (Middle English, c. 1200, 'to crush, bruise')
  • bruisier (Old French, 'to break, shatter') to bruisen (Middle English, 13th c.)
  • bruise (Modern English verb and noun, 'to injure by a blow without breaking the skin')
  • bruised (English past participle/adjective, 'bearing contusions — or, figuratively, emotional hurt')

The evolution of bruised is a history of convergence and refinement: two Indo-European streams, one native, one French, fuse in Middle English; the broad sense 'crush' narrows to the precise 'contuse'; and the physical mark becomes, in the modern age, the universal metaphor for damage that hurts but heals.
