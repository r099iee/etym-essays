- 14th–16th c. — the adverb sidlyng/sideling means “sideways, obliquely”; it is a plain directional word with no particular suggestion of stealth.
- 1570s — the altered form sidelong appears, and the idea of oblique movement becomes common in description.
- 1690s — the verb sidle is back-formed from sideling; from the start it implies a cautious, unobtrusive, or furtive approach.
- 18th–19th c. — sidle becomes a favourite of novelists for shy, guilty, or socially awkward movement, often towards a person.
- 20th c. — sidle up to develops its strong figurative sense of insinuating oneself into favour, common in political and social commentary.
- 21st c. — the word remains standard and slightly literary, used for physical movement and for anything that creeps in gradually and unnoticed.
