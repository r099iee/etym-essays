Forking here is the present participle of fork in the phrasal verb fork out, meaning to hand over money, usually reluctantly. The deep root is Latin furca, "a two-pronged fork, pitchfork, prop, yoke for punishing slaves", a farm and military word of uncertain deeper origin (no secure PIE etymology; de Vaan lists it as without clear cognates). Whether by direct borrowing or via Vulgar Latin, Old English took it as forca, "forked stake, pitchfork", already naturalised by c. 1000. Middle English forke widened to any bifurcated tool, and by the 15th century to the table fork, an Italian novelty that reached England slowly. The verb to fork — to work with a fork, to pitch hay, to divide into branches — follows naturally.

Two rival explanations compete for how pitching hay became paying cash, and both may be true. The agricultural account stresses image: to fork out is to toss or throw out, as with a hayfork — first recorded in the literal sense "throw out with a fork" in the early 17th century, then figuratively "hand over" by the early 19th. Idiom-origin studies favour this: throwing money out captures the reluctant generosity of the payer. The cant account stresses fingers: by c. 1698 canting dictionaries record fork as a pickpocket and to fork as to pick a pocket with the fore and middle fingers extended like fork tines — "thrust the fingers straight, stiff, open, very quick into the pocket". Brewer and Bailey derive "fork out" from "forks" = fingers: to fork out is to produce with the hand. Green's Dictionary of Slang records "forking out the cash" by 1816 and "fork out your balance" in Dickens's Our Mutual Friend (1865).

  • Latin furca to OE forca: borrowing of a farm implement; phonology straightforward, f-f, u-o lowering.
  • Medieval widening: from pitchfork to gallows-fork, to table fork, to any bifurcation including roads and lightning — the noun's forked shape drives metaphor.
  • Cant specialization (c. 1698-1830s): fork = hand with forked fingers; to fork = to steal; to fork out = to produce or hand over. Underworld slang migrates into general colloquial use.
  • Respectable idiom (1820s-1860s): sporting papers, Pierce Egan's Tom and Jerry (1821), Bulwer-Lytton's Paul Clifford (1830), and Dickens cement "fork out/over" as comic but respectable for reluctant payment.
  • Modern participle forking: "forking out" now profiles the ongoing, habitual aspect — "got used to forking out" = became accustomed to regular disbursement. American "fork over" is the transatlantic twin.

Wodehouse's sentence exploits the habitual nuance: if the magazine has been profitable for two years, Uncle Tom should by now be habituated to the outflow. The reluctant flavour persists even when the sums are willingly given; "fork" keeps a comic image of money being pitched or prised from fingers. Phonologically the word is stable: Latin furca with short u gives OE forca, ME forke with final -e loss, modern /fɔːk/ with lengthened vowel before voiceless velar, plus regular -ing /ˈfɔːkɪŋ/. Scholarly nuance: the Latin etymon itself is etymologically opaque, and the exact bridge (pitchfork image versus pickpocket fingers) remains debated; the OED notes both, and most historians accept blending — fingers like tines pitching coins outward. That blend is why the phrase feels both manual and miserable.
