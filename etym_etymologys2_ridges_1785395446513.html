Single root word: RIDGES (plural of the noun ridge). The chain runs from a reconstructed Proto-Germanic word for the back and spine, through Old English into modern English; the deeper, pre-Germanic ancestry is genuinely unknown and is flagged at the top. Proto-Germanic *hrugjaz "back, spine; ridge" (reconstructed noun; ~1st millennium BCE; no secure Indo-European etymon is attested — the OED records its further etymology as unknown; a conjectured link to PIE *sker- "to turn, bend" is generally rejected) → Old English hrycg "back of man or beast; also crest of a hill, ridge of land" (attested from the 8th c.; already carrying both the anatomical sense and the topographical extension) → Middle English rigge / rig "back; ridge of land" (12th–15th c.; the y of hrycg weakens to i, the final consonant resolves into the modern -dge cluster) → Early Modern English ridge "long, narrow, elevated crest of ground" (16th–17th c.; by this stage the topographical sense is primary, the anatomical sense surviving only in fossil phrases such as ridge of the nose) → Modern English RIDGES (the regular plural of the noun, recorded throughout the modern period). Note on debate: the deeper origin of *hrugjaz is the word's one genuine mystery. The old proposal of the Pokorny school linking the root to PIE *sker- "to turn, bend" was never established and is generally rejected; the OED records the further etymology as unknown, and the root is best treated as a Germanic isolate within the larger Indo-European family. The Germanic cognates are secure and numerous — Old Saxon hruggi "back," Old High German hrukki "back" (German Rücken), Old Norse hryggr "back, spine; mountain-back" (Norwegian rygg, Swedish rygg, Danish ryg, Icelandic hryggur), Dutch rug "back" — but no Gothic reflex is attested, and none is invented.
