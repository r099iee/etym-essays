The iron head of battering was forged in the late Roman Empire. Classical Latin beat people properly with verberare (whence reverberate) and struck coins with cūdere, but soldiers and fullers in Gaul had a rougher verb, battuere — to thump, to pound cloth, to wallop. Where it came from is the word's honest mystery: Roman grammarians heard clatter in it (a bang-word, like English bat), while modern Celticists note suspiciously similar forms in Gaulish and Old Irish and propose a Celtic loan that marched into Latin with the legions' laundry. What is certain is that battuere won: it shouldered the classical verbs aside and became the ancestor of French battre, Italian battere, and Spanish batir — half of Romance beating descends from this one vulgar pounding.

Phonetically the journey is beating made audible. Latin battuere passed into Old French as batre (11th century), the double t simplifying and the ending shearing off in the French manner; compounds like bataille (battle) and baston (stick, whence baton) show the same root arming itself. Middle English borrowed bateren around 1300 with the Norse-hardened northern batt- forms reinforcing it (Old Norse berja, to strike, thumped alongside). English then did what English does with French verbs: it worked them harder. Batter became the frequentative of beat — not one blow but blows plural — and battering the noun for the whole sustained event, first of fists and rams, then of everything that falls repeatedly on anything.

From laundry-beating to headlines:

  • Late Latin (3rd–5th c.): battuere pounds fullers' cloth and soldiers' shields across Gaul; St Jerome uses it in the Vulgate-flavoured Latin of late antiquity.
  • Old French (11th–13th c.): batre beats corn (threshing), drums, enemies, and wives in fabliaux; the battering ram — belier, mouton — becomes standard siege furniture at sieges like Château Gaillard.
  • Middle English (c. 1300 – 1500): bateren batters gates in romances and sinners in sermons; Caxton's chronicles batter Saracens, and cooks batter (beat) eggs into the mixture still called batter today.
  • Tudor–Stuart (16th–17th c.): artillery joins the family — battering pieces, battering charges — and Shakespeare's rebels batter down laws and doors; the cook's batter and the gunner's battering are one verb's twins.
  • Georgian–Victorian (18th–19th c.): storms batter coasts in Thomson and Byron, steam hammers batter iron at Nasmyth's works, and newspaper war correspondents batter Sebastopol and Lucknow in daily dispatches.
  • 20th – 21st c.: the word splits three ways — weather batterings on every bulletin, reputation batterings in politics and sport, and the grim 1970s compounds (wife-battering, battered-child syndrome, Kempe 1962) that made the relentless sense clinical; Tolkien's comic ram (1937) floats above it all, siege-work at teatime.

Literary attestation is a roll of thunder. Froissart's chronicles (translated by Berners, 1520s) batter town walls page after page; Shakespeare's Henry V batters Harfleur in threats and his Bastard in King John proposes to batter down the very stones of Angiers. The Authorised Version prefers beat, but the hymn-writers loved batter: John Donne's Holy Sonnet — batter my heart, three-person'd God — remains the most famous battering in English, the soul imagined as a besieged town begging its besieger for heavier artillery. From Donne's breast to Tolkien's garden gate the road is straight: both poets know that a battering is beating with intent, and both trust the reader to hear the ram's thud behind the metaphor. Modern engineering closed the circle when battering load and battering ram effect entered the handbooks — the oldest siege engine now a unit of calculation, still swinging.
