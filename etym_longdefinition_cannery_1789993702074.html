cannery is a factory or plant where perishable food is processed, cooked, and sealed into airtight metal cans or jars so it can be stored and shipped. In the source sentence Vance worked in a cannery — one stop in a Depression-era circuit of hard manual jobs alongside bellhop work and gold-dredging. The unspoken picture is seasonal, wet, noisy, and smelling of fish or fruit: sorting lines, cutting tables, steam retorts, and soldered lids.

Morphologically the word is can + -ery on the model of bakery, brewery, fishery: a place where the named thing is made or handled. The can in question is the tin can, the early-19th-century invention that made industrial food preservation possible; the -ery turns the container into an institution. The first canneries rose in the mid-1800s for oysters, salmon, and fruit, and by the early 1900s the salmon canneries of Alaska and the Pacific Northwest and the fruit and vegetable canneries of California were among the largest seasonal employers on the continent.

  • Core industrial sense: a canning factory with receiving docks, washing and sorting lines, filling machines, exhausting and seaming equipment, and retorts for heat-sterilising sealed cans. Fish canneries (salmon, tuna, sardines), fruit and vegetable canneries (peaches, tomatoes, peas, pineapple), and meat canneries each have their own seasonal calendar and occupational lore.
  • Labour and life: historically the cannery meant short-season, low-wage, high-speed hand work — gutting, slicing, packing — often done by women, migrants, and itinerants like the young Vance. Cannery towns, cannery whistles, and cannery row boarding-houses formed a distinct working landscape immortalised by John Steinbeck's Cannery Row (1945), set among Monterey sardine canneries.
  • Product and process metonymy: by extension cannery can denote the business itself or its output collective (the cannery's pack this year), and attributively it marks anything of that world: cannery worker, cannery whistle, cannery town. The verb behind it is to can (to preserve in cans), with canning for the process and season.
  • Specialised neighbours: a packing plant or packing house (meat, fruit) overlaps but need not can; a processing plant is the modern neutral term; a conservas factory is the artisanal Iberian cousin now fashionable in food culture. To say cannery rather than factory dates and flavours the scene: tin, steam, and seasonal rush.

Grammar and usage: countable noun with regular plural canneries (y → ies); typically preceded by in/at for employment (work in a cannery) and by product adjectives (salmon cannery, tomato cannery). Cannery Row as a proper noun now doubles as shorthand for any faded industrial waterfront. Register is standard but faintly historical in developed economies, where canning has automated or moved offshore; it stays current in Alaska, the Pacific, and artisanal food writing, where small-batch canneries are again prestigious.

  • Connotation: industrious, odorous, and working-class; romanticised at a distance (Steinbeck, waterfront nostalgia), remembered close-up as cold floors, long shifts, and cut hands — the texture that makes a young man's cannery spell emblematic of hard times.
  • Boundaries: reserve cannery for establishments that actually seal food in cans or jars; for mere slaughter or boxing use packing plant; for freezing or drying use processing plant. The can is definitional.

In short, cannery compresses an industry, a season, and a social class into three syllables: the loud tin building where the harvest is made to keep, and where itinerant labour briefly keeps itself.
