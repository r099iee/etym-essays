The story of decides opens with a blow of the axe. Its deepest ancestor is the reconstructed PIE root *kau-, meaning to hew, strike, or cut — the sound-world of splitting timber and felling enemies — which also feeds English hew and probably hay, the grass that is cut and dried. In Latin this root hardened into the verb caedere, to cut, fell, or kill, one of the language's great compounding engines: Romans cut in every direction, and each direction bred a dynasty of words.

Prefix de-, meaning off or away, to caedere, and the result is decidere: literally to cut off. Cicero and the rhetoricians used it for cutting short a dispute, and Roman legal procedure adopted it for the judge's settling of a cause — the metaphor already complete two thousand years ago, since a verdict cuts the knot of litigation exactly as an axe cuts a branch. Christian writers carried it into theology, deciding cases of conscience, and the Vulgate's authority fixed it in the learned vocabulary of medieval Europe.

From Rome to England:

  • Old French mediation (12th century): Vulgar Latin decidere passed into Old French as decider, keeping the sense of resolving and judging. The Norman Conquest then ferried a whole fleet of such administrative verbs across the Channel, and English, hungry for learned terms, took them aboard.
  • Middle English adoption (late 14th century): Deciden appears in Chaucer's contemporaries, first as a clerkly word of courts and councils. The third-person -eth ending of the period — he decideth — would only later soften, through northern -s, into modern decides.
  • Early modern flowering (16th–17th centuries): Shakespeare, the King James translators, and the pamphleteers made decide a word of princes and commoners alike — deciding battles, wagers, and souls. The spelling settled, the pronunciation shifted with the Great Vowel Shift, and the verb spread into every domain of judgment.
  • Institutional age (18th–19th centuries): Parliamentary procedure, codified sport, and mass elections gave decides its modern stages: the Speaker decides points of order, the final ball decides the match, the floating voter decides the seat. The sporting noun decider emerges here, proof of how deeply the verb had rooted in play.
  • Contemporary reach (20th century to today): Pollsters count the undecided, computers decide loan applications, and philosophers debate whether anything truly decides a free choice. The ancient cutting image survives unnoticed inside all of it — every decision still severs what might have been from what shall be.

Sound shifts and kin: The Latin cae- diphthong simplified in French, the infinitive -ere became -er and then English -e, and the verb joined the regular weak conjugation, spawning decided, deciding, decides without fuss. Its Latin siblings crowd English dictionaries: homicide, suicide, regicide carry -cidere, to kill; excise, concise, precise, incision carry -cidere, to cut; chisel descends from the same root through Old French. Few verb families illustrate so neatly how one physical act — the stroke of a blade — can generate the whole vocabulary of judgment.
