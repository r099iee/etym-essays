Introduction. The history of proclamation is the history of public speech turning into public documents: a word that began as a shout ends as an instrument of state, and then, in the twentieth century, becomes a rhetorical flourish anyone can borrow. Its chain runs from the reconstructed shout-root of Indo-European through the Roman forum, the medieval French chancery, the English royal court, and the newsprint of the modern world — and each stage left a deposit in the meaning.

1. The root: the Indo-European shout (c. 4500–2500 BCE)

The chain opens with a Proto-Indo-European root normally reconstructed as *kel- (or *kele-), "to call, to shout, to summon". Its reflexes show how widely the ancient world shouted with one root: Latin calāre ("to summon solemnly", used of the priest's call to assembly), Latin concilium ("assembly", literally "a calling-together"), Greek kaléō ("I call"), and the Slavic family of Russian gólos, "voice", which is regularly compared with this root. The sound is the point: these are words made by and for the human voice raised to be heard — the raw material out of which proclamation, clamour, claim, exclaim, and acclaim would all be cut.

2. Latin: clāmāre, prōclāmāre, prōclāmātiō

Latin built on the root its ordinary verb of loud speech, clāmō, clāmāre, "to shout, cry out" (the source of English clamour), attested throughout classical prose and verse, from Plautus to Cicero. With the prefix prō-, "forth, out, publicly", Latin formed prōclāmāre, "to cry out aloud, to proclaim": Cicero uses it of public protest and outcry, and the sense is always public — one proclaims to a crowd, not to oneself. The noun prōclāmātiō, "a loud calling out, a proclamation", is attested chiefly in the legal and rhetorical Latin of the empire, and it is this noun that carried the word into the Romance chanceries. It is an education in Roman political culture: the state spoke to the people by voice, and the vocabulary of public announcement was the vocabulary of shouting in public space — the forum, the comitia, the praeco, the crier.

3. Old French and the medieval chancery (13th–14th centuries)

French inherited the word as proclamacion, recorded in the thirteenth century in legal and administrative documents: proclamations summon assemblies, publish sentences, and announce the king's will. From Old French and Anglo-Norman the word crossed to England — the Middle English proclamacioun is recorded in the later fourteenth century, first in legal-administrative contexts. The medieval reality behind the word is the voice of the herald and the crier: a proclamation was literally a crying out — at the crossroads, the marketplace, or the church door — so that all whom it might concern should have notice. The town crier's "Oyez! Oyez!" is the audible relic of the age of proclaimed law.

4. The English royal proclamation (15th–17th centuries)

In England the proclamation became a constitutional instrument. The Crown announced statutes, prices, coinage, and public dangers by royal proclamation — printed from the sixteenth century and "cried" in the streets — and the great Tudor proclamations of Henry VIII survive as a printed genre. The law reports record its limits with famous precision: Henry VIII's statute of 1539 briefly gave proclamations the force of statute (repealed in 1547), and in the Case of Proclamations (1610) Chief Justice Coke ruled that the king could not create new offences or change the common law by proclamation alone. The word had become a token of the boundary between royal will and law — a dignity it has never lost.

5. Republican, revolutionary, and international proclamations (18th–20th centuries)

With the Atlantic revolutions the proclamation passed from the crown to the people: the American declaration of 1776 was a proclaimed independence; colonial proclamations such as the Royal Proclamation of 1763 mapped new worlds; and Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (1 January 1863) made the word a byword for liberation itself. The Proclamation of the Irish Republic (Easter 1916) shows the genre at its most revolutionary. In the twentieth century international bodies inherited the word's solemnity: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) is "proclaimed" in its own opening formula ("THE GENERAL ASSEMBLY proclaims..."), and proclamations multiply across the United Nations and civil society.

6. The modern career and the comic inflation (20th–21st centuries)

Meanwhile everyday language has changed down the centuries. Once a technical-heavy word of the high style, proclamation is now used with conscious grandeur for small things — "I hereby issue a proclamation: dessert before dinner!" — a playful inflation that depends entirely on the word's august past. The semantic career is thus an arc from sound to document to joke-with-dignity: every modern use still carries, faintly, the sound of the crier's raised voice in the market square, exactly as the Mayor of Wilde's The Happy Prince exploits it when he solemnly proposes "a proclamation that birds are not to be allowed to die here".
