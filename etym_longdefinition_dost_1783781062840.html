dost (v.) — an archaic second-person singular present-tense form of the verb do ("thou dost"), the early modern equivalent of modern "you do." The -st ending marks the verb for the pronoun thou (subject singular), so dost means "you do," with the intimate, reverential, or solemn register that the thou form carries in Early Modern English. It descends from Old English dēst, the second-person singular present of dōn "to do."

Grammatical senses. Because dost is an inflected verb form, its "meanings" are the full range of senses of do in the second person singular present:

  • Lexical "perform, carry out" — "thou dost good works": the full-verb sense of performing an action or duty.
  • Pro-verb (anaphoric) — "thou dost as I asked": standing in for a previously mentioned action, a characteristic English use of do.
  • Emphatic auxiliary — "thou dost speak with wisdom": the emphatic auxiliary of early modern English, stressing the truth of the assertion (a relic of do-support before it became merely grammatical).
  • Periphrastic/auxiliary "make" — "thou dost him wrong": forming causative or periphrastic constructions equivalent to "you cause to be done."
  • Idiomatic and general senses — "thou dost well," "why dost thou weep?": the broad, bleached sense of "act, fare, behave, succeed" that English do has always had.

Historical & stylistic senses.

  • Person and number: dost is unambiguously second-person singular; the -st ending is the marker of the thou-paradigm, fully productive in Early Modern English.
  • Emphatic vs. ordinary use: in the Authorized Version (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton, dost is used both as the ordinary present of do ("thou dost prefer") and as the emphatic auxiliary ("thou dost err" = "you really do err").
  • Register: the thou forms express intimacy, reverence, or solemn address; dost is thus found in prayer, invocation, scripture, and elevated poetry.
  • Archaism today: in modern English dost is a fossilized archaism used in biblical quotation, liturgy, and archaizing verse; the variant doest is also met when metre requires a syllable.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word is built on one of the oldest and most productive roots of the Indo-European family, *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, set." From "to put, place" the Germanic verb narrowed or broadened (depending on one's view) to "to do" in the general sense "to act, to cause, to perform," and the same root yields Sanskrit dádhāti, Greek títhēmi, and Latin faciō (via *dʰeh₁-). The second-person singular form dēst in Old English shows the i-mutation characteristic of the old present-tense inflections, and the Middle English and early modern dost continues it directly. The most important semantic bridge is the rise of do-support: in Early Modern English, do was still a meaningful emphatic auxiliary ("thou dost prefer" stresses the act), and only later did it become the empty, purely grammatical auxiliary of modern questions and negatives ("do you prefer?", "you do not"). Dost therefore carries a fossil of an older stage of English syntax.

Current status. In present-day English dost is obsolete outside deliberate archaism. Dictionaries label it archaic; it appears in quotations from the Authorized Version and Shakespeare, and it is a standard textbook example of the -st conjugation and of the emphatic auxiliary of early modern English.
