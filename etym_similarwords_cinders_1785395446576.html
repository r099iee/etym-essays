• Ashes: Similar because both name the residue of burned material after a fire; differs because ashes are the fine, powdery, fully mineralized grey-white dust left when nothing more can burn — the word of mourning and dust-to-dust — while cinders are coarser, heavier, blackened fragments still recognizable as what they once were.
  • Embers: Similar because both are pieces of coal or wood from a fire; differs because embers are still alight, glowing and able to rekindle — they belong to the fire's life — while cinders are far past flame, hard and brittle, though they may still glow dully.
  • Charcoal: Similar because both are burnt carbon material; differs because charcoal is deliberately produced carbonized wood — a manufactured fuel, filter, or drawing medium — while cinders are the incidental, accidental waste of a fire that has burned something else.
  • Slag: Similar because both are the waste by-product of burning or smelting; differs because slag is the fused, glassy impurity separated from molten metal in a furnace, whereas cinders in modern use are the partially burned fragments of coal, wood, or bone from a domestic or natural fire.
  • Clinker: Similar because clinker is likewise a hard, fused mass left by burning (in furnaces and fireboxes); differs because clinker is the vitrified, rock-hard residue fused into a lump, while cinders are looser, individual fragments that may still glow and can fly into the air.
  • Soot: Similar because both are fire-products; differs because soot is the fine black powder of unburnt carbon deposited on surfaces by smoke, soft and adhering, whereas cinders are solid, brittle, three-dimensional fragments lying on the ground.
  • Sinder / dross: Similar because sinder is the older native English form of the same word, naming the dross of the forge; differs because sinder survives only as a dialect word or metallurgical term, while cinder is the standard modern form with its extended fire-remnant sense.
  • Rubble / debris: Similar because both are scattered refuse left after destruction; differs because rubble and debris are the broken material of buildings and objects, while cinders are specifically the burnt, charred residue of fire — the word belongs to combustion, not to any generic wreckage.
