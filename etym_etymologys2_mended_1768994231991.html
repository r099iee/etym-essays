SINGLE ROOT CHAIN: Proto-Indo-European *mend- "physical defect, fault, blemish" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; a nominal root of brokenness — the repair verb is built on a noun of damage) → Latin menda (f.) "blemish, fault, error, especially in writing" and mendum (n.) "defect, fault" (attested 1st century BCE) → Latin ēmendāre "to free from fault, to correct" (compound ē- "out of" + menda, literally "to bring out of defect"; attested 1st century BCE) → Old French amender "to repair, correct, improve" (9th–11th c.; Latin ēmendāre with the preverb absorbed into the stem as a-) → Anglo-Norman / Middle English amenden "to mend, repair, improve, cure" (borrowed 12th–13th c.) → English mend (aphetic clipping of amend — the initial a- is dropped — 13th–14th c.) "to repair, restore, put right" → mended (past tense and past participle of mend; the inflected form fixed in Middle English as mended(e), c. 14th century). NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: Two points deserve explicit flagging. First, the reconstruction of PIE *mend- itself rests on a small set of reflexes — chiefly Italic (Latin mendum/menda) and Indo-Iranian (Sanskrit mandá- "weak, defective, slow") — and some scholars treat the Indo-Iranian form as coincidental rather than cognate; the root is not universally accepted as PIE. Second, Latin mendāx "lying, deceitful" is traditionally derived from mendum ("given to faults"), but a competing analysis connects it to the verb mentīrī "to tell lies"; the word's kinship with mendum is the traditional view and is followed here, but it is not the only one. English mend is nonetheless secure in its own lineage: amenden → aphetic mend, with amenden itself the Old French continuation of Latin ēmendāre. A related but separate doublet reached English directly from Latin as emend (15th c.), "to correct a text," keeping the preverb that mend lost.
