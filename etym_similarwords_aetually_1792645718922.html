• really: Similar because both adverbs are used to emphasize that something is true, genuine, or factually existent; differs because really is a broader, more emotionally expressive everyday intensifier (e.g., "really beautiful", "really fast"), whereas actually focuses strictly on the contrast between objective reality and mistaken assumption or expectation.
  • genuinely: Similar because both affirm the absence of falsehood, pretense, or deception; differs because genuinely emphasizes emotional sincerity, authenticity of origin, or unadulterated purity (e.g., "genuinely sorry", "genuinely handcrafted"), while actually evaluates factual occurrence and modal reality.
  • truly: Similar because both describe alignment with truth; differs because truly carries an elevated, solemn, or philosophical tone of fidelity and absolute verity (e.g., "truly grateful", "a truly great man"), whereas actually is analytical, pragmatic, and conversational.
  • in fact: Similar because both phrases introduce an objective factual confirmation that often supersedes or corrects a prior statement; differs because in fact is an explicit prepositional discourse connector that stands somewhat more formally at clause boundaries to introduce supplementary evidence, while actually can integrate fluidly as a single-word medial adverb.
  • indeed: Similar because both confirm the reality of a proposition; differs because indeed is a formal affirmative particle used to confirm and reinforce what has already been said or to introduce an even stronger claim, whereas actually frequently introduces an unexpected contradiction or correction.
  • literally: Similar in popular modern colloquial speech where both are used as emphatic intensifiers; differs strictly because literally means in the exact letter of the words without metaphor or figurative exaggeration (e.g., "the city was literally underwater"), whereas actually addresses the factual reality of a state of affairs.
  • positively: Similar because both can affirm certainty; differs because positively conveys strong subjective confidence, enthusiasm, or outright dogmatism (e.g., "she was positively beaming"), while actually remains grounded in objective, matter-of-fact reality.
  • veritably: Similar because both relate to truth (Latin veritas); differs because veritably is an elevated, somewhat archaic literary adverb used almost exclusively to qualify vivid metaphors (e.g., "a veritably volcanic eruption of applause"), whereas actually literalizes and grounds discourse in empirical fact.
  • certainly: Similar because both affirm truth; differs because certainly expresses the speaker's psychological conviction and lack of doubt, whereas actually reports on objective state-of-affairs regardless of prior doubt.
  • as a matter of fact: Similar because both serve to pivot to verified reality against prior supposition; differs because as a matter of fact is a heavier, multisyllabic idiomatic phrase that explicitly calls attention to the factual record, often with a slightly formal or pedantic rhetorical posture.
