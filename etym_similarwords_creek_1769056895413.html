• inlet: Similar because both are indentations of the shoreline admitting water; differs because inlet is the general, neutral term for any such indentation, while a creek (in the coastal sense Lewis uses) is smaller and more intimate — a natural nook hidden from the open sea.
  • cove: Similar because both are small, sheltered recesses of the coast; differs because a cove is a rounded bay with a curving shore — a basin — while a creek is a narrow, channel-like waterway defined by the land, a bend that lets the sea in rather than a hollow.
  • bay: Similar because both are places where the sea reaches into the land; differs because a bay is larger and more open than a creek, while a creek is small, narrow, and sheltered — hidden from the open water.
  • estuary: Similar because both are where water meets the shore; differs because an estuary is specifically the tidal mouth of a river where fresh and salt water meet, while a creek is a sheltered inlet that need not have a river at its head.
  • harbour: Similar because both offer shelter to boats; differs because a harbour implies a place of refuge for shipping, often improved by man-made works (breakwaters, quays), while a creek is a natural nook of the coast, unimproved and friendly in a more intimate way.
  • brook: Similar because both are small bodies of flowing water; differs because a brook is the British word for a fresh-water stream — the niche American English fills with creek — whereas a creek in the coastal sense is a tidal inlet of the sea.
  • rivulet: Similar because both name small streams; differs because rivulet (from Latin rivus, "stream") is a literary, diminutive word for a tiny fresh-water stream, while an American creek is the plain everyday word for a small tributary, and a British creek is salt tidal water.
  • stream: Similar because both name flowing water; differs because stream is the general term for any flowing body of water, while a creek is either a narrow tidal inlet (British) or a small stream specifically (American).
  • channel: Similar because both are waterways; differs because a channel is a wider, more functional navigable passage, while a creek is small, sheltered, and defined by the shore rather than by its function as a route.
  • crick: Similar because crick is simply the American dialectal pronunciation and spelling of creek; differs only in register (rustic, dialectal) and in the fact that the homophone crick "a cramp" (a bent neck-muscle) is a separate word of different origin.
