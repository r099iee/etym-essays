time-hallowed is a compound adjective meaning “hallowed — made holy or venerable — by the passage of time and long observance”. It describes customs, institutions, buildings and phrases whose antiquity has given them an aura of sanctity. In the source sentence the “bedrock of time-hallowed modes of thought and action” is custom regarded as sacred simply because it has been practised so long; the compound sets that quasi-religious authority against the mere interruptions of law.

The syntax is instrumental: time is not the moment of hallowing but the agent of it. A time-hallowed rite has been hallowed by time, the way a path is worn by feet. The adjective is attributive, almost always hyphenated, and rarely used predicatively — one says “a time-hallowed custom” far more readily than “the custom is time-hallowed”.

Primary sense: venerable through long observance

  • Of customs and practices. A time-hallowed ritual, a time-hallowed way of doing things: the length of observance itself confers legitimacy.
  • Of places. Time-hallowed walls, halls, abbey, ground: architecture whose age has made it seem sacred, a favourite note in heritage writing and travel prose.
  • Of institutions and privileges. Time-hallowed rights, time-hallowed procedures, time-hallowed exemptions: the phrase is useful to conservatives because it converts habit into authority.
  • Of language. Time-hallowed phrases and formulas: expressions preserved by repetition, their very familiarity a kind of sanction.

Secondary and ironic senses

  • Gentle irony. Applied to inefficiency, delay or prejudice, the compound becomes mocking: “the time-hallowed practice of losing the forms” — the reverence is quoted rather than felt.
  • Political rhetoric. In speeches the word lends weight to tradition without specifying any particular tradition; it is a container for whatever the speaker wishes to conserve.
  • Heritage and tourism. In modern marketing, “time-hallowed” sells age as an experience: time-hallowed inns, time-hallowed ceremonies, time-hallowed recipes.

How the compound means what it means

Hallow is the old verb “to make holy, consecrate”, now archaic outside set phrases, and hallowed is its past participle. Join it to time and the metaphor writes itself: long duration performs the work of consecration. The idea is Romantic rather than medieval — the Middle Ages hallowed things by God and the Church, whereas the modern compound hallows them by sheer age. That shift is visible in the nineteenth century, when antiquarian and nationalist writers began to treat custom itself as a form of sanctity.

Register, connotation and collocations

  • Elevated, literary and slightly archaic; it belongs to speeches, editorials, inscriptions and heritage prose rather than to conversation.
  • Connotation: reverence, stability and continuity, with an occasional whiff of dust and cobwebs.
  • Typical collocations: time-hallowed tradition, time-hallowed institution, time-hallowed ritual, time-hallowed phrase, time-hallowed walls, time-hallowed privilege.
  • Contrast with time-honoured, the more common twin: honour is social respect, whereas hallowing is closer to sanctity, so time-hallowed is the more religious and the more archaic of the two.
