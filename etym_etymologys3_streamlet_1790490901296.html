streamlet joins a very old Germanic water-word to a young French diminutive, and its history must therefore be told in two halves: the ancient career of stream and the early-modern fashion for -let littles.

Stream begins in PIE *sreu-, to flow, one of the best-attested water roots. It gives Sanskrit sravati (flows), srotas (stream), Avestan thraotah- (river), Greek rheos (stream), rhythmos originally (measured flow), Old Irish sruth (stream), Lithuanian srautas (current), Old Church Slavonic struja (flow), and Germanic *straumaz. That Germanic noun — Old Norse straumr, Old High German stroum, Dutch stroom, German Strom, Old English stream — meant at first any powerful moving water: river, sea-current, flood, even the sea itself. Old English poetry speaks of lagustream (sea-stream) and streamas for the ocean; stream was grand, not small. Phonologically PIE *sreu- with diphthong eu gives Germanic au by regular lowering (compare *deupaz to deep), then Old English ea breaking before m to stream, modern /striːm/ by the Great Vowel Shift raising Middle English /eː/ to /iː/.

  • Old to Middle English broadening: by Chaucer streme means river, current, and by metaphor any copious flow — stremes of tears, of blood, of people. It is never small; small waters are brokes, burnes, rithes, welles. The semantic field is abundance and force.
  • The -let invasion (14th-17th c.): Middle French brings diminutives in -el, -elle, -elet, -ot, -et: hamel (little home) gives hamlet, brook on analogy gives booklet (pamphlet), drop gives droplet. Latin behind them has -ellus (libellus, little book) crossed with Germanic -itt-. English writers of the Elizabethan gardening and pastoral boom love the device because it makes nature dainty and collectible. Streamlet is first recorded in 1587 (in a husbandry and surveying context), then in Philemon Holland, Drayton's Poly-Olbion (1612), and Walton's Compleat Angler (1653) — always a fishable, wadeable, pretty little water.
  • Picturesque and Romantic canonisation (1700-1830s): Thomson's Seasons, Gray's letters, Gilpin's picturesque tours, and then Burns, Wordsworth, Coleridge, and Scott fix streamlet as the approved word for upland beauty. Scott uses it dozens of times for Border burns; Wordsworth's streamlets murmur moral comfort. Pronunciation settles as /ˈstriːmlət/ with syllabic l, spelling with single m and -let never varied. The word's connotation narrows to purity, solitude, and childhood — exactly Ruskin's inheritance.
  • Victorian science keeps the poetry: geologists (Lyell, Ramsay), anglers, and sanitary reformers all write streamlet without embarrassment while mapping watersheds, stocking trout, or condemning cesspools. Ruskin himself, who drew water obsessively, uses streamlet for both Alpine threads and Scottish burns, hearing in it innocence against the polluted river.
  • Modern headwater and metaphor: Strahler stream-order (1952) gives streamlet a quasi-technical role for first-order channels, while journalism extends it to blood, light, lava, refugees, and packets. Both keep the Elizabethan diminutive force: a streamlet is a stream made small, beloved, and crossable.

There is no serious etymological debate: stream from *sreu- via *straumaz is textbook, and -let from French is textbook. The only nuance is chronological — streamlet could not exist before the French suffix became productive, so despite its Germanic base it is a Tudor word, younger than rivulet (borrowed whole from Italian rivoletto) and younger than brooklet, its closest rival.
