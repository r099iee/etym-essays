Plundering is the present participle and gerund of the verb plunder — to strip a place, a person, or a possession of its movable goods by force, especially in war, piracy, or any other violent raid — and, as an adjective, it names the agent or the act of such stripping: a plundering army, plundering dragons, a plundering raid. In Bilbo's mental flash in The Hobbit, "he thought of plundering dragons settling on his quiet Hill and kindling it all to flames," the participle does precise narrative work. The dragons are not merely wicked; they are defined by the ongoing, habitual act of stripping households and treasure-halls, and the -ing form makes their predation continuous, a matter of nature rather than a single deed. The word lifts a cozy hobbit's panic into the vocabulary of war and sack, mixing genuine fear with a touch of archaic-military grandeur. Register: vivid, slightly old-fashioned narrative English with a martial colour; it collocates with armies, invaders, raiders, pirates, loot, spoils, and fire. The historical heart of the word is not general theft but the stripping of a household: plunder begins, on the Continent, as taking the movable contents of a home — clothes, bedding, baggage — during a sack. Everything the word does today grows from that image of a house emptied of its movables by force. Core senses of plunder / plundering
  • Verb, military (primary): to seize goods by force from a captured town, region, or vessel — to sack, loot, or pillage ("the army plundered the city"). The participle plundering describes the act in progress or the habitual agent ("plundering dragons").
  • Verb, piracy and raid vocabulary: to rob a ship, a caravan, or a coast by force — "the pirates made their living by plundering merchant ships."
  • Noun: the goods so seized, the loot or spoils ("the plunder was divided among the crew"); also the act itself ("the plunder of the temple").
  • Adjective / agent use: the participle used attributively, naming creatures or forces whose nature is to strip and carry off ("plundering dragons," "a plundering expedition").
  • Figurative: to take ruthlessly or wastefully — to plunder resources, to plunder nature, to plunder the public purse; the image is of emptying something valuable by force or greed.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and modern jargon senses
  • Economic and corporate plunder: in political and economic discourse, "plunder" denotes the asset-stripping of companies, the looting of a national economy, or "plunder" as systematic enrichment at the expense of a public or an institution — a modern, abstracted extension of the military act.
  • Environmental plunder: "plundering the earth," "plundering nature" — the strip-mining of resources, deforestation, overfishing — applying the sack-and-carry image to the natural world.
  • Historical and political plunder: "the plunder of empire," colonial looting, the removal of cultural treasures — scholarly and journalistic vocabulary for the spoils of conquest.
  • War-crimes discourse: "pillage and plunder" as a fixed legal doublet in the law of armed conflict, where plundering is a war crime — the word retains its full military gravity.
  • Figurative, weakened: "to plunder time," "plundering one's memories" — a looser, half-metaphorical use where the object is emptied of intangibles; the force is reduced but the emptying image remains.
Semantic bridges The whole sense-family grows from the continental noun plunder "household goods, baggage, clothes" and the denominal verb "to strip a household of its goods." From "taking the movables of a home in a sack" the word generalized to any forceful seizure of valuables — armies, pirates, bandits — and then, as the physical context faded, to economic, environmental, and abstract "emptying." The bridge at every step is the image of carrying off what is movable by force. Plundering as a participle keeps the act ongoing, and in the Tolkien sentence it colours the dragons' imagined attack as a sack of the Hill — an emptying of Bag End's warmth and security as much as of its silver spoons.
