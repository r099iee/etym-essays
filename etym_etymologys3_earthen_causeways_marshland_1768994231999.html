earthen is a direct inheritance from the Germanic core of English. Its ancestor is the Old English word eorþen, formed by the adjectival suffix -en attached to eorþe "earth," which is itself the direct continuation of Proto-Germanic *erþō, the same word that produced German Erde, Dutch aarde, Icelandic jörð, and Gothic airþa. The underlying root, PIE *h₁er- "earth, ground," is attested across the Germanic, Celtic, and Hellenic periphery — Welsh erw "field, acre," Old Irish ert "earth," Greek éra (ἔρα) "ground" in archaic poetry — but it is conspicuously absent from Latin (which preferred terra, from *ters- "dry") and from Indo-Iranian (which used *dʰǵʰem- and *bʰuH-, the ancestors of Hindi zamin and bhoomi). The -en suffix is the English reflex of the Germanic adjective-maker *-īn-, cognate with Latin -inus and Greek -inos, and it appears in a whole family of "made of" adjectives — wooden, golden, leaden, oaten, waxen, silken — all of which, like earthen, name the material of which a thing is composed. The semantic career of earthen has been remarkably stable: from the earliest Old English records it has meant "made of earth or clay," and its two thousand years of stability testify to a durable cultural category — the humble, the handmade, the earthen pot, the earthen floor, the earthen rampart. Where other material adjectives drifted into metaphor (golden the color and the value; wooden the stiff performance), earthen stayed literal, which is precisely why a writer reaches for it when the physical fact of the soil matters.

causeways is the product of two collisions: a Norman French noun and a native English one. Its first element, causey, entered English in the fourteenth century from Anglo-French causee, a development of Old North French cauciee, the early French word for a paved highway. That cauciee descends from the Vulgar Latin phrase via calciāta, "a road trodden down," built on the past participle of Latin calcare "to tread, trample," which in turn derives from calx "heel." The founding image is entirely physical: a road is a place where feet — specifically heels — have pressed the earth flat. The word thus belongs to that ancient class of engineering vocabulary in which the verb of the body names the structure of the land; the same Latin calx "heel" produced recalcitrant (a horse that "kicks back"), calcaneus (the heel-bone), and the Romance words for road — Spanish calzada, Portuguese calçada, modern French chaussée. In the fifteenth century English fused the borrowed causey with its own native way, producing causeway, a "way" that "causes" a crossing — the raised bank thrown across the waterlogged ground. A caution must be recorded: Latin had a second, homonymous calx meaning "limestone, lime" — a Greek loanword from khálix "pebble, gravel" — the source of calculate, chalk, and calcium. The two calx words are easily confused, but the causeway descends from the HEEL, not the lime. From the raised prehistoric tracks of Britain to the Roman viae calciātae to the Giant's Causeway (the basalt columns given a folk-road origin) and the modern Lake Pontchartrain Causeway, the word has carried both its engineering function and its ancient image of the pressed-down road.

marshland is a compound of two native Germanic words, marsh and land. Its first element descends from PIE *mori- "sea, body of water," one of the family's most widespread roots: Latin mare "sea," Welsh môr, Lithuanian marios "lagoon," and the Germanic set — Old English mere "lake, pool," Old Norse marr "sea," Gothic marei "sea." The Germanic branch built an adjective in -isk- ("of the water"), *marisk-, which hardened into the English words for the waterlogged land at the sea's edge: Old English merisc and mersc, modern marsh. The deep logic is geographic: the marsh is the wetland at the mar, the sea's border, neither dry land nor open water, the threshold where the two elements mingle. Its siblings across the North Sea show the same formation — Dutch meersch, German Marsch (the coastal fenland), Old Frisian mersk. The second element, land, descends from PIE *lendʰ- "open heath, uncultivated ground," through Proto-Germanic *landą and Old English land — the root of German Land, Dutch land, and the whole English family of land compounds (highland, mainland, lowland). The compound marshland, formed by the sixteenth century, simply fuses the two: the wetland terrain as a class of land, the English Fens, the Dutch polders, the salt marshes of every coast.

  • earthen: PIE *h₁er- → PGmc *erþō → OE eorþe → eorþen → ME erthen → earthen. Two millennia of stable meaning: "made of earth or clay," the humble and the handmade.
  • causeways: Latin calx "heel" → calcare "to tread" → via calciāta "trodden road" → Old North French cauciee → Anglo-French causee → causey + way → causeways. The image of the heel-pressed road; the lime-calx homonym kept strictly separate.
  • marshland: PIE *mori- "sea" → PGmc *mari → *marisk- → OE mersc "marsh"; PIE *lendʰ- → PGmc *landą → OE land; marsh + land → marshland. The sea-border and the terrain.

All three words share a theme of elemental ground: the earth that is the material of building (earthen), the way that is pressed out of the earth by heels (causeway), and the land that is neither earth nor sea but the border of both (marshland). Together in the card's sentence they build a single prehistoric, pre-industrial landscape — the humble road of rammed earth crossing the sea-edged wetland.
