*steu- "to push, strike, knock; to thrust, stick" (Proto-Indo-European, reconstructed root, c. 4000–3000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *stukkaz "stump, log, tree-trunk, block" (extended *stuk- with the expressive geminate *-kk-, first millennium BCE) → Old English stocc "stump, trunk, post, block of wood" (attested from the 8th century CE) → Middle English stok "stock, stump, trunk; base, store" (12th–15th centuries) → English stock "a tree-trunk; a stump cut close to the ground; base, store, lineage" (15th century onward) → stock + adjectival suffix -y → stocky "short, broad, thickset, sturdy — like a tree-stock" (modern physical sense recorded in the 1890s; an earlier 16th-century sense "stock-like, stiff, wooden, immovable" existed from the same base) Note: The chain is linear and attested at every stage from Old English onward; the only nuance is that the specifically physical "thickset" sense is a late coinage (late 19th century), while the 16th-century stocky meant "like a stock" in the sense of stiff or dull. The PIE root *steu- is securely reconstructible (Pokorny; de Vaan ad Latin tundō), and its descendants include Vedic Sanskrit tudáti "he strikes, thrusts", Greek týptō "I strike, beat" (extended *steup-), and Latin tundō "to beat, pound" — but the English line passes exclusively through the Germanic *stukkaz. The comparison branches are sister cognates, not the direct ancestor of stocky.
