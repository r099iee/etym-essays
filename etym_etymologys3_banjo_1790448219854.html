The evolution of banjo is the story of an African lute remade in American slavery into a world instrument, then borrowed back by the world with its history half-erased and now painstakingly restored. West Africa possessed a family of skin-headed spike lutes: Wolof xalam, Mandinka ngoni, and in Kongo-Angola regions instruments called mbanza, banza. They share a gourd or calabash body covered with skin, a stick neck pierced through, and three to five gut or fibre strings. Enslaved Senegambians and Angolans carried the concept, if not the object, across the Atlantic in the 1600s, rebuilding it on Caribbean and Virginian plantations with gourds, barrel hoops, and horsehair. Thomas Jefferson in Notes on Virginia 1781 already notes the banjar as the instrument proper to Black musicians, proof of establishment by the Revolution.

Early American spellings betray oral transmission: banjer, banjore, banjar, bangoe, banshaw appear in newspapers, runaway ads, and diaries 1730-1800, always for Black players at dances and frolics. White observers heard an African word through English ears, vacillating on vowels and final consonants. By the 1820s-30s commercial makers in Baltimore and Philadelphia standardise spelling to banjo and form to a wooden hoop, tacked skin head, and four gut strings played stroke-style. Joel Sweeney, a Virginian minstrel entrepreneur, claims to have added the fifth drone string around 1835 and to have taught the instrument to whites in blackface, a self-serving tale that nonetheless marks the moment the banjo crosses the colour line into commercial entertainment.

  • Phonetic path: Kimbundu mbanza /ᵐbanza/ with prenasalised mb- heard by English ears as b-, final -a raised to -o/-oe on English model of buffalo, tobacco loans; stress on first syllable fixed early. Variant banjore shows intrusive -r- common in non-rhotic transcriptions. Modern RP /ˈbændʒəʊ/ with affricate dʒ reflects palatalisation of z before j-like glide, completed by 1850.
  • Minstrel era 1840s-1900s: blackface troupes make the banjo a global emblem of supposed plantation gaiety; tutors, parlour sheet music, and manufacturers like Stewart and Fairbanks sell thousands. The instrument gains metal frets, brackets, and five strings, becoming louder and more guitar-like. Genuine Black innovators like Horace Weston and James Bohee tour but are billed as exceptions to a white-owned form.
  • Folk, jazz, and bluegrass after 1900: tenor four-string banjo drives Dixieland rhythm; Appalachian whites preserve clawhammer and fingerpicking; Earl Scruggs after 1945 invents three-finger bluegrass roll that defines modern five-string virtuosity. Each style revoices the same gourd-descended box for new dances.
  • Socio-cultural repair since 1970s: scholars and players like Dena Epstein, Cecilia Conway, Rhiannon Giddens, and the Carolina Chocolate Drops document African roots, revive gourd banjos and akonting technique, and name minstrelsy as appropriation. Museums now label the banjo as African-American creation, correcting older bandore European-origin stories that once derived it from Portuguese bandurra without evidence.
  • Literary attestations: Jefferson 1781 banjar, 1784 Maryland bangoe, Dickens American Notes 1842 banjo minstrels, Mark Twain riverboat banjos, Wodehouse 1930s comic banjo-as-blackface shorthand, modern folk revival lyrics. Wodehouse line must be read in this arc: casual period racism resting on a real Black art it does not credit.

In sum, gourd to hoop to resonator, Africa to Virginia to world stage: banjo keeps its African name in anglicised dress, its drum-lute body in industrial materials, and its bright voice across genres, now increasingly heard, as Jefferson first heard it, as Black music.
