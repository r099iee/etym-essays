=== ROOT WORD: TROLL (single root, borrowed from Old Norse) === Stage 1 — Origin of Old Norse troll is UNCERTAIN / DEBATED: no secure PIE root is agreed. The usual reconstruction posits a Proto-Germanic *trullan- "troll, monster, supernatural being" (reconstructed; attested only in Germanic — cf. Middle High German trolle "lout, goblin", trollen "to run with short steps", Swedish trolla "to charm, bewitch"), with a possible prehistoric connection to a root meaning "to walk, tread, wander" and, beyond that, a possible but unprovable link to PIE *der- / *dreu- "to run" (cf. Greek tréchein "to run"). The pre-Germanic stage is explicitly uncertain. Stage 2 — Old Norse troll "a supernatural being; giant, fiend, monster" (attested in 13th-c. Icelandic sagas and the Eddas; also tǫll, plural) Stage 3 — English troll "a giant or monstrous being of Scandinavian folklore" (borrowed from Scandinavian in the 17th c., first recorded c. 1616 in antiquarian and travel writing on Norse myth) Stage 4 — Modern English troll + plural -s → trolls "trolls, plural" (the plural form used in the text) => trolls (modern English plural noun) DEBATE / NUANCE: The deeper origin is genuinely uncertain, and the OED treats the word as of uncertain origin. Three main proposals circulate: (a) connection to a Germanic root meaning "to walk, tread, wander" (the troll as the loping, roaming being of the mountains), sometimes taken back to a PIE *der-/*dreu- "run" — attractive but unprovable; (b) derivation from a base meaning "to bewitch, enchant" (cf. Swedish trolla "to do magic"), tying the word to the creature's magical arts; (c) a link to a root meaning "to rage, howl, devour". Honest scholarship stops at the reconstructed Germanic stage rather than forcing a PIE etymon. NOTE ON THE INTERNET SENSE: The modern sense "troll = online provocateur" (c. 1990s) is not a normal semantic descendant of the folklore chain; it draws partly on the monster's lurking image and partly on the unrelated Old French-derived fishing verb "to troll" (to trail a baited line, from Old French troller "to hunt in a wandering fashion"). The two lines cross-pollinated in the 1990s and fused into the modern internet sense, as discussed in the essay.
