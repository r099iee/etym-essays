INDUSTRY is a noun meaning 'habitual diligence; systematic, untiring application to work; assiduity and productive labour'. In its older, humanistic sense it denotes the continuous, orderly exertion of a diligent mind — the disciplined construction of effort 'from within' the person. In its modern, dominant sense it denotes organised economic activity: manufacturing, trade, and the production of goods and services as a sector of the economy. The word thus spans two worlds — the personal virtue of hard work and the institutional reality of factories, corporations, and economic sectors — and the relationship between the two is the central fact of its history: the modern economic sense grew directly out of the older virtue sense when the Industrial Age praised (and personified) the diligent worker as the engine of national wealth.

Polysemy and modern usage:

  • Virtue sense (the older sense; active in biography, literature, and praise): habitual diligence, untiring application, systematic hard work — 'His industry was stupendous'; 'a scholar of great industry'; the engine of sustained intellectual or manual productivity. This is the sense of the present card.
  • Economic-sector sense (the modern dominant sense): a branch of economic activity — the cotton industry, the steel industry, the film industry, the service industry; organised manufacturing and production treated as a sector, with its own institutions, workers, and capital.
  • Aggregate economic sense: 'industry' with the definite article — 'industry' as the whole world of manufacturing and commerce, as opposed to agriculture or government; 'heavy industry', 'light industry', 'the manufacturing industry'.
  • Corporate and business jargon: 'the industry' meaning the relevant business community — industry leaders, industry standards, industry insiders; and attributive uses — industry training, industry awards.
  • Historical / obsolete sense: 'skill, cleverness, purposeful contrivance' — in older English (from Latin industria and Old French industrie) the word could denote ingenuity or an occupation requiring skill, before narrowing to diligence and then to economics.
  • Attributive and adjectival uses: 'industrial' (from the same root via Latin industria) — industrial revolution, industrial society, industrial action, industrial espionage — and 'industrious' (diligent, hard-working), which preserves the old virtue sense in a single adjective.

Historical and semantic bridge: the bridge between the virtue and the economic sector is the eighteenth-century idea that national wealth is produced by diligent labour. The Latin industria meant inner diligence — literally 'building within' (from indu-, 'within', + struere, 'to build'). In English the virtue sense was primary from the sixteenth century. When political economists from the eighteenth century onward made 'industry' the engine of prosperity — Adam Smith's 'industry' as productive labour — the word began to migrate from the person to the process, and the Industrial Revolution completed the transfer: by the nineteenth century 'industry' named the factories and sectors that diligent labour had built. Both senses now coexist, the virtue sense preserved in biography and praise, the economic sense dominant in everyday and business speech.

In the literary context of this card — a biographical account: 'From that time forward his literary work was uninterrupted save by attacks of illness. His industry was stupendous.' — the word signifies the personal virtue of relentless intellectual labour: the subject's astonishing capacity for sustained, prolific, and varied production. It emphatically does not mean factories or manufacturing; the modern economic sense is entirely absent. The tone is admiring and quantitative: 'stupendous' measures the sheer magnitude of a working life that wrote on an endless range of subjects, discovered Tennyson for his generation, influenced the writing and success of Carlyle's French Revolution, and proceeded uninterrupted save by attacks of illness. 'Industry' here names the engine of that productivity — the inner, habitual discipline of constant, systematic work, the 'building within' that the word's Latin root describes so precisely.
