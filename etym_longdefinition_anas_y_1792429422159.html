Anasūyā (proper noun) — A celebrated female sage (*ṛṣikā*), forest hermit, and spiritual matriarch of ancient Indian epic and Purāṇic tradition; the wife of the venerable sage Atri (one of the canonical Saptarṣis) and mother of the divine triad (*Trimūrti*) incarnations: Dattātreya (Viṣṇu), Durvāsas (Śiva), and Candra/Soma (Brahmā). Morphologically formed from the Sanskrit negative prefix an- ("not, un-") and asūyā ("envy, malice, jealous fault-finding"), her name signifies "she who is completely free from envy or malice." In classical Hindu culture and the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Anasūyā is revered as the supreme archetype of unshakeable wifely devotion (*pativratā*) and the generous forest patron who bestowed upon Sītā divine unfading garments, ornaments, and celestial cosmetics.

1. Epic Persona in the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa: The Meeting in the Daṇḍaka Forest. In the closing chapters of the Ayodhyākāṇḍa and the inception of the Araṇyakāṇḍa, Prince Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa penetrate the threshold of the deep southern wilderness and visit the sacred hermitage (*āśrama*) of sage Atri and his wife Anasūyā:

  • The Discourse on Strīdharma: The aged, holy Anasūyā warmly welcomes Sītā with tender maternal affection. Observing that Sītā has willingly renounced imperial opulence to follow her husband into dangerous, predator-infested forests, Anasūyā delivers a famous philosophical discourse on *strīdharma* (the spiritual virtue and ethical duty of a devoted wife). Anasūyā extols that a faithful wife views her husband not merely as a worldly partner, but as her supreme spiritual teacher and deity, maintaining that steadfast devotion (*tapas*) generates spiritual merit exceeding all Vedic sacrifices.
  • The Divine Cosmetics and Unfading Garments (*Aṅgarāga*): Marveling at Sītā's immaculate purity, Anasūyā bestows upon her a miraculous set of celestial gifts: divine silks and robes that will never fray or soil, radiant jewelry, a heavenly floral garland that never withers, and a precious celestial ointment or unguent (*aṅgarāga*). Anasūyā anoints Sītā, declaring that this ointment will cause her beauty and radiance to shine eternally unmarred by the dirt, thorns, and grueling heat of the wilderness. This gift plays a crucial aesthetic and narrative role throughout the epic: even during Sītā’s subsequent terrifying abduction by Rāvaṇa and long imprisonment in the aśoka grove of Lan̄kā, she remains perpetually radiant, fragrant, and unstained by demonic filth.
  • Command over Nature through Tapas: The sage Atri recounts to Rāma how, during an ancient ten-year catastrophic drought when the earth was parched and living beings were perishing, Anasūyā performed intense, unbroken austerities (*tapas*), thereby causing life-saving fruits and roots to sprout from the desiccated earth and redirecting the sacred waters of the Gaṅgā to flow through their hermitage.

2. Purāṇic Lore: The Testing of Chastity and Motherhood of the Trimūrti. In Purāṇic literature (notably the Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.24, the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, and the Śiva Purāṇa), Anasūyā's spiritual power reaches cosmic proportions:

  • The Humbling of the Great Gods: A famous legend recounts how the three great goddesses—Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī, and Pārvatī—became envious of sage Nārada’s ceaseless praises of Anasūyā's flawless virtue. They dispatched their divine consorts—Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva—to earth to test her. Appearing at Atri's hermitage disguised as mendicant Brahmins while Atri was away, the three deities demanded food as guests (*atithi*), but added an impossible condition: Anasūyā must serve them naked (*digambara*).
  • The Transformation into Infants: Undaunted and perceiving their divine identity through her yogic insight, Anasūyā sprinkled consecrated water over the three gods, instantly transforming them into three helpless newborn infants. Having stripped herself of all lust and false shame, she nursed the crying divine babies with pure maternal love. When the frantic goddesses descended to earth begging for their husbands to be restored, Anasūyā graciously agreed, whereupon the three deities united to grant her a boon, incarnating as her divine sons: Dattātreya (the supreme guru of Advaita, incarnating Viṣṇu), Durvāsas (the fierce ascetic, incarnating Śiva), and Soma (the moon deity, incarnating Brahmā).

3. Symbolic and Ethical Meaning in Indian Philosophy. Within Hindu ethical psychology, the name Anasūyā represents an essential prerequisite for spiritual illumination. In the Bhagavad Gītā (Chapter 9, Verse 1), Lord Kṛṣṇa tells Arjuna: idaṃ tu te guhyatamaṃ pravakṣyāmy anasūyave ("To you who are free from envy (*anasūyave*), I shall reveal this most secret wisdom"). True spiritual receptivity cannot exist where there is asūyā—the petty, carping jealousy that seeks flaws in greatness. Anasūyā personifies this spotless, generous magnanimity of consciousness.

4. Cultural Veneration and Modern Pilgrimage. Today, Anasūyā remains an intensely revered figure across India. Her primary shrine, the *Sati Anasuya Ashram*, stands nestled in the dense forests of Chitrakoot along the Mandakini River, drawing millions of pilgrims. In modern literature and film, her narrative is celebrated as an enduring testament to the transcendent spiritual autonomy and miraculous power achievable through selfless ethical discipline.
