curator — the biography of the heed. The word curator is the biography of the heed. Its heart is the Latin cūra, 'care, concern, anxiety', which Michiel de Vaan traces through Proto-Italic *kʷoizā to the Indo-European root *kʷeys-, 'to heed, to perceive, to take care'. The ancient meaning of cūra was twofold from the start: it was the positive care one lavishes on a charge — and it was the negative anxiety that gnaws at the mind. Virgil's Dido, 'wounded by a grave care' (gravi cura saucia), suffers from the same cūra that a Roman magistrate spent on the city's works. The double meaning is preserved in the modern family: to be 'careful' is to be full of care, anxious; to be 'secure' is to be without care, free of it; and to curate is to take the caring half and make it an office. From cūra, Latin derived the verb cūrāre, 'to take care of, to attend to', and from the verb the agent noun cūrātor, 'one who has charge of a thing' — an overseer of roads, of finances, of temples, of the games. In Roman administration the curator was a figure of responsible, accountable charge: the curator aquarum looked after the water supply, the curator operum publicorum the public works. This is already the modern word in embryo: a curator is one who has been given the care of something and answers for it. Medieval Latin pressed the same word into the service of the church. The cūrātus was the priest responsible for the cura animarum, the 'care of souls', whence English curate and French curé — the spiritual sibling of the museum office, caring for persons where the curator cares for things. The path to English ran through the legal and administrative registers. Old French curatour carried the word into England in the fourteenth century, first for legal guardians and overseers — a curator of an estate, of a minor's affairs, of an inheritance — and by the seventeenth century for the custodian of a museum or collection. The decisive shift of the modern period is the transfer of care from persons to things: the Roman curator cared for the city's water; the medieval curateur cared for the ward's estate; the modern curator cares for the museum's objects, selecting, conserving, interpreting and displaying them. The curatorial function then spread sideways through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries — from museums to libraries, archives, zoos and botanical gardens — and in our own century it has expanded again, ironically, to digital data: a 'curator' now heeds and orders playlists, feeds and archives of content, an overseer of attention itself. The root idea of the heed — of taking charge of something and caring for it — has proved astonishingly portable, because care, once it becomes an office, can be given to anything. The same root flowered across the whole Latin-derived vocabulary, and it is worth seeing how rich the single root is: accūrātus, 'done with care', gave accurate; sēcūrus, 'without care, free from anxiety', gave secure and sure; cūriōsus, 'full of care for knowledge', gave curious; prōcūrāre, 'to manage, to see to', gave procure; neglegere, 'not to heed', gave neglect — the antonym of the whole family. Every one of these words is a reflex of the ancient double meaning of cūra: the anxiety that wounds and the care that preserves. All of this lies latent in the sleeping Rottcodd. A curator is, before anything else, one who cares — and Peake lets the word's deep sense of anxious vigilance hang in the air of a hall where no one comes, where the heed has hardened into habit and the care into a long, dreaming watch over carvings that never wake. The irony is not that Rottcodd fails to be a curator; it is that he is the purest form of one — a man whose entire being has become the caring he is no longer needed to perform.
Summary of the evolutionary path
  • Root: PIE *kʷeys- 'to heed' → Proto-Italic *kʷoizā 'care' → Latin cūra 'care, anxiety, charge'.
  • Roman office: cūrātor, the overseer of works, finances and persons — accountable charge.
  • Medieval branch: cūrātus, the priest with the care of souls — English curate, French curé.
  • English entrance: Old French curatour (14th c.), the legal guardian and overseer.
  • Museum turn: 17th-century curator, the official in charge of a collection — care of things displacing care of persons.
  • Widening: libraries, archives, zoos, gardens (19th–20th c.); exhibitions and the creative 'curatorial turn' (20th c.).
  • Digital extension: 21st-century content curation — care applied to streams of information.
  • Literary use: Peake's Rottcodd, the curator of an empty hall, is the word's paradox made flesh — care without an audience, a vigil that has become pure ritual.
