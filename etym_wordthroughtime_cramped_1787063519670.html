- - 14th century: English borrows crampe from French as a noun for a painful muscular spasm and for a bent iron clasp or hook; the participle "cramped" at first means simply "afflicted with cramp".
- - 15th–16th centuries: the adjective extends from the cramping body to the space that causes the feeling — a "cramped room" is one that presses its occupants together like a cramp.
- - 17th century: "cramped" is used of writing — letters pressed and crowded together on the page, echoing the image of a bent, compressed shape.
- - 18th–19th centuries: figurative use for restricted freedom and stunted development appears; "cramped circumstances" (poverty, confinement) becomes a stock phrase in novels.
- - 20th century: "to cramp one’s style" becomes a fixed idiom; the word is ubiquitous in housing discourse ("cramped flats") and descriptions of urban living.
- - Today: dual emphasis — literal physical discomfort (space, limbs, handwriting) and figurative restriction of growth or freedom; a slightly stuffy, oppressive connotation persists.
