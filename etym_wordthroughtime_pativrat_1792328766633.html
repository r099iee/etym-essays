- c. 1500–1000 BCE — In Vedic antiquity, women participated alongside husbands as sahadharmiṇī in ritual sacrifices, with marital fidelity understood as a reciprocal domestic virtue.
- c. 500 BCE–300 CE — Crystallized in Epic and Purāṇic literature (Rāmāyaṇa, Mahābhārata) as a distinct spiritual archetype possessing supernatural tapobala, exemplified by Sāvitrī and Sītā.
- c. 2nd c. CE–10th c. CE — Codified in Brahmanical legal texts (Manusmṛti) as the supreme domestic duty (strīdharma), formalizing the ideological elevation of husband-devotion into a woman's primary religious path.
- c. 12th–17th c. CE — Reinterpreted by medieval Bhakti mystical poets as an allegorical model for the soul's passionate, unconditional devotion toward the Supreme Godhead.
- 19th c. — Debated during colonial-era Indian social reform movements concerning widow treatment, education, and the boundaries of female agency.
- Modern Era — Analyzed by contemporary feminist theorists and sociologists as both an ideological instrument of patriarchal containment and an enduring cultural symbol of female resilience and spiritual dignity.
