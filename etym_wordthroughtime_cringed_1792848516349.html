- c. 1200 — simply bending: Middle English crengen means to bend, yield, or give way, of boughs and bodies alike, with no shame attached.
- 13th–14th c. — fear bends it: the verb attaches to cowering before blows and lords, and the physical crouch takes on servility.
- 16th–17th c. — the courtier's word: cringing flatterers crowd satires and sermons, the posture now a moral diagnosis of abjectness.
- 18th–19th c. — domesticated dread: novelists make servants, schoolboys, and dogs cringe, spreading the verb from courts to kitchens.
- Late 20th c. — the inward turn: cringe becomes a noun for second-hand embarrassment, and comedy built on it (cringe comedy) makes observers cringe at awfulness.
- Today — the full-body wince: from torture-memories to awkward television, cringed marks every folding-away, physical or social, with pity or contempt to taste.
