- [13th century: "to wonder at miracles"] - Merveillen enters Middle English from Old French; the first senses are religious — wondering at miracles and God's works; wonder is a sacred emotion.
- [14th–15th centuries: secular marvels] - Chaucer and the romancers use it of strange lands, magic and beauty; "marvelling" becomes the stock response of knights and travellers.
- [16th–17th centuries: intellectual wonder] - The King James Bible fixes "Marvel not at this" (John 5:28); the Renaissance wonder-cabinets and voyages of discovery make marvelling a virtue — wonder at nature and art.
- [18th–19th centuries: domesticated delight] - Science and the novel domesticate it: people marvel at machines, landscapes and discoveries; the word loses sacred awe and gains delighted curiosity.
- [Contemporary: literary and playful] - Slightly bookish or affectionate: "I was marvelling at how..." — warm, admiring, often tinged with irony; the spelling marvelling (British) versus marveling (American) marks the reader's dialect.
