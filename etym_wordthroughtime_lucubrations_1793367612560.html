- Late 16th Century - Borrowed into Early Modern English directly from Latin *lūcubrātiō*; used respectfully to designate serious, dedicated nocturnal study or literary treatises composed by candlelight; regarded as a mark of heroic scholarly discipline.
- 17th Century - Popularized in authorial prefaces, academic titles, and theological collections as a modest or dignified term for one's own writings (e.g., styling published essays as 'scholarly lucubrations'); perceived with reverence in scholarly circles.
- 18th Century - Shifted under Enlightenment periodical essayists (Addison, Steele) and satirists (Swift, Pope); began to be used playfully or ironically to poke fun at the cloistered, dusty pedantry of university scholars whose writing smelled of the lamp.
- 19th Century - Cemented as a devastating pejorative by caustic literary critics in periodicals like *The Edinburgh Review* and *Quarterly Review*; applied to dismiss tedious, over-labored, and self-important literary works.
- 20th Century to Present - Almost exclusively ironic, sarcastic, or archaic; deployed to satirize overly dense, pretentious, and pedantic academic or philosophical tracts.
