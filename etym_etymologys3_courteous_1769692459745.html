The history of courteous is the history of a fence. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *gher-, "to grasp, enclose, surround," a root that built fences and yards before it built courts. Across the Indo-European world it enclosed space: in India it became Sanskrit gṛha "house" (modern Hindi ghar "home"); in Germanic it produced *gardaz, giving English yard and garden and German Garten; in Slavic it formed gradŭ "fortified city" (Russian gorod). In Latium the enclosure-root produced hortus "garden, enclosed yard," and from com- "together" + hortus came cohors, "an enclosed yard," which expanded to mean the men quartered together in it — a military unit (our cohort) or a retinue.

The turn from yard to court happened in the dissolving Latin of the early Middle Ages. In Vulgar Latin the heavy cohors contracted and softened into *curtis or *cortis — the courtyard of a manor, and by extension the manor itself with its household. Feudalism then did the rest: in Old French cort became the court of a king or great lord, the enclosed household that governed a realm and, crucially, set the standard of behaviour. Around the 11th–12th centuries Old French formed the adjective curteis (later courtois) — literally "of the court" — to name the polished, chivalric manner expected of courtiers. Chivalry, courtly love, and the courtesy books (the cortesie that taught noble children table manners and bowing) made the court the school of refinement, and curteis the badge of the refined.

English took the word in the 13th century through Anglo-Norman: Middle English curteis, curteous meant gracious, courteous, mannerly, and the sense widened from courtiers to anyone of good breeding. Then came a remarkable, purely orthographic event: in the 16th century, English scholars, eager to make spelling transparent, "etymologized" the word, respelling curteous as courteous to make the court visible. The pronunciation [ˈkɜːtiəs] was unaffected; only the spelling changed. The old form survives in the surname Curtis. Meanwhile the ideal itself was democratizing: the manners of the court spread through gentry and middle class until "courteous" meant not "of the court" but simply "polite and considerate to everyone."

Austen's usage in Pride and Prejudice (1813) catches the word at the hinge of that history. Sir William Lucas, a tradesman raised to knighthood, is "all attention to everybody" — his courtesy is not the old aristocratic polish but an obliging, inoffensive, status-conscious attentiveness. The word by then describes a social virtue anyone could claim, even as it still glitters faintly with the court it once belonged to.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *gher- → Latin hortus → cohors → Vulgar Latin *curtis (syncope of -ho-) → Old French cort → curteis → Middle English curteis → English courteous [ˈkɜːtiəs]; the -eous ending is a suffix respelling, not a sound shift.
  • Semantic drift: "enclosure" (yard/garden) → "enclosed household" (military cohort, manor) → "royal court" → "manners of the court" → "general politeness and consideration."
  • Socio-cultural factors: feudalism concentrated refinement at the royal court; chivalry and courtly love codified courtesy; the courtesy-book genre spread it; urbanization and democracy generalized it from an aristocratic to a universal ideal.
  • Literary attestations: medieval courtesy books; Chaucer's curteisie; Austen's Pride and Prejudice (Sir William Lucas "made him courteous"); the word's modern career as a staple of polite correspondence and diplomacy.
  • Modern situation: courteous is fully democratic — it describes considerate politeness available to anyone, with only the faint afterglow of the medieval court in its spelling.

Few words carry their whole social history in their spelling as courteous does: open the word and you find the yard, the fence, the cohort, the feudal manor, the chivalric court, and finally — democratized — a simple ideal of consideration for other people.
