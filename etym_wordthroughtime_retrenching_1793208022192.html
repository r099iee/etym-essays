- 1580s — the soldier spade: retrenching first means cutting secondary earthworks inside a fortress or camp, the engineer art of the fallback line.
- 17th c. — the blade turns civilian: writers extend it to cutting back superfluities, expenses, and privileges; the military digging becomes a general metaphor of reduction.
- 18th c. — the virtue of economy: moralists and ministers praise retrenching luxury and public expense; the word smells of ledgers, audits, and Augustan self-discipline.
- Early 19th c. — the radical slogan: peace and retrenchment rally reformers against war budgets and sinecures, and the source sentence plays the military sense against the political one.
- Late 19th–20th c. — the military sense fossilizes: modern artillery and manoeuvre make retrenchments quaint, while the fiscal sense thrives in treasuries and boardrooms.
- Today — economy owns the word: governments and firms are retrenching when they cut spending or shed staff, while the spade survives only in siege histories.
