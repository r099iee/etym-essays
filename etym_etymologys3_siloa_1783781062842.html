SILOA is a Semitic name with no Indo-European ancestry whatsoever, and its biography is the biography of water in a dry land. It descends from the Biblical Hebrew noun Šīlōaḥ, built on the well-attested verbal root š-l-ḥ, "to send." The name is not arbitrary. In the eighth century BCE King Hezekiah, preparing Jerusalem against the Assyrian siege (2 Kings 20:20; 2 Chronicles 32:30; Isaiah 22:9–11), cut a rock tunnel more than 533 meters long beneath the City of David to carry the waters of the spring of Gihon — the only dependable water source of the old city, which lay outside the fortifications — into a pool inside the walls at the mouth of the Tyropoeon Valley. The pool and its channel were the "sending" of water: the conduit that sent the spring's flow into the city. The Siloam Inscription, found in the tunnel, commemorates the meeting of the two gangs of cutters and confirms the reality behind the name. The word Šīlōaḥ therefore began as engineering terminology, a functional description of a waterwork, and only secondarily became the place-name of the pool. In the prophets the name began its climb from hydraulics to theology. Isaiah 8:6 sets "the waters of Shiloah that go softly" against the flood of Assyria: the quiet, trusting stream of the holy city is the image of humble faith in God, while the overwhelming river is the invading empire. The gentle flow becomes a standing emblem of trust, and the physical softness of the water becomes a moral and spiritual value. The pool also witnessed a great national thanksgiving: Nehemiah 3:15 records the royal garden at the pool of Shiloah in the restoration of the city walls, and Isaiah 12:3's "wells of salvation" was read by later tradition in the same waters. The New Testament completed the semantic apotheosis. In John 9:7 Jesus sends a man born blind to wash in the pool of Siloam, and the evangelist pauses to gloss the name: "Siloam, which is by interpretation, Sent." The washing returns sight, and the episode is one of the most densely symbolic in the Gospel: the Sent One sends the blind man to the place called Sending; the water restores the light of the world to the eyes of a man born in darkness. Patristic exegesis from Irenaeus and Tertullian onward read the pool as a type of baptism — the washing that gives spiritual sight — and the blind man as the catechumen, so that the hydraulic name becomes a font of sacramental theology. John Chrysostom and Augustine both dwell on the name itself as the key to the miracle. The transmission of the name is a small anthology of Bible-translation history. The Septuagint (3rd c. BCE) transliterated it Σιλωάμ, and the Gospels carried that form into Greek; the Vulgate of Jerome (c. 405 CE) used Siloë and Siloa, shortening the stem. English translation divided on the same fault line: Tyndale (1530) and the King James Version (1611) gave "Siloam," following the Greek; the Latin-derived "Siloa" survived in patristic and humanist writing and was taken up by Milton in Paradise Lost (1667), where "Siloa's Brook that flow'd / Fast by the Oracle of God" is set against the "Aonian Mount" of the pagan Muses. Milton's choice is doctrinal as well as metrical: the true inspiration of his epic is the Spirit that brooded on the biblical waters, not the muses of Helicon, and Siloa is the biblical brook that carries it. Later English poetry and hymnody kept the name alive in its devotional register. The hymn "Siloam's shady rill" (with lines about the "silent waters" that flow) made the pool an image of gentle grace sung in Sunday schools for generations, while archaeologists from the nineteenth century to the present day excavated the very pool and channel the name describes. The living Arabic name of the neighborhood, Silwan, preserves the ancient appellation over the same ground. In every register — engineering, prophecy, Gospel, epic, hymn, archaeology — the name keeps its single root meaning: water sent, and the God who sends. Sound and orthographic notes:
  • Hebrew š- (ש) is rendered s- in Greek (Σ) and Latin (S), the familiar Semitic sibilant shift in Bible transmission.
  • The two English forms reflect two Greek stems: Siloam from Σιλωάμ (with final -m, the Hebrew -aḥ becoming -ám), Siloa from the shortened stem used in Latin patristic texts.
  • Milton scans Siloa with two syllables, keeping the word metrically light beside the four-syllable "Aonian Mount."
