- Old English (9th–11th c.) — tīdung: “announcement, event, news” — an ordinary word for reports and occurrences
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) — “tidings” (plural dominant): news of war, of arrivals, of miracles — the everyday word of chronicles, courts and tavern talk; construed as singular or plural indifferently
- 16th–17th c. — Bible English fixes the sacred register: Tyndale's “tydynges of greate ioye” (1526), the King James “good tidings of great joy” (1611) — the word gains an evangelistic ring it never loses
- 18th–19th c. — still standard in newspapers and novels: “tidings from the front”, “glad tidings” — serious, warm, slightly formal
- 20th c. — “news” displaces it in journalism; tidings retreats to literary, historical and religious prose, and to the carol “tidings of comfort and joy”
- Today — archaic by default; survives in set phrases, fantasy and historical fiction (Tolkien), and festive or ironic use; “no tidings” still reads as grave and old-fashioned
