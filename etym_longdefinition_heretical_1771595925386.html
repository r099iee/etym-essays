heretical — of, relating to, or constituting heresy; holding or advocating a belief that runs counter to the established doctrine of a church — and, by extension, any opinion that flouts the received truth of a community. In this literary context Ruskin is describing the reception he expects for his own early volume of Modern Painters: the work "advocated opinions which will sound heretical to the ordinary connoisseur." The word is chosen with deliberate, self-aware theatricality. Heresy in the Christian tradition was the gravest of intellectual crimes — punishable by anathema, by exile, and historically by fire — so to call one's own aesthetic theories "heretical" is a provocation: Ruskin casts the art-critical establishment as a kind of secular church whose dogmas are not to be questioned, and himself as the man who questions them. The tone is at once defiant, ironic, and faintly amused, because Ruskin does not concede that he is wrong; he announces that his opinions will sound heretical, locating the heresy in the ear of the orthodox judge rather than in any error of his own doctrine. The modal "sound" is crucial: it makes heresy a matter of reception, of what the connoisseur will hear, not a claim about the truth of the opinions themselves. Polysemy in historical order.
  • Sense 1 (the religious core, from the 14th c.): "of or relating to heresy; contrary to the defined doctrine of a church; holding a belief formally censured as heresy" — a heretic, a heretical opinion, a heretical sect.
  • Sense 2 (the Greek root sense, historical/technical): "of a chosen school of thought" — in Greek philosophy and medicine a hairesis was a deliberately adopted school (the medical "sects" of the Hippocratics and of Galen), and the earliest sense of the adjective was simply "given to choosing for oneself," without any charge of error.
  • Sense 3 (the extended secular sense, 19th c. onward): "at odds with what is generally accepted" in any field — artistic, scientific, political — so that a bold theory or a radical style may be called heretical without any religious meaning.
  • Sense 4 (attitudinal): knowingly defiant, provocative, icon-smashing — the tone of the person who flouts received wisdom with pleasure; the charge may range from genuine scandal to playful provocation.
  • Related formations: heresy (n., c. 1225, via Old French heresie and Latin haeresis), heretic (n., 14th c.), heretically (adv.), hereticalness (n., rare), heretication (rare), to hereticate (rare).
The semantic bridge. The whole family descends from the Greek verb haireisthai, "to take for oneself," which developed the sense "to choose": what you deliberately take up becomes what you have chosen. The noun hairesis meant "a taking, a choosing," and then — crucially — "a chosen system of thought, a school, a sect, a party." The physician who followed the hairesis of Hippocrates chose a medical allegiance; the philosopher's school was his hairesis; in Greek politics the word could even mean a faction. The sectarian overtone was already present in Greek before Christianity adopted the word, but it was in Christian usage that hairesis was sealed as "false doctrine, deviation from the teaching of the Church" — a choice, but the wrong choice, made for oneself against the authority of the community. Heresy is, etymologically, the crime of choosing for yourself. Boundaries against near-synonyms.
  • Unorthodox is the mild, neutral description of anything that departs from custom and carries no charge of censure.
  • Heterodox is the scholarly, almost clinical word for divergence from a received system of doctrine or theory — the polite learned cousin of heretical, without the charge of spiritual error.
  • Blasphemous attacks the sacred directly — insulting God or holy things — whereas heretical attacks the Church's teaching itself, not necessarily the deity.
  • Sacrilegious profanes sacred objects or places; it is an act of desecration, not of belief.
  • Iconoclastic smashes venerated images and institutions but implies a stance of attack against symbols rather than a divergent doctrine.
  • Dissident is the political and secular word for one who disagrees with an established order; it carries none of the theological gravity.
  • Apostate names one who abandons the faith altogether, which is the step beyond heresy — the heretic keeps his own belief, the apostate leaves the fold.
Grammar and register. Heretical is a relational adjective formed from heretic + the Latinate suffix -al, recorded in English from about 1500. It is used of opinions, doctrines, books, persons, and — by figurative extension — of anything that flies in the face of established practice. The register is formal and, in religious contexts, grave; in secular use it ranges from the mildly alarming to the playfully provocative. The word is common in academic and critical prose precisely because it allows a writer to stake a bold claim with a wink: "a heretical view of the economy," "heretical ideas about the nature of time." Ruskin's use belongs to this last, theatrical mode — the critic plays the heretic in order to expose the orthodoxy of the connoisseurs. Modern use. In contemporary English heretical appears most often in (1) religious and doctrinal writing, (2) science and intellectual history, where bold theories are called heretical to their reigning paradigms (Copernicus, Galileo, Darwin), (3) art and literary criticism, where new styles flout received taste, and (4) everyday rhetorical exaggeration, where a controversial opinion — about food, football, or fashion — is jokingly pronounced heretical. The word has travelled from the fires of the Inquisition to the mild irony of a dinner-table disagreement, but it has never lost the shape of its root: the heretical is whatever a community has decided may not be chosen.
