- [Late 16th c. entry (c. 1580)] - Borrowed from French prémonitoire, from Late Latin praemonitorius 'belonging to a forewarning'; it appears in the plays of Thomas Kyd meaning simply 'conveying an advance warning, giving notice beforehand'. The tone was neutral and dignified - an honourable cousin of 'admonitory'.
- [Centuries of neutral foresight (17th-19th c.)] - For three centuries the word stayed a colourless, respectable adjective for any outward sign that speaks before the event: a premonitory symptom, a premonitory tremor, a premonitory hint. It carried no dread, only foreknowledge, and lived mostly in formal and medical prose.
- [Drift toward the dark (19th-20th c.)] - The word gradually darkened under the pull of its noun 'premonition': it began to attach itself to chills, signs of disaster, and intuition of the unwelcome, so that 'premonitory' took on an ominous, faintly supernatural colouring of approaching doom.
- [Modern register] - Today the ominous sense dominates public perception - a premonitory dream or a premonitory dread feels like a warning of something bad - yet the older, neutral sense ('serving to indicate beforehand') survives intact in formal scholarly prose, as when a preface concedes it permits 'no such convenient premonitory paradigm' for its ideal reader.
