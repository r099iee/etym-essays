- (Old English–Middle English, hangen, present participle) - Purely physical: “suspended from above.” Used of lamps, weapons on walls, people on crosses; tone neutral and concrete.
- (14th–15th c.) - The participle hardens into noun and adjective: hangings = tapestries and wall coverings, a lordly word for hall décor; hanging = execution by the gallows, already the state’s grimmest word, crystallized in the treason formula “hanging, drawing and quartering.”
- (16th–17th c.) - The overhang sense flowers: hanging cliffs, hanging rocks, and the “hanging gardens” of Babylon in Renaissance books. Meanwhile the figurative uses begin — a “hanging matter” slips from “a matter of life and death” to simply “an unsettled matter.”
- (18th–19th c.) - “Hanging fire” (slow-burning muskets) contributes hesitation; “hang by a thread” becomes the idiom of extreme precariousness; geology adopts hanging valley and hanging glacier; mining names the hanging wall. Tone: science forms its vocabulary around the old image.
- (20th–21st c.) - Typography (hanging indent), grammar (hanging participle), butchery (hanging carcass), and the “hanging chad” of the 2000 U.S. election put the word on world screens. Tone: the execution sense recedes in law as capital punishment is abolished or marginalized; hanging today feels clinical, technical, or antique rather than terrifying.
