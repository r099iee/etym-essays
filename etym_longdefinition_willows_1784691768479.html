Willows (plural; singular willow) are deciduous trees and shrubs of the genus Salix, family Salicaceae, comprising some four hundred species, typically found beside rivers, streams, springs and damp ground across the cool and temperate regions of the northern hemisphere. The word is used of the trees themselves, of the genus collectively, and in extended senses of the cultural and literary meanings the tree has gathered. Senses and usages:
  • Botanical sense. Willows are characterised by long, narrow, often silvery leaves; by catkins flowering in early spring (the familiar "pussy willow" is the downy catkin of certain species, prized for cutting); by rapid growth and shallow, thirsty roots, which bind riverbanks; and above all by wood of extraordinary pliancy, which can be bent, twisted and woven without breaking. Some species are large trees, others dwarf shrubs of arctic and alpine ground; a few (such as Salix babylonica and its hybrids) grow in the drooping, weeping form.
  • The weeping willow. The drooping "weeping" willow, with its long pendulous branches, has served in European poetry and painting as the very emblem of sorrow, mourning and forsaken love — the tree that bows its head as in grief.
  • Materials and craft. Willow twigs — osiers and withes — have been used since antiquity for basketry, wattle fencing, thatching ties, fish traps and binding; willow wood provides cricket bats, and willow bark, rich in salicin, is the historical source of remedies of the aspirin family.
  • Cultural and literary sense. The harps of the exiles are hung upon the willows of Babylon in Psalm 137; Ophelia in Hamlet drowns beneath a willow; "to wear the willow" is to mourn a lost lover; the "willow pattern" of porcelain design depicts a romantic landscape of willows, bridges and lovers. In all these the willow is the tree of grief, memory and quiet water.
  • Derived and transferred senses. Willowy (tall, slender and pliant, like a willow); willow-warbler, willow-herb, willow-grouse (plants and animals named from habitat or resemblance); osier and withy as the craft names of willow material; "weeping willow" used loosely of other drooping trees.
  • Grammatical range. The plural willows is the ordinary plural of the countable noun (the trees in the context sentence); willow is also used as a mass noun for the wood and as a modifier (a willow basket, a willow wand).
In the context sentence from The Hobbit — "Wind got up, and willows along its banks bent and sighed" — the word is used in its plain botanical-plural sense, but Tolkien chooses the tree deliberately for its double life. The willows line a riverbank and are the first witnesses of the change in the weather: the supple trees bend rather than break under the sudden gusts, and they sigh, giving the rising wind an almost human voice. There is already a hint of melancholy in the image, the tree of sorrow bending and sighing by the water, and Tolkien, who knew his trees, was later to make willows genuinely sinister in the enchanted riverland of Mirkwood. Historical semantic bridge: the English word descends from Old English welig/wilig, the old Germanic name for the bending tree, itself built on the Indo-European root *wel-, "to turn, roll, wind, wrap" — the root of Latin volvere and English involve, revolve, volume. The willow is the one tree that the Indo-European world agreed to name after motion rather than shape: pliant, twisting, woven, bending — its very name says "the bending tree". The Latin genus name Salix (whence the botanical Salix and Salicaceae) is an entirely different, older name, unrelated to the Germanic welig, and is not a stage in the English word's history.
