carcase (spelt carcass in the now-dominant form) is a noun whose core denotation — continuous in English since around 1300 — is the dead body of an animal, above all of a large beast left to rot, be flayed, or be scavenged. From that plain biological core it has spread over eight centuries into an unusually wide network of senses: a human corpse viewed with contempt or horror; the living human body regarded with jocular self-deprecation; the stripped framework of a ship, building, or machine; a fire-throwing projectile of seventeenth-century siege warfare; and the professional unit of the modern meat industry. The word exists in two spellings, carcase and carcass, which have competed since the Middle Ages and remain both current, the -se form surviving chiefly in British reference and trade usage.

Primary sense — the dead body of a beast. In its oldest and still central use, a carcase is the body of a dead animal, especially a large one, and especially in a state of decomposition or dismemberment. This is the sense of the hunt, the battlefield, the roadside kill, and the predator's larder: it is what the vultures and carrion crows find, what rots in the sun, what is stripped to bone. The word is neutral and descriptive in natural history and veterinary writing — a herd of deer leaves carcases behind in a hard winter — yet it can never quite shed a whiff of decay: a carcase is a body past the point of dignity, meat that has outlived its owner.

  • Sense 2 — the human corpse, and the human body as mere flesh. By the fourteenth century the word is firmly applied to dead human bodies, almost always with a note of revulsion, contempt, or bleak physicality: the corpse reduced to matter. From the sixteenth century it reaches the living body, used of oneself with rueful humour (“I must drag my poor carcase out of bed”) or of others in abuse. The semantic bridge is a degrading metonymy: to call a person a carcase is to strip them of personhood and reduce them to the flesh they carry, whether alive or dead.
  • Sense 3 — framework, hulk, and shell. From the sixteenth century carcase names the structural body of things whose coverings have been removed or destroyed: the carcase of a ship is its bare hull, the carcase of a house the standing shell after fire, the carcase of a carriage or car its undecked frame. The bridge is anatomical: the skeleton and ribcage as the load-bearing frame of a body, transferred wholesale to the frames of buildings and vessels — the same transfer that later gives ships their “ribs”.
  • Sense 4 — the military carcase. In seventeenth- and eighteenth-century siege warfare a carcass was a hollow iron shell stuffed with incendiary composition, fired burning into a town to set it alight. The name plays grimly on both “body” and “corpse”: the projectile was the dead body of a bomb, all force and destruction sealed inside matter, delivered like a burial from the sky.
  • Sense 5 — the butchery and meat-trade carcase. In modern commerce a carcase is the slaughtered animal after bleeding, skinning, and evisceration — the dressed body that is weighed, graded, and sold. Carcase weight, carcase yield, and carcase composition are standard technical terms, and in this domain the word is entirely neutral, a unit of account. Curiously, it is here that the older -se spelling has proved most durable, persisting in British and Commonwealth trade usage long after general prose shifted to carcass.
  • Sense 6 — collateral and figurative uses. In slang, carcase/carcass serves as a dysphemism for a person (“get your carcase over here”), in boxing and rodeo slang for a flattened or beaten body, and in zoology for any dead animal body considered as an ecological resource. Phrases such as “the carcase of an empire” or “a political carcase” fossilise the image of a once-living structure now hollow and rotting.

Pronunciation and spelling. Both spellings are pronounced identically, /ˈkɑːkəs/: stress on the first syllable, the second a schwa. The -ss form, reinforced by the French spelling carcasse, became the global default during the nineteenth century, while carcase — the older English regularisation of the ending — survived as the headword form preferred by Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) and as the style retained in British trade practice. Modern usage accepts either; consistency within a text is the only binding rule, and both forms belong to the same word with the same full range of senses.
