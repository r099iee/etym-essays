Flanked, the past participle of flank, means "situated on each side of, or on one side of, someone or something." It is the passive form of the verb to flank, and it describes an arrangement in which two (or occasionally one) entities stand at the side of a central object, person, or space, usually with a sense of deliberate symmetry and often of protection. In the sentence "The ding was flanked on one side by a smaller antique bronze vessel with a pattern of gold inlay and on the other by a crystal bowl," the word performs a quiet piece of stagecraft: it takes two unrelated objects and makes them the balanced attendants of a single centre, turning random placement into a composition.

The word is heavily polysemous because flank itself carries a long chain of senses, and the participle inherits them all:

  • Passive of the placing verb (core sense): to have something positioned at one's side or at each of one's sides — a king flanked by guards, a house flanked by trees, a doorway flanked by pillars. The participle is used of objects, persons, and arrangements alike.
  • Military sense: from the noun flank "the side of an army or body of troops," the verb to flank means to guard or defend the side of a force, or to attack or pass round the flank of an enemy. A flanking movement and a flanked position are tactical terms; the participle carries the defensive, guarding temperature of the battlefield.
  • Anatomical sense: the flank is the fleshy side of the body between the ribs and the hip; flank steak is the cut of beef from that region. By extension anything "flanked" is understood in relation to the two sides of a body.
  • Architectural sense: towers, pavilions, or wings that stand at the sides of a central structure are said to flank it; a flanked facade is one with symmetrical projecting elements on each side, giving the building a guarded, balanced front.
  • Figurative and metaphorical use: anything arranged symmetrically about a centre can be "flanked" — a path flanked by roses, a street flanked by shops, a person flanked by friends — the participle softening into a neutral, descriptive word for symmetrical placement while retaining a faint ghost of order and protection.

In this scene the word is architectural, and it quietly imports a military and bodily metaphor into the decor. To flank is to take up position at the side — the way soldiers guard the flanks of an army, the way ribs guard the flanks of a body — and so the sentence presents the ritual ding as the guarded centre of a composition, supported on either hand by its two attendants: the small gold-inlaid bronze, token of the family's wealth in metal, and the crystal bowl, token of its clarity and refinement. The nuance is symmetry with protection: the arrangement is not random placement but a posted guard, each side covered, the central object raised to the dignity of a commander or a treasure. It is exactly this sense of orderly, deliberate balance that characterises the noble life — every object in its place, every side covered.
