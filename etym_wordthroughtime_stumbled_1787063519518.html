- [13th–14th c.] - [Middle English Germanic Emergence] Adopted into Middle English as stomblen / stumbeln from Scandinavian and Low German roots, describing blind physical tripping, staggering, and falling over obstacles.
- [14th–16th c.] - [Biblical & Moral Expansion] Integrated into Early English Bible translations (Wycliffe, Tyndale, KJV) to translate theological concepts of falling into sin, moral vulnerability, and spiritual "stumbling blocks."
- [17th–18th c.] - [Rhetorical & Vocal Faltering] Expanded to describe speech impediments, public oratory slips, lapses in memory, and social faux pas.
- [19th–21st c.] - [Serendipity & Modern Polysemy] Developed the ubiquitous contemporary idiom stumbled upon (accidental discovery of truth, art, or opportunity), alongside modern applications to economic hiccups and technical blunders.
