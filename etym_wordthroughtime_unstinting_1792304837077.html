- Old English (c. 10th c.) — Ancestral verb styntan meant "to blunt, dull, or make stupid"; derived from the Proto-Germanic adjective for blunt or curtailed (*stuntaz).
- Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.) — The verb evolved into stinten / stenten, shifting semantically from physical bluntness to ceasing, stopping, or pausing an action (frequently used in Chaucer: "he wolde not stinte").
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — Developed the specialized economic sense of "to limit, ration, or restrict supplies parsimoniously"; prefixation with privative un- generated the adjective unstinted and participle unstinting to describe boundless generosity.
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Eras) — Consolidated across formal English prose, aristocratic patronage letters, and Victorian moral literature to praise selfless philanthropic expenditure and maternal devotion.
- 20th–21st c. — Firmly enshrined in formal English registers, serving as the premier rhetorical term in academic acknowledgments, honorary degree citations, eulogies, and international humanitarian commendations.
