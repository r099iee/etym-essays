- 1911–1919 (Avant-Garde Inception) - Founded as Éditions de la Nouvelle Revue Française (NRF) by Gaston Gallimard, André Gide, and Jean Schlumberger; initially perceived by Parisian society as an insular, highbrow coterie of intellectual snobs, famously rejecting Proust before redeeming itself by winning the 1919 Prix Goncourt for À l'ombre des jeunes filles en fleurs.
- 1920s–1930s (Ascent to Institutional Hegemony) - Reincorporated as Librairie Gallimard in 1919 and Éditions Gallimard in 1932; establishes the iconic cream-jacketed "Collection Blanche," acquires Jacques Schiffrin's luxury "Bibliothèque de la Pléiade" in 1936, and secures absolute dominance over French interwar fiction.
- 1940–1944 (The Crucible of the Occupation) - Endures the moral ambiguities of Nazi-occupied Paris; Gaston Gallimard balances nominal concessions (handing the directorship of the NRF to collaborationist Drieu la Rochelle) while quietly funding clandestine Resistance journals (Lettres françaises) and sheltering resistance authors, emerging largely untarnished at the Liberation.
- 1945–1970s (The Golden Age of Postwar Canonization) - Reaches the zenith of international prestige as the global hub of Existentialism and the Nouveau Roman (Sartre, Camus, Beauvoir, Ionesco, Céline); the rue Sébastien-Bottin becomes revered as the secular Vatican of world literature, capturing an unbroken string of prestigious literary awards.
- 1980s–Present (Modern Conglomerate and Canonical Heritage) - Under Antoine Gallimard, expands through strategic acquisitions into Groupe Madrigall, preserving multi-generational family independence against predatory media conglomerates while reigning as the most Nobel Prize-decorated literary publisher on Earth.
