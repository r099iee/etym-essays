In this context. The sentence places the word inside an inventory of the topics treated by the English Language Series — history and traditions, sound patterns (phonology), grammar, and then “its lexicology, its rich variety and complexity in speech and writing”. Lexicology here names the scholarly discipline concerned with the vocabulary of English: the stock of words themselves, their structure, meanings, and relationships. The tone is bookish and taxonomic — a quiet catalogue of the sub-disciplines into which the study of English divides.

Core meaning. Lexicology is the branch of linguistics that studies the vocabulary of a language: the form, meaning, structure, and historical development of its word-stock (the lexicon) treated as a system. It asks how words are formed, how they relate to one another (synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, word-fields), how the vocabulary is organised socially and regionally, and how it changes over time. The word is built from Greek lexikon (“word-book, dictionary”) + -logia (“study, discourse”).

Polysemy: the full range of senses.

  • Theoretical / descriptive lexicology. The study of a language’s word-stock as a system: word-formation (derivation, compounding, blending), semantic relations among words, the stratification of the vocabulary (native versus borrowed layers — the Anglo-Saxon, French and Latin strata of English), and the internal structure of the lexicon. This is the modern, technical sense.
  • Historical / comparative lexicology. The study of how vocabularies develop and change across time, and how the word-stocks of related languages correspond — a vocabulary-level complement to historical phonology and morphology, and the bridge to etymology.
  • Applied / computational lexicology. In computational linguistics and natural-language processing, the formal modelling of lexical knowledge: lexical databases, wordnet ontologies, semantic networks, and distributional word embeddings — where the discipline’s traditional concerns are automated and scaled.
  • Loose / general sense. Simply “the study of words” — the learned-sounding name for interest in word origins, coinage, and vocabulary, used loosely where “etymology” or “the science of words” would also do.
  • As a course or field label. A named subject of study and teaching (as in our sentence), listed alongside phonology and grammar among the topics a series covers.

Semantic bridge. The word entered English in the late seventeenth century meaning simply “the knowledge of words”, a rare scholarly near-synonym of lexicography. For nearly two centuries it lay dormant in dictionaries. The rise of structural and descriptive linguistics in the twentieth century gave it a precise modern sense: the discipline that treats the vocabulary not as a list but as a system — layers, structure, change. The computational age then extended the bridge from paper dictionaries to automated semantic knowledge graphs, but the object of study, the word-stock, has never changed: the Greek root lexikón was a “word-book”, and lexicology remains the science of the word-hoard.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. Lexicography is the applied craft of writing and editing dictionaries, while lexicology is the theoretical, descriptive science of the word-stock and how it is organised. Morphology studies the internal grammatical structure of words — roots, prefixes, suffixes — and is narrower in scope. Semantics studies meaning in general and is not specifically about words as dictionary units. Etymology is the historical study of individual word origins — a method and instrument that lexicology draws upon but is not reducible to. Lexicology is the broad umbrella that studies the lexicon as a system, connecting all of these.
