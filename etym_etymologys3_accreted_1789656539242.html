The ancestor of accreted is a reconstructed Indo-European root for growing. PIE *ker- (2), to grow — distinct from *ker- (1), horn or head — is posited by Pokorny and retained by Watkins and Etymonline as the source of a great Latin fertility family: crescere, to come forth, spring up, grow, thrive or swell; Ceres, goddess of grain; creare, to bring forth or create; crescens, the waxing crescent moon; and Armenian serem, to bring forth. Greek kouros (boy) and korē (girl) are cited as possible cognates. The semantic constant is organic increase, swelling into being.

Latin prefixes ad-, to or toward (itself from PIE *ad-, to, near, at), to crescere to make accrescere: to grow progressively toward something, to increase or become greater by addition. Classical authors use it for the waxing moon, for crops, for empires and for reputations. Its noun of action on the past-participle stem is accretio (genitive accretionis, accusative accretionem), an increasing or growing larger. De Vaan’s Latin etymology accepts crescere and accrescere without controversy; the formation is regular and fully attested, so no guesswork is needed.

  • 1610s: English accretion, “act of growing by organic enlargement”; 1650s, “that which is formed by continued growth from without.” Learned, Latinate, medical-botanical.
  • Mid-15th c. side branch: accrue (as acreuen), to fall as increment, via Old French acreue, feminine of acreu, past participle of acreistre (modern accroître), itself from accrescere. This French route gives finance its verb for interest and benefits; it is the cousin, not the mother, of accrete.
  • 1630s: accresce, direct rare verb from Latin; 1784: accrete, “grow by adhesion,” back-formed from accretion on the model of deletion-delete (American Heritage). Both rare at first; accrete wins.

Sound history is conservative: Latin -cc- /kk/ simplifies, stress moves to the second syllable in English, the -tion noun keeps Latinate weight while the back-formed verb is clipped and practical. Spelling stabilises as accretion, accrete, accreted, accretionary. Meaning specialises in the 19th century as geology needs a word for continents sutured from terranes, astronomy for planets swept from discs, and biology for shells and calculi. Growth from without — adhesion, apposition — becomes the defining contrast with intussusception, growth from within.

The figurative extension to culture — accreted customs, doctrines, reputations and, as here, word-meanings — follows naturally: intangible things also pile until the pile looks solid. The past participle accreted is the perfect adjective for that complaint, naming both patience (it took decades) and illegitimacy (it was never meant). To speak of accreted pejorative associations is thus to use a geological metaphor with full etymological warrant: meanings, like reefs, grow grain by grain.

Attestation layering confirms the timeline: accretion dominates the seventeenth century, accrue the fifteenth through commerce, accrete only the late eighteenth as a back-formation, and accreted as participial adjective the nineteenth onward in geology and criticism. Each newcomer narrows its niche while older forms persist, so modern English keeps the full set without confusion.
