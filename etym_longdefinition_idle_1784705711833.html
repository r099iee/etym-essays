IDLE is an adjective meaning 'without foundation, groundless, vain, or producing no useful effect', and by extension 'without purpose, value, or occupation; not working; not in use; doing nothing'. Etymologically the word denotes physical emptiness — that which is void, hollow, or empty — and by extension that which is empty of worth, foundation, or result. The word's senses radiate from this single core image of emptiness: an idle claim is empty of foundation, an idle threat is empty of intent, an idle machine is empty of work, an idle person is empty of occupation.

Polysemy and modern usage:

  • Groundless / unfounded sense (abstract, literary): of speech, claims, and beliefs — idle talk, idle rumours, idle threats, idle gossip, idle fears — that have no foundation, substance, or intended result; 'an idle boast' is an empty boast.
  • Futile / vain sense (abstract, intellectual): of activities and speculations — idle speculation, idle questions, idle debate — that can produce no result or effect; the sense at work in 'It is idle to speculate on the possible results of a different treatment', where the speculation is condemned as inherently hollow and sterile.
  • Without purpose or seriousness: idle curiosity, idle thoughts, idle words — attention or speech that is purposeless, casual, or lacking serious intent.
  • Not working / unemployed sense (of persons): an idle labourer, idle hands, idle workers — people without work or occupation; the source of the moralising proverb 'the devil makes work for idle hands'.
  • Not in use / inactive sense (of things): an idle machine, an idle engine, idle capital, idle time, idle facilities — equipment or resources not being used, producing nothing.
  • Lazy / indolent sense (of character): an idle person, the idle rich — habitually avoiding work or effort; the colloquial, mildly disapproving sense, close to 'lazy' but with the older connotation of emptiness rather than mere disinclination.
  • Mechanical and technical jargon: of an engine or machine, 'to idle' is to run while disengaged from its task — an idling car engine, idling speed, an idle pulley or idle gear that transmits no power; the verb 'to idle' and the noun 'idling' are standard terms in mechanics.

Historical and semantic bridge: the bridge from 'physically empty' to all these senses is the metaphor of emptiness itself. In Old English, īdel meant literally 'empty, void' — an empty vessel, an empty space — and by a natural extension it came to mean 'empty of value or result': words empty of truth, efforts empty of effect, people empty of work. Each modern sense is a different thing that can be empty: a claim empty of foundation, a threat empty of intent, a machine empty of work, a life empty of occupation. The moral charge the word sometimes carries ('idle hands') arises because a person empty of occupation was, in the older moral economy, a person empty of worth — the void where mischief enters. In mechanics the word was adopted for a machine that runs while empty of its proper function, transmitting no useful work, and from that concrete use the verb 'to idle' passed into everyday speech for engines left running.

In the literary context of this card — 'It is idle to speculate on the possible results of a different treatment' — the word carries its abstract, philosophical sense of futility rather than its colloquial sense of laziness. 'Idle' here does not describe a person loafing; it describes an activity that is vain: speculation that can yield nothing, a line of inquiry condemned to be without issue. The tone is one of crisp intellectual dismissal — the speculation is not merely unprofitable or difficult but inherently hollow, an exercise that is empty of foundation and cannot produce a result. It is the language of an author ruling a question out of court as fundamentally sterile. In register, 'idle' spans the everyday and the literary: 'idle hands' and 'an idling engine' are colloquial, while 'idle speculation' and 'idle threats' belong to careful, formal English, and the word's double life between emptiness of occupation and emptiness of substance is exactly what makes it so useful.
