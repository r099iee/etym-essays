Folds, the plural of fold, names in the source sentence the hollows and enfolding dips of rolling country — the creases in the darkened land into which a village and its pool quietly disappear from sight.

In the passage the travellers watch the land swallow a settlement: it vanishes into the folds of the darkened countryside, as a letter slips into the folds of a garment. The metaphor is textile and tender — the earth as cloth, creased and draped, hiding what it loves — and it belongs to the oldest sense-cluster of the word: to bend, to wrap, to enclose.

Core meaning in detail: Modern fold braids together two Old English strands that met and married: falod, an enclosure for sheep (the fenced pen on the hillside), and fealdan, to bend or wrap (the verb behind every crease). From the verb come the crease-senses — a fold in cloth, in paper, in strata, in the land itself — and from the noun the enclosure-senses — the sheepfold, the flock, the church as the fold of the faithful. The landscape sense used here is the verb's child: ground that bends like fabric, valley tucked against hill.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hollows and undulations of land (literary landscape sense, Tolkien's use): the folds of the downs, the folds of the darkened land. The bridge is the cloth metaphor: hills are drapes, valleys the shadowed creases between them.
  • A crease from bending (primary concrete sense): a fold in a dress, a map, a geological stratum. The bridge is the verb made visible: wherever anything flexible bends back on itself, the bend is a fold.
  • A sheep-pen or enclosure (ancient noun sense, Old English falod): to gather sheep into the fold. The bridge is functional: hurdles bent into a ring make the pen, so the bending names the enclosure.
  • A flock, congregation, or community (figurative extension): welcoming strays back to the fold, whether sheep, parishioners, or party members. The bridge is pastoral allegory, fixed by the New Testament parables and two millennia of sermons.
  • An embrace or clasp (poetic): to hold within the folds of a cloak, to fold in the arms. The bridge is the verb's tenderness: bending around a thing is the body's first shelter.
  • Doubling and multiplication (mathematical and rhetorical): twofold, manifold, a threefold cord. The bridge is the crease counted: each bend doubles the layers, so -fold becomes the suffix of increase.
  • To collapse or give way (verbal figurative): the chair folds, the business folds, the poker player folds. The bridge is the crease as surrender: what bends flat has yielded its shape.

Register and connotation: folds in the landscape sense is gentle, pastoral, and protective — the Downs of Sussex and Wessex are all folds, and guidebooks reach for the word whenever hills soften. Unlike ravines, which threaten, or hollows, which merely dip, folds enfold: the valley holds the village the way arms hold a child. At nightfall, as here, the tenderness turns mysterious — what the land enfolds, the dark keeps — but the word never wholly loses its shepherd's warmth, the memory of the pen where the flock sleeps safe.
