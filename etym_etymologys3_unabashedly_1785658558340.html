The two parents. The biography of unabashedly is the biography of two parents of entirely different blood whose marriage produced one of English's most characterful adverbs. The prefix un- is as native as English gets. It descends from the Proto-Indo-European negative *ne-/*n̥-, a root so ancient that it surfaces in nearly every branch of the family: Vedic Sanskrit a-/an- (in amṛta, "immortal," literally "not-dead," and ananta, "endless"), Greek a-/an- (in anarchy, "without a ruler," and anhedonia, "without pleasure"), Latin in- (invalid, invisible, innocent), Lithuanian ne-, Russian ne-, Old Irish an- — and, in the Germanic branch, Proto-Germanic *un-, which became Old English un- and has remained the most productive negative prefix in the language for more than a thousand years: unhappy, undone, unkind, unwilling, unsure. When unabashed was coined in the 1670s, un- was simply the ordinary, native English way of negating an adjective; there is nothing exotic or learned about it. The exotic parent is the stem, abash. Abash is a Romance word whose own roots reach back into the pre-history of Italy. At the bottom stands *bassus, a Late Latin adjective meaning "low, short, thick-set," which has no secure Indo-European etymology; scholars widely suspect it is a substrate word — a relic of some pre-Roman language of the peninsula, perhaps Oscan, absorbed into everyday Latin. From *bassus the Romans coined the verb *bassiāre, "to lower," and that verb generated the whole "low/lower" family of the Romance tongues: Italian abbassare, Spanish abajar, French baisser, and, through bassus itself, the English words base and bass ("low" in pitch — the musical bass). Through France. The chain to English ran through France. Old French baissier, "to lower," was joined with the intensive prefix es- (from Latin ex-) to make esbaissier, "to bring down, to astound," and here the meaning curdled in a fascinating way: to make a man's gaze drop, to knock his composure to the floor, to astonish him so thoroughly that he is disarmed. A second Old French verb, esbahir, "to astonish," hovered nearby with the same sense — probably from Vulgar Latin *batare, "to gape, be open-mouthed" — and the two verbs were thoroughly mixed in Anglo-Norman. From this tangle English took abaissen in the early fourteenth century, "to put to shame, disconcert, confound," a word the mystic writers used of the soul that stands abashed before God. The participle abashed, "embarrassed, disconcerted," then gave the language its ordinary adjective for the loss of composure, and from abashed a simple negation in the 1670s produced unabashed, "not disconcerted or ashamed," said of one who keeps composure under scrutiny. From state to manner. For its first two centuries the adjective stayed morally neutral: it described a blushless youth, a composed soldier, a maiden who would not be put down — with no special praise or blame. The decisive event was the creation of the adverb unabashedly around the 1840s. English had long used -ly to turn adjectives of state into adverbs of manner; now the state "not disconcerted" became the manner "in a not-disconcerted way," and the word could attach itself to any verb of doing, saying, looking, declaring, or behaving: to smile unabashedly, to beg unabashedly, to be unabashedly proud. It arrived at exactly the moment Victorian culture was perfecting the arts of reserve, euphemism, and concealment, and it became the favorite adverb of writers who wanted to praise people who refused to be disciplined into hiding — the man unabashedly proud of his humble origins, the artist unabashedly loyal to garish color, the honest eccentric who would not apologize. The modern approving charge. That approving charge is the word's dominant modern tone. Unabashedly today means authenticity worn without apology: reviews praise an "unabashedly sentimental" film, profiles describe an "unabashedly ambitious" founder, and the word has travelled from describing mere absence of shame to describing positive, self-assured candor. Yet the negative potential remains live: used of a wrongdoer, the same absence of composure becomes shamelessness, and "unabashedly" can cut either way. This is the double life the adverb has led since the 1840s — admired candor and censured cheek, separated only by context. Sound and form. In sound, the word is a regular English composition: the negative prefix un- (/ʌn/), the stem abash, and the adverbial suffix -ly, with the secondary stress and schwa of the pattern /ˌʌn.əˈbæʃ.t.li/. It is one of the longer single adverbs of everyday literary English, and its very length gives it a faint, deliberate, essayistic flavor — a word that announces itself. Spelling, morphology, and meaning have been stable since the 1840s, and the word shows no sign of leaving the lexicon; if anything, the 20th and 21st centuries' celebration of "authenticity" has made unabashedly more frequent, not less. Key points. - Two origins: native Germanic negative prefix (PIE *ne-/*n̥-) + Romance stem (substrate *bassus, "low"). - Semantic bridge: "to lower a person's gaze" (esbaissier) → "to disconcert, put to shame" (abaissen) → "not disconcerted" (unabashed, 1670s) → "without shame, as a manner" (unabashedly, c. 1840s). - Register drift: from morally neutral negative to approving badge of candor, with a live negative twin (shamelessness). - The word is a creation of the 19th-century cult of sincerity, and its tone is set by the pressure it denies — the shame the actor refuses to feel.
