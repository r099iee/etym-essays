- [c. 1300–1450: Middle English besegen / bisegen] - Coined by combining Old English intensive prefix 'be-' with Anglo-Norman 'seger' / Old French 'siegier' (to sit down before, lay siege); denoted literal military investment, encirclement, and assault of fortified castles and cities during medieval warfare.
- [c. 1500–1650: Early Modern & Renaissance English] - Extended figuratively to describe spiritual temptations, emotional torment, and psychological distress (e.g., 'besieged by doubts', 'besieged with grief' in Shakespeare and Elizabethan drama).
- [c. 1750–1900: Industrial & Victorian Era] - Broadened to social, medical, and political contexts, describing statesmen besieged by petitions, businesses besieged by creditors, or the physical body besieged by illness and decay.
- [c. 1950–Present: Modern Usage] - Frequently employed across journalism and everyday discourse to depict individuals or institutions inundated by media scrutiny, digital communication, or relentless organizational crises while retaining its powerful military resonance.
