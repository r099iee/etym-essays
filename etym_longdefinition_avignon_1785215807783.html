AVIGNON (proper noun) — a historic city of southeastern France Avignon is a historic city in southeastern France, on the left bank of the Rhône river in the department of Vaucluse, the capital of the old Comtat Venaissin and the seat of the famous Palais des Papes (Palace of the Popes). Its claim to world fame is the Avignon Papacy: from 1309 to 1377 the city was the seat of the Roman papacy, home to seven popes and two rival claimants, who ruled the Western Church from a vast fortress-palace on the rock of Avignon. Since the nineteenth century it has also carried a distinct literary identity, for it is the place where John Stuart Mill spent the last years of his life beside the grave of his wife. The sense in the Mill context In the biography of John Stuart Mill the name means something narrower and more intimate than the tourist landmark. Mill retired to Avignon after the death of his wife Harriet Taylor Mill, who died there in 1858 and was buried in the cemetery of Saint-Véran. He settled in a modest house on the city's outskirts, near the cemetery, and spent the last decade of his life — he died there in 1873 — writing his later works, including The Subjection of Women, Utilitarianism, the later editions of his Logic and Political Economy, and the Autobiography, in the shadow of her grave. The word "retired" in the context, combined with "the pleasant little house where the happiest years of his life had been spent in the companionship of his wife," frames Avignon not as a landmark but as a sanctuary of grief and memory: the place where the happiest years had been lived and where the disinterested labours of a philosopher were continued in solitude. The historical referents of the name (its "polysemy" across time)
  • The Gallo-Roman town (Avennio): a town of the Cavares, a Gallic or Celto-Ligurian people, on the Rhône at the confluence with the Durance; recorded by Pliny the Elder in the 1st century CE and a civitas of the Roman province of Gallia Narbonensis.
  • The seat of the papacy (1309–1377): the Avignon Papacy, when seven popes — from Clement V to Gregory XI — and, during the Great Schism, two rival claimants, ruled the Western Church from the Palais des Papes; the period was long called the "Babylonian captivity" of the papacy.
  • The papal enclave (14th–18th c.): the city and the surrounding Comtat Venaissin remained a papal territory within France until the Revolution; Avignon and the Comtat were annexed to France in 1791.
  • Mill's Avignon (19th c.): the city of Mill's retirement, of Harriet Taylor Mill's grave in the cemetery of Saint-Véran, and of the writing of his later works — the emblem of a philosopher's devoted, grieving retirement.
  • The modern city (20th–21st c.): a celebrated tourist destination of Provence — the Palais des Papes, the Pont d'Avignon (the broken bridge of the children's song, the Pont Saint-Bénézet), the Festival d'Avignon, one of the world's great theatre festivals — with a UNESCO-listed historic centre within its medieval walls.
Pronunciation and related names The French pronunciation is [aviɲɔ̃], roughly "ah-vee-NYON," with a nasal final vowel; the English pronunciation used here is given in the ipaRP field. The city should be distinguished from the surrounding department (Vaucluse), from the historically separate Comtat Venaissin (the papal county of which Avignon was the capital), and from the many towns called Avenas or Avignonet; the name belongs to the same Celtic river-word family as the English river Avon. As a proper noun, Avignon has no synonyms — its "similar words" are its own historical names and the places and monuments with which it is most easily confused.
