- Classical Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE): Greek dekasyllabos was used by Hellenistic grammarians to classify quantitative meters containing ten syllables.
- 1100s–1400s: The Old French décasyllabe (4+6 caesura) became the supreme epic meter of the Chanson de geste, while Chaucer imported and modified continental decasyllabic lines into Middle English, laying the foundation for English iambic pentameter.
- 1500s–1700s: Decasyllabic verse reached its aesthetic peak in Renaissance drama (Shakespeare, Marlowe), epic poetry (Milton's Paradise Lost), and Neoclassical heroic couplets (Pope, Dryden).
- 1750s–1850s: English grammarians and prosodists formally adopted the adjective decasyllabic to analyze and categorize classical, Romance, and English metrical systems.
- 1960s–Present: Utilized in structural linguistics, generative metrics, and literary theory (e.g., Roland Barthes) to examine how classical prosodic memory haunts modern free verse and experimental prose.
