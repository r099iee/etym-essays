Part I: The Etymological Evolution of SPINDLY

The history of spindly is rooted in the ancient world of hand-spinning wool. Derived from the name of the central wooden rod around which thread was wound, it shows how pre-industrial technology provided Western culture with its primary metaphor for physical slenderness and structural fragility — a word that moved from the spinning-wheel to the skeleton, and from the skeleton to the seed-stem.

1. Prehistoric Roots: PIE *(s)pen-

Linguists trace spindle and spindly to the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)pen-, which signified "to draw, stretch, or spin." This ancient root spread across the major Indo-European language families. In Germanic it produced Proto-Germanic *spinnaną ("to draw out fibre and twist into thread"), giving Old English spinnan and modern English spin; in Latin it produced pendēre ("to hang, to be drawn out, to be suspended"), which entered English as pendant, pendulum, depend, append, and suspend. The deep semantic core is extension: the drawing-out of thread from a mass of fibre, and by extension anything stretched thin along its length. The sound correspondence between Germanic sp- and Latin p- is a regular reflex of the same PIE cluster, one of the well-understood laws of comparative grammar.

2. The Hand-Spindle in History and Language

Before the spinning wheel, spinning was done with a drop spindle — a thin, tapering wooden rod, weighted at one end, on which the newly spun thread was wound. The spindle was drawn out long and thin so that thread could wrap tightly around its shaft, and for millennia it defined the standard of slenderness in Western technology. Its name passed through Old English spinel and Middle English spindel into modern spindle. Because the object was proverbially thin, the spindle became the culture's standing image of thinness: "spindle-shanks" meant skinny legs, and a spindle was the measure against which frailty was judged.

3. The Coining of SPINDLY in the 17th Century

In the 17th century English added the adjectival suffix -y to spindle to form spindly, "long and thin as a spindle." The word was applied first to legs and limbs stretched out beyond strength — the thinness of a body that had been drawn out too far. The suffix -y here carries its characteristic colloquial, almost affectionate diminutive force: spindly things are thin and weak, but with a touch of pathos rather than pure contempt. The new adjective entered a language already rich in spindle metaphors and instantly found its place describing the ungainly thinness of the human frame.

4. The Expansion of the 18th–19th Centuries

From bodies the word spread to plants and furniture. In the 18th and 19th centuries spindly came to describe seedlings stretched pale and weak by lack of light, chair legs thin to the point of buckling, and any structure whose length outran its strength. The botanical sense, in particular, gave the word a second life in horticulture and science: etiolated plants are spindly because they reach for light they cannot find. The semantic drift is entirely consistent — every sense preserves the core image of elongation beyond strength — but the word's reach widened from the human skeleton to the whole natural and artificial world.

5. The Figurative Extension

By the 20th century the word had completed its figurative leap: arguments, institutions, and schemes stretched too thin to support their own weight are called spindly — a spindly alibi, a spindly budget. Here the mechanical image of the load-bearing structure is the bridge: what cannot carry its own burden is spindly, whether it is a leg, a stem, or a plan.

6. Literary Attestations

In Jack Vance's Turjan of Miir, the word is deployed with surgical precision: the creature's "great head on a small spindly body" is a living contradiction of scale — a weight of head that the thin body cannot support. The word's whole history — from the spinning rod to the skeleton to the seedling — is concentrated in that single image of an organism drawn out beyond its strength.

Part II: The Etymological Evolution of RHEUMY

The history of rheumy is the history of an ancient theory of the body. Its ancestor is a Greek root meaning "to flow," its medieval meaning was the watery discharge read as a leak in the humoral economy, and its modern meaning is the pitying, literary description of weeping, filmy, aged eyes — a diagnostic word that outlived its diagnosis.

1. Prehistoric Roots: PIE *sreu- and the Flowing World

Linguists trace rheumy to the Proto-Indo-European root *sreu-, "to flow, stream." This root generated the flowing vocabulary of the ancient world: Sanskrit srávati ("he flows," whence Hindi srot, "stream"), Proto-Germanic *straumaz ("stream," whence English stream), and the Greek family built on rhein, "to flow." The image is of water moving — a river, a stream, any fluent current. In Greek the root produced rheuma, "a flow, a stream, a bodily discharge or fluxion," and it is this noun that gives us our word.

2. Greek Medicine and the Doctrine of Fluxions

In the medical theory of ancient Greece, the body was governed by fluids and their flows. A rheuma was any flowing discharge — the running nose of a cold, the watering of the eyes, the flux of disease. The physician Galen and his school systematised the doctrine of fluxions: illness was often the result of a flow of humours into a part of the body. From the same root came rheumatismos, the flow of humours into the joints, which gives English rheumatism — the aching of joints caused, on the old theory, by an evil flow. The two words, rheum and rheumatism, are thus siblings: both are flows, one of discharges, one of joint-pain.

3. Latin and the Romance Bridge

Latin borrowed the Greek noun as rheuma, "a flow, a catarrhal discharge," keeping it within the technical vocabulary of medicine. Old French took the word as reume / rheume, meaning a cold, catarrh, or watery discharge, and English borrowed it in the 14th century as reume / rheum. Throughout the medieval period rheum was a standard medical term: the watery discharge of the eyes or nose was rheum, and a cold was a rheum. The word belonged to the everyday technical vocabulary of humoral medicine, which explained everything from a runny nose to melancholia as a disturbance of bodily flows.

4. The Coining of RHEUMY

English formed the adjective rheumy from rheum + -y in the 17th–18th centuries, meaning "affected with or producing rheum," especially of the eyes. As the humoral theory died in the 19th century, the diagnostic force of the word faded — but the word survived in literature, and its meaning settled into the pitying description we know today: rheumy eyes are the weeping, filmy, moisture-laden eyes of age, sickness, or feebleness. The word made the transition from medical diagnosis to literary observation, exactly as gout, hysterical, and melancholic did.

5. Literary Attestations

  • Shakespeare uses rheum for the physical weakness of age — the old man's rheumy eye.
  • The 18th- and 19th-century novelists use the adjective for the pathos of decrepitude.
  • Jack Vance, in Turjan of Miir, uses it for the creature's "weak rheumy eyes" — the moisture of weakness, the final failure of the body's vessels to hold.

From the flowing river of the Indo-European root to the weeping eye of the aged creature, rheumy has always been about the body's inability to keep its fluids in place.

Part III: The Etymological Evolution of FLABBY

The history of flabby is short but revealing: a word born in the late 17th century from the imitative family of flap, it has grown from a description of loose flesh into a universal metaphor for slackness of every kind — bodily, literary, moral, and institutional.

1. The Imitative Family: flap, flop, and flab

Unlike most words in this vocabulary, flabby has no securely reconstructable Indo-European root. It belongs to the expressive, sound-symbolic family of flap, flop, and flab — words whose very sound imitates the motion or texture they name. A flap is the loose, slapping motion of something hanging free; a flop is the same motion without energy; the initial fl- cluster in Germanic repeatedly names loose, flat, hanging, or soft things (fling, float, flow, flounce, flabby). The OED and standard etymologies treat the origin as obscure or imitative, and no regular PIE derivation is agreed upon. This is not a failure of scholarship but the nature of the beast: some of the most physical words in the language are born from the mouth's imitation of the world.

2. flab and the Coining of flabby in the 1690s

English had the noun flab, "soft, loose, hanging flesh," attested in the late 17th century, probably as a back-formation or dialectal member of the flap family. To it the language added the adjectival suffix -y to form flabby in the 1690s: "soft, loose, lacking firmness," specifically of flesh without muscle tone. The word's debut in the late 17th century places it in the age of Restoration satire and coffee-house critique, when English was coining scores of vivid, contemptuous physical words. Its earliest sense was squarely tactile: flabby flesh is flesh that yields under pressure and flops about — soft dough that never springs back.

3. The Expansion Beyond Flesh

In the 18th and 19th centuries the word stretched beyond the body. Flabby prose is loose and wordy; a flabby argument has no structure or force; a flabby character is weak and indecisive, lacking moral backbone. The bridge is the image of lost tone: just as flabby flesh cannot hold its shape, flabby writing and flabby reasoning cannot hold their own weight. The figurative uses rapidly became as common as the literal one, and the word acquired its full modern range — from the physical to the intellectual to the moral.

4. The Modern Institutional Sense

In the 20th and 21st centuries the metaphor completed its extension to institutions and economies: a flabby bureaucracy is over-stuffed and inefficient; a flabby company has lost its competitive tone; a flabby budget is bloated without purpose. Every sense of the word preserves the same image — the soft, unresilient mass that will not hold its shape — whether it is a belly, a paragraph, a character, or a corporation.

5. Literary Attestations

In Jack Vance's Turjan of Miir, the creature has a "flabby button of a nose" — the word's tactile softness applied to a detail of anatomy, making the nose the very emblem of the creature's unformed, unresilient flesh. The word's short history — from Restoration slang to universal metaphor — gives it an immediacy and homeliness that the older, Latin-derived words for the same quality (flaccid) cannot match.
