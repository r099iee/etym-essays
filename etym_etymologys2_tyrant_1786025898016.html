Pre-Greek / Anatolian (c. 8th c. BCE)
*tūrannos / *turs-
"fortress master, lord, prince"
↓
Ancient Greek (7th c. BCE)
τύραννος
"absolute sovereign, unconstitutional ruler"
↓
Latin (1st c. BCE)
tyrannus
"despot, absolute monarch, cruel ruler"
↓
Old French (12th c. CE)
tiran / tirant
"cruel lord, oppressor"
↓
Middle English (14th c. CE)
tiraunt / tyraunt
"unlawful despot, cruel master"
↓
Modern English (16th c. CE–present)
tyrant
"cruel and oppressive absolute ruler"

Note: The ultimate origin of Greek τύραννος is widely accepted by modern linguists (e.g., Beekes, Chantraine) as a non-Indo-European loanword from an Aegean or Anatolian substrate (likely Lydian or Etruscan Turan "mistress/lady"), rather than an indigenous Proto-Indo-European stem. The unetymological final -t in French and English was acquired through phonological analogy with present participles ending in -ant.
