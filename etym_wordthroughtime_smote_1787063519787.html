- Old English (8th c.) - 'to strike, kill; also to smear, defile' - an everyday strong verb with both a violent and a dirty sense
- Middle English (12th-14th c.) - 'to strike, beat, slay' - the 'smear' sense dies; the verb is purely violent and judicial
- 1611 - King James Bible fixes 'smote' - solemn, punitive, numinous register in religion and law ('smite the rock', 'I will smite')
- 18th-19th c. - retreats to poetry and archaism - perceived as biblical, old-fashioned, grandiose
- 20th-21st c. - 'smitten' thrives in playful emotional use ('smitten with a crush'); 'smote' survives in fantasy, hymns, and proverbs - epic, archaic, or knowingly humorous
- Today - recognised but archaic; writers choose 'smote' deliberately when they want a single stroke to feel enormous
