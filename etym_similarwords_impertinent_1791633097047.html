• insolent: Similar because both words describe brazen disrespect toward recognized authority or social norms; differs because insolent carries a much harsher degree of arrogant contempt, haughty defiance, and overt hostility, whereas impertinent frequently describes intrusive cheekiness, bad manners, or presumptuous meddling in matters outside one's purview.
  • impudent: Similar because both denote a lack of due modesty or respect; differs because impudent emphasizes shameless, unabashed effrontery and gall (from Latin pudere, to be ashamed), whereas impertinent retains a strong flavor of irrelevance, inappropriate boundary-crossing, and meddlesomeness.
  • presumptuous: Similar because both involve taking liberties and exceeding proper boundaries; differs because presumptuous centers on overstepping one's station or making unwarranted assumptions regarding one's privileges, whereas impertinent focuses directly on the rude, inappropriate, or intrusive nature of the utterance or inquiry itself.
  • pert: Similar because both convey forwardness and lack of deference; differs because pert belongs to an informal or affectionate register, often describing lively, jaunty, or coquettishly bold behavior, whereas impertinent carries formal censure and social reproof.
  • irreverent: Similar because both convey a failure to display respect; differs because irreverent specifically denotes a lack of veneration for solemn, holy, or deeply cherished institutions, traditions, or figures (often accompanied by witty humor), whereas impertinent is an interpersonal violation of social decorum or conversational relevance.
  • saucy: Similar because both denote disrespectful cheek; differs because saucy is an informal, colloquial, and often light-hearted descriptor of mild impudence, lacking the formal severity and intellectual censure of impertinent.
  • irrelevant: Similar because both share the original logical meaning of having no connection to the subject; differs because irrelevant is a purely neutral, objective, cognitive term devoid of moral judgment, whereas impertinent in everyday English implies rude intrusion, surviving in its purely logical sense only in formal legal pleadings.
  • officious: Similar because both describe meddlesome behavior; differs because officious describes an annoying eagerness to offer unwanted help, advice, or petty administrative authority, whereas impertinent describes intrusive rudeness or inappropriate boundary violations.
