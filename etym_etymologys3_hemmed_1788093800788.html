1. The Germanic stem. The verb descends from Proto-Germanic *hamjaną, "to restrain, enclose, confine", with the precise reconstruction of the stem debated in detail but the family secure across the branch: Old Norse hemja "to curb, check, restrain", Old High German hemmen "to hinder, stop", German hemmen "to brake, impede" — with the abstract noun Hemmung "inhibition, restraint", which Freud made a technical term of psychoanalysis in Hemmung, Symptom und Angst (1926). Behind the Germanic stem, some scholars have placed a PIE root meaning "to cover", seeing hemming as covering and enfolding, but the Indo-European connection is not established; the honest chain therefore stops at *hamjaną, and this is said explicitly rather than guessed at.

2. Old and Middle English. Old English hemman is recorded in the senses "to hem in, enclose, shut up, check" — used of enclosing a space and restraining motion — and beside it grew the nominal form: the noun hem, "a border, the edge of cloth", is recorded from Middle English (hemme, 14th century), and the exact priority of noun and verb is debated, since a "hem" is by definition something that encloses and edges. In Middle English both lives run together: "to hem" a garment (sew its border) and "to hem in / about" (enclose with a boundary), with flexible spellings (hemmen, hemyn) before the modern forms settle. The two senses were felt as one: edging a garment is enclosing its raw edge, so "border" and "confine" were the same mental picture.

3. The 16th–17th-century phrase. The participle constructions thicken in early modern prose and verse: things are "hem'd with woods", "hem'd about with waves", "hemmed in by the hills" — the frame "hemmed [all] about with X" is already idiomatic in the 16th–17th centuries, exactly the frame Tolkien uses four centuries later in "hemmed all about with goblins and wolves". The reinforcement matters: bare "hemmed" increasingly requires the adverb — in, about, round — precisely because the verb's enclosing sense was otherwise too weak to carry the picture alone, while the sewing sense needed no reinforcement at all.

4. The sewing specialisation. As the language of physical confinement filled with Latin-derived words — enclose, surround, encircle, confine — the native verb's spatial sense retreated to the workshop: "hem" became, above all, the tailor's and seamstress's word for edging cloth, while the confining sense survived as the phrasal participle "hemmed in". The split hardened in the 19th century with industrial sewing (hemming machines, hemming stitches) on one side and the metaphorical "hemmed in" — by poverty, by circumstances, by regulations — on the other. The enclosure movements of the 18th–19th centuries even gave the word a land-historical echo: hedges and fences hemmed fields in, and the language of "hemming" rural land was literal before it became figurative.

5. Sound and today. Sound shape: Old English hemman /ˈhem.mɑn/ → Middle English /ˈhɛm.ən/ → modern /hɛm/, with loss of the final syllable; the simple past hemmed /hɛmd/ is regular. Distribution today: the participle "hemmed in / about" dominates (literal and metaphorical — cities hemmed by mountains, people hemmed by rules), the sewing verb is universal in clothing contexts (hem, hems, hemmed, hemming, hemline), and the unrelated imitative "hem and haw" supplies the false friend that every dictionary entry must flag. Tolkien's sentence is the word's full biography in one line: an ancient Germanic verb of enclosing, a ring of enemies pressing close, and the language of the fold — "hemmed all about" — still doing its old job of drawing a circle round the helpless.
