Introduction. Scrumptiously is a young word with an ancient spine. Its adverbial shell — the -ly ending — is the oldest machinery of English, but the body of the word is a nineteenth-century joke: a playful corruption of the grand Latin-derived adjective sumptuous, which itself descends in a straight line from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to take". The history of scrumptiously is thus three stories in one: the ancient chain from PIE *em- to the lavish Roman word, the medieval journey of sumptuous through French into English, and the Victorian nursery's mischievous shrinking of that grand word into a cosy mouthful of delight.

The ancient root and the Roman word.

  • The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *em- "to take, to distribute", reconstructed from the evidence of Latin emere, Greek némein "to distribute", and other branches. In Latin the root yields emere "to take, to buy" — attested from the earliest Latin texts — and its extended compounds.
  • From sub- "under" + emere, Latin forms sumere "to take up, to undertake, to spend", with the assimilation of the prefix (*subm- > summ-). Its past participle and noun sumptus means "taken, spent; expense, outlay" — the money taken out and spent.
  • From sumptus Latin builds the adjective sumptuōsus "costly, expensive, lavish" (the suffix -ōsus, "full of", giving "full of expense"). This is the direct ancestor of sumptuous: a word whose core is not pleasure but cost — grandeur measured in money spent.

The medieval journey through French.

  • Latin sumptuōsus passes into Old and Middle French as somptueux (from the 13th–14th centuries), keeping the sense "lavish, magnificent, costly". French is the vehicle by which the word crosses the Channel.
  • Middle English borrows it in the late 15th century as sumptuous "lavish, costly, magnificent" — used of feasts, clothing, buildings, and display. From the start it is a word of expense and show: to be sumptuous is to be expensive enough to impress. Sumptuary laws regulating dress and feasting are the socio-cultural background that keeps the word anchored in cost.
  • Modern English retains the full Roman weight: sumptuous means "splendidly costly, lavish", and its adverb sumptuously stresses display and grandeur (a sumptuously furnished room, a sumptuous banquet).

The Victorian joke: scrumptious is born.

  • Around 1830–1850, English slang produces scrumptious — generally explained as a playful, jocular alteration of sumptuous. The change is not a regular sound shift but a deliberate mangling: the -mp- cluster is eased, an r- is inserted (a common "expressive" rhotacism in playful words), and the vowel is shortened, so that the grand two-step of "sumptuous" collapses into the cosy, bouncy "scrumptious".
  • The semantic shift is the interesting part. Where sumptuous means costly and grand, scrumptious means delicious and delightful — the idea of expense is shrunk to the mouth. A sumptuous feast is a lavish display; a scrumptious cake is simply a wonderful thing to eat. The Victorian nursery and the slang of the street perform the same trick as a child imitating an adult: taking a word of grandeur and making it intimate, warm, and small.
  • The exact formation is debated: some connect the r to dialect forms (scrumptious, skrumptious), and others toy with a link to 19th-century slang scrum "food" or to the expressive scr- cluster (scrunch, scrump); the standard account remains "an alteration of sumptuous, probably under playful influence". Honest dictionaries say the precise path is uncertain.

The adverb: scrumptiously.

  • The adverbial form scrumptiously (scrumptious + the Old English suffix -ly) is recorded from the mid-19th century, and it functions chiefly as an intensifying degree-adverb in front of adjectives of approval: "scrumptiously good", "scrumptiously sweet", "scrumptiously crunchy". The -ly is the oldest part of the word — the same suffix that made "quickly" in Old English — so the newest word carries the oldest machinery.
  • From its first records the adverb is informal, affectionate, and slightly playful — the language of sweets, feasts, and pampered appetites — and it remains rare, which keeps its effect fresh: every use is a small wink of delight.

Literary and socio-cultural notes.

  • Scrumptious flourishes in the Victorian and Edwardian nursery and in popular writing about food; it is the adjective of the treat, the tea, the pudding.
  • The related nursery coinages scrummy (clipped) and Roald Dahl's scrumdiddlyumptious (1964) show the word's family continuing its playful life in children's literature.
  • In 1937, Tolkien gives the rare adverb its most famous moment in The Hobbit, when the famished Gollum asks whether Bilbo is "scrumptiously crunchable". The nursery word in the mouth of a starving predator is a masterstroke of irony: the cosy vocabulary of the sweet-shop becomes the language of menace, and the ancient chain from "to take" to "costly" to "delicious" completes a strange circle — the creature who would take and eat speaks the word of delight in the dark.

Conclusion. Scrumptiously compresses three thousand years into one playful mouthful. Its root is the Indo-European verb of taking, which Latin turned into cost (sumptus, sumptuous), which French and English carried into the language of lavish display, which Victorian slang then lovingly broke and reshaped into a cosy word of delight — and whose adverb English now uses to say that something is not just good but scrumptiously, delightfully, mouth-wateringly good. It is a grand, costly, sumptuous history hidden inside a child's cry for sweets.
