- Old Norse roots — preparing and dressing: buask means to get oneself ready, and English borrows it as busk — to prepare, to dress, to hurry about one's business.
- 1540s — the frequentative flurry: bustle appears as the busy, repeated form of busk — not one preparation but many, not dressing but darting — noisy activity as a habit.
- 1600s–1700s — domestics and officials bustle: Shakespeare's and Fielding's minor powers — hostesses, stewards, beadles — bustle in stage directions and novels; the verb becomes the sound of small authority.
- Late 1700s — the fashion collision: a separate noun bustle (a pad puffing out skirts) appears from unknown sources; wearers of bustles bustle doubly, rustling in dress and in deed.
- Victorian era — the kindly bustler: Dickens fixes the warm type forever — landladies, nurses, and farmers bustling with towels, tea, and beer; fuss becomes love made noisy.
- Today — hustle's partner: hustle and bustle names the beloved noise of cities and holidays, while Maggot's in-and-out bustle remains the perfect portrait — hospitality that cannot sit still.
