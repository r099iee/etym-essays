BRUISED (past tense and past participle of the verb "bruise") describes an injury in which a blow or pressure ruptures small blood vessels — capillaries and venules — beneath the skin without breaking the skin itself, so that blood leaks into the surrounding tissue and shows through as a tender, discoloured patch, the familiar "black and blue" mark that darkens slowly from red through blue to purple-green and then fades to yellow. The clinical term is "contusion"; the everyday word is "bruise". In the passage from The Hobbit, the dwarves (and Bilbo) are "rubbing their bruised and damaged legs and feet": the word is unglamorous and physical, part of the steady tally of the travellers' exhaustion after the goblins' attack in the tunnels. It catches the raw, blotchy ache of battered limbs rather than any heroic wound — the dwarves are not cut or bleeding, just knocked and sore, and the pairing with "rubbing" shows the pain is stubborn and inward: a bruise does not bleed and needs no surgeon; it simply hurts, dully, wherever it was struck. The tone is one of dogged, unheroic endurance; Tolkien chooses the homeliest word for a homely misery, grounding the fantastic adventure in the plain physics of flesh.

SENSES AND USES

  • Literal, of the body (core): "bruised" of a leg, knee, arm or shoulder — marked by a blunt blow; "she fell and bruised her knee"; "a badly bruised heel".
  • Of fruit and food: a "bruised apple" or "bruised peach" is a fruit with a soft, discoloured, damaged patch where it has been dropped or knocked; the word extends to any soft object's impact-marked surface.
  • Figurative, of feelings (from the 19th century): "bruised pride", "bruised ego", "emotionally bruised" — hurt but not broken, consciously lighter than "wounded"; a person who has come through disappointment intact but sore.
  • Of an animal or quarry: a "bruised bird" or "bruised hare" is one that has been battered without being broken, keeping the old active sense of "crushed, pounded".
  • Historical / cooking sense (now rare): in old recipes "bruised" means crushed or pounded — "bruised cinnamon", "bruised herbs" — a survival of the word's original violent sense before it narrowed to the flesh mark.
  • Metaphoric extension to institutions and enterprises: a "bruised economy", "bruised by defeat" — damaged and marked but still functioning; the metaphor draws on the mark's visibility and its inwardness.

The semantic heart is the hidden, inward hurt: a bruise leaves the surface whole, so the word carries a note of concealed damage that "cut" or "wound" never do. All the figurative senses flow from this — you can be bruised where nothing shows, and the ache is dull and persistent rather than dramatic.

CONTRASTS AND BOUNDARIES

  • "Cut" is an open wound that breaks the skin and bleeds; a bruise leaves the surface whole.
  • "Contused" is the clinical synonym, reserved for medical reports, while "bruised" remains the everyday word.
  • "Injured" and "damaged" are far broader — every kind of harm, with no specific image — whereas "bruised" names a particular, familiar household injury.
  • "Wounded" suggests a more serious, usually open wound and, figuratively, deep lasting trauma; "bruised" is lighter — hurt pride or shaken spirits, not a broken spirit.
  • "Battered" implies repeated heavy blows over time and general rough handling, rather than the single discrete mark of a bruise.
  • "Sore" names the feeling (the pain), not the mark; "black and blue" is the vivid colloquial phrase for the bruise itself.
