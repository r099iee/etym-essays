Cradles is the plural of the native English noun cradle: small beds for infants, classically mounted on rockers so that gentle motion soothes the child. By extension the word covers any supporting frame that holds or rocks something fragile — a phone cradle, a rifle cradle, a boat resting in its cradle. The plural stacks the idea: many such beds, or many makeshift holders. In Gandalf's comic catalogue of the neighbourhood's unheroic kit — 'shields as cradles or dish-covers' — the plural is pure domestic demotion: war-gear has been reassigned to nursery furniture and kitchen lids. The joke is cultural as much as material: a land where heroes are scarce has already converted the shield's defensive curve into a baby-bed or a cover for a plate. The tone is wry, hobbit-scale irony; the wizard is explaining why he chose burglary over open war, and the image is not sentimental nursery pastoral but anti-epic inventory. Primary senses
  • An infant's bed, especially one mounted on rockers so that a gentle rocking motion soothes the child — the oldest and central sense of the word.
  • By extension, any supporting frame or holder shaped like a cradle: a ship's launching cradle, a telephone or phone-charging cradle, a rifle cradle, a suspended workman's cradle hanging from a building.
  • Figuratively, the place where something begins or is nurtured: 'the cradle of civilization,' 'the cradle of democracy,' 'from the cradle to the grave.'
  • As a verb, to cradle: to hold gently and protectively, as one holds an infant (to cradle a cup in one's hands).
Polysemy, secondary senses, and modern uses
  • Infant care — the rocking bed, and by metonymy the baby's early months ('they were friends since the cradle'); cradle-songs are lullabies sung over the bed.
  • Engineering and transport — a cradle is the framework that holds a ship on the slipway during building, repair, or launching; in construction and cleaning, a suspended 'cradle' is the platform on which window-cleaners and builders are lowered down a facade.
  • Domestic and personal technology — a cradle is the base that holds a handset (the old telephone cradle), and today a charging cradle holds a phone or tablet upright while it charges; the protective-holder logic is unchanged.
  • Metaphor of origin — a place where a culture, movement, or institution began is its cradle: the 'cradle of civilization' (a phrase already classical in geography books), the 'cradle of liberty.' The semantic bridge from the infant bed is transparent: what is cradled is early, fragile, and protected, so the cradle becomes the image of beginnings.
  • Compounds and idioms — cat's cradle (the string game, whose woven loops evoke a cradle or frame), cradle-robber and cradle-snatcher (slang for a person who pursues a much younger partner), from the cradle (from infancy), cradle-to-grave (a whole-life span, used of welfare and care systems), cradle cap (the infant scalp condition — a medical collocation).
  • Hunting and military history — a 'cradle' is a frame holding a crossbow, rifle, or weapon in position; the word's 'holder' sense is the oldest secondary meaning and survives in modern firearms usage.
Near-synonyms and boundaries
  • A crib is a fixed-sided infant bed without the rocking association; a bassinet is lighter and often portable; a cot (British English) is a small barred bed, not necessarily rocking; a hammock suspends rather than rocks on a frame; a rocking chair rocks an adult, not an infant. Cradles keeps the baby-bed and the supporting-frame sense together.
  • Preserve the plural: Gandalf's list stacks multiple repurposed shields among the blunt swords and tree-axes of a quiet countryside — several shields, several cradles, several dish-covers.
Word origin and debate Cradle is native English, attested from the late tenth century as cradol, and its documented history is long and steady; but its deepest root is genuinely unknown. Standard dictionaries mark the origin as uncertain. Proposed deeper links — to a Germanic word for a woven basket or crate (cf. Old High German kratto, Middle Dutch krade), to Celtic forms such as Welsh crud 'cradle,' or to the Latin wickerwork word cratis (source of English crate) — are all plausible on the basket analogy but none is universally accepted. The responsible account roots the word in Old English and flags every earlier stage as speculative.
