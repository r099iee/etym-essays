- 4th c. BCE — the historical iconoclast: Zoilus of Amphipolis lectures across Greece and writes his nine-volume polemic Against Homer's Poetry, earning the infamous epithet Homēromastix ('Scourge of Homer') for pedantically ridiculing mythological inconsistencies.
- Hellenistic & Roman Era — the proverbial carping critic: Vitruvius records legends of his execution for insulting Homer; Latin writers Ovid, Martial, and Quintilian adopt Zoilus as the universal emblem of envious, small-minded literary detractors.
- Renaissance & Enlightenment — humanistic defense of genius: Erasmus (*Adagia*) and Alexander Pope invoke Zoilus in treatises and poems (notably in *An Essay on Criticism*) to brand pedantic detractors who seek unearned fame by defaming artistic monuments.
- 19th c. — Victorian aesthetic battleground: John Ruskin and his contemporaries use Zoilus to expose reviewers who attack revolutionary creators like Turner or Keats, diagnosing their fault-finding as the malice of mediocrity.
- 20th–21st c. — classical archaism and figurative archetype: surviving in scholarly discourse and high literary prose (alongside derivatives zoilism and zoilist) to denote bad-faith nitpickers, contrarian review-bombers, and pedantic cultural commentators.
