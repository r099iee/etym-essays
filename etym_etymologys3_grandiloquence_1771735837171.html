“Grandiloquence” is a bilingual marriage performed in Latin, and its biography must be told in two independent strands because it unites two roots that have no common ancestor within the word itself: grandis, “great,” and loquī, “to speak.” Each strand has its own history, its own family, and its own problems of origin; only at the final Latin compound do they fuse. SECTION 1 — the grandis strand. The adjective grandis is an ordinary, old Latin word of broad application — great in size, rank, or degree, full-grown, abundant — used of men, fields, wealth, and plenty from the earliest Latin literature (Plautus, 3rd c. BCE). Its pre-Latin origin is uncertain: no secure Proto-Indo-European reconstruction has been made, and reputable dictionaries mark the origin obscure. An old speculation links grandis to a base meaning “grown,” kin to “green” and “grow,” but the evidence is thin, and the honest chain begins at Latin itself. From grandis, Latin built a productive family: the verb grandescere, “to grow great”; grandire, “to make great, to ripen”; and, in the educated idiom of imperial Rome, the compound grandiloquus, “speaking in a grand style.” Outside the verb, grandis fathered the Romance grande (Italian, Spanish, Portuguese) and French grand, which gave English grand in the fourteenth century, grandeur in the seventeenth, grandee from Spanish in the sixteenth, and grandiose from Italian through French in the nineteenth. Every member of the family keeps the root’s core sense of greatness in scale, rank, or dignity. SECTION 2 — the loquī strand. The verb loquor, loquī, “to speak,” is equally opaque at its deepest level: some scholars suspect sound symbolism in its repeated liquid consonants, but no reliable PIE etymon is agreed upon, and the chain begins at Latin. Within Latin, however, loquī proved extraordinarily fertile. With the prefix con- it gave colloquium, “a speaking together,” whence our colloquy and colloquial; with ex- it gave eloquī, “to speak out,” whence eloquentia and our eloquence and eloquent; with solus it formed soliloquium, “a speaking alone,” whence soliloquy; with ad, allocūtiō, a formal address, whence allocution; and with inter, interlocūtiō, whence interlocutor. The adjective loquāx, “talkative,” produced loquacity and loquacious. The base sense of “speaking” runs through them all, and each prefix shades the manner of the speaking — together, out, alone, to, between. SECTION 3 — the fusion. Somewhere in the workshop of derivative speech, a Roman writer joined grandis to loquī to form grandiloquus, “speaking in a grand style,” and from that adjective Latin derived the abstract noun grandiloquentia — the habit or manner of grand speaking. It is telling that Rome itself felt the need for a term distinguishing grand speech from ordinary talk: the Romans, heirs of Greek rhetoric, were keenly alive to the three styles taught by the rhetorical handbooks — the plain, the middle, and the grand — and grandiloquentia named the grand manner as a habitual quality of a speaker or writer. SECTION 4 — the journey into English. English received grandiloquentia in the late sixteenth century, first attested around 1580, taking it from Latin through French grandiloquence, and the adjective grandiloquent followed in the mid-seventeenth century. From the very beginning the English word carried a double edge: it could describe a genuinely lofty, Ciceronian style, or it could mock an inflated one. The double edge is built into the word’s morphology — the grandeur of grandis yoked to the plain act of loquī — and into its social history, for the age of the English Renaissance and the Augustan period were exactly the centuries when English prose was learning to imitate the Latin period, and when the danger of imitation was inflation. SECTION 5 — semantic and cultural career. Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries grandiloquence was the natural term for the high rhetorical style — the pulpit oratory of a Donne or a South, the parliamentary eloquence of a Pitt, the stately prose of a Gibbon. In the nineteenth century, as criticism sharpened and plain style became a virtue, the word tilted toward suspicion: grandiloquent came to mean grand-sounding but hollow, and grandiloquence sat next to bombast, fustian, and rodomontade in the vocabulary of rhetorical excess. Its career thus mirrors the fate of its cousin grandiose: both words began as neutral-to-admiring descriptions of great scale and ended as words that must be rescued from their own excess by context. SECTION 6 — the literary moment in the passage. In describing Gibbon, the author exploits both faces at once. Gibbon’s prose is indeed grandiloquent — grand in scale, Latinate, ceremonious, balanced as a period of Cicero — and the word’s reverent use acknowledges his mastery of high Roman rhetoric. Yet the sentence places “grandiloquence of phrase” beside “a grandiose subject” and then dissolves both under the claim of “intimacy.” The biography of the word thus ends where Gibbon’s readers begin: grandiloquence is the splendid, public, historic costume of his style, and it is precisely by seeing through that costume — knowing the grandiloquence and still hearing the intimate voice beneath it — that the historian is loved. In sum, the word’s history is a history of style itself: two Latin roots, one of size and one of speech, fused in imperial Rome, transmitted through the rhetorical culture of the Renaissance and the prose of the Enlightenment, and preserved in English as a permanent reminder that grand speaking is both the highest art of language and its most constant temptation to overreach.
