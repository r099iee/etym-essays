- Old English hēdan: to guard, protect, watch over — the shepherd's and sentinel's verb.
- 13th c. – the noun heed 'care, attention' is born from the verb; 'take heed' begins its long career.
- 14th–17th c. – the Bible and the sermon make 'take heed' the formula of moral warning; heed is the language of conscience.
- 18th–19th c. – the word retreats from everyday speech into formal and literary register; it starts to sound a little archaic.
- 20th–21st c. – heed survives in set phrases (take heed, pay heed, give heed), official notices and journalism; its slight formality is now its strength — it means attention with consequence, the vigilance that guards you.
