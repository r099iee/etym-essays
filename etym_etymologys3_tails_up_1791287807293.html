Tails up is a colloquial British exhortation meaning cheer up, keep your spirits high, do not be downcast, addressed here by Bertie Wooster to his Aunt Dahlia. It belongs to the large family of up encouragements - chin up, head up, keep up, buck up - that map physical elevation onto emotional resilience. Its history lies in field sports and animal observation rather than in abstract psychology, and Wodehouse uses it as period schoolboy slang.

Tail: Old English taegl originally meant a horse's tail, a tuft or appendage, cognate with Old High German zagel, Old Norse tagl. The PIE base is disputed but often linked to a root meaning appendage or tuft. By Middle English tail it generalized to any animal's hind appendage and by extension to the rear, train or underside of anything (tail of a coat, tail of a comet, tail-end). The plural tails in sporting slang could mean the tail itself, or by metonymy the animal's condition as read from its tail carriage.

Up: From PIE *upo- from under, up, seen in Greek hypo under, Latin sub under, Sanskrit upa toward. Germanic reversed the sense to upwards: Proto-Germanic *upp, Old English upp and up, Old Norse upp. As adverb, preposition and particle it became one of English's most productive directional words, combining with hundreds of verbs (get up, cheer up, lift up) where up adds completion, elevation or improvement. The cheering sense of up is already Old English: to lift the heart is to lift the body.

  • Hunting and hounds (18th-19th c.): A foxhound or terrier with tail erect and wagging signals health, scent-found and eagerness; tail down or between legs signals fear, exhaustion or sickness. Huntsmen read tails as barometers. Tails up as a kennel observation - hounds are running with tails up - is well attested in sporting literature and stable talk.
  • Transfer to horses and schoolboys (late 19th c.): Sporting papers applied the same image to horses going with tails up and then to public-school games: a team playing with tails up is confident and attacking. From there it became general slang for high spirits, recorded in school stories and regimental journals.
  • Imperative encouragement (early 20th c.): Tails up!, Keep your tails up!, parallel to Chin up! and Buck up!, emerges in military, school and club slang around World War I as a jocular order to stop moping. Wodehouse, steeped in Edwardian school slang, uses it as Bertie's breezy, slightly fatuous cheerfulness in the face of Aunt Dahlia's gloom about money.
  • Modern survival: Still heard in British equestrian circles and hunting metaphor, and more broadly as a quaint period encouragement. Americans more often say tails up only for animals, preferring chin up for people, so the phrase now marks British comic prose and nostalgic stiff-upper-lip ethos.

Phonetically both elements are stable: tail with the Middle English ai diphthong, plural tails, up with short vowel. No irregular sound change marks the idiom; its novelty is entirely semantic - reading animal body language as moral advice. Socio-culturally the phrase encodes the stiff-upper-lip ethos: low spirits are a posture to be corrected like a drooping tail, best cured by brisk exhortation rather than analysis. Bertie's buoyant delivery, immediately undercut by Aunt Dahlia's sombre reply, is the joke: the formula fails on contact with real worry about cheques and Boudoir bills, and the reader feels both affection for his bounce and knowledge that Jeeves will be needed.
