The etymology of dratted constitutes a classic case study in linguistic taboo deformation and the historical mechanics of English minced oaths, tracking the dilution of a mortal religious curse into a harmless colloquial adjective.

1. The Ancien Curse and Religious Taboo:

  • The Theology of Imprecation: In medieval and early modern England, profanity centered on the violation of religious taboos—specifically invoking the name, body parts, or judgments of the Christian deity. Among the most severe curses of the Stuart era was God rot [thee / it]!, which invoked God's wrath to inflict putrefactive decay upon the body, soul, or possessions of an adversary.
  • Roots of the Compound: The first component ascends to Proto-Indo-European *gʰuto- ("invoked one", Old English God). The second component descends from Proto-Indo-European *h₁rewd- / *rewd- ("to decay, putrefy", Old English rotian, Proto-Germanic *rutōną).

2. Minced Oaths and 18th-Century Phonetic Clipping:

  • The Practice of 'Mincing': To avoid legal prosecution under blasphemy statutes (such as the 1606 Act to Restrain Abuses of Players) and severe social reproach, English speakers routinely substituted, shortened, or deformed divine names (e.g., gog, gad, od).
  • The Emergence of 'Od Rot: By the early 18th century, God rot had softened into 'od rot, od-rot, and od-drot. Playwrights of the Augustan era and Georgian comedies frequently placed 'od rot it! into the mouths of comical squires and frustrated rustics.
  • Phonetic Compression to Drat (c. 1815): By the Regency era, the phrase had contracted into the single monosyllable drat, functioning as a standalone interjection (e.g., "Drat the cat!"). The origin in "God rot" had become opaque to the general public.

3. Victorian Participial Formation and Literary Adoption:

  • Formation of Dratted (c. 1840s): During the Victorian era, English speakers naturally converted the interjection into an emphatic participial adjective by appending the suffix -ed, yielding dratted. It became an essential tool for middle-class speakers, nurses, and schoolmasters who required a strong vent for frustration without resorting to coarse or ungentlemanly profanity.
  • Victorian Prose Currency: Charles Dickens and his contemporaries made frequent use of dratted to flavor dialogue with authentic working-class and lower-middle-class cantankerousness (e.g., Mrs. Gamp in Martin Chuzzlewit).

4. Tolkien's Usage in The Hobbit (1937):

  • The Dwarves in Mirkwood: In chapter 8 of The Hobbit, when Bilbo vanishes during the chaotic spider attack in the dark forest, the desperate dwarves search for their burglar: "They shouted and called: 'Bilbo Baggins! Hobbit! You dratted hobbit! Hi! hobbit, confusticate you, where are you?'"
  • Nuance and Register: Tolkien—a professional philologist intimately familiar with the historical decay of oaths—uses dratted paired with confusticate (a mock-Latin pseudo-curse) to convey the dwarves' comical bluster mask covering genuine terror and devotion to their lost friend.
