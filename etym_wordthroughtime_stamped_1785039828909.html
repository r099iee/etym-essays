- Old and Middle English - 'Stampian' means to pound, crush, or beat with a heavy implement, the percussive sense from which foot-stamping grows; the word is grounded in thudding labour rather than expression.
- 15th-17th centuries - Foot-stamping becomes the classic public gesture of anger, impatience, and emphatic demand, while the same pounding logic extends to striking a die onto metal or paper - so the word splits into two lives, the noise and the imprint.
- Contemporary sense - Stamped still means both slamming the foot down in a percussive thud of feeling and being marked or endorsed (a stamped document, a stamped-out fire); in either use it keeps an emphatic, final, forceful register.
