PIE *h₃rei- (also *reie-) "to flow, run, stream" (reconstructed) → Latin rīvus "stream, brook, small river" (attested from early Latin) → Latin rīvālis "one who uses the same stream, a neighbour sharing a watercourse; competitor, rival" (Classical Latin; "rival in love" from Ovid, late 1st century BCE–early 1st century CE) → Old French / Middle French rival "competitor, rival" (12th–13th century) → English rival "one who competes with another for the same object" (early 17th century, from French) → English rivalled (past participle of the verb to rival, "to compete with, to seek to equal") — participial adjective → English unrivalled = un- (negative prefix) + rivalled (first recorded 1590s) Note on debate: English river is NOT a descendant of Latin rīvus; it comes from Old French riviere, from Latin rīpārius "of a riverbank" (rīpa "bank"), a different root, so no Germanic "river" stage belongs in this chain. The word unrivalled is an English formation on the verb to rival, first recorded around 1590, and has been in continuous use ever since. Zero guesswork: all forms cited are attested; only the PIE root is reconstructed and flagged as such.
