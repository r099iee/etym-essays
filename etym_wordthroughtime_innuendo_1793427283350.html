- 16th c. — legal formula in Law Latin: originated in English common law pleadings as a Latin parenthetical gerund (literally 'by nodding to, meaning thereby') inserted into court records to explain who or what was meant by an ambiguous libelous phrase.
- Early 17th c. — transition to noun: shifted from an explanatory legal formula into a recognized noun in English jurisprudence, denoting the formal legal explanation of defamatory meaning in an action for slander.
- Late 17th to early 18th c. — political satire and censorship: leaped from the courtroom into the press during the vicious Whig-Tory pamphlet wars; satirists like Swift and Defoe mockingly noted that government censors punished anything that looked like an 'innuendo'.
- 19th c. — Victorian social innuendo: broadened into polite society and literature to describe veiled social snubs, malicious gossip, and oblique hints about private scandals that could not be voiced openly.
- Mid-20th c. — comedic and bawdy double entendre: became a staple of British comedy, music hall traditions, and cinema (notably the Carry On films), where sexual innuendo was cultivated into an art form.
- Late 20th c. to present — modern jurisprudence and political discourse: remains a vital technical concept in defamation law (requiring proof of true vs. false innuendo) and serves in public commentary to identify veiled, deniable political attacks.
