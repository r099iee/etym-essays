• Ash-heap: Similar because both are heaps of the residue of fires; differs because an ash-heap is ashes specifically (from hearth or cremation), while a dust-heap is the whole mixed mound of ashes, cinders, and household refuse.
  • Midden: Similar because both are refuse piles; differs because a midden is the archaeological or rural term for a manure-and-refuse mound of great age (shell middens, farm middens), while a dust-heap is the modern urban rubbish mound.
  • Dunghill: Similar because both are farmyard mounds of waste; differs because a dunghill is animal manure (with its proverb about the crowing cock), while a dust-heap is household ashes and refuse, not excrement.
  • Rubbish heap / Rubbish tip: Similar because both hold discarded household waste; differs because the rubbish terms are the general modern words for thrown-away goods and municipal dumps, while dust-heap is the older, ash-centred word with a Victorian and literary resonance.
  • Dump: Similar because both are places where waste is thrown; differs because dump is the plain modern word (often for large, often municipal or illegal sites) with little colour, while dust-heap is a piled mound with folklore, poverty, and Dickens attached.
  • Landfill: Similar because both are waste-disposal sites; differs because a landfill is a modern engineered burial of waste in lined pits, methodical and environmental, while a dust-heap is the old open mound of ashes and sweepings.
  • Scrap-heap: Similar because both are heaps of discarded things; differs because a scrap-heap is specifically scrap metal and machinery (the scrapheap of failed projects), while a dust-heap is the domestic ash-and-rubbish pile.
  • Junk-yard: Similar because both hold discarded goods; differs because a junk-yard is a commercial yard dealing in second-hand and salvaged goods, while a dust-heap is an unmanaged heap of refuse.
  • Refuse heap: Similar because both name a pile of waste; differs because refuse is the formal, sanitary vocabulary of waste management, while dust-heap is the old, homely, ash-centred word for the same pile.
  • Compost heap: Similar because both are garden mounds of organic matter; differs because a compost heap is deliberately built vegetable and garden waste rotting into fertiliser — a useful pile, not a discarded one.
  • Dustbin of history (phrase): Similar because both are images for the discard of history; differs because the dustbin phrase (long attributed to Trotsky, 1917) is the fixed rhetorical image for oblivion, while dust-heap itself remains a physical mound with its Victorian past.
