- 17th century - English takes 'palpitation' from Latin 'palpitatio,' the physicians' term for the rapid, irregular beating of the heart; 'palpitating' is born a clinical, exact word of the pulse.
- 18th century - the word leaves the consulting room for the page: novelists and poets use the palpitation of the heart to render fear, love, and suspense, giving the ancient vocabulary of the racing heart an emotional charge.
- 19th century - Victorian medical and literary usage overlap: palpitations are both a nervous complaint of an anxious age and a stock figure of thrilling fiction — the heart in the mouth of the heroine or the hunter.
- Contemporary - 'palpitating' remains precise and slightly elevated, the standard word for a heart beating rapidly and irregularly under fear or excitement; its quasi-medical dignity lets writers dignify dread, as when Tolkien names a palpitating tale.
