- Late 14th c. — martial and physical triumph: borrowed from Anglo-French and Latin into Middle English as prevailen, meaning to be efficacious, to conquer in battle, or to exert superior military power.
- 15th c. — moral and legal efficacy: extended into scholastic, theological, and legal spheres to describe arguments, laws, or divine powers that prove efficacious and binding over heresy or doubt.
- 16th c. — interpersonal persuasion: develops the distinctive phrasal construction to prevail on/upon (and formerly with), meaning to induce or win someone over through earnest petition and urgent entreaty.
- 17th c. — biblical and majestic cadence: immortalized in the King James Bible (e.g. Jacob wrestling at Peniel: 'for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed'), imbuing the word with dignified solemnity.
- 18th c. — state of general currency: regularized in literary and philosophical prose (as in Swift) for both persuasive overcoming and broad societal dominance ('a custom that prevailed throughout the province').
- 19th c. to present — enduring resilience: maintained in modern English as a dignified term for truth, justice, or common sense outlasting adversity, alongside technical terms like 'prevailing winds' and legal 'prevailing party'.
