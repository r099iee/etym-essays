The noun elation names a state of intense, buoyant happiness and exhilaration, with a persistent sense of being lifted or raised above ordinary cares. Unlike quiet contentment, elation implies verticality: the spirit feels inflated, raised, and carried upward, as if a psychological ascent has left gravity behind. In literature it describes the swelling joy of relief, triumph, or unexpected good fortune — the emotion of a protagonist who has just achieved a long-held ambition, survived an ordeal, or received a sudden revelation of love. It radiates a brilliant, uncontainable energy that often spills into physical expression, yet it keeps a dignified, literary grace.

The word's anatomy is transparent: the base elate ("to lift up, raise; to make joyful") plus the noun suffix -ion (from Latin -iō, forming nouns of action or state). To be elated is literally to be raised aloft, and elation is the condition of having been so raised. The metaphor is bodily and ancient: strong emotion "lifts" the chest, "raises" the spirits, and "carries away" the heart — and the Latin verb that feeds the whole family, efferre, "to carry out, up, or away", supplies the image of a person borne aloft by feeling.

Core senses:
  • Great happiness and excitement; a feeling of euphoric uplift and well-being.
  • A heightened, expansive mood in which energy, optimism, and self-assurance feel uncontainable.
Exhaustive polysemy and modern senses:
  • Everyday positive sense — the dominant modern meaning: soaring, exhilarating joy (as in "the beauty of the moon seemed to increase their elation").
  • Older moral-religious sense: "exaltation of spirit", which in Renaissance and early modern writing could mean inflated pride or vainglory — an "elation of heart" was often a sin of self-importance. This negative shade is now obsolete except in deliberate archaism.
  • Clinical-psychological sense: in psychiatry, elation denotes an abnormally elevated mood, one of the cardinal features of mania or hypomania; distinct from normal joy by its intensity and its possible disconnection from circumstances.
  • Rare technical-rhetorical sense: in older criticism, elation as a "lifting up" of style or tone — grandeur, sublimity of expression.
Historical bridge: the whole family rests on Latin efferre "to carry out, up, or away", whose past participle ēlātus "carried up, raised, exalted" produced ēlātiō(n-) "a lifting up; exaltation of mind". Classical Latin used ēlātiō both for physical elevation and for the elevation of spirit — which could be glorious or blameworthy, since the same word described both noble exaltation and puffed-up pride. English inherited both readings: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries an "elation of mind" was as likely to mean conceit as joy. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the positive sense won decisively, and the proud reading died away. The card's mnemonic — "elate-ion: being elated/happy" — points to the same root: to be elated is to be raised aloft by happiness.

Register and usage:
  • Formal and literary; slightly romantic; appropriate to prose describing intense, dignified joy.
  • Distinguished from euphoria (which may be drug-induced or pathological), exhilaration (physical and adrenaline-charged), and jubilation (public and ceremonial).
