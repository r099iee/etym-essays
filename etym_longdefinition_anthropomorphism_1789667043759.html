ANTHROPOMORPHISM is the attribution of human shape, behaviour, emotion, intention or reasoning to something non-human — to animals, plants, objects, forces of nature, machines or gods. In the source sentence — the charge of anthropomorphism, into which he almost never descends — it names the standing critical risk for any nature writer: to describe a falcon as morose, vengeful or noble is to lend it a human interior it may not possess, trading accuracy for readability. The word is therefore both descriptive and accusatory: to practise anthropomorphism is to humanise; to be charged with it is to be accused of sentimentality, projection or bad science.

The mechanism is universal because human cognition is social: we predict others by modelling minds, and we extend the habit to dogs that look guilty, cars that refuse to start, storms that rage and algorithms that decide. Developmental psychology finds children spontaneously anthropomorphise; robotics finds adults cannot help hearing intention in a voice assistant. Religion institutionalises the tendency in gods with faces, hands and anger; fable and children's literature cultivate it deliberately in talking animals; science polices it through Morgan's Canon and behaviourist method, preferring the leanest non-mental explanation. Good nature writing navigates between the two: vivid enough to convey a life, austere enough not to invent a person.

  • Primary sense — literary and everyday humanisation of animals and things: describing non-humans in human terms. Examples: the morose falcon, the cruel sea, the jealous computer, the wise owl. This is the source sense. The semantic bridge from Greek anthropos human plus morphe form is compositional: giving human form to the formless, then by extension giving human mind to the mindless. The suffix -ism marks it as practice or doctrine, parallel to archaism or euphemism. The charge arises because the device is pleasurable and therefore suspect: readers love a sulky hawk, scientists distrust one.
  • Secondary sense — theological doctrine of a human-shaped God: the belief that the deity has a human body, passions or limitations. Early Christian heresiologists condemned Anthropomorphites who read God's hands and eyes literally; Xenophanes already mocked Ethiopians for black gods and Thracians for blue-eyed gods. Here the bridge is from metaphor to metaphysics: what begins as picturing God in prayer hardens into asserting God as picture. Modern theology uses the word neutrally for unavoidable religious language — all speech about God borrows from human life — while distinguishing crude pictorial anthropomorphism from refined analogical speech.
  • Tertiary senses — scientific error-term; design and AI usage: in ethology, psychology and AI, anthropomorphism is a methodological caution: to say the rat expects, the AI understands or the robot wants is to over-attribute. Researchers counter with anthropodenial as the opposite error — refusing obvious continuities — and with critical anthropomorphism as disciplined, evidence-based use of human terms for closely related species. In design, friendly anthropomorphism is a deliberate affordance: faces on cars, names for assistants, eyes on robots elicit care and legibility. In animation and branding, it is pure craft. The bridge in each case is the same human projection, valued differently: vice in explanation, virtue in communion or commerce.

Distinctions fix the boundaries. Personification gives human qualities to abstractions for rhetorical effect and owns the artifice; anthropomorphism often smuggles it as fact. The pathetic fallacy attributes feeling to nature in sympathy with a speaker's mood; zoomorphism does the reverse, lending animal form to humans or gods. Animism endows the world with spirits; anthropocentrism makes humanity the measure of value. To almost never descend into anthropomorphism, as the source praises, is therefore to hold a high, hard line: to let a falcon be huddled without making it morose in our sense, to convey otherness without colonising it.

  • Domain bridges: literary criticism (risk and resource of vividness); theology (God-talk and idolatry); biology and psychology (Morgan's Canon, theory of mind, ethology); child development (animistic thinking); robotics and AI (social interfaces and over-trust); art and fable (talking beasts as moral shorthand).
