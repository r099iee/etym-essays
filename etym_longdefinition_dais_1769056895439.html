dais (noun) — a low platform, usually at the upper end of a hall, on which seats of honour are placed so that distinguished persons sit physically above the general company. In the ruins it is the 'anatomical centre' of the banqueting hall — the vertical lift that once separated royalty from court. Here the word is spoken by an excited Peter, who has just recognised the ruined hall as the throne-room of the old Narnian kingdom: 'That was the dais where the High Table was, where the King and the great lords sat.' The tone is ceremonial and reverent, touched with wonder — a child suddenly recognizing the geography of his own lost coronation. Primary sense. In ordinary architectural use a dais is a raised platform for seats of honour: the block at the head of a great hall, banqueting chamber, or council room, sometimes approached by steps, on which a throne, the high table, or a presiding dignitary's seat stands. The word always implies hierarchy: it raises its occupants above the common floor, making rank literally a matter of height. Secondary and extended senses.
  • In formal and ceremonial English 'dais' extends to any raised seat of authority — a judge's bench, a speaker's platform at a formal dinner, a throne-block — provided seating is involved.
  • In ecclesiastical usage a 'dais' can be a raised part of a chancel or hall, and the related French sense shifted the word onto the canopy that covers a throne or altar (the covering taking the name of the platform it protects).
  • In wedding and ballroom vocabulary the 'dais' is the platform where the bridal party or honoured guests sit — still the medieval high table in modern dress.
  • In technical and trade jargon the word survives in stagecraft and banqueting logistics for any temporary raised seating platform.
  • Historically, the Middle English word could also mean the high table itself, before the platform sense won; the transfer from 'table' to 'the block that carries the table' is the word's great metonymic shift.
The semantic bridge. The word's history runs through 'dish' — literally the thrown disc of Greek athletics — to the round plate of a Roman table, to the table-board of the medieval hall, to the platform that raised the table. Every stage is a metonymy: the object named gives its name to its container or to what it stands upon. Understanding that chain explains why the word for a raised platform is cousin to the word for a dish and a desk. Nearest companions. Synonyms must be separated with care.
  • 'Platform' is the generic ancestor: any raised horizontal surface, from a railway platform to an oil rig, with zero implication of honour.
  • 'Stage' implies performance and spectacle — actors, music, speeches — and belongs to theatres; a dais is not for performing but for presiding.
  • 'Podium' is small and functional, for a single speaker or conductor, and never for seating; it raises one person briefly.
  • 'Rostrum' is the Roman beaked prow, hence the speaker's platform in an assembly — a political, not a banquet, word.
  • 'Pulpit' is the raised enclosed desk for preaching in a church, with unmistakably clerical overtones.
  • Only 'dais' (and its close partner the 'high table') names the raised dining-and-counsel platform of a great hall, the seat of authority at a feast.
