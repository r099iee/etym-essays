The word ducked embodies an exceptionally coherent etymological arc within the Germanic branch of Indo-European languages, where an ancient root denoting physical depth and downward immersion evolved into animal nomenclature, martial maneuvers, and modern psychological metaphors.

The ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰewb- (or zero-grade *dʰub-), signifying "deep, hollow, or to plunge into depth." In accordance with Grimm's Law, the Proto-Indo-European voiced aspirated stop *dʰ shifted in Proto-Germanic to the voiced stop *d, while *b shifted to *p. This root proved immensely fertile across Germanic languages, yielding a major cluster of interrelated words: English deep (from Proto-Germanic *deupaz), dive (from Old English dȳfan), dip (from Old English dippan), and dump. Across other Indo-European languages, cognates appear in Lithuanian dubùs ("deep, hollow") and Old Church Slavonic duno ("bottom").

Within Proto-Germanic, an intensive verbal stem was formed with a long high back vowel and a voiceless velar stop: *dūkaną, meaning "to dive, bend down, or plunge." This verb gave rise to cognates across the Northwest Germanic languages, including Middle Dutch dūken (Modern Dutch duiken, "to dive"), Middle Low German dūken, and Old High German tūhhan (Modern German tauchen, "to dive, immerse," and ducken, "to crouch, duck down").

In Old English, the verbal root was manifested in the nominal agent derivative dūce (literally "the diver" or "the bird that plunges underwater"), which permanently displaced the older Indo-European noun ænid (cognate with Latin anas and German Ente) as the standard English name for the waterfowl. Although direct textual attestations of the Old English verb *dūcan are scarce in surviving manuscripts, its continuous spoken existence is proven by its vigorous emergence in early Middle English as douken or duken.

During the Middle English period (c. 1200–1450), the verb functioned regularly with weak dental preterite inflections (past doukede, ducked). It initially designated literal immersion into rivers, tubs, or ponds. By the 14th century, it was institutionalized in the legal apparatus of the "ducking stool" (also called the cucking stool), an engine of community humiliation where social transgressors, fraudulent bakers, and scolds were strapped to a chair and repeatedly plunged into water before village crowds.

By the 16th century, during the English Renaissance, the verb expanded into the spatial and martial lexicon. In swordplay, fencing manuals, archery, and street brawls, dropping one's head swiftly to allow a weapon to pass overhead was termed ducking. Over the subsequent centuries, this physical motion was generalized into sporting terminology (such as boxing slips and cricket bouncers) and figurative discourse, where ducking an issue, responsibility, or inquiry became an indispensable idiom of modern English conversation, illustrating how an ancient physical diving root transitioned into high-frequency behavioral metaphor.
