Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*medʰyo-
"middle, central, between"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*meðyos
"middle, in between"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE)
medius
"middle, intermediary, intermediate"
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th c. CE)
mediāre (pp. mediātus)
"to be in the middle, mediate"
↓
Late / Medieval Latin (c. 13th c.)
immediātus (in- + mediātus)
"without anything in the middle,"
"having no intermediary"
↓
Middle French (c. 14th c.)
immediat
"direct, unmediated"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
immediate + -cy
"state of direct connection"
↓
Modern English (c. 1600s)
immediacy
"direct presence; lack of delay"

Note: Late Latin immediātus combined the negative prefix in- ('not, un-') with mediātus, the past participle of mediāre ('to halve, intervene, mediate'). The abstract noun immediacy was formed in English during the late Elizabethan era using the productive suffix -cy.
