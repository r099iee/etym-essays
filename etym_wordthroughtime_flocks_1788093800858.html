- (Old English, single isolated attestation) flocc - a “troop or band” of MEN: the first recorded sense is a company of people, not animals at all.
- (13th c.) Middle English flok - a herd of sheep or goats; the animal sense takes over daily use, and the Norse-flavoured word spreads through the agrarian economy.
- (14th c.) Applied to birds — “flocks of fowls” — and the biblical metaphor fixes “the flock” as Christ's congregation under his shepherds.
- (15th–17th c.) “Flocks” of every gregarious creature — geese, starlings, deer — and the verb “to flock” follows; the proverb “birds of a feather flock together” sets the word in idiom.
- (18th–19th c.) Natural history and travel writing make “flocks” the plain collective of bird life; metaphorically of clouds, thoughts, and crowds of people.
- (Modern) Still the default word for sheep and birds; “the faithful flock” survives in churches; science and computing borrow “flocking” for the mathematics of swarms — the shepherd's word now steers simulated birds in code.
