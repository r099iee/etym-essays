The word quoits (Middle English coite, coyte) presents an intriguing etymological narrative spanning ancient discus traditions, Anglo-Norman baronial games, medieval royal prohibitions, and Celtic megalithic archaeology.

  • 1. Etymological Origins and Anglo-Norman Roots:

    The immediate ancestor of quoit is the Old French and Anglo-Norman noun coite, coite, or quoite (c. 13th century), which meant "a flat stone, flat throwing disc, or cushion." The deeper origin is debated among etymologists:

    • The Latin Coitus / Culcita Theory: One school traces it to Latin culcita ("mattress, cushion", yielding Old French coite "cushion, flat pad"), where the flat shape of a throwing disc resembled a thin compressed cushion.
    • The Celtic Discus / Flat Stone Theory: Another prominent etymological view connects it with Celtic/Old French dialect terms for a flat, smooth river stone used for skimming and throwing. In classical antiquity, Greek and Roman soldiers pitched horseshoes and flat circular stones; Roman legionnaires introduced these throwing games to Roman Britain, where native Britons and Anglo-Saxons hurled flat stones (flint-stones or pitching stones) at wooden stakes.
  • 2. Middle English Attestations and Royal Prohibitions:

    By the 14th century (c. 1380), the sport was firmly established in Middle English as coyte or koyte. The game became so enormously popular among the working peasantry and soldiers that it drew the ire of the English Crown:

    • The 1388 Statute of Cambridge: King Richard II, along with King Edward III before him, passed royal statutes banning commoners from playing "coiting, football, dice, and ninepins" on Sundays and holidays because these pastimes distracted young men from mandatory longbow archery practice. Despite royal decrees, quoits remained an unbreakable staple of English rural village life.
  • 3. Early Modern English Evolution: From Stones to Forged Iron:

    During the 15th and 16th centuries, the sport transitioned from using naturally flat river stones to manufactured, circular forged-iron rings with a central aperture. The spelling stabilized in Early Modern English as quoit or coyt. Shakespeare mentions the sport in Henry IV, Part 2 (Act 2, Scene 4), where Falstaff describes throwing quoits with ease ("quoit him down, Bardolph, like a shove-groat shilling"), demonstrating that quoit had also become an active transitive verb meaning "to pitch, hurl, or throw flatly."

  • 4. Cornish Archaeology and Megalithic 'Quoits':

    During the 16th to 18th centuries in Cornwall and southwest England, antiquarians and locals began referring to giant Neolithic portal dolmens as quoits (such as Lanyon Quoit). Local folklore held that giants had played games of quoits with these massive 20-ton flat granite capstones, hurling them across the moors.

  • 5. Maritime Adaptation and Modern Legacy:

    In the 19th and 20th centuries, quoits adapted to Victorian luxury ocean liners as deck quoits using braided rope rings. Today, quoits survives as a revered traditional sport in rural Britain and the Americas, as well as a nostalgic symbol of peaceful, pastoral village pastimes.
