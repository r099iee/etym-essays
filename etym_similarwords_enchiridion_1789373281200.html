• handbook: Similar because both refer to a portable, accessible volume providing essential information or instructions; differs because handbook is the plain Germanic loan-translation (calquing Greek enchiridion and Latin manuale) used across ordinary modern contexts, whereas enchiridion carries a classical, philosophical, or humanist dignity with centuries of scholarly resonance.
  • manual: Similar because both denote practical guidebooks deriving from the anatomical word for "hand" (Latin manus vs. Greek cheir); differs because manual predominantly suggests industrial, technical, procedural, or operational instructions (e.g., automobile repair manual, employee manual), whereas enchiridion implies moral, ethical, philosophical, or spiritual rules of life.
  • vademecum: Similar because both are compact, pocket-sized reference guides meant to be kept permanently within arm's reach; differs because vademecum (Latin for "go with me") often denotes a pocket directory, technical compendium, or portable physical kit carried everywhere for immediate practical utility, whereas enchiridion focuses primarily on systematic textual principles and treatises.
  • compendium: Similar because both synthesize a substantial body of knowledge into an accessible, organized format; differs because a compendium is a comprehensive abridgment or concise summary of a larger scientific or literary work, often running to substantial multivolume length, without the strict handheld, pocket-portable character of an enchiridion.
  • catechism: Similar because both present fundamental tenets of belief or practice for instructional mastery; differs because a catechism relies strictly on a standardized question-and-answer pedagogical framework designed for memorization and religious initiation, whereas an enchiridion is composed of continuous aphorisms, maxims, or expository essays.
  • breviary: Similar because both are compact handheld volumes consulted daily for guidance; differs because a breviary is a specific liturgical prayer-book containing the canonical Divine Office recited daily by Roman Catholic clergy, whereas an enchiridion is open to secular, ethical, philosophical, and legal disciplines.
