consummate — (adjective) carried to the highest degree of excellence, skill, or perfection; complete, finished, total, and incapable of improvement. Etymologically the word enshrines a metaphor of accumulation and ascent: something consummate has been 'summed up' (Latin consummāre, 'to add up, to bring to a total'), so that all its parts have been gathered to their highest point and nothing remains to be added, removed, or altered. It is thus not merely 'very good' but total — a perfection that is quantitative as much as qualitative, the full sum of every excellence a medium allows.

Senses of consummate

  • Adjective, of persons: supremely skilled, masterly — a consummate craftsman, a consummate actor, a consummate politician; the word crowns a practitioner at the very ceiling of the craft.
  • Adjective, of works, acts, and qualities: finished, flawless, carried to the last degree — consummate skill, consummate artistry, consummate tact.
  • Verb (formal): to bring to completion, to finish — 'to consummate a deal, an agreement'; and, with its strongest and oldest charge, 'to consummate a marriage' — to complete it physically. The verb keeps the older, ritual sense of finality: Latin consummatum est, 'it is finished' — the Vulgate's words of Christ on the cross.
  • Historical bridge: from the image of adding a column of figures to its top (Latin summa, 'the total') the word moved to mean completing a process, and then completing it perfectly — so that what began as arithmetic became a moral and aesthetic judgement of terminal excellence.
relegated — (past tense and past participle of relegate) consigned, dispatched, or removed to a place of secondary, inferior, or peripheral standing; sent away from the centre of affairs to a position of diminished importance. The Latin compound relegāre means literally 'to send back or away with a commission,' from re-, 'back, away,' and lēgāre, 'to send as an envoy, to depute' — the same verb that yields legate and delegate. Relegation is thus a formal, deliberate, almost bureaucratic act: a dispatch, not a mere fall.

Senses of relegated

  • Of persons and things: sent to an inferior or peripheral position — relegated to the attic, to the footnotes, to the margins of history; in the passage, the carvings 'were subsequently relegated to the Hall of the Bright Carvings,' dispatched from the scene of judgement to a secondary hall.
  • Of institutions and leagues (modern sports): a club 'relegated to a lower division' — the standard public term for official demotion, carrying the precise shade of a recorded, administrative fall.
  • Historical bridge: Latin relegāre could mean 'to banish, to send into exile' as well as 'to send on a mission'; the modern word keeps the bureaucratic formality of the envoy (the thing is dispatched officially) while adding the downward shade of exile — demotion with paperwork.

In the passage

  • The two words work as a pair, opposite in direction: the carvings are first judged 'the most consummate' — raised to the absolute ceiling of the craft — and are then 'relegated,' dispatched away from the centre of the judgement to the Hall of the Bright Carvings. The phrase mimes a double motion: up to the summit of value, then out to the margin of the castle. Only three were chosen; the rest, implicitly, were left outside.
  • The tone of both words is ritual, hierarchical, almost legalistic — consummate is the vocabulary of final adjudication, relegated of official dispatch; together they describe the two poles of the Earl's world, the summit of perfection and the periphery to which perfection is consigned.
