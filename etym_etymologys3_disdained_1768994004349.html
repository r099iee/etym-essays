The line of disdained begins at the PIE root *dek-, "to take, to accept" — a root with two faces, grasping and judging, since what one accepts is what one counts worthy. Its Indo-Iranian daughter kept the taking: Sanskrit daśa, "ten," the ten fingers that take and count, which passes straight into modern Hindi das and Odia dasa; the same root in Sanskrit dāś- means "to offer, to honor," showing the root moving from the hand toward respect. Greek took the root into the mind: dechomai, "I receive," then dokē, "opinion" — what the mind accepts as true — engendering dogma and doxa (and, in modern times, paradox). But the decisive branch grew in Latin.
  • From taking to worth (Latin): in Latin *dek- yielded decet, "it is fitting" — the sense of what is proper to accept — and from that family came decor (honor, comeliness, hence English decent, decorous, decorate) and, most fatefully, the adjective dignus, "worthy." The morphology is eloquent: dignus is literally "acceptable," "that which can be taken as fitting," so that the very concept of worth is framed as a judgment of what may be accepted.
  • The double negation (Latin verb): Latin derived the denominative verb dignari, "to deem worthy," and — by prefixing the negative dis- ("apart, away, un-") — dedignari, "to deem unworthy," used already by classical authors (Pliny the Younger, Tacitus) for the contemptuous refusal that takes no account of an inferior thing. The word is thus a negation of a judgment: not merely "to refuse," but "to pass a verdict of worthlessness."
  • Passage through French (medieval): Romance carried the verb north. Old French desdeignier ("to refuse with contempt") evolved regularly from *dedignare, and from it Anglo-Norman and Middle English produced disdeinen in the thirteenth century (first recorded c. 1290), with the noun disdain, the adjective disdainful, and the inflected form disdained all in continuous use thereafter.
  • Sound and form in English: the Middle English disdeinen lost its medial syllables and levelled to disdain; the past participle disdained, formed by the regular English suffix -ed, preserves the perfect, sealed quality of a completed act — which the passage exploits ("who disdained the services of Martian guides," a deed finished before the journey began).
  • Semantic trajectory: the history is a study in inversion: a root meaning "to take" is negated into "to refuse," and a root meaning "to accept as worthy" is negated into "to reject as worthless." The physical image of a hand that will not reach out becomes the psychological attitude of the person who will not even acknowledge. Meanwhile the positive half of the family lived on in dignity, dignitary, deign, indignant, indignation, and decent — so that English still carries both sides of the original "taking" judgment: what is worthy is what is accepted, and the disdainer is precisely the one who refuses to accept.
  • Socio-cultural register: from its medieval entry the word attached to aristocratic haughtiness — the cold superiority of those who judge things beneath their dignity — and in the 17th–18th centuries it stretched beyond rank into intellectual and aesthetic judgment, a quiet, cool verdict of unworthiness distinct from hot anger. Today it remains formal and literary; the past form disdained seals the refusal as accomplished, carrying a note of self-dooming pride that is exactly right for the hard-bitten earthmen who walked into the Chaur alone.
The word's literary power in the passage derives directly from this long history. To say the earthmen "disdained the services of Martian guides" is to say that they applied an ancient standard of worth — descended from a root that once meant "to take into the hand" — and refused to take. The refusal is not the noise of anger but the silence of judgment, and the past tense makes it a completed arrogance that the reader, forewarned by the queer tales of the Chaur, recognizes as the preface to disaster.
