Chronologique (/kʁɔ.nɔ.lɔ.ʒik/) is the French adjective meaning "chronological" — belonging to chronology, or arranged according to the order of time. In the source it is the second word of the title Abrege Chronologique de l'Histoire de France, where it modifies Abrégé and tells the reader exactly how the abridgement is organised: not by theme, province or reign, but by date.

The word is invariable in gender. French adjectives in -ique have a single form for masculine and feminine, so un abrégé chronologique and une table chronologique use the same adjective; only the plural adds -s (chronologiques). In the title it agrees with the masculine Abrégé, and the capital C is simply the capitalisation of a title word.

Primary sense: ordered by time

  • Arranged in the sequence in which events occurred: un ordre chronologique, un tableau chronologique, classer des documents par ordre chronologique.
  • Applied to a narrative or account: one that follows events in their temporal order rather than in an order of argument, theme or importance. The Abrégé chronologique is precisely such a narrative, its chapters governed by years and reigns.
  • Applied to a list or table: one whose entries are sorted by date, as opposed to alphabetical or systematic order. Library and archival description uses chronologique in this strict sense.
  • The adjective implies correspondence between the order of the telling and the order of happening; a flashback is therefore a departure from chronological order, not a different chronology.

Secondary sense: belonging to chronology as a discipline

  • Of or relating to chronology as the science of dating: les systèmes chronologiques des anciens, une question chronologique.
  • In history and philology, chronologique can mean "pertaining to the fixing of dates" — the technical work of reconciling calendars, eras and regnal years.
  • In criticism and linguistics the adjective contrasts with logique and thématique: a chronological arrangement follows time, a logical one follows argument, a thematic one follows subject.
  • In astronomy and geology it names systems of time-reckoning and dating, where English would speak of chronological or chronometric scales.

Grammar and collocation

  • Position: normally after the noun (ordre chronologique, récit chronologique), as in the title.
  • Common collocations: ordre chronologique, cadre chronologique, repères chronologiques, bornes chronologiques, table chronologique, frise chronologique (a timeline), chronologique vs anachronique.
  • Antonyms: anachronique (out of its time), intemporel (timeless), alphabétique, thématique.
  • The noun is chronologie, the practitioner a chronologiste or chronologue; the adverb is chronologiquement.

The English counterpart

  • English chronological (/ˌkrɒn.əˈlɒdʒ.ɪ.kəl/) answers to the French word exactly, and the rarer chronologic is a learned variant.
  • Everyday collocations: in chronological order, chronological age (as opposed to biological or developmental age), a chronological account.
  • The phrase chronological snobbery — the assumption that what is newer is therefore truer — was given currency by C. S. Lewis, who credited it to Owen Barfield; it shows how far the adjective has travelled from mere date-order into cultural criticism.
  • Unlike the French word, English chronological is not used for the discipline itself; that is chronology, and a practitioner is a chronologer or chronologist.

Why the word matters in this title

A title in the eighteenth century was a promise about method. Chronologique promised the reader a book in which the year 1744 would be found under 1744, with the king's illness at Metz entered in its place between the campaigns of Flanders and the relief of Alsace. That promise shapes the passage on this card: the anecdote is dated, framed by a sequence, and offered as one entry in a continuous record. Without the adjective, Abrégé de l'Histoire de France could be a thematic essay; with it, the book declares itself a timeline in prose.
