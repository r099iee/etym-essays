=== ROOT WORD 1: TOOTH (first element) === Stage 1 — PIE *h₁dónt- "tooth; that which bites or eats" (c. 4000–3500 BCE; the classic dental root, also behind Latin dēns, dēntis "tooth", Greek odṓn / odṓnt- "tooth", Sanskrit dánt "tooth", Hittite kānt- "tooth"; possibly itself built on the PIE root *h₁ed- "to eat", the tooth as "the eater" — a debated but attractive etymology) Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *tanþs "tooth" (c. 500 BCE–200 CE; with Verner's-law alternation *tunþ- in oblique cases — Gothic tunþus, Old Norse tǫnn, Old High German zand, German Zahn) Stage 3 — Old English tōþ "tooth" (attested 8th c.; regular raising of PGmc *a to ō before a nasal + consonant cluster, as also in gōs "goose" < *gans and mōnaþ "month" < *manþ) Stage 4 — Middle English toth, tooth, tooþ "tooth" (12th–15th c.; plural tēþ by i-umlaut, modern teeth) Stage 5 — Early Modern English tooth (16th c.) Stage 6 — Modern English tooth "one of the hard bony structures in the jaws used for biting and chewing" === ROOT WORD 2: -SOME (suffix) === Stage 1 — PIE *sem- "one, together, whole, same" (c. 4000–3500 BCE; the source of English same and Latin similis "like, similar") Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *samaz "same, together" and the adjectival suffix *-samaz "characterized by, sharing in, tending to" (reconstructed; cf. Old High German -sam in heilsam "healing, wholesome") Stage 3 — Old English -sum "characterized by, tending to" (adjective-forming suffix, attested 8th–11th c. in wynsum "joyful", līþsum "gentle", the ancestor of modern winsome) Stage 4 — Middle English -som / -sum (12th–15th c.) Stage 5 — Modern English -some "having the quality of, tending to" (as in wholesome, handsome, wearisome) COMBINATION: tooth + -some → toothsome "pleasant to the tooth, appetising, delicious" (native English formation, first recorded c. 1565) => toothsome (modern English adjective) DEBATE / NUANCE: The PIE root *h₁dónt- for "tooth" is one of the most securely attested roots in the Indo-European family. The -some suffix's connection to PIE *sem- "one, same" is standard in the handbooks, but the precise semantic route from "same/together" to "having the quality of" is reconstructed and debated in detail; the suffix itself is certainly ancient Germanic. The word toothsome is a purely native English coinage with no inherited ancestor — its history begins in the sixteenth century, not in the distant past.
