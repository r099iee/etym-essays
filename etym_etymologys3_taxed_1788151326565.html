The evolution of taxed: from touching, to censuring, to levying, to draining. The story of taxed is the story of the English verb tax seen through its most-used inflected form — the past participle that doubles as an adjective for every kind of imposed burden.

1. Roots: touching and handling

The chain begins with the Indo-European root *tag- / *tāg-, "to touch, handle, grasp", which produced the Latin verb tangere ("to touch"). Latin then formed a frequentative, taxāre, whose sense "to touch repeatedly" shaded into "to handle sharply, to censure, to charge (with a crime), to assess, to compute". This double profile — the judgmental and the computational — is the seed of everything that followed, and it explains why the English word can mean both "to accuse" and "to charge money".

2. Old French and Middle English: levy and blame

French inherited taxer with both profiles (12th c.). Anglo-Norman carried it into English as taxen in the 14th century. For medieval speakers the primary sense was to charge someone with a fault — "he was taxed with covetousness" — and only secondarily to impose a financial levy. Chaucer and Gower use the verb in both legal and moral contexts. The participial form taxed appears early for the state of being charged: a man "taxed" was a man accused; a shire "taxed" was a shire assessed for royal dues.

3. The fiscal hardening: 14th–19th centuries

The financial sense steadily overtook the moral one as the English fiscal state grew. Medieval tallages and fifteenths, Tudor subsidies, and Stuart impositions all put the word at the heart of political grievance: "taxation without representation" became the rallying cry of the American Revolution, and the British income tax of 1799 (temporary, then permanent from 1842) made tax a word of everyday life. Benjamin Franklin's quip that "in this world nothing can be said to be certain, except death and taxes" (1789) fixed the idiom. Meanwhile the participial adjective moved in two directions: taxed = "levied upon" (of goods, incomes, people) and, by constitutional extension, "oppressed by the state".

4. The figurative leap: taxing patience, taxing belief

Already in the 17th century the moral "charge" sense had begun to re-migrate from accusation toward demand: something "taxes" you when it makes a heavy claim on you. "His patience was taxed", "the memory is taxed", "the mind is taxed" — by the 19th century these were stock literary expressions, which is exactly the register Wordsworth exploits in the source context of this card: "Days of sweet leisure, taxed with patient thought / Abstruse". Here the day's ease is charged, almost harnessed, for intellectual labour — the fiscal metaphor (a day conscribed for thought) doing serious poetic work.

5. Sound and form: a remarkably stable word

Phonetically tax is one of the most stable verbs in English: Old French taxer → Middle English taxen → modern tax show almost no change, and the participial taxed /tækst/ has stood since the 15th century. What changed was not the form but the semantic centre of gravity, which slid from accusation (14th c.) to fiscal exaction (15th–19th c.) to generalised strain (17th c. onward).

6. Modern and contemporary usage

In the 20th–21st centuries taxed proliferates: the taxed cost "assessed" in court; the "taxing" job that drains; the American slang "he taxed me" meaning he robbed or cheated me; the financial idiom of "pre-taxed" and "post-taxed" returns. The word's long career — from Latin frequentative touch, to French-and-English censure, to constitutional levies, to the poetry of mental strain — makes it a compact case study in how fiscal vocabulary generalises into the language of effort and exhaustion. The moral sense "to charge with a fault" survives only in literary and legal archaism, but its shadow remains in the modern collocation "taxed with ingratitude".

  • 3rd–2nd c. BCE: Latin tangere "to touch"; frequentative taxāre "to censure, charge, assess".
  • 12th c.: Old French taxer, both "levy" and "accuse".
  • 14th c.: Middle English taxen; particitial taxed attested.
  • 18th–19th c.: fiscal sense dominant; "death and taxes" idiom.
  • 20th–21st c.: "taxing" adjective; colloquial "taxed" = drained, robbed.
