Normandy (French Normandie) is a historical province and, since 1 January 2016, an administrative region of north-western France. It faces the English Channel from the chalk cliffs of the Pays de Caux to the granite headlands of the Cotentin, and its name means, quite transparently, “land of the Northmen” — the Viking settlers who were granted the lower Seine in the early tenth century. Few European regions have a name that so exactly records their origin, and fewer still have shaped another nation’s language and law as Normandy shaped England’s after 1066.

Geographical sense

Normandy is bounded by the Channel to the north and west, by Picardy and the Île-de-France to the east, and by the Loire country to the south. Its two traditional halves are Upper Normandy (Haute-Normandie), centred on Rouen and Le Havre, and Lower Normandy (Basse-Normandie), centred on Caen and including the Cotentin peninsula and the bocage. The Seine runs through Rouen to the sea; the Orne, the Vire and the Eure drain the interior. The landscape is divided between the open, grain-growing campagne of the east, the hedged farmland of the west, and the tidal marshes and dunes of the coast.

  • Rouen — the historic capital, where Joan of Arc was burned in 1431 and where the dukes kept their exchequer.
  • Caen — William the Conqueror’s foundation, with the abbey of Saint-Étienne and the Matilda abbey of the Holy Trinity.
  • Bayeux — home of the eleventh-century tapestry that narrates the conquest of England.
  • Mont Saint-Michel — on the Norman-Breton border, the great abbey-fortress of the tidal flats.
  • The D-Day beaches — Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword, from the Cotentin to the Orne estuary.

Historical sense: the duchy

The medieval duchy of Normandy was created in 911, when the Carolingian king Charles the Simple granted lands around the lower Seine to the Viking leader Rollo in the treaty of Saint-Clair-sur-Epte. In exchange Rollo accepted baptism and did homage. His successors expanded west and south until the duchy covered roughly the modern region, and they built one of the strongest feudal states of western Europe. Norman knights went to southern Italy and Sicily, to Antioch, and in 1066 to England.

The conquest of England by Duke William in 1066 made Normandy the ancestral homeland of an English royal dynasty and, for a century and a half, part of a cross-Channel empire. That connection ended in 1204, when Philip II of France confiscated the duchy from King John; the Channel Islands, curiously, remained loyal to the English crown and are still, in a sense, the last remnant of the duchy.

Later history

Normandy was a principal theatre of the Hundred Years’ War: the siege of Rouen (1418–19), the burning of Joan of Arc (1431), the decisive French victory at Formigny (1450) and the recovery of the province by Charles VII in 1449–50. In the religious wars it was a stronghold of both Huguenots and Catholics. In 1944 the Normandy landings of 6 June — Operation Overlord — made the name synonymous with the liberation of western Europe, and the bocage fighting of the following weeks was among the costliest of the campaign.

Cultural sense

Normandy is a byword for a particular kind of French provincial identity: Romanesque abbeys, half-timbered market towns, cider orchards, Camembert and Livarot, Calvados, and the Impressionist landscapes painted around Giverny, Honfleur and Étretat. Its literary heritage includes Corneille, Flaubert, Maupassant and Proust’s Balbec, a thinly disguised Cabourg.

Modern and extended senses

  • The administrative region — since 2016 the two former regions have been merged into a single Normandy with its capital at Rouen; the merger is unpopular with some inhabitants of the west.
  • “Normandy” as shorthand for D-Day and the liberation of Europe — in journalism and memorial discourse, “Normandy” stands for the whole operation and its sacrifice.
  • The Normandy format — since 2014, the diplomatic framework bringing France, Germany, Russia and Ukraine together, so named because the first meeting was held in Normandy during the seventieth anniversary of D-Day.
  • À la normande — a culinary term for dishes with cream, apples, cider or Calvados, and for the garnishing of fish with mussels and mushrooms.
  • Normandy sauce — in English cookery, a cream and mushroom sauce, from the French repertoire.

Semantic bridges

All the modern uses radiate from one historical fact: the settlement of Vikings on the Seine. The province is named for its founders; the dynasty is named for the province; the invasion of 1066 is named for the dynasty; and the twentieth-century battle is named for the invasion. Even the diplomatic “Normandy format” reaches back, through the anniversary ceremonies, to the same coast. The word is thus a chain of metonymies, each link a real historical event rather than a metaphor.
