PART ONE — THE CELTIC HALF: from an uncertain Indo-European root to the Greek and Roman ethnonym.

The first half of Celtiberian is the ancient name of the Celtic peoples themselves, and its biography begins with a genuinely unsolved problem. The English word Celt, the Greek Keltoí, and the Latin Celtae all point back to a Celtic self-designation *kelto-; but when we ask what that name originally meant, the handbooks divide. The traditional and still most widely printed etymology connects *kelto- with the PIE root *ḱel-, 'to hide, conceal, cover', so that the Celts would be 'the hidden ones' or 'the concealed people'. A rival school prefers the homophonous root *ḱel-, 'to strike, to drive', which would make the name mean 'the strikers', 'the warriors'. Each side can cite parallels — other peoples named for hiding, other peoples named for fighting — and neither theory can be settled from the attested record alone. Honest scholarship therefore states the dilemma explicitly: the ultimate root of the Celtic ethnonym is DEBATED, while the attested chain that follows is secure. This is exactly the kind of nuance the etymological record requires: we distinguish what is documented from what is reconstructed, and we say 'uncertain' when we must.

What is not in doubt is the documentary history of the name in Greek and Roman literature. The Greeks knew the Celts from the 6th century BC, when Ionian writers such as Hecataeus of Miletus reported them as a great transalpine people; by the 5th century BC Herodotus can speak of the Keltoí living near the sources of the Danube. The form Keltoí reached Latin as Celtae, used by Caesar in the Gallic War — where the Celtae are one of the three great divisions of Gaul — and by Pliny and the Roman geographers. It is at this point, on the frontier between the Latin ethnonym and the geography of Spain, that our compound is born. When Roman armies and historians encountered the mixed peoples of the Spanish interior, they coined the double name Celtiberī — literally 'the Celts among the Iberi' — attested in the second-century sources for the Numantine and Celtiberian wars, and so the two halves of the peninsula's pre-Roman population entered the Latin language as one word. The attested chain Keltoí → Celtae → Celtiberī is solid documentary history.

The English form is a product of Renaissance scholarship. Latin Celtiberī, fitted with the adjective suffix -ian (Latin -iānus), gave English Celtiberian in the 17th century, when scholars of the classical texts and of the newly recovered inscriptions began to use it as a precise geographical and ethnic label. In the 18th and 19th centuries, with the first systematic study of the Spanish inscriptions — the Botorrita bronzes were not discovered until the twentieth century, but earlier finds like the Luzaga Bronze entered philology in the nineteenth — the word slipped from a name for a people to a name for a language: the linguist's term for the Q-Celtic speech of the Meseta. The semantic career of the Celtic half thus runs: hidden/warrior people (disputed root) → Greek Keltoí → Latin Celtae → Latin Celtiberī → English Celtiberian.

  • Literary attestations. Caesar, Bellum Gallicum; Livy and Polybius on the Celtiberian wars; Appian's Iberica; Pliny's Natural History. In modern scholarship the pivotal attestations are epigraphic: the Botorrita bronzes, the Luzaga Bronze, the Peñalba de Villastar inscription.
  • Socio-cultural setting. The name crystallises a real historical fusion: Celtic-speaking incomers of the late Bronze Age and Iron Age mingling with a non-Indo-European substrate in the Spanish highlands, producing the warlike highland societies the Romans called Celtiberi and had to fight for generations.
  • Sound-shift footnote. The Q-Celtic conservatism of the language (preserved *kʷ, written ku) is the phonological signature that lets linguists assign the inscriptions to the Celtic family despite the alien Iberian script in which they were written.
  • Why the ethnonym matters. Ethnonyms preserve identity: calling the Celtiberians 'the Celts among the Iberi' recorded a perceived double ancestry, and modern Spaniards of the region have again embraced the name as a claim about who they are.

PART TWO — THE IBERIAN HALF: a pre-Indo-European river-name with no PIE root.

The second half of Celtiberian is the more mysterious. Iber, and the country-name Iberia, go back to the ancient river-name Ibēr, the Greek and Latin name of the Ebro. Greek geographers, beginning with the Ionian writers of the 6th–5th centuries BC, applied the name first to the river and its valley, then to the whole eastern coast, and finally to the entire peninsula: Ibēría became Europe's learned word for Spain long before the Romans had conquered it. The native peoples of these coasts, the Ibēres (Latin Iberī or Hiberī), were not Celts at all. They were speakers of a non-Indo-European language — the mysterious tongue preserved in well over two thousand inscriptions in the Iberian script, still only partially deciphered — and their own name for themselves is lost to us. The word Iber itself therefore has NO Indo-European root. It belongs to the pre-Indo-European substrate of the western Mediterranean, the unknown speech of the peninsula before the arrival of Indo-Europeans, and its origin must be honestly labelled: unknown.

The one hypothesis with real currency is a Vasconic (Basque-related) connection. Basque has ibai 'river' and ibar 'valley', and it is tempting to see the ancient Ibēr in these modern forms — a river-name that would then mean simply 'the river', the Ebro as THE river of the country. But the tempting comparison is also the dangerous one. The Basque forms are modern, the phonetic correspondence is not watertight, and no ancient text says the Iberians called the river 'the river'. Responsible sources therefore present the Basque link as probable-but-unproven, a promising lead rather than an established fact. What the substrate does teach us is negative and important: Iberia is a name that refuses an Indo-European pedigree by nature, and any honest tree of this word must begin not with PIE but with the unknown languages that preceded the Celts and Romans. Stating that 'no PIE root exists' is not a failure of the chain; it is the accurate description of the record.

The two halves converge at the Latin compound Celtiberī ('the Celts among the Iberi'), formed by the Roman historians and ethnographers of the 2nd century BC for the mixed peoples of the interior. The compound is exact history in miniature: a Celtic ethnonym joined to a pre-Celtic regional name, the two great layers of the peninsula's ancient population locked into a single word. Latin Celtiberī passed through the Renaissance revival of the classics into modern scholarship, and the 17th-century English adjective Celtiberian carried the whole double story with it — a word whose two halves name two populations, exactly as the card's own mnemonic insists.

  • Confusion to avoid. In Latin the form Hiberia was easily confused with Hibernia, the Roman name of Ireland, but the two are unrelated: Hibernia is Celtic (compare Welsh Iwerddon), while Iberia is the substrate word for Spain.
  • Modern extension. Since the 19th century 'Iberian' has been the standard learned adjective for Spain and Portugal together, and 'Iberia' the geographical name for the peninsula — both ultimate descendants of the ancient river-name of the Ebro, applied by the Greeks to the whole country and inherited by modern geography.
  • Attestations. Hecataeus and Herodotus (fragments and the Persian-War ethnography), Polybius, Appian, Livy, Pliny; epigraphically, the whole corpus of Iberian-script inscriptions from the eastern and southern coasts, from which the non-Celtic character of the Iberian language is certain.
  • Semantic bridge. The shift from river-name to country-name to people-name is the classic Greek habit of naming regions after their great river (compare how the Nile named Aegyptus in Greek usage); the Ebro was simply the river of the country, and the name spread from its banks to the whole peninsula.
