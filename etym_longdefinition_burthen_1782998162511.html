BURTHEN is the archaic spelling of burden, meaning 'a load or weight; a heavy obligation or spiritual load', and it is the form that English kept for the word's solemn, scriptural, and poetic uses. In this card it appears at the pivot of Book First of William Wordsworth's The Prelude (1805): 'Trances of thought and mountings of the mind / Come fast upon me: it is shaken off, / That burthen of my own unnatural self, / The heavy weight of many a weary day / Not mine, and such as were not made for me.' The -th- spelling is not a mere typographical nostalgia: it preserves the older pronunciation and gives the word a heaviness that plain burden has lost.
  • Primary sense — a physical load: a burthen is something carried on the back or shoulders — a pack, a beam, a cargo — as in the old phrase 'a beast of burthen' (a pack-animal) and the legal-maritime term 'a ship of 400 tons burthen', where burthen means carrying capacity or tonnage.
  • Secondary sense — an obligation or duty: a moral or legal charge one is bound to bear: 'the burthen of office', 'the burthen of proof' (the older form of the legal maxim now usually 'burden of proof').
  • Spiritual / emotional sense: the heavy weight of sin, sorrow, guilt, or care, with a strong scriptural colour: 'Cast thy burthen upon the LORD' (Psalm 55:22, Authorised Version) uses burthen for the Hebrew maśśā' , a weight laid upon someone.
  • Nautical / technical sense: in shipping law and records, burthen denotes a vessel's cargo-carrying capacity or registered tonnage ('a ship of burthen'); this is a precisely quantifiable use with a long documentary history.
  • Poetic / archaic register: the -th- spelling was deliberately revived by the Romantics (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Keats, Shelley) to give the word antiquity and weight; today it survives as a conscious archaism in poetry and liturgical prose.
The relationship between burthen and burden is that of variant spellings of one word, not two different words. Both descend from Old English byrþen; the -th- spelling preserves the older consonant (the dental fricative þ), while burden, the standard modern form, shows the shift of that fricative to the stop -d- in many dialects and, later, in the literary standard. In Wordsworth's line the choice of burthen matters: it slows the rhythm, echoes the Bible, and gives the abstract 'weight' a physical, ceremonial heft that modern burden would not have. Discriminating the family:
  • Load: a neutral physical quantity carried by any means, with no necessary sense of oppression; a lorry-load of bricks is not a burden.
  • Weight: the measurable force of gravity, or metaphorically any deadening pressure; weight is impersonal where burthen is felt by a bearer.
  • Onus: a formal, legalistic obligation or responsibility laid on someone; it lacks the physical and emotional imagery of burthen.
  • Encumbrance: any hindering load, especially financial or legal (an encumbrance on an estate); more technical and detached.
  • Cargo / freight: goods transported commercially; purely commercial, with no bearer who feels the weight.
In Wordsworth's lines the burthen is identified in apposition with 'the heavy weight of many a weary day' — an accumulation of weariness, anxiety, and self-consciousness that the poet did not choose and that was 'not made for' him. The verb that governs it, 'shaken off', casts the load as a physical oppression that can be dropped by a bodily, almost convulsive act of release, and because the archaic spelling echoes the language of the King James Bible and of hymns, the casting-off reads as a kind of spiritual conversion — a return to an authentic, unburdened being.
