Cellars (plural of cellar) are underground rooms or vaults used for storage — classically of wine, food, fuel, or household goods — cool, dark, and subordinate to the main living floor of a building. A cellar is built partly or wholly below ground level, which keeps it cool for provisions, and it is distinguished from a mere basement by its storage purpose: the cellar is where wine is laid down, potatoes and preserves are kept, and coal or fuel is stacked. In Tolkien's The Hobbit, the plural appears in the comic catalogue of Bag End's single-level design: No going upstairs for the hobbit: bedrooms, bathrooms, cellars, pantries (lots of these), wardrobes (he had whole rooms devoted to clothes), kitchens, dining-rooms, all were on the same floor, and indeed on the same passage. The joke is architectural and social: hobbits have built their home into a hillside, so even the underground storage rooms sit on the same floor and passage as the kitchens — no stairs at all — and the plural cellars (more than one, alongside 'lots of' pantries) is part of Tolkien's loving inventory of bourgeois-hobbit abundance, in which even storage multiplies. The tone is cozy-material and comic-cataloguing: the well-stocked undercroft of a prosperous hole rather than any gothic dungeon.

Polysemy — the range of senses. The base noun has several distinct uses:

  • Underground storage room: the core sense — a below-ground vault for food, drink, or fuel, e.g. wine cellar, root cellar, coal cellar.
  • Metonymic the wine cellar = the wine collection: a house may be praised for an excellent cellar, meaning the stock of wines laid down in it — the container standing for the contents, a standard metonymy of the wine trade.
  • Salt cellar: the small table-top container for salt — historically the same word-family, though by a folk-etymological reshaping of Old French saliere 'salt box' (see etymology), and the source of the medieval phrase above/below the salt for rank at table.
  • Storm cellar / root cellar / food cellar: specialised American and rural senses for underground shelters and storage, the storm cellar doubling as a tornado shelter.
  • Sports jargon in the cellar: in baseball and North American sport, a team in the cellar (a cellar-dweller) is in last place — the bottom of the standings as a metaphorical underground, a colourful extension.
  • Gaming / computing usage: in games, a cellar may be a literal dungeon-like map area; in some software, cellar appears archaically for a data vault — loose modern transfers of the underground-storage image.

Semantic boundaries. The bridge among the senses is the single image of a cool, dark, below-ground (or at least earth-cooled) storage volume. From the room itself the word extends to its contents (the wine), to specialised kinds (root, coal, storm, salt), and metaphorically to the bottom of a ranking (the cellar of the league). Distinguish from near-synonyms: a basement is a general understory of a building, not necessarily for storage and often finished as living space; a crypt is ecclesiastical or a burial vault; a vault stresses arched construction, security, or bank storage; a pantry (the next item in Tolkien's list) is a smaller food-and-ware room usually at living level; a larder is specifically for provisions and meat; a storehouse or storeroom need not be underground; a cave is natural, not built; and an undercroft is the architectural term for a vaulted basement room in a church or castle. Cellars keeps the underground, earth-cool storage idea — and in Bag End's case the comic fact that even the storage multiplies on a single level. Preserve the plural: the hobbit has more than one such room, matching the comedy of 'lots of' pantries nearby.
