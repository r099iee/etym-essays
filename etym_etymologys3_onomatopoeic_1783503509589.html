A WORD MADE OF TWO WORDS: THE NAME, ONOMATO- Onomatopoeic is a compound, and its two halves descend from two entirely different Proto-Indo-European roots. The first half, onomato-, continues the PIE root *nō-mn- "name," the same root that gives Greek ónoma (and dialectal ónyma), Latin nōmen, English name, German Name, and Sanskrit nā́man. Because *nō-mn- is among the most securely reconstructed words in the entire Indo-European family, the "name" element of onomatopoeic is on the firmest possible comparative footing: a Greek speaker saying ónoma and an English speaker saying name are continuing, through millennia of regular sound change, the same ancient word. In Greek, the inflected stem of ónoma is onomat-, and it is this combining stem — onomato- "name-" — that supplies the first element of the compound. FROM "NAME" TO "NAME-MAKING": THE GREEK TERM Greek joined onomato- to the verb poieîn "to make" to produce onomatopoiía (ὀνοματοποιΐα), literally "name-making," "the making of names." In classical rhetoric the word had a precise meaning: it named the figure by which a speaker or writer creates a word, most famously by imitating the sound of the thing named. Aristotle discusses the creation of words by imitation in the Poetics, and the later rhetorical handbooks (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, the treatise On the Sublime) use onomatopoiía for the coining of sound-imitative words. From its first use, therefore, the "making of names" was the "making of words that sound like what they name." THE NARROWING FROM NAME-MAKING TO SOUND-IMITATION The semantic history of the Greek term is one of progressive narrowing. Originally onomatopoiía could mean the general creation of words — any coinage, whether imitative or not. But because the most celebrated instances in Greek poetry were sound-imitations (the crash of battle in Homer, the buzz and hum of insects), the term attached itself specifically to acoustic imitation. By the time Latin borrowed it as onomatopoeia in late rhetorical writing, the word already meant "sound-imitative word-formation" and little else. English received the noun in the mid-sixteenth century (first recorded c. 1553) as a technical term of rhetoric and grammar, and it has kept the narrowed sense ever since: onomatopoeia is the formation of a word by imitation of the sound associated with what is named. THE ADJECTIVE AND ITS VARIANTS The adjective onomatopoeic is a later, English-made formation of the nineteenth century, built on the Greek-Latin stem with the English adjectival suffix -ic; alongside it stand the variant spellings onomatopoetic and the older onomatopœic with the ligature. The word-formation mimics the Greek adjectival pattern in -poiikós (the -poeic of onomatopoeic answers to Greek -poiikós "of making"), so that even the adjective's own shape is a small history of Greek composition: onomat(o)- "name" + -poiic "of making." In modern linguistics the noun and adjective remain the standard labels for sound-imitative formation, opposed to the broader and less precise phonaesthesia "sound suggestiveness," and the same "name" element appears in a large family of -onym words — synonym, homonym, antonym, acronym — all sharing the ancient root for "name." THE MAKING HALF: FROM "HEAPING UP" TO "MAKING", THE ROOT *kʷey- AND GREEK poieîn The second half of onomatopoeic, -poeia, continues the Greek verb poieîn "to make, do, compose," whose Proto-Indo-European root is standardly reconstructed as *kʷey- "to heap up, pile up, build, make" (Beekes; older handbooks vary in the laryngeal detail, and some give *kʷey- with a glottal element, but the identification of the Greek verb with the "pile up" root is the received view). The semantic bridge from "heap up" to "make" is natural and is paralleled in many languages: to make a wall or a poem is to build it up, layer upon layer. Greek poieîn is attested from the very beginning of Greek literature — Homer uses it constantly for making, doing, and composing — and from it Greek built one of the most productive word-families in the language: poiētḗs "maker, poet," poiēsis "a making, a poem," poiēma "a thing made, a poem," and the nominal combining form -poiíā/-poia "a making" that appears in hundreds of compounds. THE POET AS MAKER The most famous offspring of poieîn is poiētḗs, "maker" — the Greek word for poet. For the Greeks the poet was pre-eminently a maker: the one who builds verses as a craftsman builds a house or a ship. Latin borrowed the word as poēta, and English poet descends from it in a straight line, carrying poetry and poetic with it. The -poeia of onomatopoeia is the same root, in the same nominal form: onomatopoiía is literally "name-making," and an onomatopoeic word is one that "makes its own name" by sounding like what it denotes. The connection is visible in English spelling: onomatopoeic contains the -poe- of poe-try, and the "making" is as much a poetic act as a linguistic one. THE COMPOUND IN THE ANCIENT WORLD Greek joined onomato- "name" to -poiia "making" to form onomatopoiía, first in philosophical and rhetorical contexts: the Sophists debated whether language is phúsei "by nature" — the view that the first words arose by onomatopoeic imitation of things — or thései "by convention"; the Stoics inherited the debate; and Aristotle's Poetics treats the creation of words by imitation as one of the natural sources of names. In all of this the "making" element is essential: onomatopoeia is not the mere resemblance of sounds but the deliberate making of a word, a name constructed so that it echoes its referent. THE LATIN AND ENGLISH AFTERLIFE Latin took onomatopoeia as a learned loanword in late rhetoric, and English received the noun in the mid-sixteenth century (first recorded c. 1553), with the adjective onomatopoeic emerging in the nineteenth century. The two halves of the word have remained transparent to anyone who knows Greek roots: onomat(o)- "name" and -poeia "making." In modern use the "making" half is still productive — new onomatopoeic words are coined constantly (beep, zap, whir), each one a fresh act of name-making in the ancient sense. And because poieîn is also the root of poet, the word quietly preserves the ancient Greek conviction that to name a thing well is a kind of making, and that the poet, like the onomatopoeic word, makes his name by making his meaning sound.
