- Late 14th c. (c. 1380s) — Middle English philosophical metaphor: coined as thurgh-fare to denote an open, temporary passageway; famously used by Chaucer in The Knight's Tale to describe human mortal life as a transitory passage full of woe.
- 15th–16th c. — Legal & municipal codification: transitioned from abstract transit to physical infrastructure; defined in English common law as a public road open at both ends, giving rise to official warning notices of No Thoroughfare on private dead-end alleys.
- 18th–19th c. — Industrial urban boom & Haussmannization: expanded to describe bustling, crowded metropolitan avenues and commercial arteries in cities like London and Paris, teeming with horse-drawn omnibuses and pedestrian crowds.
- 20th–21st c. — Civil engineering & modern infrastructure: standardized in urban planning as major traffic arterial networks; metaphorically extended to maritime shipping lanes, airline flight corridors, and digital information thoroughfares.
