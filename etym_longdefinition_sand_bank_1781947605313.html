In the opening lines of The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), the sand-bank is the family home itself: the four little rabbits live with their Mother in a sand-bank, underneath the root of a very big fir tree. The word names a natural ridge or slope of sandy earth, and Potter chooses it with care because it does double duty — geological and domestic at once. A sand-bank is soft enough and dry enough for rabbits to burrow into, yet solid enough to hold the form of a warren; the fir-tree root above it gives shelter and concealment. The compound's two elements work together: sand supplies the yielding, diggable substance that makes such a home possible, while bank supplies the raised, sloped shape — a natural embankment — into which a burrow can be driven.

Primary sense. A sand-bank is a deposit of sand forming a shallow area, ridge, or bank — a raised shelf, slope, or mound of sandy material, whether on a riverbed, along a coast, in an estuary, or on an inland hillside. The compound is head-final: a bank of sand, with the first element specifying the material. In natural history and geography it is semi-technical; in rural narrative it keeps a warm, homely sense of a sandy slope of earth.

Polysemy and extended senses:

  • Riverside/coastal landform: a ridge or shoal of sand in a river, estuary, or along the coast, often exposed at low tide and submerged at high water — a navigational hazard to boats.
  • Inland slope: a sandy hillside or embankment, the sense Potter uses for the rabbits' burrow-home; here the word shades into "a raised, sandy part of the ground."
  • Habitat sense: a soft, dry sandy slope in which burrowing animals (rabbits, badgers) dig their homes — the domestic, cozy reading of the tale.
  • Nautical/geographical jargon: "sandbank" in hydrography names submerged or partially submerged sand ridges that ground ships; distinct from the navigable channel.
  • Financial metaphor (rare, modern): "sandbank" is occasionally used metaphorically for a stable, firm reserve or foundation (e.g. a financial reserve as a "sandbank"), though this is not standard economic terminology.
  • Spelling variation: sand-bank (hyphenated, older and literary), sand bank (open), sandbank (closed, modern standard).

Register and collocation. The word belongs to natural history and geography, with a slightly bookish, descriptive air; its two transparent native elements keep it plain and warm in fiction. Collocations: to live in a sand-bank, a burrow in the sand-bank, the side of the sand-bank, a soft sand-bank, a river sand-bank, an estuarine sandbank. It is this warmth that Potter exploits: by calling the rabbits' home a sand-bank rather than a burrow or a warren, she keeps the dwelling a part of the natural landscape, cozy and wild at once, grounded in the very earth beneath the fir-tree's root.
