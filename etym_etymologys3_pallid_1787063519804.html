Pallid is a Latin word that English borrowed in the late 16th century, and its history shows how a root meaning "pale" spread across the Indo-European languages into colour words, disease vocabulary and finally literary criticism.

I. The Indo-European root

Pallid goes back to the Indo-European root *pel- (also written *pel / *pol), meaning "pale, grey, dusky".

  • This root is extraordinarily widespread. In Greek it gave polios "grey, hoary"; in Sanskrit palita- "grey, hoary"; in Germanic it produced Old English fealu, modern English fallow (pale brown/yellow, of land left unploughed).
  • In Latin the root produced the verb pallēre "to be pale, to turn pale, to look wan" — a verb of physical and emotional states, since fear, disease and cold all make the face pale.
  • Two Latin adjectives were built from it: pallidus "pale, wan, greenish-yellow" and pallens "pale" (the present participle). English borrowed both — pallid from pallidus, and the rarer pallent from pallens.

II. The Latin verb and its ancient connotations

In Latin, pallēre had rich associations.

  • It described the paleness of fear: Roman writers speak of soldiers, lovers and criminals pallentes — pale with terror or longing.
  • It described illness; pale skin was a recognised symptom from the Hippocratic physicians onward.
  • It described the paleness of grief and mourning; the adjective pallidus could characterise anyone whose colour had drained away under strong emotion.
  • The verb pallere also had a figurative sense: to be insignificant, to lose one's brilliance — a meaning that resurfaces in modern English "pallid" applied to weak art and writing.

III. The borrowing into English

English took pallid directly from Latin in the 1580s.

  • The Renaissance habit of borrowing Latin adjectives wholesale gave English pallid alongside the older, French-derived pale (from Old French pale, itself from Latin pallidus). The two words are therefore doublets — the same Latin word arriving twice by different routes.
  • From the start, pallid was the more literary and clinical of the two: it named a striking, bloodless whiteness rather than mere lightness of colour.
  • Shakespeare and his contemporaries used it for faces changed by fear and grief, cementing its emotional power in English.

IV. The semantic drift into criticism

The modern story of pallid is its march from skin-colour to a general word for weakness and dullness.

  • In the 18th and 19th centuries the word extended from faces to light (a "pallid sun", a "pallid moon"), to colours (pallid grey, pallid green) and to anything washed-out.
  • By the 19th century, critics used it of art and writing: a "pallid style", a "pallid imitation" — work that lacked force, colour and vitality. This is a clear case of metaphorical extension: the visible paleness of a face becomes a metaphor for the invisible paleness of quality.
  • This critical sense is now common. In modern usage, calling a performance, argument, speech or painting "pallid" is a precise insult: it means bland, feeble, uninspired, bloodless.

V. Modern profile

  • Modern pallid has three co-existing senses: (1) medical/physical paleness of the face; (2) dim, weak light or washed-out colour; (3) figurative feebleness in art, writing or behaviour.
  • The clinical noun pallor (from Latin pallor, "paleness") is its medical twin.
  • The word remains literary and formal; it is the kind of adjective a writer like Tolkien chooses for a weak, colourless, eerie glow — "a little globe of pallid light" — where the paleness of the light itself is eerie and feeble rather than bright.

VI. Summary

From the Indo-European root for "grey, pale", Latin built pallēre and pallidus, a word of fear, illness and grief. English borrowed it in the 1580s as the literary doublet of pale, extended it from faces to light and colour, and in the modern period gave it a pejorative figurative sense of feebleness. Pallid remains, at heart, a word for things that should have life and colour but do not.
