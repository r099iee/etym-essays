Jaded is in its dominant modern sense an adjective for a particular kind of tiredness: not the honest fatigue of muscles after labour but the dulling of appetite itself, the state of someone who has seen, tasted, or enjoyed too much and can no longer be pleased, surprised, or moved. A jaded traveller yawns at cathedrals; a jaded critic shrugs at masterpieces; the palate is intact but the pleasure is gone.

In the source sentence the word measures wonder against exhaustion: whatever has been found will prod even the most jaded of viewers to awe, which means the discovery is so overwhelming that even those dulled by overexposure, the critics who have seen everything, will feel amazement again. Jaded here is the foil of awe: the burnt-out appetite that this one marvel can still rekindle.

Core meaning in detail: The fatigue of jaded is satiety turned sour: overindulgence, repetition, or disillusion has worn the edge off feeling. It shades from the mild, fashionable boredom of the tourist who has photographed too many sunsets to the darker exhaustion of the aid worker or nurse who has witnessed too much suffering and can no longer grieve properly. Psychologists hear in it an early name for what they now call compassion fatigue and burnout: the emotional reserves are spent, and novelty can no longer make a deposit.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Dulled by overindulgence or overexposure (primary modern sense, from 1770s): the jaded palate, the jaded audience, the jaded traveller. The bridge runs from the ridden-out horse to the worn-out appetite: just as the overworked nag no longer answers the spur, the overstimulated mind no longer answers novelty.
  • Cynical and world-weary from experience (figurative extension): a jaded view of politics, jaded lovers who trust no promises. The bridge carries physical tiredness into moral tiredness: disappointment after disappointment rides the spirit the way miles ride a horse.
  • Physically worn out, exhausted (older sense, 1630s, now literary): jaded horses, jaded limbs after a long chase. This was the original adjectival sense, directly from the verb to jade, to ride hard, and it survives in historical novels and hunting memoirs.
  • Made dull or yellowed, said of colours and appetites (transferred sense): critics speak of jaded greens in overworked paintings or jaded appetites at rich banquets. The bridge is visual and gustatory at once: what was fresh has been handled too much and lost its bloom.
  • Sated to the point of distaste (gastronomic and erotic register): the jaded gourmand who pushes away the third course, the jaded senses of the libertine. The bridge is satiety curdling into aversion: enough becomes too much, and pleasure flips into nausea.
  • Horse-worn and worthless, said of nags (root echo, archaic): the jade itself, the broken-down mare of Middle English. Modern readers meet this sense only in etymology notes, but it is the stable from which every other sense bolted.

Register and connotation: jaded is urbane and slightly confessional: to call oneself jaded is to boast a little, to imply one has lived enough to be bored, while to call another jaded is gently to pity them. It belongs to reviews, travel writing, and late-night conversation rather than to clinics, though doctors recognise the condition. Unlike exhausted, which asks for rest, jaded asks for meaning: sleep will not cure it, only some genuine novelty or renewed purpose can.
