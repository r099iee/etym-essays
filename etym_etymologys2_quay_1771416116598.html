*kagh- (Proto-Indo-European root; prehistoric, c. 4000–2500 BCE) — “to catch, to seize, to enclose, to weave” (the ultimate PIE stage is debated; the attested Celtic forms point to a sense of enclosure/fencing, but the precise PIE shape and root meaning are not beyond dispute) → Proto-Celtic *kagyom (reconstructed; Celtic, 1st millennium BCE) — “pen, enclosure, fold” → Gaulish caium / caiō (attested in Classical glosses of Gaulish, Roman period, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — “enclosure, wickerwork barrier” (Gaulish wicker hurdles, filled with earth and stone, became river-bank retaining walls and landing embankments) → Old French cai, quai (attested from the 9th–11th c.) — “sandbank, riverbank, embankment, landing-place, wharf” → Middle French quai (13th–16th c.) — “wharf, quay, embankment” → Anglo-Norman / Middle English key, kaye, caye (borrowed from Old French, attested 14th c. in London shipping documents) — “wharf, landing place”, pronounced “key” → Modern English quay (17th–18th c. respelling of “key” to the French form quai; the pronunciation /kiː/ was retained) — “solid landing-platform for ships” NOTE (debated/nuanced): The PIE stage *kagh- is inferred from the family of “enclosure” words across Celtic (Welsh cae “hedge, field, enclosure”), Germanic (Old English haga “enclosure, hedge”), and Slavic, but the meaning attached to the root at the Proto-Indo-European level is a reconstruction; some scholars prefer to begin the chain at Proto-Celtic *kagyom and mark the ultimate origin as uncertain.
