fortunes (plural noun) — /ˈfɔːtʃuːnz/. (1) The successive conditions, circumstances or "ups and downs" that befall a person, family, institution or people. (2) Wealth or material prosperity, especially great wealth. (3) (Archaic and poetic) the power personified as Fortune, chance itself. In Durant’s sentence — "the precarious fortunes of the chase" — the word means the uncertain turns of luck that govern the hunter’s livelihood: the game may vanish, the hunt may fail, and a people that depends upon such luck can never consolidate civilization.

I. Primary senses in modern use

  • Circumstances, the drift of affairs. Plural fortunes is the standard word for the changing condition of something over time: the fortunes of the company, the fortunes of war, the rise and fall of a family’s fortunes. The pattern the fortunes of X is a fixed collocation of journalism, history and business writing; it is essentially neutral, describing vicissitudes without specifying their cause.
  • Wealth. Fortune (singular) is "great wealth": to make a fortune, a small fortune, an inherited fortune. The plural appears as fortunes meaning "large amounts of money" or "the collective estates of a group" (fortunes were made and lost in the gold rush). The bridge from "luck" to "money" is historical and easy: in a commercial age luck shows itself in money; to have good fortune is to make one’s fortune.
  • Chance personified (archaic). The goddess Fortuna of Rome — blind, wheel-turning, generous and cruel by turns — survived in poetry and philosophy as "Fortune." Set phrases like the wheel of fortune, Fortune favours the bold and a knight of fortune preserve this older world.

II. Why the plural?

  • The plural forms a "state of affairs" noun: like affairs, circumstances, prospects, it names not one event but the whole developing situation — hence his fortunes declined, the nation’s fortunes revived. The singular fortune picks out a single stroke of luck or a single accumulated stock of wealth; the plural tracks a life or history unfolding.
  • This pluralisation is ancient in the family: Latin fortūnae and Middle English fortunes both appear in the sense "one’s lot in life."

III. Senses that shade into one another

  • Fate or destiny. In older prose fortune could mean simply "destiny, the ordained course of life" (the sense in which astrology speaks of one’s fortune in the stars). Modern usage has largely secularised the word: fortune is no longer fate but probability, chance, market, weather.
  • Good or bad luck as it affects undertakings.To have the fortune to do something and by good fortune are common phrasal relics: here fortune means "a piece of luck," countable but usually singular.
  • Foretelling.To tell fortunes (fortune-telling) treats fortunes as the sized-up prospects of a life, read in cards, palms or stars — a live folk sense that keeps the goddess on the street.

IV. Polysemy bridges

One chain explains every sense: chance (what is brought to one by fate, from Latin fors) → outcome (the luck one has) → condition (the resultant course of one’s life: plural fortunes) → prosperity (the good condition, in money) → wealth (the money itself). Each sense is a step from cause to effect to substance; the word never lost any of the steps, so all are still available, chosen by context.

V. Register and collocation notes

  • Fortunes in the "vicissitudes" sense is formal to mid-register: common in historiography, biography, sports writing and business journalism; less at home in speech than luck or money.
  • Fixed pairs: fortunes of war, reversed fortunes, fortunes revived/declined/plummeted/soared, to restore one’s fortunes, make one’s fortune(s), fortunes smiled on X.
  • In the source text the collocation precarious fortunes of the chase welds the two great senses together: the fortunes (luck-driven outcomes) of the hunt are precarious (uncertain), which is precisely why a hunting people cannot bank on a settled surplus.

VI. Distinguish from neighbours

  • Luck is pure chance without the narrative of a life; fortune is luck as it accumulates into a condition. Fate/destiny imply necessity or decree; fortune implies hazard. Wealth/property are material and static; fortunes tracks change over time. (See similarWords for the full contrasts.)
