Note on historical debate and semantic pejorization: The etymology of Latin servus ("slave") remains a classic problem in historical linguistics. Ancient Roman grammarians derived it by popular etymology from servāre ("to save, preserve"), claiming slaves were war captives whose lives were spared rather than slain. Modern philologists generally reject this folk etymology, linking servus either to PIE *ser- ("to protect, watch over", where a slave was originally an overseer or herd-keeper) or treating it as an ancient Etruscan loanword into early Italic. In English, subservient underwent profound semantic pejorization: when introduced in the early 17th century by Francis Bacon and contemporary scholars, it had an entirely neutral or positive philosophical sense ("subordinate or instrumental in promoting an end"). Only during the 18th century did the word absorb the negative moral connotations of servile, coming to denote spineless, humiliating personal submission and groveling obsequiousness.
