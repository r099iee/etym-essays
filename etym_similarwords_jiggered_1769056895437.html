• dumbfounded: Similar because it is struck speechless with astonishment, and is a close synonym of the exclamatory sense; differs because dumbfounded is the plain, modern word for being rendered mute with surprise, while jiggered is the playful, dated euphemism of an earlier age that lets out the shock without swearing.
  • gobsmacked: Similar because it expresses shocked disbelief, and is the vivid British idiom of the same family of astonishment; differs because gobsmacked is modern slang (as if smacked in the mouth, recorded from the 1930s), while jiggered is the genteel, half-jocular oath that keeps its nonsense gentleness and its Edwardian date.
  • astounded: Similar because it means overcome with amazement; differs because astounded is the formal, unmarked word for overwhelming surprise with no emotional colouring of amusement or politeness, while jiggered is colloquial, self-mocking, and slightly comic.
  • flabbergasted: Similar because it means utterly astonished, and shares jiggered's playful, almost comical register; differs because flabbergasted is a nonsense word of 18th-century origin whose whole force is comical bafflement, while jiggered keeps a mild oaths flavour and never loses its historical euphemism.
  • staggered: Similar because it means overwhelmed by surprise, with the image of being knocked off balance; differs because staggered is mainstream and literal in its metaphor (to stagger is to reel), while jiggered is slangy and secondary, built on the obsolete "to jolt, to shake" verb rather than on a current physical verb.
  • stunned: Similar because it means rendered senseless or incapable of reaction by shock; differs because stunned is plain and can be physical (a blow stuns), while jiggered is purely social and verbal—a gentleman's polite exclamation, never a medical state.
  • blowed: Similar because it appears in the identical self-directed oath formula, "I'll be blowed!"; differs because blowed is coarser and lower in register, closer to the profanity it replaces, while jiggered is the gentlest, almost whimsical member of the minced-oath set.
  • blessed: Similar because "I'll be blessed!" is the same self-directed oath of astonishment; differs because blessed carries pious, churchy colouring and is used by speakers who want a religious resonance, while jiggered is pure nonsense—no piety, no profanity, just surprise.
  • knackered: Similar because it is a chiefly British colloquialism of roughly the same era and register; differs because knackered means exhausted or broken—the "all jiggered up" sense of damaged beyond repair—rather than astonished, so it covers the damaged meaning of jiggered, not the exclamatory one.
  • buggered: Similar because jiggered is widely held to be its minced form, and both can mean "broken, ruined" or "confounded"; differs because buggered is the strong, profane original that the euphemism softens, and it carries coarse, offensive force that jiggered entirely lacks.
  • tipsy: Similar because it describes the mild drunkenness that the American sense of jiggered names; differs because tipsy is the standard, gentle word for slight intoxication, while jiggered in this sense is rare, dated, and derived from the bartender's jigger measure rather than from any general word for drink.
  • sozzled: Similar because it is a colloquial word for being drunk, in the same jocular register; differs because sozzled means thoroughly drunk (soaked through with drink) and is not genteel at all, while jiggered means only mildly intoxicated and keeps its prim, euphemistic air.
