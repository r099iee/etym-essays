The biography of "teamsters" begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *deuk-, "to lead, draw, pull," one of the great roots of motion and command. In the Italic world it became Latin dūcere, "to lead," and from that single verb Europe drew an empire of words: the dux, the leader, who became the duke; the ductus, the leading, which became the duct and the aqueduct; the leading-out that is education; the leading-forward of produce and the leading-back of reduce; the leading-aside of seduce; and the leading-into of introduce. In the Celtic world the root led on in Middle Welsh dygu, "to bring, lead." But the line that matters for "teamster" runs through Germanic.

The Germanic line. Proto-Germanic *teuhaną, "to draw, pull," became Old English tēon, "to draw," whose cognates run through Gothic tiuhan, "to lead," Old High German ziohan, and modern German ziehen, "to pull." The Germanic world then formed from the same root a noun, *tauh-maz, "a pulling" — and from that noun comes the word's true ancestor, Old English tēam. It is a striking fact of sound history that English "team" and Latin "duct" descend from the same PIE root: the same pulling that drew a Latin aqua through a ductus drew an Old English plough across a field.

Old English tēam: the double meaning. Old English tēam is a word of two meanings, and both grow out of the root's "drawing."

  • Family and lineage. On one side it meant "family, lineage, progeny" — the children who are "brought forth," drawn out of the parents; the word is kin to "teem" in the sense of producing abundantly.
  • The yoked animals. On the other side it meant "a set of draft animals yoked together" — the oxen or horses that draw the plough or wagon as one pulling unit.

Into modern English. Middle English carried the word as teme, and by the time of Shakespeare "team" was established in the harnessed, yoking sense — a team of horses drawing a coach — the sense from which the modern sporting "team" would later grow, when men themselves were yoked together in pursuit of a common pull. The agent suffix -ster, which English inherited from Old English -estre (originally a feminine agent ending — spinster, songster), was generalized in the later Middle Ages to masculine agents — gamester, punster, trickster — and on "team" it produced "teamster," recorded from 1777 for one who drives a team. The plural "teamsters" in the passage names the class of men who managed animal power on the American roads.

The American career. The word's modern fortune is the story of its American career.

  • The nineteenth-century frontier. The teamster was the linchpin of overland freight — the man of the six-mule team and the great wagon, whose long moustaches and wide-brimmed, low-crowned hat marked the trade as unmistakably as a uniform. His was the class that moved the gold rush, the Texas freighting roads, and the westward expansion.
  • 1903. The drivers organized themselves into the International Brotherhood of Teamsters, and the word followed the profession from the animal to the engine.
  • Contemporary. Today a "teamster" is commonly a truck driver, a union man, and the animal origins of the name survive only in the word's own etymology and in the union's horseshoe emblem.

McCarthy's "after the fashion of teamsters" looks back to the mid-century moment when the teamster's look was the sign of the road itself — long moustaches, widebrim hat, low round crown — and the plural name places the stranger in that vanished brotherhood of men who drew the freight of the republic behind them, exactly as the PIE root *deuk- said they always had.
