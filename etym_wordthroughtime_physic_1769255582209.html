- The one word of nature (1300s–1500s) — 'physic' enters Middle English as fisyk, at first one undivided word meaning the knowledge of nature: it names natural philosophy in general and, by a natural narrowing, the medical art in particular, since medicine was the branch of natural knowledge most concerned with the body.
- The healing art — Through the 1500s–1600s 'physic' settles on medicine as its ordinary sense: a 'doctor of physic' is a learned physician, and the word carries the dignity of a learned art rather than a modern clinical profession.
- The bitter dose — The phrase 'to take one's physic' slides the word down from the art to the single dose of medicine — often, in the old pharmacopoeia, a purgative — so that 'physic' briefly gains a comic, everyday sense of a laxative draught.
- The great split (18th century) — 'physics' is re-borrowed from Latin physica to claim the 'science of nature' sense, and the two words — once one — become different words; 'physic' is stranded with only its medical senses, and even these begin to be taken over by 'medicine.'
- The archaic relic — Over the 1800s–1900s 'physic' vanishes from ordinary speech, surviving only in print and period vocabulary; its deliberately archaic ring is exactly what C. S. Lewis counts on when he lists 'Law, Physic, Alchemy, and Astronomy' in Caspian's princely education.
