- Late 18th Century (Augustan & Georgian Eras, c. 1770–1790) - First coined in British English as a learned neoclassical agent noun combining Greek *pyro-* (fire) and *techne* (art/craft) with *-ist*; used to describe authors of scientific treatises on artificial fireworks and gunpowder chemistry.
- Early to Mid-19th Century (Romantic & Regency Eras) - Formalized as a prestigious court and municipal appointment; the "Royal Pyrotechnist" designed monumental fireworks over London and Paris celebrating royal coronations and peace treaties.
- Late 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Celebrated in Victorian literature and satire (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*), contrasting the craftsman's technical skill with the absurd, pompous vanity of the personified fireworks; developed metaphorical senses for flashy, dazzling literary stylists.
- 20th Century (Modern Industrial Era) - In regulatory law and entertainment production, gradually gave ground to *pyrotechnician* for commercial licensing, while *pyrotechnist* was preserved in historical treatises, prestigious guild titles, and fine literature.
- 21st Century (Contemporary Usage) - Retained as a distinguished, evocative title for master fireworks artists and grand international display choreographers, as well as a metaphor for virtuoso intellectual brilliance.
