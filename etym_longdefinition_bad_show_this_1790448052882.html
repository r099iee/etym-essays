Bad show this is Bertie Wooster's Edwardian public-school idiom for this is bad behaviour, bad luck, a poor business — an exclamation of sympathetic disapproval. When Bertie says Bad show this, my dear old flesh and blood to Aunt Dahlia, he means what a wretched business, I am sorry, you have been having a sticky time. It is not a literal judgement of a theatrical performance but a transferred use of the schoolboy's good show / bad show for conduct on the field: to play the game well is a good show, to let the side down is a bad show. The trailing this is deictic and commiserating — this business before us — softening censure into pity.

Grammatically the phrase is an elliptical exclamation with the copula omitted: (It is a) bad show, this. Bertie's word order Bad show this fronts the verdict for emphasis, a pattern common in school slang (Good egg that, bad egg he). It functions pragmatically as condolence, apology, and gentle moral verdict at once, which is why Bertie follows it immediately with I'm afraid you've been having a sticky time.

  • Schoolboy conduct verdict (origin sense): on the playing field or in the house, a boy's action is a good show (sporting, plucky, loyal) or a bad show (cowardly, selfish, caddish). E.g. bad show to funk the jump, good show to own up. The bridge to theatre is that life is figured as a performance judged by spectators; Wodehouse, a Dulwich schoolboy, keeps the metaphor alive into adulthood.
  • Adult sympathy formula (Wodehouse extended sense): bad show for any misfortune or misconduct viewed with pity rather than anger; e.g. bad show about the prize-giving mix-up, bad show losing the income. Here disapproval of the situation replaces disapproval of the person: Bertie does not blame Dahlia, he laments her sticky time.
  • Bad form / bad egg kinship: Edwardians distinguish bad show (the act or event) from bad form (the manners) and bad egg / bad hat / bad lot (the person). To say bad show this carefully avoids calling Dahlia herself bad; it condemns the turn of events while embracing flesh and blood.
  • Theatrical irony: because show literally means a staged entertainment, bad show always carries a flicker of the footlights — as if misfortune were a flop requiring kindly applause. Wodehouse exploits this throughout: catastrophes are shows to be endured with a smile.
  • Modern survival: good show, bad show, jolly good show persist in British English as consciously old-fashioned, hearty commendations, often ironic. To say bad show today marks the speaker as playful, nostalgic, or deliberately Wodehousian.

To use Bad show this correctly, feel both its school-judge sternness and its Bertie warmth: it points the finger at fate while putting an arm round the victim. That double gesture — verdict plus sympathy — is why it opens Bertie's consolation of his dear old flesh and blood rather than any blunter how awful.
