Foraged is the past tense and past participle of the verb forage: to range over an area in search of food, provisions, or other needed supplies, and to gather what is found. The word covers everything from a bear digging for roots to a lost hiker surviving on wild berries, and from an army feeding itself off the countryside to a chef serving wild mushrooms. The participle form carries completed action — the searching is finished, the gathering done — and it is used both as a verb (they foraged for berries) and as a premodifying adjective (foraged greens, foraged ramps), where it has acquired a strong cultural flavour in modern food writing.

Polysemy and sense structure:
  • Military-logistical sense (the historical core): troops or parties foraged the countryside for grain, livestock, and especially horse feed. In medieval and early modern warfare armies lived off the land, and a "foraging party" was a formal detachment sent out to strip an area of provisions. This sense shades into requisition, levying, and sometimes outright plunder; historical writing preserves it in phrases like foraged provisions and foraged from the surrounding villages.
  • Ethological sense (animals): any animal that searches widely for food can be said to forage — birds foraging in leaf litter, bears foraging along a river, ants foraging for sugar. In biology "foraging behaviour" is a standard technical term for the search strategies animals use to find food, balanced against the energy cost of searching.
  • Survival and disaster sense: humans stranded in the wilderness, shipwreck survivors, or people cut off by catastrophe forage for whatever is edible or useful. The tone here is desperate and resourceful — foraging as emergency supply-gathering rather than leisure. This is exactly the sense of the example card, where hikers survive on wild berries they foraged along the trail.
  • Culinary and food-culture sense (the modern prestige extension): since the late twentieth century, foraged as a premodifier (foraged mushrooms, foraged herbs, foraged garlic) signals ingredients gathered wild rather than cultivated — local, seasonal, ecological, and fashionable. High-end restaurants advertise foraged produce; the word has moved from pillage to gastronomy.
  • Figurative and technical extensions: to forage for information, forage through archives, or forage for clients; computer science and economics use "foraging" (e.g. distributed search by agents, information-foraging theory) as a model of search behaviour. In all of these the core meaning — ranging widely to find what one needs — remains intact.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridges: every secondary sense grows out of the root image of ranging over a territory in search of sustenance. The military sense supplied the verb's first English life; the animal sense reanalysed that ranging as biology; the survival sense kept the desperation that military foraging always carried; and the culinary sense flipped the word's prestige, rebranding wildness as virtue. The participle's premodifying use (foraged greens) is a recent grammatical development, made possible once the foodie sense made foraged an attractive label.

Register and collocations: foraged sits between formal and neutral. Collocations include foraged for, foraged through, foraged berries, foraged greens, foraged food, foraging party (historical), foraging behaviour (science), went foraging, foraged supplies. The emotional register of the participle now splits: gritty in survival contexts, artisanal and positive in culinary ones.

Boundary work among near-synonyms:
  • Scavenged implies taking discarded remains, carrion, or refuse — lower prestige, waste-stream associations — whereas foraged more readily suggests wild natural produce or systematic supply-seeking (though ethology uses both words for animals).
  • Rummaged means searching through enclosed clutter (drawers, bags, rooms), not ranging over open terrain; you rummage a drawer but forage a forest.
  • Gleaned historically means gathering leftover grain after the harvest — narrower, agrarian, with a legal-historical frame — and metaphorically collecting residual information; foraging is broader wild or opportunistic search.
  • Ransacked implies a violent, disorderly search, often for valuables; foraging can include coercive military taking but equally covers peaceful wild gathering and does not inherently mean wrecking a place.
  • Harvested normally assumes cultivated crops or a planned yield; foraged implies non-cultivated, ad hoc environmental finding.
  • Gathered and collected are neutral and general, without the ranging-over-territory image or the survival and prestige associations.
  • Wildcrafted is the recent specialist term for harvesting wild plants, overlapping the culinary sense; foraged is broader and older.
Morphology: foraged is the regular weak past tense and past participle (forage + -ed), pronounced /ˈfɒr.ɪdʒd/; it is used with auxiliaries in perfect constructions (had foraged, has foraged) and in the passive (was foraged by the army).
