DERIVATION CHAIN FOR "INLAY" — TWO ROOTS (compound word):

ROOT 1 — the prefix "in":
Stage 1 — PIE root: *en "in, inside" (reconstructed preposition of being within; one of the most stable shapes in the family, surviving nearly unchanged in Greek en, Latin in, Germanic in).
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic: *in "in" (inherited from *en).
Stage 3 — Old English: in "in" (preposition; also the noun inn "dwelling, lodging," from the same base).
Stage 4 — English: in, used as the first element of the compound "inlay."

ROOT 2 — the base verb "lay":
Stage 1 — PIE root: *legh- "to lie (down), to lay, to be at rest" (reconstructed; cf. Greek lékhos "bed, couch," Latin lectus "bed," "that on which one lies").
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic: *ligjan "to lie" and *lagjan "to lay" — the causative pair, "to make (something) lie" (cf. Gothic ligan / lagjan).
Stage 3 — Old English: licgan "to lie" and lecgan "to lay" — the inherited pair.
Stage 4 — Middle English: lien "to lie" and leien "to lay" — the medieval forms.
Stage 5 — English: lay "to cause to lie, to set down," the direct reflex of lecgan; beside it English "lie" from licgan.

THE COMPOUND:
Stage 6 — English verb: to inlay (16th century) — in + lay, literally "to cause (something) to lie in (something else)," to set one material flush into the surface of another; the past and participle form is "inlaid."
Stage 7 — English noun: inlay (19th century) "the inserted material; the technique of setting one material flush into another."
Stage 8 — English: INLAY.

NOTES ON COGNATES AND DEBATED POINTS (explicit):
The PIE root *legh- has clear reflexes in Greek lékhos, Latin lectus (whence French lit, Italian letto), and the Germanic pair; it has no secure Indo-Iranian reflex, and the attachment of Sanskrit lag- "to cling" to this root in earlier condensed treatments is rejected by modern handbooks. The two elements of the compound are both native Germanic in form, so the word is transparently "in" + "lay" with no Latin intermediary in the compound itself. The spelling "inlay" has been stable since the 16th century.
