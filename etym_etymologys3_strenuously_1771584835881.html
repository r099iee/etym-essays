The story begins at a horizon of Proto-Indo-European firmness. The reconstructed root *ster-, “stiff, rigid, hard”, is the ancestor of a broad family in which the notion of unbending rigidity was lexicalised in almost every branch of the family: Greek stereós (στερεός) and sterrós (στερρός) “hard, solid, stiff” (whence English stereo-, stereometry, stereogram in scientific compounds), Sanskrit sthirá- (स्थिर) “firm, steady, fixed” (giving Hindi sthir, “still, stationary”), and the Germanic words for staring and stiffness—Old English starian “to look fixedly” (whence stare), Old English stearc “stiff, severe, stark” (whence stark), German starr “rigid, stiff” and starren “to stare fixedly”. Some scholars group Old English styrne “severe, stern, strict” (whence stern) under the same *ster- family, though its origin is debated. What matters for our word is the semantic template: the root’s core was not speed or effort but stiffness—a body or a will that does not bend. From this root Greek also produced strēnēs (στρηνής), “harsh, rough, intense”, which most etymologists treat as the closest kin—possibly the actual model—of Latin strēnuus, though both de Vaan and Beekes register the chain as uncertain, and Beekes even floats a Pre-Greek origin for strēnēs. Latin strēnuus itself means “active, brisk, vigorous, prompt”—a soldier’s adjective, at home in Plautus and Cicero—and the leap from “stiff” to “energetic” is the key event of the biography. Stiffness of sinew becomes stiffness of purpose: a man who is strēnuus applies himself without slackening, straining against resistance the way a rigid beam resists bending. The Latin adverb strēnuē “vigorously” carried the same value, and it is this adverb that supplies the -ly shape of our word. Early Modern English borrowed the adjective directly from Latin in the 1590s as strenuous, “vigorous, active, swift”, and within a generation or two the adverbial form strenuously was in use, built with the native suffix -ly (c. 1650). No Romance intermediary was involved—the word travelled straight from Roman Latin into Tudor English—and that direct path is visible in the word’s perpetually bookish, Latinate register: strenuously is the adverb of formal address and solemn objection, not of gossip or everyday chat. In English the word then split into its two modern faces. The first is physical: strenuous exercise, strenuous effort, the strain of the body under load. The second is quasi-moral: strenuously denied, strenuously resisted, strenuously opposed—the emphatic, formal adverb of objection and refusal, which is exactly the sense at work in The Story of the Stone, where Dai-yu’s “strenuous” resistance of the seat of honour is a performance of good breeding as much as an expenditure of will. Semantically, the stiff core explains the word’s peculiar blend of vigour and refusal: the strenuous resister is unbending, does not slacken, pushes against the structure of rank and etiquette without ever breaking it. Sound, spelling, and register:
  • Sound change: PIE *ster- (with s-mobile and preserved -r-) → Greek strēnēs (the -ēn- element unexplained); → Latin strēnuus (the -nu- element unexplained, hence the scholarly uncertainty); Latin strēnuus → (loan into English) strenuous (1590s); strenuous + -ly → strenuously (c. 1650).
  • Semantic drift: “stiff, unbending” → “brisk, vigorous, active, prompt” (Latin, a soldier’s virtue) → “energetic, arduous” (early English praise) → “requiring great effort; done with determined exertion” (modern), with a special emphatic register in formal denial and resistance.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the Roman military and civic ideal of promptness and vigour; the English Protestant and imperial valuation of hardihood in the 17th–19th centuries; the modern legal and journalistic formula “strenuously denied” as a dignified, forceful denial.
  • Literary attestations: Plautus and Cicero for strēnuus; Early Modern English prose for strenuous; Hawkes’s The Story of the Stone for strenuously resisted; and modern public discourse for the fixed phrase “strenuous objection”.
  • Anti-merge guard: Latin sternere “to spread out” belongs to PIE *sterh₃- “to strew, spread” (English strew, stratum, Latin strāmen) and is not related to strēnuus; the two *ster- roots must be kept separate.
