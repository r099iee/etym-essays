Strand One: the folding root *plek-. The real ancestor of the modern word is the Indo-European root *plek- "to plait, weave, fold" — one of the great craft-roots of the family, with cognates in Greek plékō "I plait, weave" (whence English "complex" and "plexus" via Latin), Latin plectere "to plait, weave" and plicāre "to fold", the Slavic verbs for braiding (Russian plestí), and probably English "flax" (the plaited fibre plant). Latin plicāre "to fold" is a workaday verb of the classical period, and from it Romance built a rich family of folding words: French plier "to fold, bend" (English "ply", "pliant", "apply" — "to fold onto"), déplier, and the noun pli "a fold, crease".

From "fold" to "condition". In Old French the past participle of pleier "to fold" was pleit (later pli), and the participle used as a noun meant "a fold" — and then, by a natural figurative step, "the way a thing lies, the arrangement, the condition": a person's state is the way his affairs are folded together. Anglo-French carried the word into England as plit/pleit, and Middle English adopted it around 1200 as plit, meaning simply "condition, state" — at first entirely neutral. Fourteenth- and fifteenth-century English can speak of a man "in good plit" or "in evil plit": the word is a blank slate, coloured only by its adjective.

Strand Two: the native pliht. Meanwhile English had its own, unrelated word: Old English pliht, from Proto-Germanic *plehti-, meaning "danger, peril, risk" on one side and "pledge, obligation, duty" on the other — the same dual sense visible in its close relatives: Old High German pfliht, modern German Pflicht "duty", Middle Dutch plicht "duty, obligation". (The deeper Indo-European ancestry of *plehti- is uncertain and debated: some scholars look to a root meaning "to be engaged, bound", others see a distant link to the folding root itself — an obligation as a "bond" — but no reconstruction is secure, and honest dictionaries mark the origin as uncertain.) In Old English both senses are attested: a man stands "on plihte" (in peril) or gives "pliht" (a pledge).

The collision of the 16th century. For three centuries the two words coexisted: French plit ("condition") and native pliht ("peril, pledge"). Then, around 1550, the spelling of the French word began to appear as plight, crossed with the native word — and with the spelling came the meaning. The neutral "condition" word absorbed the dark colouring of the peril-word: a "plight" ceased to be any state and became a bad state. The stock phrase "in a sorry plight" crystallized, and by the 17th century the word's polarity was fixed: "plight" = miserable condition. The pledge-sense of the native word survived separately — most famously in the marriage service of the Book of Common Prayer (1549), where the bridegroom vows "and thereto I plight thee my troth" — and that archaic verb remains in the language as a fossil of the other, Germanic word.

Modern life.

  • 18th century: dictionaries record both the neutral "condition" sense and the darker "peril"-coloured sense; English usage steadily prefers the dark one.
  • 19th century: the word gains its modern humanitarian register — Victorian social writing is full of "the plight of the poor" and "the plight of the working classes", and reformers call on readers to relieve it.
  • 20th–21st centuries: "plight" becomes a staple of journalism, aid work, and politics — "the plight of refugees", "the plight of endangered peoples" — always with the sense of a suffering group caught in circumstances beyond its control; the neutral "state" sense is gone from living speech, and the "pledge" homograph survives only in archaism and legal history.

Phonetic and structural notes. Latin plicāre → Gallo-Romance → Old French pleier, participle pleit → Anglo-French plit → Middle English plit (c. 1200) → spelling plight (c. 1550). The -gh- is purely graphic — borrowed from the native word — and is silent in pronunciation; the modern pronunciation /plaɪt/ continues the Middle English vowel of plit. Key attestations in brief: Latin plicāre; Old French pleit; Middle English plit (c. 1200); Old English pliht (pre-1100); German Pflicht; spelling "plight" (c. 1550); Book of Common Prayer (1549), "I plight thee my troth"; Victorian social writing; Tolkien 1937 ("cheered them greatly in their plight"). The word that began as a fold in cloth now names the deepest kind of trouble: the modern plight is a condition folded shut around you — the peril-word's shadow falling across the state-word, as it has since 1550.
