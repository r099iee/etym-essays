Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *yek- "to speak, utter; play, jest" (reconstructed; the derivation of Latin iocus from *yek- is widely accepted but not beyond doubt — a debated stage). Stage 2 — Latin iocus (later jocus) "joke, jest, sport, game" (attested from the 2nd century BCE; Plautus and Catullus use the word freely). Stage 3 — Latin iucundus / jucundus "pleasant, delightful, agreeable" (attested in classical Latin; traditionally connected with iuvare "to help, please," but colored in both form and sense by iocus — the relationship between iucundus and iocus is debated among Latinists, and this is a genuinely nuanced stage). Stage 4 — Italian giocondo "cheerful, merry, jovial" (the regular Romance development of Latin iucundus/jucundus; attested in medieval and Renaissance Italian). Stage 5 — Italian Gioconda, the feminine form of the surname Giocondo, the family name of Francesco del Giocondo, Florentine silk merchant (the family is attested in 15th–16th-century Florence). Stage 6 — La Gioconda, the Italian title of Leonardo da Vinci's portrait of Lisa Gherardini del Giocondo (painted c. 1503–1506): literally "the Giocondo woman." Stage 7 — English GIOCONDA, the art-historical and literary name of the painting, fixed in the English literary imagination by Walter Pater's celebrated 1869 passage (published in The Renaissance, 1873). Final node: GIOCONDA. Notes on debate and nuance: - The iucundus/iocus connection is historically layered: iucundus is older and probably unrelated in origin (linked to iuvare "to help, please"), but the resemblance to iocus ("joke, jest") influenced its form and meaning, giving "pleasant, delightful" a playful coloring. - Do not confuse the cheerful surname root with a separate "smile" etymon: the portrait's fame for the smile is cultural, not morphological. - English jocund, jocular, jocose, and joke all descend from this same Latin cluster (iucundus / iocus) by various channels; Gioconda is the onomastic terminus of the family. - The regular Latin-to-Italian sound development of iucundus > giocondo shows the expected shift of the velar (c > g) and the Italian rendering of the Latin -uc- vocalism.
