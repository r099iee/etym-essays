The biography of coronet begins not at a coronation but on a curved battlefield. The ultimate source is the Proto-Indo-European root *sker-, "to turn, bend, curve," the same root that gives the curve of a bow, the bend of a knee, the ring of a circle, and the English words "curve," "circus," "circle," and "ring." In Greek this root flowered into the adjective κορωνός (korōnós) "curved," and from it came κορώνη (korṓnē), literally "the curved thing": the bend of a bow, the tip of a horn, the arch of a doorway — and, by a natural extension, the circular garland woven of bent branches, leaves, and flowers that Greeks hung on doorposts, gave to athletes, and laid on the heads of victors and the honoured dead. The leap from "curved branch" to "wreath of honour" is the semantic engine of the whole word-family: the wreath is the circle that marks achievement, and every later meaning of the family — garland, crown, coronet, corona, corolla — is a development of that single image of the encircling ring. From Greek wreath to Roman crown. Latin borrowed κορώνη as corōna "garland, wreath, crown" (by the 2nd century BCE), and the meaning immediately expanded and imperialised. Where the Greek wreath was the honour of gods, athletes, and symposiasts, the Roman corōna became a graded system of military decoration — the grass crown (corona graminea) for a general who saved an army, the civic crown (corona civica) of oak for a citizen who saved a comrade, the naval and mural crowns for sailors and wall-breakers — and then the literal golden crown of emperors, and finally the abstract symbol of sovereignty itself. The word travelled with the Empire: the Romance descendants (French couronne, Italian corona, Spanish corona) all descend from the Latin form. Christianity then reseeded the word across Europe: the crown became the diadem of Christ, the reward of the martyrs and saints, and by the Middle Ages every crowned head of Christendom ruled through the corōna. The medieval diminutive does its quiet work. It was at this medieval point that the coronet was created as a distinct object. Latin built a diminutive, coronella "a small garland," and Old French formed coronete (diminutive of corone) "a small crown." The diminutive carried a meaning the base word never had — a crown, but not THE crown: the deliberately lesser, rank-coded crown of the nobility below the king. The morphology did the sociology: as kingship absolutised itself, the full crown became unique to the monarch, and the aristocratic orders needed their own graded insignia, so the "little crown" was scaled to each peerage rank. The word entered English as "coronet" around 1400, travelled from French courts into English heraldry and statute, and over the following centuries hardened into the strict, quasi-legal protocol of leaves and pearls that still governs British peerage regalia — ducal strawberry leaves, marquesses' leaves and pearls, earls' leaves on tall spikes, viscounts' and barons' rows of pearls — a crown that tells your exact rank at a glance. Sound and spelling. The word's phonetic journey is a chain of regular borrowings rather than native sound change: Greek κ- became Latin c- (both /k/), the Latin ō opened and diphthongised in Old French (corōna → corone → couronne), and Middle English took the diminutive form complete with the French diminutive suffix -et. The English pronunciation settled as /ˈkɒrənət/ (plural /ˈkɒrənəts/), the -et now a silent little crown of its own, pronounced as /ət/ rather than the French /ɛ/. Literary and cultural life. In English literature the coronet is a word of pageantry and pathos. It appears in the vocabulary of Elizabethan and Shakespearean drama, where it names the contested insignia of dukes and princes; in heraldry manuals and peerage debates it is a technical term; and in historical fiction it is the badge of a noble world. It is precisely the word's ceremonial gravity that C.S. Lewis exploits: set the plural "coronets" in a "bitterly cold" chamber of "forsaken and long ago" treasure, and the same word becomes an instrument of elegy — crowns whose hierarchical code no one now remembers how to read, reduced to objects alongside "tusks of ivory" and stones "piled anyhow." Semantic drift in summary.
  • PIE *sker- "to turn, bend, curve" → Greek κορώνη "the curved thing; a garland" — a circular object made of bent branches.
  • Latin corōna "garland, wreath, crown" — the garland becomes imperial, military, and sacred insignia.
  • Late Latin coronella / Old French coronete "a small crown" — the diminutive creates the graded lesser crown of the nobility.
  • Middle English coronet (c. 1400) → English coronet — the heraldic, rank-coded small crown of the peerage.
  • Modern extensions — "coronet band" in equine anatomy (the rim that crowns a horse's hoof), metonymy for aristocratic rank ("to succeed to the coronet"), and the corona/corolla family in science and botany.
The word carries its whole derivation inside it — from bent garland, to imperial symbol, to rank-badge, to dust-covered relic — and the Narnian sentence asks us to feel every layer at once. The coronet, the small crown that once told the court exactly who everyone was, is now itself unidentified: treasure without a living heraldry to decode it. That is the quiet sorrow Lewis packs into a single plural noun.
