• Anglo-Norman: The precise linguistic term for the variety used in England after 1066; effectively synonymous with “Norman-French” in its English sense, but it is the preferred scholarly label and avoids confusion with the continental dialect.
  • Old Norman: The medieval dialect of the duchy itself; similar as the parent variety, but it refers to the continental speech, not to the colonial language of England.
  • Norman: The adjective and the people; similar as the root, but it names a population or a dialect family rather than the French language as such.
  • Law French: The fossilised legal register descended from Anglo-Norman; similar in origin and vocabulary, but it is a professional jargon, not a living vernacular.
  • Old French: The umbrella language of northern France; similar as the ancestor, but it is broader and includes Picard, Champenois and Francien, of which Norman is one dialect.
  • Francien: The dialect of the Île-de-France that became standard French; similar as a langue d’oïl, but it is the rival and eventual winner, and its forms often differ sharply from Norman (for example chanter against canter).
  • Langue d’oïl: The northern French dialect group; similar as the family to which Norman belongs, but it also includes Picard, Walloon and Francien, and it is defined by the word for “yes” (oïl).
  • Jèrriais: The Norman dialect of Jersey; similar as a descendant of Norman, but it is an insular modern variety, not the medieval administrative language.
  • Guernésiais: The Norman dialect of Guernsey; similar as an insular survival, but it has its own literary tradition and differs from Jèrriais in pronunciation and spelling.
  • Picard: The neighbouring northern dialect; similar in retaining /k/ before /a/ and in sharing many features with Norman, but it is a separate langue d’oïl with its own literature.
  • Parisian French: The standard language of the Île-de-France; similar as French, but it is the prestige norm against which Norman-French was measured, and Chaucer’s Prioress is mocked for not knowing it.
  • Norman patois: A loose, often dismissive label for the modern rural dialects; similar in reference to the surviving varieties, but it carries a pejorative connotation that “Norman” or “Jèrriais” does not.
