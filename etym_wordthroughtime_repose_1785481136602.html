- [Middle English arrival (c. 1400)] - From Old French reposer, the verb reposen meant simply "to rest," and the noun repos "rest, stillness" entered English as a plain word for halting activity and laying a burden down.
- [Extension to the mind (16th c.)] - Repose widened from physical rest to inner calm — "tranquillity of mind" — and took on the dignified, euphemistic "final repose" for the dead, resting that suggests gravity and ceremony.
- [Ennobled register of stillness] - The word settled into its distinctive colour: a calm, deliberate, dignified rest of a body or object — stillness with poise, almost sculptural immobility — rather than the plain everyday stopping that rest names.
- [Modern register] - Today repose carries an elevated, literary feel; its quietest sense (the undisturbed resting of a thing no one troubles) can even edge toward irony — peace that is also neglect, as of books gathering dust on a shelf.
