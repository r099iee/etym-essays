STRATAGEMS is the plural of stratagem: a cunning plan, scheme, or trick, especially one devised to deceive or outwit an enemy. In the passage from The Hobbit, the dwarves trade tales of dragon-slayings and the "different arts devices and stratagems" by which such feats were accomplished — the word gathers all the clever tricks, feints, and deceptive plans a dragon-slayer might employ. It is a word that smells of war and chess, of generals and trickery, and it has kept that flavour for over five hundred years of English.

1. The military sense — the core meaning. In its primary and oldest English sense, a stratagem is an artifice or device of war: a trick, feint, ambush, or deception designed to deceive an enemy and win an advantage without depending on sheer force. The plural stratagems often appears in the literature of generalship as a catalogue of such tricks — feigned retreats, disguised troops, false signals, surprise routes. It is the vocabulary of the crafty commander rather than the brave one.

2. The general sense — any cunning scheme. From the battlefield the word broadens to any clever scheme or trick in business, politics, courtship, or daily life: a stratagem to win a contract, a stratagem to avoid an awkward meeting, a stratagem for getting a child to eat vegetables. In this extended sense it remains formal and slightly theatrical; the scheme is always clever and always involves a degree of deception.

3. Stratagem versus strategy. A crucial boundary: strategy is the overall long-term plan of a campaign or enterprise — the grand design; a stratagem is one specific trick or device inside that design. Strategy is broad and ongoing; stratagems are singular, concrete, and deceptive. Confusing them is a classic error; separating them is essential to both military and business writing.

4. Collocations and usage patterns.

  • Classical/military idiom: "the stratagems of war," "to win by stratagem," "a stratagem to deceive the enemy" — the historic register.
  • Narrative and fiction: "his stratagems were foiled," "a clever stratagem" — common in adventure, espionage, and fantasy prose, as in Tolkien.
  • Rhetoric and politics: "a stratagem of the press," "electoral stratagems" — cunning devices of persuasion, often with a critical edge.
  • Game theory and chess: a gambit or deceptive plan is readily called a stratagem.

5. Secondary shades of meaning.

  • Neutral cleverness: in older and literary use, a stratagem may be simply a skilful plan, without moral blame — the art of getting things done ingeniously.
  • Negative deception: in modern use the word leans negative — a stratagem is something you hide, a trick to get the better of someone; "mere stratagem" implies dishonesty.
  • Theatrical and playful: in everyday speech, "it was just a stratagem" often means "a little ruse," comically elevating a small trick to the dignity of generalship.

6. Register and tone. The word is formal, slightly archaic-flavoured, and grand. It suits historical writing, epic and fantasy prose, and mock-serious comedy; it is rare in plain conversational speech. Its tone is double-edged: it admires the cleverness of the schemer even as it marks the deception.

7. The semantic bridge. The Greek ancestor stratḗgēma means literally "an act of generalship" — something a stratēgós (general) does. Every later sense, military or domestic, retains that image: a stratagem is a piece of generalship applied to any contest, a commander's trick transplanted into ordinary life. Grasping the "general's move" image makes the whole family of senses — from armies to office politics — one coherent idea.
