Belisarius (prop. n.) — Flavius Belisarius (c. 500–565 AD), the most celebrated general of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I. Born at Germania in the Balkans (Thracian/Illyrian stock), he rose to command the armies that reconquered much of the Western Roman Empire: North Africa from the Vandals (533–534) and Italy from the Ostrogoths (535–540), besides campaigns against the Persians in the East. In this card's context he is named as a contemporary of the historian Procopius, Simplicius, and Tribonian in the "rich and polite Constantinople" of the sixth century.

Primary sense (the historical general). Belisarius is the instrument of Justinian's restitutio imperii, the recovery of the Roman West. His career includes the suppression of the Nika revolt in Constantinople (532), the lightning conquest of Vandal Africa, the sieges and recapture of Rome (536, 546), and the defence of the eastern frontier against Persia. He is remembered as a loyal, humane, and brilliant commander — and, in Procopius's Secret History, as a man repeatedly humiliated by his wife Antonina and suspected by his emperor.

Polysemy & secondary modern meanings:

  • The tragic archetype ("blind Belisarius") — a late legend, unsupported by the contemporary sources, told that Justinian had the general blinded and reduced him to begging on the streets of Constantinople: "give an obol to Belisarius." This motif became a staple of European art, drama, and opera (e.g., paintings by David and Gérard, the opera Belisario by Donizetti, 1836). Semantic bridge: the historical theme of a great servant rewarded with ingratitude was compressed into the legend, so that the name came to signify fallen greatness and the cruelty of power — the exact valency exploited in modern retellings.
  • "The last of the Romans" — historians apply this title to Belisarius (as to Aetius and others) for being the last general to wage war in the grand Roman manner; the name is thus metonymy for the twilight of classical military science.
  • Belisarius in Gibbon and historical literature — Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall gives Belisarius a long, admiring narrative; the name in English historiography always evokes the pair Justinian–Belisarius and the dream of restoring Rome. In this card's context (from the same prose tradition), the name anchors the marvel of sixth-century Constantinople's learned brilliance.
  • Namesake usages — Belisarius appears as a given name in the Balkans and in historical fiction; a genus of moths and various ships and places carry the name, all echoing the general.
  • Etymology and onomastics — the name is usually analysed as a Thraco-Illyrian compound, *beli- "white, bright" + *sari- "spear" (cf. the Macedonian sarissa pike), hence plausibly "bright spear"; the analysis is, however, debated, and the name's origin is uncertain (see etymology fields). The semantic bridge from etymology to history is opaque — Belisarius's fame rests entirely on his deeds, not on any meaning his name carried for contemporaries.
  • Legendary and folkloric Belisarius — in Byzantine popular tradition he became a folk-hero of the epic-romance; in Western literature a moral exemplum of ingratitude. The two Belisarii — the faithful general and the betrayed beggar — coexist in every later reference.

Sense development summary. From a Balkan war-leader's name to the last great Roman general, from there to the archetype of the betrayed hero, and finally to a historical byword: the word's trajectory shows a proper noun undergoing the classic shift from individual designation to general symbol. In the card's context the name does double duty — it is both the historical person contemporary with Procopius and, by echo, the emblem of a brilliant civilisation that was itself fading, like its general, under the weight of its own history.
