- cringing (present participle, used adjectivally) — Old English ancestor cringan: "to yield, fall in battle, sink down"; the earliest idea is of warriors dying and falling, a connotation that is fatal and martial, not servile.
- Middle English crengen — "to bend low": the word shifts from battlefield death to the living posture of bending; the register moves from heroic to debasing.
- 16th–17th century — "cringing" becomes a moral slur: the obsequious courtier, the fawning servant, the sycophant before the throne; a cringing man has lost his honour and his upright manliness.
- 18th–19th century — broadened to any physical shrinking before a threat or fear: children cringing from punishment, dogs cringing from a raised hand; now more pathetic and pitiable than contemptible.
- 20th century — the emotional turn: "cringing" begins to describe inner recoil from embarrassment, awkwardness, and secondhand shame, not only outer posture.
- 21st century / internet era — "cringe" is re-nominalised into a cultural verdict; "cringing" now names the audience's visceral, comedic-social reaction to cringe comedy, viral awkwardness, and public self-humiliation — the old fear-shrinkage reborn as digital embarrassment.
