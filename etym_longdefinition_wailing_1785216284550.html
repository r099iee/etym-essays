WAILING is the present participle of the verb to wail, and it names the act of uttering a prolonged, high-pitched, mournful cry of grief, pain, or despair — lamenting aloud in a sustained, piercing, often inarticulate voice. A wail is loud and continuous, pitched high, and largely wordless: it is grief forced out as pure sound, the voice pushed beyond speech into a rising and falling ululation. To wail is not merely to cry; it is to cry aloud, with the full force of the breath, in a sound that carries and cannot be ignored. The word has always belonged to the register of mourning and desperation — the noise of a funeral, a disaster, a soul in extremity — and it retains that intensity today.

In The Hobbit, the word appears at one of the bleakest moments of the journey. Gollum, having lost the Ring, sits down in the darkness of the goblin tunnels and begins to weep — "a whistling and wailing," as the sound reaches Bilbo through the gloom, "horrible to listen to." The wailing is the raw keening vocalisation of his misery, bursting out alongside his articulate cursing: the cursing gives him words, the wailing shows the wordless despair beneath them. The participle's -ing suffix makes the sound continuous, an unending lament that drifts through the dark toward the listening hobbit — shrill and self-pitying, rising and falling, not far off. It is grief in its most naked, animal form, exactly the sound of Gollum in his utter helplessness.

The word's senses and family:

  • Of human mourning (primary): to cry out in grief or pain with a long, high, mournful sound — the classic sound of funerals, of mothers over children, of the bereft. "Wailing and gnashing of teeth" (from the Bible) fixes the word forever as the sound of hopelessness.
  • Of lament and lamentation: wailing overlaps with formal lament but is distinguished by being inarticulate — the cry rather than the spoken complaint.
  • Of non-human sound (modern extension): sirens wail, the wind wails, bagpipes wail, trains wail in the night; anything that produces a high, sustained, mournful, piercing sound may be said to wail. This transferred sense dates from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
  • As a noun: "a wail" — a single such cry; "the wails of the bereaved." The plural is common in descriptions of disasters and grief.
  • Music: wailing names a style of blues and jazz singing or instrumental playing — the mournful, bending, high-pitched notes of a saxophone or guitar that "cry" like a human voice ("wailing guitar").
  • Literary fixed phrases: "to set up a wail," "to break into wails," "to give a wail" — phrases that dramatise sudden, uncontrollable outbursts of sorrow.

The word's history is the history of a cry. It is built on the Proto-Indo-European interjection *wai-, "ah! alas!" — a primal exclamation of woe so close to the natural sound of human distress that it survived almost unchanged across the daughter languages: Latin vae ("vae victis — woe to the vanquished"), Welsh gwae, Old Irish fāe, and in Germanic, Old English wā, the ancestor of modern woe. In Old Norse the cry vei was turned into a verb, væla, "to utter woe," and Middle English borrowed that verb around 1300 as waylen; smoothing of the diphthong produced wail. The word thus begins as a sound, becomes a cry, becomes an action, and finally becomes a continuing state — wailing is woe that cannot stop itself.
