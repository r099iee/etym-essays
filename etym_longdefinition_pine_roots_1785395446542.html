PINE-ROOTS (plural compound noun) names the roots of pine trees — the anchoring and water-absorbing system of the conifer — and in the passage from The Hobbit it pictures the exposed root-mat lying among the bracken far below the company after the rockslide. Pines do not grow a deep taproot like an oak; they spread a wide, shallow, fibrous system of lateral roots that run out from the trunk just beneath the soil surface, each large root dividing into finer and finer feeders, with a strong central root that anchors the tree but remains relatively shallow. On steep, thin-soiled slopes these cable-like roots lie at or near the surface, gnarled, twisted and interlocked, gripping the rock and the loose earth; the crashing stones of the slide go "bounding and spinning among the bracken and the pine-roots", and the compound names the rough, root-laced ground where the avalanche comes to rest. The plural is essential: these are not a single root but a whole exposed network, a tangle of cables and tendrils that the stones must roll among, so the image is one of entanglement, not isolation. The tone is concrete and naturalistic: the exposed roots belong to the same tough, high-altitude pines that stood in the climbing wood above, and the word lands the reader's eye on the raw, weathered underbelly of the mountain — roots and fern and scattered stones — the stripped-down stage on which the episode's violence finally comes to rest.

SENSES AND USES

  • Literal plural (the sense of this card): the roots of pine trees, especially as a visible, exposed feature of pine forest or of a mountainside; the whole mat of cables lacing a thin-soiled conifer slope.
  • Singular "pine-root": a single root of a pine, or the whole root system of one tree; also used attributively ("pine-root soil", "pine-root kindling").
  • Fuel and craft use: pine roots are resinous and burn well even when green, so "pine-root" appears in rural craft and fire-lighting contexts; resinous pine-root knots were used as kindling and torchwood, and the word carries this practical warmth.
  • Timber and forestry register: in woodcraft "pine-roots" describes the anchoring system that holds the tree in shallow soil — a practical, economic term for the forester.
  • Landscape and topographical use: in nature-writing and fiction the plural is drawn on to picture a whole tangled, cable-like root system lying at the surface of thin-soiled slopes — shorthand for an exposed, root-laced mountain flank.
  • Figurative resonance (through the second element): the general noun "root" is one of English's most productive metaphors (the root of a problem, one's roots, root-and-branch reform), and compounds built on it inherit a faint echo of these; "pine-roots" itself stays strictly literal.

The word is a transparent endocentric compound: PINE specifies the species, ROOTS names the organ, and the compound denotes a kind of roots — the meaning is exactly the sum of its parts, with no idiom and no hidden sense. Its register is plain and concrete, and its plural has settled into descriptive prose as shorthand for an exposed, root-laced mountainside.

CONTRASTS AND BOUNDARIES

  • "Root" is the general botanical term for the underground organ of any plant, absorbing water and minerals; "pine-roots" narrows it to one species' system.
  • "Taproot" is a single deep, tapering main axis — typical of oaks and carrots, but weak or absent in pines, which spread wide, shallow roots instead.
  • "Fibrous roots" are the fine branching masses typical of grasses and pines; a pine's root system is described as fibrous.
  • "Rhizome" or "rootstock" is an underground stem, not a true root, though it resembles one — bracken's underground runner is a rhizome, and lies among the pine-roots in the same sentence.
  • "Stilt-roots" or "buttress-roots" are the above-ground supporting roots of mangroves and tropical trees — the opposite of the hidden pine root.
  • "Radicle" is the embryonic first root of a germinating seed; "rootlet" a fine side-branch of a root — technical sub-terms of the general system.
