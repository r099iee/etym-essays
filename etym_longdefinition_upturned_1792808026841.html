Upturned is the past-participial adjective of upturn: turned up, directed upward, or flipped upside down. It describes faces lifted toward the sky, palms opened to the rain, noses tilted at the tip, boats capsized with keels in the air — anything whose natural ‘up’ has been rotated into view.

In the source sentence the hobbits lift their faces to Gandalf's firework flowers: ‘before they touched their upturned faces’ — faces tilted back in wonder, eyes wide, mouths likely open. Upturned here is pure receptivity: the face as a bowl catching light, the posture of astonishment and delight.

Core meaning in detail: The word splits into two physical pictures: (a) tilted upward while staying in place — upturned faces, upturned palms, an upturned nose, upturned leaves showing silver undersides; and (b) flipped over entirely — an upturned boat, upturned crates used as tables, upturned soil. Sense (a) dominates for bodies; sense (b) for objects. Both share the idea of a surface meant to face down or inward now facing up and outward.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Tilted or lifted upward (primary, from 16th c.): ‘upturned faces’, ‘upturned collar against the wind’. The bridge is literal composition: up + turned — rotated toward the sky while rooted in place.
  • Flipped upside down, capsized (from 17th c.): ‘an upturned dinghy on the beach’, ‘upturned buckets’. The bridge extends the rotation past ninety degrees to full inversion — turned up so far that the bottom becomes the top.
  • Turned up at the tip — noses (specialised, 19th c.): ‘a charming upturned nose’. The bridge narrows the general ‘tilted up’ to the nose's tip alone; lexicographers call this the retroussé nose, and novelists have praised it for two centuries.
  • Dug up, exposed — soil and roots (agricultural): ‘upturned earth’, ‘the plough's upturned furrows’. The bridge runs through the verb upturn = to turn over with a spade: what was buried now faces the sky.
  • Overturned metaphorically — plans and lives (figurative): ‘an upturned existence’, rarer than the noun upheaval but the same image. The bridge carries capsizing from boats to biographies: a life flipped keel-up by war, love, or fortune.

Register and connotation: upturned is gentle and observational — the novelist's word, not the engineer's. Upturned faces suggest wonder, prayer, or pleading; upturned boats suggest pause and patience (the voyage done, the hull drying). Even the capsized sense feels temporary and recoverable: what is upturned can be righted again. The emotional charge is openness — nothing hidden, everything offered upward. Artists and photographers reach for it constantly: the upturned face is the universal shorthand for awe, painted on shepherds at the Nativity and photographed on crowds beneath every firework finale ever staged.
