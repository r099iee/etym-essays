Late 16th c. — Late Elizabethan introduction: borrowed directly from Latin aversus, initially preserving a literal physical sense of turning one's face or body away from an object, but rapidly shifting to mental alienation.
- 17th c. — Jacobean and Caroline moral psychology: deployed in theological and political controversies to describe minds, souls, or wills that were turned away from God, divine truth, or lawful monarchical allegiance.
- 18th c. — Neoclassical grammatical debates: embroiled in a fierce stylistic battle among grammarians (such as Samuel Johnson and Robert Lowth) over whether one should be 'averse from' (preserving Latin ab-) or 'averse to' (following natural English vernacular idiom).
- 19th c. — Victorian polite litotes: became a beloved instrument of British social understatement and polite concession, commonly appearing in double negatives ('not averse to') to indicate gentlemanly acquiescence or quiet indulgence.
- 20th–21st c. — Technical and economic compounding: exploded in modern financial, psychological, and behavioral economics literature as an essential hyphenated modifier (risk-averse, loss-averse, conflict-averse), standardizing the preposition to across worldwide English.
