Heretical — the biography of a chooser. The adjective travels an unusual road that begins not with doctrine but with the hand. Its ultimate root, Proto-Indo-European *ser-, meant "to take hold of, grasp, seize" — a physical action of grabbing and holding fast. Greek inherited this in the verb hairein (αἱρεῖν), "to take, seize, grasp," and, crucially, in its middle voice, haireisthai, "to take for oneself," which developed the sense "to choose": what you deliberately take up becomes what you have chosen. This is the decisive semantic leap on which the whole word-history turns — from grasping a thing to choosing a belief. It must be said, for the record, that the deepest link to *ser- is a hypothesis: Pokorny proposed it, and Beekes, always cautious, allows that the Greek verb may be of Pre-Greek origin; but the derivation from haireisthai onward is entirely secure, and no serious scholar doubts it. The Greek invention of the school. From haireisthai the Greeks formed the noun hairesis, "a taking, a choosing," and the word soon acquired its fateful social meaning: a hairesis was not just any choice but a chosen system of thought — a school, a sect, a party. The physician who followed the hairesis of Hippocrates chose a medical allegiance against rival theories of disease; the philosopher's school was his hairesis, a self-consciously adopted allegiance to a founder; in Greek politics the word could even mean a faction. The physician Galen and his predecessors described the medical "sects" — the Dogmatists, the Empiricists, the Methodists — as haireseis. Crucially, in this Greek and Greek-influenced usage the word was value-neutral or even positive: choosing a school of thought was what a thinking person did. The sectarian overtone was present before Christianity adopted the word, but the charge of error was not yet attached. The Christian seizure. It was in Christian usage that hairesis was sealed as "false doctrine, deviation from the teaching of the Church." The New Testament uses the word for "factions" and "divisions" within the community — the "heresies" Paul condemns among the Corinthians are cliques, groups choosing for themselves against the unity of the church. As the early Church developed a fixed creed and a hierarchy of bishops, the hairesis came to mean a doctrine chosen by an individual or group against the authoritative teaching of the Church — a choice, but the wrong choice, made for oneself against the community that alone defined truth. The adjective hairetikos, "given to choosing for oneself," was reshaped by the Christian writers into "heterodox, heretical." The journey into English. Late Latin took the adjective in as haereticus, the language of the Church fathers and the Vulgate tradition; Old French passed it on as eretique and heretique; Middle English as heretik, the noun "heretic," recorded by the fourteenth century. The noun heresy had come still earlier, around 1225, via Old French heresie from Latin haeresis. The adjective HERETICAL is a later, purely English formation — recorded from about 1500 — built simply by adding the Latinate adjectival suffix -al to "heretic." English had no need of a Greek or Latin adjective; it manufactured one from its own stock. The centuries of fire. For the next three centuries the word lived at the centre of the most violent conflicts in European history. Heresy was the gravest of intellectual crimes in medieval canon law — anathema, excommunication, and, for the impenitent, execution by fire. The Reformation multiplied the heretic: each side of the confessional divide declared the other heretical, and the word became a weapon hurled in both directions. The trial of Galileo made "heretical" the accusation against science itself; the wars of religion made it the battle-cry of contending churches. Every later sense of the word preserves the profile of the original: the heretic is the one who chooses for himself against the community's right to decide what may be believed. Ruskin's secular heresy. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries secularized the word without cooling it. In Ruskin's preface the adjective does something delicate: he predicts that his own opinions "will sound heretical to the ordinary connoisseur," casting the art-critical establishment as a secular church whose dogmas — the received judgements of taste, the fashionable names, the inherited rules — are not to be questioned, and himself as the man who questions them. The phrase is at once defiant and ironic, because Ruskin does not concede that he is wrong: he locates the heresy in the ear of the orthodox judge rather than in any error of his doctrine. The word performs its whole history in one clause — the weight of the Inquisition, the heat of the Reformation, and the modern pleasure of the provocateur who declares himself a heretic of taste. Today. In modern English heretical has broadened to any opinion that flouts the received truth of a community — artistic, scientific, political — while keeping its theological shadow. A bold scientific hypothesis is "heretical to the established paradigm"; a controversial aesthetic is "heretical to the academy." The charge ranges from the genuinely scandalous to the playfully exaggerated, but it always retains the root's logic: the heretical is what a community has decided may not be chosen, and the heretic is the one who chooses it anyway. That is why the word fits Ruskin so perfectly — he is, by his own account, the man who insists on choosing.
