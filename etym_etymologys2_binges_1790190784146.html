Proto-Germanic (dialectal / expressive root) (uncertain date)
*beng- / *bing-
"to swell, soak, saturate with liquid"
↓
English Dialect (Midlands / Northamptonshire) (c. 18th-19th c.)
binge
"to soak a wooden vessel in water to make it watertight"
↓
British University & Naval Slang (c. 1854)
binge
"a heavy drinking bout, to soak oneself in liquor"
↓
Colloquial English (plural noun & 3rd person verb) (c. 1880-1920)
binges
"boisterous parties, extended sprees of carousing"
↓
Modern English (20th-21st c.)
binges

Etymological Note: Originates in 19th-century English provincial dialect (recorded in Northamptonshire by Anne Elizabeth Baker in 1854), where the verb binge meant to soak a wooden tub, churn, or beer barrel in water so the timber staves would swell and seal any leaks. Adopted into university slang as a humorous metaphor for soaking one's body in alcohol, it evolved into modern sprees and clinical overconsumption.
