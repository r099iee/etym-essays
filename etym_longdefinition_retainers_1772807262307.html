In the narrative context, figures fleeing after a crime and 'leaving only a few retainers who were not involved', retainers are the residual household staff and long-service followers of a powerful man, here linked to Xue Pan's circle. They are not the active henchmen who committed the offense and melted away; they are the kept dependents who remain on the premises, marked by loyalty or inertia rather than by agency in the crime. The word's tone is faintly archaic and hierarchical: it evokes a great household's entourage, people 'held' in service, not modern employees with contracts and rights. DEFINITION, PRIMARY SENSE. Retainers, the plural of retainer, denotes servants or followers of a noble or wealthy person, especially ones of long standing. A retainer is someone retained in the service of a lord or great household: a personal attendant, liveryman, or dependent who remains attached over time. In the feudal and early modern world, retainers were the kept core of a magnate's following, distinguished from hired day-laborers by personal loyalty and sometimes by the badge or livery of their master. POLYSEMY. RETAINERS is genuinely polysemous, and its senses all rest on the Latin verb retinere, 'to hold back, keep.' The full inventory:
  • Long-standing household followers (the sense of the card): servants and dependents kept in the service of a noble or wealthy person; faithful retainers, household retainers, a lord's retainers. Literary and historical register.
  • Legal and professional retainer (modern, common): a fee paid in advance to secure the continuing services of a lawyer, barrister, consultant, or other professional; also the document recording such an engagement, and in journalism a retainer writer is one kept on standby.
  • Dental retainer (modern, technical): a custom-made device, usually a wire and plastic splint, worn after orthodontic treatment to hold the teeth in their corrected positions. The idea is the same: the device 'retains' the alignment.
  • Mechanical and industrial senses (technical): a retainer is also any part that holds something in place, such as a bearing retainer or a retainer ring that keeps a component from shifting; and an animal keeper may be called a retainer in old-fashioned usage.
  • Related forms of the same verb family: retain (keep, engage), retention (keeping; memory), retentive (able to retain), and the phrase water-retaining (of soil holding moisture).
SEMANTIC BRIDGE. The bridge from household to law to dentistry is the single verb 'hold.' Latin retinere, from re- 'back' plus tenere 'to hold,' means to hold back, keep, or preserve. A feudal retainer is someone held in service, kept on. A lawyer's retainer fee is the sum that holds the lawyer available, keeping the service in reserve. A dental retainer is the device that holds the teeth where they are. The same mental picture, keeping something bound and in place, generates all three senses across six hundred years. COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER. Common collocations include faithful retainers, household retainers, a lord's retainers, a loyal retainer, and (in law and finance) a retainer fee, pay a retainer, on retainer. Register is literary, historical, or legal. In casual modern speech, staff, employees, or hangers-on are more common. In fiction set in imperial China, medieval Europe, or aristocratic households, retainers is the natural choice for long-standing personal followers. In the passage at hand, the plural retainers means the leftover, lower-agency part of a noble or wealthy man's people: the fixed household core that remains when the more culpable companions have fled.
