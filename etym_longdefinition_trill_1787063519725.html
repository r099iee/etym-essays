Trill (/trɪl/) is a noun and a verb. As a noun it names (1) a rapid alternation between two closely spaced notes — a standard musical ornament; (2) a bird's fast, quavering, warbling series of notes; and (3) in phonetics, a speech sound made by the rapid vibration of a speech organ, such as a rolled r. As a verb it means to produce such a rapid, fluttering, quavering sound. In the reading context the old thrush gave a sudden trill — a brief, bright burst of song.

Primary senses

  • Music: the rapid alternation of a principal note with the note a step above it (occasionally a step below), traditionally notated with the symbol tr — an ornament central to Baroque, Classical, and Romantic keyboard, string, and wind writing. A trill is executed as fast as the player's hand or breath allows, shaking between the two notes.
  • Bird song: a rapid succession of quickly repeated, similar notes, or a quavering warble — the thrush's trill, the trill of the nightingale. This is the most common everyday sense.
  • Phonetics: a consonant produced by the rapid vibration of an articulator against a surface — the tongue-tip trill [r] of Spanish, Italian, Russian, and Scots (the rolled r), or the uvular trill [ʀ] of some French and German varieties.

Secondary and extended meanings

  • The human voice: a quavering, warbling, or high fluttering sound — a trill of laughter, a soprano's trill, the trilled syllables of delight or mockery.
  • Instruments: any rapid trembling effect on violin, flute, piccolo, or keyboard imitating the vocal ornament.
  • Nature sounds: the vibrating, buzzing call of insects — the trill of crickets and cicadas on a summer night.
  • General sound: any fast, trembling, bubbling, or ringing noise — a telephone trilling, a trill of static, the trill of a boiling kettle in playful writing.
  • Verb uses: to trill is to sing, warble, or pronounce with rapid vibration of voice or tongue — she trilled her r's, the bird trilled in the hedge; also to trill is to laugh brightly and quaveringly.

The historical bridge

English gathered trill by a double route. A native Middle English verb trillen to turn, spin, twirl, roll (from Scandinavian, ultimately from a PIE root meaning to turn, bore, rub) supplied the idea of a rolling, trembling motion. Then in the 17th century, when Italian dominated European musical vocabulary, English borrowed the musical noun and verb from Italian trillo and trillare to quaver, warble. The two strands — to turn or roll and to warble — fused in English into the single modern idea of a rapid, trembling, rolling sound.

Register and nuance

  • In music and phonetics trill is precise technical terminology.
  • Of birds and voices it is bright, positive, and musical.
  • As a verb it implies lightness, speed, and brilliance — a sound that shakes, flutters, or tumbles quickly through the air.
