“Cradle” entered English naming the infant’s bed — a small frame mounted on rockers so that gentle motion could soothe a child.
“Cradle” through the centuries extended the supporting-frame idea: any framework that holds or rocks something fragile — a ship in its cradle, a cradle for a phone or a rifle — the word meaning “a frame that supports and often rocks.”
“Cradle” also ran a figurative career — “the cradle of civilization,” “to cradle something in one’s hands” — the image of holding and protecting like an infant.
“Cradles” today keeps all of it at once: the rocking baby-beds, the supporting frames, and the protective-holding image — which is why a shield “used as a cradle” is such a precise joke about a peaceful land converting war-gear to nursery furniture.
