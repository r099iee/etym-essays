- 1530s–1540s "glue-like, sticky, viscous" - Borrowed into Early Modern English from Middle French and Latin as a physical and medical descriptor for animal cements, bodily humours, and sticky wound dressings.
- 17th–18th c. "botanical and geological adhesion" - Natural philosophers, botanists, and mineralogists adopt the term for gummy plant saps, mucilaginous seeds, and cohesive clay soils that impede agricultural tillage.
- 19th c. "culinary and biochemical expansion" - Enters global trade and culinary literature to translate Asian sticky rice ("glutinous rice"), coinciding with early laboratory investigations into cereal proteins (wheat gluten).
- Early 20th c. "satirical sentimentality" - British humorous literature (most notably P. G. Wodehouse) cements the figurative application of the word to cloying romantic gush, mushy dialogue, and suffocating sentiment.
- Late 20th c.–Present "bipartite technical and literary survival" - Persists in culinary science and gastronomy (often requiring labeling clarification for celiac diets) alongside a robust literary life as an evocative critique of sticky, unctuous sentimentality.
