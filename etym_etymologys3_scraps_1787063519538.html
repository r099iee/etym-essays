The etymological evolution of scraps provides an exceptional demonstration of how Viking Age North Germanic vocabulary reshaped the everyday domestic and material lexicon of English, expanding from kitchen scrapings into heavy industry and colloquial pugilism.

1. Indo-European Roots and Germanic Scraping Verbs:

  • Proto-Indo-European Base: The root ascends to Proto-Indo-European *skreb- (an onomatopoeic extension of the fundamental root *sker-, "to cut, incise, notch, or divide"). The root imitated the harsh, abrasive sound of a sharp flint or blade scratching across a hard surface, generating terms related to peeling, grating, and incising across the Germanic and Balto-Slavic branches.
  • Proto-Germanic Morphology: Proto-Germanic formed the weak verb *skrapōną and strong verb *skrepaną ("to scrape, scratch, pare away"), which developed into Old Norse skrapa, Middle Dutch schrapen, and Old High German schraffen.

2. Old Norse Loanword in Middle English:

  • Old Norse Skrap: In Old Norse, the neuter noun skrap meant "scraps, trifles, rubbish, chattering, or things scraped together" (closely related to skrapa, to scrape).
  • Adoption into Middle English (c. 1380): During the late 14th century, the term entered northern and east-midland dialects of Middle English as scrappe. It immediately established itself in household vernacular for the small morsels and fat trimmings scraped off plates and butcher blocks, often cited in medieval sermons warning against gluttony while the poor starve on mere scrappes. In early cooking manuscripts, recipes for potted meats frequently called for collected meat scrappes.

3. Industrial Metallurgical Shift and Pugilistic Slang:

  • Industrial Revolution (19th Century): With the rise of modern blast furnaces, rolling mills, and steam locomotives, scrap became the standard trade designation for recycled iron, copper, and brass (scrap metal, scrap iron). The economic infrastructure of the scrap-heap and scrapyard became a defining feature of Victorian industrial towns, where rag-and-bone men and scrap merchants traded recovered materials.
  • The Boxing Ring (1870s): In Victorian pugilism, the noun scrap emerged as slang for an informal boxing match or street brawl, likely influenced by the adjective scrappy (combative, aggressive like a terrier fighting over food scraps).

4. Tolkien's Attestation in The Hobbit (1937):

  • The Mirkwood Famine: In chapter 8 of The Hobbit, Tolkien evokes the dire desperation of Thorin's company after running out of provisions in the elven forest: "they did not even dare to search on the ground for scraps of food for fear of becoming separated again."
  • Lexical Precision: Here, scraps perfectly conveys the pitiful, microscopic fragments of stale lembas or dried meat that might have fallen into the deep leaf carpet, underscoring that even the search for these crumbs is too dangerous in the spider-infested blackness.
