The history of skulk (and hence of the participle skulking) is a Scandinavian-tinted story: the word entered English in the Middle Ages from the language of the Norse settlers of northern England, its origin is genuinely obscure (a rare case in which even the Germanic roots are uncertain), and its meaning has stayed remarkably faithful to a single idea — hiding or moving in secret — while its moral colouring has deepened steadily toward disapproval.

The Scandinavian connection. English skulk first appears around 1200 in Middle English as skulken, meaning “to lie in wait, lurk, sneak”. The standard view, accepted by the OED and most authorities, is that it was borrowed from a Scandinavian source: Old Norse had the related verb skulka (compare modern Norwegian skulka, “to sneak, to lie in wait, to shirk”, and Danish skulke, “to shirk, malinger, play truant”). The presence of the sk- cluster is a strong hint of Norse origin, since the initial sk- sound is characteristic of Scandinavian loanwords in English (compare sky, skirt, scare, scold). The Vikings who settled the Danelaw in the 9th–10th centuries left a large footprint of such words in northern English dialects, and skulk emerged into written English in the centuries after the Conquest, first in northern texts.

Obscure deeper roots. Beyond the Scandinavian stage, the trail goes cold, and it is important to say so plainly. The deeper Germanic ancestry of the Norse word is uncertain. Some scholars connect it to a Germanic base meaning “to lurk” or “to be crooked/oblique”, and there are suggestive relatives — Old English sćeolh (“wry, squinting, oblique”), Middle Dutch schulken (“to lie in wait, to sneak”) — but the reconstruction of a single Proto-Germanic *skulk- with a secure Indo-European etymon is not possible with the evidence available. The word may ultimately be imitative or expressive, its harsh sk- onset capturing the abrupt, hidden darting movement of a lurking creature. Any confident PIE derivation would be guesswork, and responsible dictionaries decline to give one.

Middle English and early senses. In Middle English, skulken meant primarily “to lie in wait, to lurk” — a hunting and ambush word, describing a person or animal hiding to spring on prey. The sense of “to move stealthily” is closely attached from the start. The word appears in medieval romances and moral literature, always with the flavour of secrecy and deception. It was a vivid, colloquial, Germanic-flavoured word, sitting alongside the more courtly Romance vocabulary for deceit, and it carried a northern, earthy energy that the more polished French-derived words lacked.

Shakespearean and early modern use. By the early modern period, skulk and skulking were established literary words. Shakespeare uses skulk in Richard III (“Skulking in corners, and starting at the sound of a thunder”) and the word appears throughout Elizabethan and Jacobean drama in contexts of cowardice and treachery. During the same period the military sense developed: a “skulker” was a soldier who hung back or hid to avoid combat — a coward and a shirker — and this usage gave the word a strongly contemptuous edge that it has never lost. In the disciplined armies of the age, to be called a skulker was to be accused of the worst kind of disloyalty to one's comrades.

Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the 18th–19th centuries, skulking appears regularly in fiction of adventure and crime, in political writing (as an insult for evasive politicians), and in military narratives where “skulking” is the opposite of heroism. The participle becomes a standard epithet for suspicious figures: “a skulking vagrant”, “skulking thieves”. The word also spread into American English with the same meanings. Its register remained colloquial-to-literary, always pejorative, and it became a stock element of the gothic and sensational fiction that dominated popular reading.

Modern usage. In contemporary English, skulking thrives in crime fiction, thrillers, gothic and fantasy literature — precisely the genres where lurking villains and hidden threats do their work — and in journalism for sneaky or evasive behaviour. It is not archaic, but it is vivid and slightly literary, and its moral charge is intact: to skulk is always to hide for a bad reason, whether cowardice, guilt, predation or treachery. Tolkien's use of the word in Smaug's taunt taps straight into this eight-hundred-year-old core of meaning: the dwarves outside are not merely waiting — they are skulking, and the dragon wants Bilbo to feel the sting of being their cowardly shadow-worker.
