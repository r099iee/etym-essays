THE BIOGRAPHY OF GUFFAWING: A SHORT, BRIGHT SCOTS HISTORY 1. THE SHORTEST PEDIGREE IN THE ENGLISH LAUGHTER FAMILY Unlike most English words, which can be traced back across a millennium of documents and beyond into reconstructed roots, guffawing has a short, bright history that begins in the dialect speech of Scotland in the early modern period. Its first recorded ancestor is the Scots verb and noun gawf, attested in the early sixteenth century as "a loud, noisy laugh." The OED and Etymonline are in agreement that the word is imitative in origin: it does not descend from Latin or Greek or Proto-Indo-European, and no PIE root should be forced onto it. Instead, the syllables gu-ff-aw seemed to Scots speakers to mimic the sound itself — a deep, hollow, breathy peal that begins in the chest, bursts through the open lips, and trails off in a long open vowel. It is a sound-word, born in the mouths of Scots speakers, not inherited from an ancient root; its entire biography is the story of a noise becoming a noun. 2. THE CROSS-INFLUENCE OF GUFF Beside gawf stood a second Scots word, guff, meaning "a puff of breath or wind; foul air; a whiff." This is the kind of rank gust that can be felt and smelled at close range, and its imagery of an expelled breath fed directly into the new word. A guffaw is precisely a laugh that is also a gust — an explosive, breathy burst expelled from the open mouth with almost no restraint. The two words merged in feeling: the loud laugh (gawf) and the expelled breath (guff) together produced the compound guff-aw, the laugh that is a blast of air. This is not strict etymology but sound-symbolic cross-influence, and it is exactly how imitative words actually grow — by association of sounds and images, not by rule. 3. THE RECORDED DATES: 1720 AND 1721 In 1720 the noun guffaw appears in Scots records, meaning "a loud, coarse, boisterous burst of laughter," and the verb follows almost immediately in 1721, meaning "to laugh loudly and coarsely." The near-simultaneity of the two records tells us something about how the word was born: the sound and its name arrived together, noun and verb springing from the same imitative act. Eighteenth-century Scotland was a society of convivial taverns, of Lallans verse, and of robust oral culture, and the word belongs to that world — the laugh of the hearth, the alehouse, and the fair, not of the drawing room. 4. THE MIGRATION SOUTH: 18TH–19TH CENTURIES During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries guffaw migrated south from Scots dialect into standard English. The reason it was adopted so readily is that no older English word conveyed its specific combination of qualities: suddenness, loudness, and coarseness all at once. Roar was loud and deep but could be joyful or triumphant; belly-laugh was deep but genial; cackle was shrill and broken; chuckle was soft and inward. Novelists and essayists of the Romantic and Victorian periods prized guffaw precisely because it could mark a character instantly — the unrefined, open-mouthed laugher, hearty but without restraint or refinement. Dickens, Thackeray, and the humorists of the age used it to sketch everything from bluff innkeepers to bullying oafs, and the word entered the language permanently as the laugh of no self-restraint. 5. THE MORPHOLOGY OF THE PRESENT PARTICIPLE The present participle guffawing adds duration and repetition to the noun's image. A single guffaw is one explosive burst; guffawing is a sustained volley — laugh after laugh, echoing and re-echoing, each one bursting out in the same coarse, breathy peal. The form is intrinsically plural and continuous, and in literary prose it is therefore the natural instrument for describing a crowd, a chorus, or a creature that laughs again and again without tiring. It pictures laughter that goes on, reverberating, rather than a single event. 6. TOLKIEN AND THE GIANTS (LITERARY ATTESTATION) Tolkien deploys exactly this quality in The Hobbit, chapter three: "They could hear the giants guffawing and shouting all over the mountainsides." The word is chosen for its full modern weight. The giants' laughter is loud, coarse, and unfeeling — the amusement of forces that cannot be reasoned with. The repeated present participle makes it a volley that rolls across the peaks, indistinguishable from the thunder in the dark; the word tells the reader everything about the giants' monstrous detachment without a line of direct description. A philologist by profession, Tolkien knew the word's dialect history and its coarse, folk flavour, and he used it with precision: the giants are not amused and delighted (that would be merriment); they are guffawing — elemental, childish, and heartless, laughing at the storm as at a game. 7. WHY IT MATTERS THAT GUFFAW HAS NO PIE ROOT The absence of a Proto-Indo-European ancestry is itself the word's most instructive feature. Most English vocabulary sits on ancient roots and can be traced through sound laws across millennia; guffawing cannot, because it was never inherited — it was invented, on the spot, to match the sound of a laugh. This makes it part of a small but precious class of English sound-words (cackle, chortle, snort, haw-haw) that document the onomatopoeic creativity of the language at its most direct: the human body makes a sound, and the language reaches for it. The word's short, bright, Scottish history is a perfect illustration of how living speech grows. KEY STAGES AT A GLANCE. - gawf (Scots, early 16th c., "a loud, noisy laugh") — the first recorded ancestor. - guff (Scots, "a puff of breath or wind") — cross-influencing image of the expelled breath. - guffaw (Scots noun, 1720, "a loud, coarse, boisterous burst of laughter"). - guffaw (Scots verb, 1721, "to laugh loudly and coarsely"). - Scottish English → Standard English (18th–19th c.) — the word migrates south. - guffawing (ModE present participle, "laughing boisterously and coarsely, in repeated bursts"). THE COARSE ROAR IN THE DARK. Guffawing is the sound-word that English needed for the laugh that cannot be controlled and does not care to be — and in the storm-lashed night of the Misty Mountains, it is exactly the laugh of the stone-giants.
