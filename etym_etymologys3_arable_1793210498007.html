The history of arable is the history of the plough itself, carried inside a single Latin adjective. Its deepest root is the reconstructed PIE verb *h₂erh₃-, meaning to plough or break ground — one of the farming words that spread with agriculture across ancient Europe, surfacing in Greek aroō, Gothic arjan, Old English erian, and Old Norse erja, all naming the same honourable act of opening the earth.

In Latin the root settled as arāre, to plough, the everyday verb of Virgil's Georgics and Cato's farm manual, conjugated by every schoolboy and swung by every peasant. From it Roman agricultural writers formed arābilis, that which can be ploughed — an adjective of capability built with the same -bilis suffix that gives English durable and fertile. Varro and Columella use the family constantly when they sort the estate: this field is arabilis, that slope is fit only for vines or olives, the marsh must be drained before the share can touch it. The word already carries its modern opposition — ploughland against pasture, vineyard, and wood.

Through Vulgar Latin and into Old French the adjective survived almost unchanged as arable, fit for the plough, borrowed into Middle English around 1400 just as England's open fields were being surveyed, leased, and fought over. Manor rolls, leases, and statutes divide the parish into arable, meadow, pasture, and wood, each with its customs of common, tithe, and fence. Tudor enclosure made the word political: converting arable to sheep pasture depopulated villages, and preachers thundered against graziers who turned corn ground to wool. Later improvers reversed the charge, breaking old downs to arable with turnips and clover, and Cobbett's generation read steep roof-pitch ploughland as proof of what English labour could do.

Sound and form across the centuries:

  • PIE *h₂erh₃- to Latin arāre: the laryngeal root simplifies into the long open vowel of arāre, keeping its agricultural sense intact across two thousand years.
  • Latin arāre to arābilis: the capability suffix -bilis turns the act into fitness — not ploughed, but ploughable — a Roman bureaucrat's distinction as much as a farmer's.
  • Latin to Old French arable: vowel levelling and loss of quantity give the clean three-syllable form English still spells; the Norman clerks who wrote England's surveys carried it across the Channel.
  • Middle English to modern: spelling stabilises while pronunciation shifts the central vowels toward the modern /ˈærəbəl/, and the noun use — so many acres of arable — grows out of estate shorthand.

Modern policy has made arable a measured category rather than a mere description: agricultural censuses return arable as temporary crops plus bare fallow, subsidy regimes pay on the arable area, and soil maps grade it from excellent to very poor. Yet Cobbett's steep Surrey fields remind the reader what the statistics summarise — men and horses hauling the plough up slopes where a walker must lean forward, turning soil that gravity itself seems to forbid, because bread corn paid and the parish had to eat.
