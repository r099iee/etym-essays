jungle-up is an American phrasal verb — a compound of the noun jungle and the aspectual particle up — meaning "to set up a makeshift camp, especially in the open, in a rough or outdoor area." It belongs to the slang of American hobos, tramps, and itinerant railroad workers of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and it names the act of establishing the informal, rough-and-ready encampments those wanderers called "hobo jungles": a fire, a spot of dry ground near water, a temporary bed, and a meal cooked in the open.

PRIMARY SENSE — to set up a rough outdoor camp (hobo slang)

  • To jungle-up — to stop for the night, make camp, prepare food, and sleep outdoors, as tramps did beside railroad tracks, rivers, and highways. In the context card, tramps "come wearily down from the highway in the evening to jungle-up near water" — the verb names the whole ritual of settling down to rest outdoors.
  • The hobo jungle — the noun from which the verb is built: a rough, informal encampment of vagrants, usually in a brushy or overgrown spot near a rail line or water source, where a fire pit, cooking gear, and shelters were shared among travellers.
  • The social meaning — "jungle-up" is not glamorous camping; it is the improvised, survivalist bedding-down of people without homes, carrying the communal customs of hobo culture: sharing a fire, "boiling up" a stew, and passing the evening in talk.

SECONDARY SENSE — informal, improvised camping of any kind

By extension from the hobo world, "jungle-up" came to describe any rough, makeshift, impromptu outdoor camp, even when the campers are not vagrants — hikers, fishermen, scouts, and hunters "jungling up" in the woods. In this use the word keeps its colouring of roughness and informality: to jungle-up is to make do, to sleep rough, to improvise a camp without the amenities of a proper campsite.

MODERN AND EXTENDED USES

  • Historical slang in literature — the word appears in American fiction and reportage of the early twentieth century, including the tramp-slang of the railroad era; it is famously associated with the Depression-era migrant world of John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men and The Grapes of Wrath.
  • Revival in camping and hobbyist contexts — as hobo culture faded, "jungle-up" survived as period slang, sometimes revived ironically or affectionately by bushcrafters, reenactors, and traditional campers to describe roughing it in the open.
  • The component words — jungle (from Hindi/Sanskrit for wild land) and the Germanic particle up combine in a characteristically English phrasal-verb pattern (compare camp up, fix up, rig up), in which up marks the completed act of assembling or establishing something — here, a camp.
  • Register — "jungle-up" is informal, historical, and faintly romantic: it evokes the hard but communal life of the road, never formal usage, and its tone sits between gritty survival and nostalgic Americana.
