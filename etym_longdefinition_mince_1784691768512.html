To mince is to cut or chop food, especially meat, into very small pieces — finer than ordinary chopping, closer to particles than to chunks. In British English the noun mince denotes the minced meat itself, what Americans call ground meat. The word’s core idea is reduction to fineness: the knife working repeatedly until matter becomes particles. From this single root image the word has grown three distinct families of sense, all traceable to the same “make-small” concept. Primary culinary sense
  • Verb: to cut, chop, or grind food into very small pieces. “Mince the garlic and parsley until finely pulverized.”
  • Noun (British): minced meat, especially beef — “a pound of mince.”
  • Derivatives: mincer (the kitchen machine or hand-cranked tool), minced (adjective, “finely chopped”), mincing (the action).
  • Compounds: mince-pie (a pie filled with mincemeat), mincemeat — which, despite the name, is classically a mixture of finely chopped dried fruit, suet, and spice (and, originally, minced meat); to make mincemeat of someone is to defeat or destroy them utterly.
Figurative senses
  • To mince one’s words (or not to mince matters): to soften, weaken, or dilute the force of what one says; the opposite of blunt speech. Recorded from the seventeenth century. The idiom pictures the speaker chopping his sentences into small, careful, evasive pieces rather than delivering them whole.
  • A mincing gait: to walk or move with small, dainty, affected steps, as if afraid to take full strides; a mincing manner is primly, self-consciously delicate. The connection is again the root idea of making things small — the walker minces his steps as the cook minces meat.
  • Mincing (of speech or manners): affectedly refined, prissy, over-delicate — “a mincing voice,” “mincing airs.”
Word-use notes and register Mince is a mild, domestic, kitchen word, and its power in literature often comes from that very mildness. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the trolls debate how to cook the captured dwarves — “whether they should roast them slowly, or mince them fine and boil them, or just sit on them one by one and squash them into jelly.” The comedy lies in the collision of registers: the vocabulary of the cookery recipe (mince fine, boil, squash into jelly) is applied to the murder of twelve intelligent beings. Mince, the mildest and most domestic of culinary verbs, is made to carry the chapter’s most brutal proposition — that dwarves are ingredients — and the detailed, methodical enumeration makes the horror arrive sideways. Culinary neighbours and boundaries
  • Chop is coarse and quick, producing uneven pieces; mince is finer and more thorough.
  • Dice cuts into neat, uniform cubes; mince produces smaller, less regular particles.
  • Grind and hash crush or shred into irregular fragments, often by machine; mince is the knife’s repeated, finer cutting (though in modern cooking minced and ground meat are near-synonyms).
  • Shred produces ribbons or strips rather than particles.
Origin in brief Mince comes from Old French mincier, “to cut into small pieces,” built on Latin minutus, “made small,” from minuere, “to lessen” — the same family as minute (“tiny”), minutiae (“trifling details”), minus, and minimum. The word entered English in the late fourteenth century, a Gallicism of the post-Conquest kitchen, and its “make-small” idea proved so suggestive that it soon spread from the chopping board to walking and speech.
