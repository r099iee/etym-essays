I. The Romance origin and its obscurity

Trifling descends from Middle English trifle (noun and verb), which English borrowed from Old French trufle, a by-form of trufe “deceit, trick, mockery”. The French word's own origin is unknown — the Oxford English Dictionary and the great Romance etymological dictionaries all mark it obscure. What can be said is that the family is Romance-wide: Old Provençal trufa “mockery, jest”, Italian truffa “fraud, trick” and truffatore “a cheat”, Spanish and Catalan trufa “mockery”. The same root later gives French truffe — which in modern French means “truffle”, the subterranean fungus, the sense-connection being that the truffle, like a trick, is something hidden in the ground. An older theory deriving the whole group from Latin turbāre “to disturb, stir up” is phonologically impossible and has been abandoned; a Germanic (Frankish) origin has been floated, since the mocking of the jongleur tradition crossed the Rhine, but no Germanic etymon is attested. This is a genuine gap: the prehistoric Romance word for “a trick” is unrecoverable. What is certain is the suffix: -ing is native Germanic — Old English -ung/-ing, Proto-Germanic *-ingō — the same element that forms verbal nouns and participles in walking, charming, and interesting.

II. Middle English: from trick to trifle

Middle English adopts trifle in the early 13th century in the full Old French meaning: “a false or idle tale, a trick, an act of mockery”. The verb triflen, attested from the later 14th century in Chaucer's circle, means “to deceive, to mock, to make sport of” — “to trifle with” is literally to play a false trick on someone. In late-14th-century usage the noun also slides toward its modern core: a trifle is “a thing of small value or importance”, and Gower and Chaucer use it for worldly vanities. The semantic engine is mockery: what is made sport of is by definition of no account — so the word that meant “a trick” comes to mean “a nothing”.

III. The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries: the participle hardens

By the 15th–16th centuries trifling is established as an adjective: “of the nature of a trifle — trivial, petty, insignificant” — a trifling matter, a trifling sum, a trifling error. The Elizabethans love it: Shakespeare uses trifle and trifling across the canon — “trifles light as air” in Othello are the jealous mind's confirmations, while in the comedies “trifling” talk is idle banter — and the participle fixes the register: polite, dismissive, mildly contemptuous. Simultaneously the old verb sense survives in “to trifle with” — to toy with a person's affections or promises, to deceive by dalliance — the exact sense of the source context, “You have been ___ with me”, a reproach of mockery, not of triviality. The dessert trifle appears in the late-16th-century cookery books — a dish of custard, fruit, sponge, and sack — named as “a trifle”, that is, a light nothing, easy to assemble; the name itself witnesses the word's slide from trickery to insignificance.

IV. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: gentility and condescension

In the age of the novel, trifling becomes the favourite dismissal of the drawing-room and the letter: “a trifling favour”, “no trifling matter”, “trifling objections”. Johnson's Dictionary glosses trifle as “a thing of no importance”, and the word carries the class economy of politeness — dismissing a servant's cost or a lady's whim as trifling while insisting one's own cause is “no trifling matter”. The moral identity of the trifler — the idler who makes a game of serious things — is a fixed character in moral essays from Addison and Steele to the Victorian conduct books; “trifling” was a recognised sin of youth, and sermon literature forbade the “trifling with time”.

V. The twentieth and twenty-first centuries

  • “Trifling” as “trivial” is fully standard, slightly formal or literary in tone — a trifling error, a trifling delay, a trifling cost.
  • “To trifle with” survives in formal reproach and in the phrase “trifling with the affections” — a staple of Edwardian fiction and period drama.
  • In African-American Vernacular English, “trifling” developed its own sharpened sense: a person who is shiftless, unreliable, good-for-nothing, or a cheat — a usage documented from the mid-20th century and now mainstream slang. It is a striking case of the old mockery-sense resurfacing through the triviality-sense.
  • The dessert keeps the word in common mouth as a harmless noun; the verb “to trifle” (except in “trifle with”) has faded from active use.

VI. Sound change

The modern diphthong of trifling /ˈtraɪflɪŋ/ shows that the vowel was long by Early Modern English and passed through the Great Vowel Shift: the long (i:) of late Middle English “trifle” was raised and diphthongised, on the same vowel path as time and line, while the suffix -ing kept its short vowel throughout. Twelve centuries of sound history are thus compressed into one vowel: a French trick-word that English naturalised, lengthened, shifted, and turned into the mildest shrug in the language — “a trifling matter”.
