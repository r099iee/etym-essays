Outlandish literally means "of the out-land", of the country beyond the border, and in the verse-portrait of Bao-yu in Chapter 3 of Dream of the Red Chamber it is the world's word for his strangeness: "His acts outlandish and his nature queer; / Yet not a whit cared he how folk might jeer!" The adjective describes behaviour so bizarre, uncouth, and non-conformist that it seems to come from a foreign, outlying world rather than from a noble household. The tone is mocking and condescending — this is the voice of conventional society passing judgement on an alien — and yet there is a grudging energy in the word, a recognition that Bao-yu's acts are not merely wrong but vividly, unplaceably other. He is a social alien within his own class, beyond the bounds of Confucian duty, and "outlandish" marks him as someone who does not belong to the "in-land" reality of filial obedience. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES "Outlandish" has a layered set of senses that have drifted over the centuries:
  • Original sense (Old English, now obsolete or archaic): foreign, of or from another country — an outlandish merchant, outlandish lands, outlandish wares. In Old English "utlendisc" was a neutral geographical word meaning simply "of a foreign land"; even in Middle English it was still close to "foreign", though already shading toward "barbarous".
  • Central modern sense: bizarre, wildly unconventional, grotesquely unfamiliar — outlandish clothes, outlandish ideas, an outlandish story, outlandish behaviour. This is the sense in the card's verse: behaviour so alien it seems to come from another world. The word here is pejorative or at least mockingly amazed; it judges as much as it describes.
  • Sense of absurd exaggeration: absurdly out of proportion or implausible — an outlandish price, an outlandish claim, an outlandish scheme. Here the word foregrounds the impossible, the exaggerated, the grotesquely far-fetched, without necessarily implying literal foreignness.
  • Archaic extended sense: outlying, remote, of the countryside beyond the settled or cultivated land — outlandish farms, the outlandish marches. This sense keeps the word's geography visible: the "out-land" is the land outside the village, the border country.
  • Colloquial hyperbolic use (modern informal): strikingly odd or funny in appearance or manner — "that hat is outlandish!" — where the word is used with affectionate or amused exaggeration rather than real condemnation.
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The historical bridge of "outlandish" is one of the clearest examples in English of a word's meaning being pulled along by its connotations. The Old English compound "utlendisc" was formed from "ut" ("out"), "land", and the adjective suffix "-isc", and it meant simply "foreign". But in a society where the foreigner was also the barbarian — the Viking, the pagan, the man beyond the border — "foreign" never stayed neutral for long: by the 14th century "outlandish" was sliding from "foreign" toward "foreign and therefore uncouth", and by the 16th–17th centuries it had fully acquired the modern senses "barbarous, bizarre, grotesque". The word thus carries its whole social history inside it: every time it is used of behaviour, it quietly reminds the listener that the behaviour is being classed as foreign — as belonging to the out-lands beyond the bounds of civilised custom. In the verse-portrait this residual geography is precisely what the poet wants: Bao-yu's strangeness is not merely odd, it is unplaceable, alien, "out-land". SYNONYM BOUNDARIES AND REGISTER The synonym set helps pin down the precise flavour. "Strange" is the broadest word, simple unfamiliarity with no judgement attached. "Bizarre", a French loanword, implies wildly incongruous deviation from the norm, the grotesque and the surprising. "Eccentric" (Greek "ekkentros", "out of the centre") suggests a person whose oddness is harmless, a quirk of character rather than a trespass. "Exotic" carries glamour — foreign, but attractive and intriguing. "Odd" is mild and statistical. "Weird" suggests the uncanny and supernatural. "Alien" suggests a different species or world. "Outlandish" is stronger and more pejorative than all of these: it retains its literal geography, so that strangeness feels like trespass from another country — it is the voice of settled society scandalised by the visitor from the out-lands. In the novel it is the perfect word for the narrator's rueful, mocking catalogue of Bao-yu: a boy whose very virtue is that he does not belong to the world that judges him.
