expressly (/ɪkˈsprɛsli/) is an adverb with one central meaning — ‘in express, i.e. explicit, terms; clearly and intentionally said or done’ — and one important extension, ‘for the specific and stated purpose; on purpose; especially’. It is the adverbial form of the adjective express in its sense of ‘explicitly set forth’ (an express term, an express command), and it stands in the same word-family as expression and express the verb (‘to press out, to utter’).

Primary sense — ‘explicitly, in so many words’. Used with verbs of saying, forbidding, requiring or granting, expressly marks the utterance as unmistakable: ‘he was expressly forbidden to enter’, ‘the law expressly provides that…’, ‘Dante himself expressly connects the origin of miniature-painting with Paris’ (Pater). The word insists that the content is not implied, inferred or to be guessed — it has been pressed out into plain words.

Secondary sense — ‘deliberately, for that very purpose’. By a natural slide from ‘stated clearly’ to ‘intended explicitly’, expressly often means ‘on purpose and for no other reason’: ‘I came expressly to see you’ (that was the whole point of the journey); ‘the room was expressly designed for concerts’ (built precisely for that use). In this sense it overlaps purposely, specifically and on purpose.

Domain senses and extensions.

  • Law and contract: the distinction between express and implied terms makes expressly a drafting word — ‘expressly agreed’, ‘expressly excluded’, ‘unless expressly stated otherwise’. A clause operates expressly when its words carry the obligation; anything else is implication, inference or trade practice.
  • Statute and regulation: ‘expressly permits/requires/prohibits’ is standard in legislative drafting and in constitutional commentary, marking powers or duties given in so many words.
  • Theology and philosophy: ‘expressly commanded’, ‘expressly revealed’, ‘expressly renounced’ appear in doctrinal texts to rule out allegorical loosening.
  • Rhetorical register: in plain prose the word adds formality and emphasis; in speech it sounds deliberate if slightly bookish (‘I expressly told you…’).

Semantic bridge. Underneath every sense lies the Latin perfect participle expressus, ‘pressed out’ (ex- ‘out’ + premere ‘to press’). What is pressed out of a container is released and distinct; by metaphor what is pressed out of the mind is uttered — language, in the Latin image, is the squeezing of thought into speech, and from ‘pressed out, distinct’ the adjective express came to mean ‘explicitly put’, from which expressly takes all its force: explicit, deliberate, done for that stated purpose.

Register and nuance. Expressly is the formal partner of explicitly, with an added note of intention and authority: ‘explicitly’ describes the plainness of a statement, ‘expressly’ the deliberate, commitment-bearing character of it. In ordinary conversation it is the adverb of emphatic denial and emphasis (‘that was expressly not the plan’), which keeps its Latinate weight alive in direct speech.
