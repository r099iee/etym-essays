- [14th–15th century] - "Assail" (from Old French assaillir, "to leap upon") enters English as a military verb meaning to attack by armed force; "unassailed" simply means not physically attacked — a neutral, martial description.

- [16th–17th century] - The verb widens into figurative warfare: to assail someone with words, arguments, doubts, or temptations; "unassailed" now describes a reputation, conscience, or mind that has escaped these verbal and moral assaults.

- [18th–19th century] - In Austen's prose, "unassailed" becomes a psychological and social term — a person's judgment left untouched by flattery or vanity, an unbreached mental fortress, carrying cool, ironic praise.

- [Contemporary sense] - "Unassailed" is a formal, somewhat rhetorical word used mainly in literary or journalistic prose for an idea, record, or position that stands wholly unchallenged and untroubled.
