The "going" sense of repaired is built from two Indo-European elements meeting in a single Latin compound. The first is the prefix *re- ("back, again"), the inseparable element of countless Latin verbs — referre ("to carry back"), reddere ("to give back"), redire ("to go back") — and the ancestor of the English prefix re- in return, recover, and repeat; its own PIE pedigree is debated (sometimes derived from *wret-, zero-grade of *wert- "to turn") and is best treated as a Latin-internal element. The second is the root *pəter- / *ph₂tér- ("father"), one of the most ancient kinship words in the whole family, whose reflexes span the Indo-European world: Sanskrit pitṛ ("father"; Rigveda), Hindi and Odia pita, Greek patēr, Latin pater, and, on the Germanic side, Proto-Germanic *fadēr — the ancestor of English father, German Vater, and Dutch vader.

The crucial step happens in Latin. From pater grew patria ("fatherland," the land of one's fathers), and in Late Latin the Church and the administration formed a new verb, repatriare, "to return to one's native land" — the word from which our modern repatriate descends directly. At this stage the meaning is still thoroughly concrete and patriotic: repatriating is going home to one's fathers, a physical and emotional return to the soil of the clan. It is worth pausing on the semantic content: the word originally described not any motion but a homecoming, a movement toward the place where one belongs by birth and inheritance — and that gravitational pull toward "one's proper place" is exactly what the modern sense quietly preserves.

Old French received the verb as repairer (and repatrier), and there the great widening began:

  • From homeland to any destination (12th–13th c.): in Old French the verb slipped from "to return to one's country" to "to return home, to go back," and then simply "to go, to betake oneself to a place" — the country of one's fathers receded, and what remained was the idea of proceeding, formally and purposefully, to wherever one's business or duty lay.
  • The English borrowing (14th c.): Middle English repairen took over the generalised sense, and by the fifteenth century repair was an established formal verb of motion in English, characteristically with "to": one repaired to one's chamber, to the hall, to dinner, to the church.
  • The age of standard formality (16th–18th c.): in the prose of the early modern and eighteenth-century period — letters, memoirs, legal and administrative narrative, and the novel — "repaired to" was unremarkable formal usage; it described the dignified, purposeful movement of gentlemen, servants, courts, and armies to their appointed stations, and it is exactly this register that Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone deliberately recreates when a functionary "repaired to take up his duties."
  • The retreat (19th–20th c.): as the plain verb "go" and its informal synonyms took over everyday usage, the "going" sense of repair became increasingly literary and archaic; by the twentieth century it survived mainly in historical fiction, in ceremonious or mock-formal prose, and in the shadow of its far commoner homograph.
  • The homographic split: meanwhile the other repair — "to mend," from Latin reparare — grew from a minor medieval borrowing into the dominant modern word. The result is a classic etymological doublet: two verbs of unrelated origin (one "to return home," one "to make ready again") converged on the identical spelling repair, and only context now separates them.

The word thus carries its whole history folded into its tone. A modern sentence "he repaired to the dining room" is immediately archaic because the modern ear hears the wrong verb — fixing things — where the older prose heard a dignified homecoming. The ghost of the fatherland survives in the register: to repair to a place is still, beneath the surface, to go to the place where one properly belongs. That is why the word is perfect for the ceremonial, duty-bound movement in The Story of the Stone: the character is not merely going somewhere; he is proceeding, as a loyal functionary should, to the station his obligations require of him.
