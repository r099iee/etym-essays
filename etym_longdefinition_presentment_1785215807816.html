PRESENTMENT is the act of presenting, setting, or laying something before the mind or the eyes — a representation or depiction so vivid that it presents the fact itself rather than a summary of it. In the sentence before us, from a biographical study of John Stuart Mill, the word names the force with which "the lively and vivid PRESENTMENT of concrete fact" arrives as a "delightful and agreeable shock" to a mind absorbed in abstract speculation. The nuance is visual and experiential: presentment is not mere description or narration but a setting-forth that makes the concrete particular appear present before the mental gaze, with the liveliness of immediate encounter. The tone is appreciative and sensuous, contrasting the vivid presentation of fact with the thinness of abstraction — the concrete world, newly attended to, presents itself with the freshness of something seen for the first time. The word is deliberately high and formal, and it carries into this aesthetic context a gravity inherited from its long career in law.

  • Sense 1 — the general aesthetic sense: "the act of setting something before the mind or the eyes; a representation, depiction, or rendering," as in a vivid presentment of life, a graphic presentment of a scene, the dramatic presentment of a character. This is the sense Mill uses, and it is the sense in which the word survives in literary and aesthetic criticism.
  • Sense 2 — the legal sense (grand-jury presentment): in English law, "presentment" is the formal statement of an offense made by a grand jury on its own knowledge or observation, without a bill of indictment being preferred. It is distinguished from an indictment, which is framed by a prosecutor; the presentment is the jury's own laying-before of the facts to the court. This technical sense is old and durable and continues in Anglo-American law.
  • Sense 3 — the commercial/legal sense (presentment of a bill): in commercial law, "presentment" is the formal offering of a bill of exchange, promissory note, or check to the drawee or maker for acceptance or payment; the presentment of a bill for payment is the act of presenting it in due form.
  • Sense 4 — the historical church/patronage sense: in older ecclesiastical usage, "presentment" meant the formal nomination or offering of a candidate to a benefice by a patron — the presenting of a person to a living, a sense now largely historical but illustrative of the word's range.
  • Historical bridge: all senses share the root idea of "placing before." The noun derives from the verb present, from Latin praesentare "to place before," built on praesens "being before, present." The aesthetic sense is the direct exhibition of an object to the mind; the legal sense is the formal laying-before of facts before a court or authority; the commercial sense is the laying-before of a bill for payment. The word's formality everywhere reflects the dignity of the occasions on which things are solemnly presented.
  • Register and connotation: bookish, formal, and slightly archaic in general prose; still a live technical term in law and commerce. In aesthetics it appears in collocations such as vivid presentment, graphic presentment, dramatic presentment, presentment of life. It is never casual; a writer who says presentment rather than presentation or depiction signals both elevation and precision.
  • Differentiating terms: presentation (the neutral, general act of offering or showing), representation (standing for something else, as a symbol for its object), depiction/portrayal (artistic rendering), exhibition (public display) — presentment is distinct in stressing the direct, formal, and vivid laying-before of the thing itself.

Form, derivatives, and pronunciation. Presentment is a noun formed with the suffix -ment from the verb present; it is not to be confused with presentiment ("a foreboding, an anticipation of something to come"), a word of entirely different origin and meaning. The plural presentments is regular. In RP the word is pronounced /pɹɪzˈɛntmənt/, with stress on the second syllable, matching the verb present rather than the noun present.
