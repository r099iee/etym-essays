BEWITCHED joins intensive prefix be- “upon, thoroughly” to the ancient and still-debated noun-verb family witch / wicche / wiccian. Its deep root is uncertain beyond Germanic, which is itself part of its story: English has always known what a witch does more surely than where the word comes from. From Old English sorcery-law through Middle English spell-casting to Shakespearean allure and modern romance, bewitched tracks how fear of magic softened into language for charm.

  • PIE *wek- (?) “to speak” and the witch problem (c. 4000–2500 BCE): Etymonline and OED agree the Germanic witch-family has no certain Indo-European cognate outside Germanic. A favoured but unproven guess links it to PIE *wek- “to speak, utter” (Latin vox, Sanskrit vac-), via “one who speaks spells, consecrates.” Pokorny lists *wek- under speech-words. Rival links to *weyk- “to consecrate, set apart” or *weg- “to be lively” are even thinner. The honest statement is: Proto-Germanic *wikk- is isolated, and deeper origin remains debated.
  • Proto-Germanic *wikkōną / *wikkjaz “to practise sorcery; sorcerer” (c. 500 BCE–200 CE): Reconstructed from Old English wicca / wicce / wiccian, Old High German wicken “to soothsay,” Middle Dutch wicken, Old Norse vikki (?) “sorcery.” The stem meant secret craft with words and herbs, distinct from priestly religion. Gender was marked: masculine *wikkjaz versus feminine *wikkjōn-, a split English kept as wicca / wicce.
  • Old English wicca “male magician,” wicce “female magician, sorceress,” wiccian “to practise witchcraft” (pre-1150): OED records all three from the earliest glossaries and laws. Alfred’s laws condemn wiccan, Ælfric preaches against wiccecraeft, and leechbooks give remedies against wicca. The verb already meant to bewitch in effect, though the prefixed bewiccian is not yet the norm. Prefix be- / bi- from Proto-Germanic *bi- “around, upon” (PIE *h₁epi “near, upon”) waited to intensify it.
  • Middle English wicche and biwicchen / bewitch “to cast a spell upon” (c. 1225): Etymonline dates bewitch to c. 1225 as biwicchen. Layamon’s Brut and early sermons use it for malign enchantment. The be- is applicative-intensive: not merely to witch, but to witch upon a victim thoroughly. Past participle bewicched meant spell-bound, the direct ancestor of tale-formula “must be bewitched.”
  • Early Modern witch-craze and literary softening (1570s–1600s): Pamphlets, Reginald Scot’s Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584), and King James’s Daemonologie (1597) fix bewitched as legal-medical diagnosis for fits, wasting, and strange adhesion. Simultaneously Shakespeare (“bewitched with the rogue’s company”), Marlowe, and ballads use it for erotic and musical captivation, building the modern double sense of curse and charm together.

Modern bewitched thus keeps both poles: the goose that will not roast and the lover who cannot look away. Its debated root reminds us that magic-words often hide their own origins, while its transparent be- + witch + -ed shape makes its grammar clear — magic laid upon someone, completed and still holding.
