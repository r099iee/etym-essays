• Pork-pie: Similar because it is a traditional British pie with a distinctive sealed filling and a sturdy pastry case; differs because it is savoury (chopped pork and jelly in hot-water crust), eaten cold and in the hand, with none of the mince-pie’s sweet spiced Christmas content.
  • Mincemeat: Similar because it shares the mince element and names the very filling of the mince-pie; differs because it is the filling alone (dried fruit and suet, once literal minced meat), not the pastry case — a noun of substance rather than of dish.
  • Pie: Similar because a mince-pie is a subclass of pie, a pastry case with a filling; differs because pie is the generic container word covering sweet and savoury, hot and cold, and carries none of the mince-pie’s festive associations.
  • Tart: Similar because it is a small filled pastry; differs because a tart is shallower and usually open or latticed, with no enclosing lid, and tends toward fruit or custard rather than the spiced mincemeat of the closed mince-pie.
  • Pasty: Similar because it is a compact, portable, individually portioned pastry; differs because the pasty is a crimped semicircular hand-pie, typically savoury (Cornish pasty: beef and potato), not a sweet Christmas pastry.
  • Christmas pudding: Similar because it is a dense, spiced, fruity British Christmas dish that once contained meat; differs because it is a boiled or steamed pudding, not a pastry, and it is a single shared dessert rather than individual pies.
  • Eccles cake: Similar because it is a small British sweet pastry enclosing dried fruit (currants); differs because it is a flat, flaky, currant-filled cake from Lancashire, without suet, spices, or the Christmas institution-status of the mince-pie.
  • Turnover: Similar because it is a single-serving sweet or savoury pastry with a fruit or meat filling; differs because a turnover is made by folding one piece of pastry over the filling, with no separate lid, and lacks the mincemeat-and-spice identity.
