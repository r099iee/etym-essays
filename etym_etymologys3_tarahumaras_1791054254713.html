Tarahumaras is the English plural of the Spanish exonym Tarahumara for the Rarámuri people of the Sierra Madre Occidental in Chihuahua, Mexico, speakers of a Uto-Aztecan language famed for long-distance running. Its evolution is therefore a contact history between an Indigenous endonym, a colonial corruption, and an English plural, not an Indo-European derivation. Understanding it requires separating three layers: what the people call themselves, what Spaniards heard, and what English writers pluralised.

The deepest layer is Uto-Aztecan, one of the largest Indigenous families of North America, stretching from the Great Basin to Mesoamerica and reconstructed to c. 3000 BCE. Within it the Tarahumaran branch (Tarahumara, Guarijío, Tubar) diverged in northern Mexico during the first millennium CE as maize farmers adapted to canyon country. The endonym rarámuri (singular rarámuri, plural rarámuri or rarámuris in Spanish orthography) is universally reported as the self-designation. Early ethnographers — Norwegian Carl Lumholtz (1890s), Bennett and Zingg (1935) — glossed it as foot-runner, those who run fast, light feet or they who walk well, linking rara foot to running prowess in rarajipari ball races where men kick wooden balls over relays of tens of kilometres while women drive hoops with sticks. Later linguists caution that the running gloss may be folk etymology; in everyday Rarámuri rarámuri functions simply as people, humans, we, opposed to chabochi outsiders, mestizos. Both glosses can be true at different levels: etymological runner and synchronic person, with running as cultural emblem rather than literal morpheme-by-morpheme proof.

Phonetic reshaping explains the Spanish form:

  • Rarámuri to Tarahumara: Spanish speakers replaced initial r- (trilled, hard for Spanish in initial position without strong trill convention for native words) with t-, lenited intervocalic -r- to -h- (-j- in Spanish spelling), and suffixed -a by analogy with tribal feminines; thus rara-muri becomes tara-humara, with stress shifting to Tarahumára in Spanish and Tarahumara in English.
  • Orthographic fixing: 17th–18th c. Jesuit mission reports (Tomás Guadalajara, Ratkaj) write tarahumares, tarahumaras, tarahumara interchangeably; 19th-c. Mexican administration fixes tarahumara as the official ethnonym for district, mission and language.
  • English borrowing: first recorded 1870–1875 per Dictionary.com as Tarahumara for person and language; Cambridge and Merriam-Webster now list plural options Tarahumara, Tarahumaras, Rarámuri, Rarámuris, with Tarahumaras marking the fully nativised English -s plural used in this passage.

Semantic history in English mirrors changing anthropological fashion. Nineteenth-century travel writing presents the Tarahumara as cliff-dwelling primitives exemplifying ingenuity — kernel-baited fibres to snare birds, as here. Early twentieth-century physical anthropology (Balke 1965 on endurance physiology) recasts them as natural athletes with extraordinary running economy. Late twentieth-century ultra-running literature (Born to Run, Caballo Blanco, Copper Canyon races) romanticises them as light-footed philosophers of running, while Mexican indigenismo and contemporary human-rights reporting stress dispossession, logging pressure, drug-violence displacement and defence of communal lands. The bare bird-snare sentence belongs to the earliest stage: technical ingenuity catalogued without moralising, the people reduced to a plural actor who eats the birds that ate the kernels.

Key attestations and socio-cultural markers:

  • Jesuit missions (1607 onward): San Pablo Balleza and Sisoguichi missions fix the exonym in baptismal registers and grammars; Tarahumara language codified as Uto-Aztecan with five dialect zones.
  • Lumholtz Unknown Mexico (1902): popularises foot-race descriptions, kicking balls over mountain relays, tesgüino maize-beer feasts and violin music sanctifying labour.
  • Bennett and Zingg (1935): dream of every youth to become a great runner; night as day of the moon cosmology; soul in the heart strengthened by lungs.
  • Modern endurance science: Rarámuri huarache running cited in barefoot-running debates; Lorena Ramírez (light-footed woman) as international ultra icon.
  • Language vitality: c. 70,000 speakers of Rarámuri dialects today, largest Indigenous group in Chihuahua, with ongoing Spanish pressure and bilingual schooling.

The English plural Tarahumaras thus encodes a full colonial arc: Indigenous self as runner-person, Spanish ear as Tarahumara, English pen as countable tribe members who set kernel snares. Careful modern style often prefers endonym Rarámuri for people and reserves Tarahumara for language and Sierra region, but the historical text here uses the older exonymic plural without pejoration, simply to name the bird-catchers.
