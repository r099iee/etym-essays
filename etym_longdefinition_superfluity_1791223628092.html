Superfluity means an excess amount beyond what is needed, required or sufficient — a surplus, overflow or redundant abundance. It denotes both concrete surplus goods (food, money, possessions) that can be shared or wasted, and abstract excess (words, details, staff, evidence) that is unnecessary. The source sentence uses the concrete moral-economic sense: friends supply deficiencies from any superfluity of their own, i.e., from whatever they have left over after meeting their own needs.

Detailed senses:

  • Material surplus / plenty: An amount over and above necessity. Example: "a superfluity of corn," "living in superfluity." Often with of: superfluity of goods, of wealth, of food. Implies ability to give, store or squander. Near-synonym surplus, but superfluity carries literary-moral overtones of luxury.
  • Luxury / lavish abundance (moral sense): Excessive indulgence beyond decency, traditionally a vice in Christian and Stoic ethics. Example: "rebuking the superfluity of the court." Bridges to charity doctrine: what is superfluous to you belongs to the needy.
  • Redundancy / needless excess (abstract): More than is wanted for purpose. Example: "a superfluity of detail," "staff superfluity," "superfluity of evidence." In rhetoric, superfluity of words = verbosity, pleonasm. In law and HR, superfluity can euphemize redundancies / layoffs in British Commonwealth usage via superfluous posts.
  • Superfluities (plural): Luxuries, non-essentials. Example: "cutting superfluities from the budget." Common in 17th-19th-century prose: "the superfluities of life."
  • Theological / King James sense: "Superfluity of naughtiness" (James 1:21) = overflowing wickedness. Archaic but still quoted, showing the overflow metaphor extended to evil.

Semantic bridges: all senses share the image of liquid overflowing a container (Latin super-fluere). From literal overflow of rivers and cups, Romans extended to overflowing possessions, then to overflowing speech. English preserves both concrete and figurative overflow. The moral bridge — surplus implies obligation to share — explains why early travellers and missionaries constantly pair superfluity with liberality: superfluity is what makes generosity possible without self-harm.

Register and grammar: superfluity is formal, literary, slightly archaic; everyday speech prefers surplus, extra, excess, overflow, redundancy. Countable (a superfluity, superfluities) and uncountable (much superfluity). Typically followed by of + noun. Adjective superfluous is far commoner than the noun; adverb superfluously, noun superfluousness compete. Collocations: in the midst of superfluity, amidst superfluity, embarrassed by superfluity. Connotation is mildly negative (wasteful, needless) except when praising sharing of superfluity, where it becomes positive capability.

In short, superfluity always signals more-than-enough judged against need: whether praised as generous overflow to share with friends or criticized as wasteful luxury and wordy redundancy, it invites the ethical question of what to do with excess — hoard it, display it, trim it, or give it away.
