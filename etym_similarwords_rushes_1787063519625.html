• Reeds: Similar because both are tall, water-loving graminoid wetland plants growing in dense riparian stands; differs because reeds (family Poaceae) possess hollow, jointed, woody stalks with broad leaves and feathery inflorescences, whereas rushes (family Juncaceae) have solid, pithy, unjointed cylindrical stems.
  • Sedges: Similar because both thrive in damp marshlands and possess non-showy floral structures; differs because sedges (family Cyperaceae) are famed for having solid, distinctly triangular stems with edges ("sedges have edges, rushes are round"), whereas rushes feature smooth, round stems.
  • Cattails / Bulrushes: Similar because they share wetland habitats and are harvested for weaving and biomass; differs because cattails (genus Typha) produce massive, velvety, cigar-shaped brown seed heads and flat, broad leaves, possessing a far heavier structural profile than slender rushes.
  • Canes: Similar because they are flexible, tall wetland or waterside monocots used in traditional crafts and furniture; differs because canes are rigid, woody, hollow bamboos or grasses with prominent nodes, far firmer and less supple than herbaceous rushes.
  • Tules: Similar because they are round-stemmed freshwater wetland emergent plants utilized for indigenous basketry, matting, and boats; differs because tule specifically designates large giant bulrushes (Schoenoplectus acutus) of North American marsh ecosystems, being vastly thicker and taller than typical Old World rushes.
