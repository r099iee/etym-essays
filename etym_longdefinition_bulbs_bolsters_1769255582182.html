bulbs (plural noun; RP /bʌlbz/) — rounded, fleshy underground plant-storage organs (onion, tulip, daffodil, lily), and by extension any rounded swelling. bolsters (plural noun; RP /ˈbəʊl.stəz/) — long, firm, cylindrical pillows running the full width of a bed; by extension, anything that supports or props from beneath. The two words appear together in the text inside the Dwarf Trumpkin's oath, "Bulbs and bolsters!" 1. The words in this literary context. The Dwarf Trumpkin in C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian blurts out "Bulbs and bolsters!" as an oath of exasperation. Bulbs are the most earthy, stolid, unromantic objects a farmer's imagination can reach for — fat things that sit in the soil swollen with stored food; bolsters are the homely, full-width bed-pillows on which a tired head rests. To swear by them is to swear by kitchen-garden and bedroom reality, and the words' heavy, round, almost comic weights (single stressed syllables closed by two consonants) make them perfect material for a gruff dwarf's mock-curse. Trumpkin's tone is warm, baffled, and half-amused: his indignation is real, but his ammunition is vegetable and household. 2. Bulb — polysemy and technical senses. The word bulb spreads across four registers in modern English:
  • Botany (primary sense). A short underground stem sheathed in fleshy scale-leaves that store food — the onion, tulip, daffodil, and lily. Strictly distinguished from a corm (a solid swollen stem base without layered scales: crocus, gladiolus), a tuber (a swollen root or stem full of stored starch and dotted with "eyes": potato, dahlia), and a rhizome (a creeping horizontal stem: ginger, iris). Laypeople call them all "bulbs"; gardeners and botanists distinguish them precisely.
  • The light bulb (19th-century metaphor). Any round glass swelling that gives light — the word's most famous modern home, preserving the sense of roundness swollen from within: the light bulb is the onion given a job.
  • Anatomy and instruments. The bulb of the eye (the eyeball), the bulb of a thermometer, the hair-follicle bulb; and, in neurology, bulbar refers to the medulla oblongata (the brainstem "bulb"), as in bulbar palsy.
  • Mechanical and marine. A bulbous bow is the protruding underwater bulge that improves a ship's fuel efficiency; a bulb is any rounded swelling in a structure. The adjective bulbous ("swollen, rounded") and the noun bulbil (a small offset bulb) complete the family.
3. Bolster — polysemy. The word bolster spreads across three registers in modern English:
  • The bed pillow (primary sense). A long, firm, cylindrical pillow running the full width of a bed, placed under the ordinary pillows — the support on which a tired head rests; also a cushioned back-support on a sofa, and a padded length placed under an injured limb.
  • The verb (from the 16th century). To bolster something is to prop it up, support it, or strengthen it — "to bolster morale," "to bolster an argument," "to bolster the economy." The verb keeps the pillow's propping function and turns it abstract: what is bolstered is held up from beneath.
  • Technical and historical. A bolster is also a horizontal member of a beam support in building, a padded saddle piece, and a thick strip used in upholstery and joinery.
4. Semantic bridges and boundaries. The bridge from plant to light bulb runs through roundness: the word has always meant "a swollen, rounded thing," and each new sense is another swollen round thing given the old name. The bridge from pillow to verb runs through support: a bolster holds a head up, so to bolster is to hold anything up from beneath. In the noun set, bulb is set against corm, tuber, and rhizome by botanical structure; bolster is set against pillow (a shorter, individual head-support) and cushion (a seat-pad) by shape and position. Only a bolster can "bolster" confidence — the noun's verb, and the verb's noun, are inseparable.
