- Old Norse gapa - 'to open the mouth wide'; an expressive, imitative word from the Viking north
- 13th–14th c. - English borrows gape: the physical act of opening the mouth wide, and staring in wonder
- 15th–17th c. - 'to gape for/after' = to long or crave; Shakespeare plays on gape as wonder and as hunger
- 17th–19th c. - the adjective 'gaping' hardens: gaping wounds, gaping holes, gaping doors in Gothic and Romantic prose
- 20th c. - 'gap' (the opening itself) becomes a huge modern noun (gender gap, generation gap); 'gaping' runs alongside it
- Today - gaping still means wide-open and open-mouthed, from medical wounds to 'gaping holes' in budgets
