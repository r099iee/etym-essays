CHAIN — SHIMMERY.
1. PIE *sḱei- "to shine, gleam, be bright" (traditional Pokorny base, c. 4500–3000 BCE), with a notorious twin: the same traditional base is also credited with the words for "shade, shadow," so that light and its absence may share one root; modern scholars, notably Beekes, treat the joining of the shine-set and the shadow-set with caution.
2. Proto-Germanic *skīnaną "to shine" (whence Old English scīnan "to shine" → English shine; German scheinen "to shine, to seem," Gothic skeinan) and, beside it, the derived noun *skīmô "light, gleam, ray" (whence Old English scīma "light, brightness, splendour").
3. Proto-Germanic *skimarōną, an iterative verb formation with the frequentative *-r- — "to gleam repeatedly, to glimmer" (reconstructed on the attested Old English verb).
4. Old English scimrian / scimerian "to glimmer, glisten" (attested from c. 8th–10th c.), continuing the iterative *skimarōną.
5. Middle English schimeren "to glimmer, to shine tremulously" (14th c.).
6. English shimmer (verb "to gleam tremulously," 15th c.; noun "a gleam, a glimmer of light," 16th c.).
7. English shimmery, adjective formed from shimmer with the suffix -y "having the quality of" (attested from the 19th c.).
8. English shimmery "shining with a soft, slightly wavering light."

NUANCE NOTE: the one debated stage is the PIE base itself — whether the "shine" words (shine, shimmer, sheen, German scheinen, schön) and the "shade" words (Greek skiá "shadow," Sanskrit chāyā "shade," Old Irish scáth "shadow, protection") really belong to a single root *sḱei-. The traditional Pokorny grouping unites them; Beekes is cautious about the Greek shadow-word. The Germanic chain from *skīnaną / *skīmô through scimrian to shimmer is securely attested, and no stage has been invented or merged.
