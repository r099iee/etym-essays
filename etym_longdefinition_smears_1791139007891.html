smears is the plural of the noun smear (and the third-person singular of the verb to smear): blurry daubs, blotches or streaks of a greasy, oily or coloured substance spread across a surface, and by extension any blurred mark, optical blur or act of defamation. In Dickens — “like ruddy smears upon the palpable brown air” — candle-light seen through brown coal-fog becomes reddish blotches smeared across the air itself, as if the atmosphere were a dirty window.

Exhaustive senses in modern use:

  • Greasy / dirty blotch (core noun): a mark left by rubbing oil, blood, paint, food, lipstick, soot. “Grease smears on the wall, blood smears, fingerprints smeared.” Implies substance spread by contact, not a clean dot.
  • Blur / smudge / optical smear: an indistinct patch where detail is lost. “Ink smear, mascara smear, the headlights were smears in the rain.” Artists speak of smears of colour; photographers of lens smear and motion smear.
  • Medical smear (technical noun): a thin film of bodily material spread on a slide for microscopy. “Blood smear, Pap smear (cervical screening), sputum smear.” Here the blotch is deliberate and diagnostic, not accidental.
  • Defamation (figurative noun): a slur on reputation. “Smear campaign, smear tactics, political smears.” Treats honour as a clean sheet that can be dirtied. “The article was a smear.”
  • Verb senses (he smears): to daub (“smear butter, smear ointment”), to blur (“smear ink”), to slander (“smear a rival”), to crush (“smear the opposition” in sport), to spread thinly (“smear the slide”). Past: smeared; progressive: smearing.
  • Culinary / cosmetic (positive): “a smear of hummus, a smear of mustard” — fashionable menu language for a deliberate dab. The dirt is revalued as art.

Grammar, collocation and nuance:

  • Countability: countable blotches (“three smears”) vs uncountable substance (“covered in smear”). Adjectives: ruddy, bloody, greasy, oily, faint, blurry.
  • Verbs with it: leave, wipe away, clean off, spread, blend. Prepositions: smear of blood, smear on glass, smear across sky.
  • Register: everyday for dirt and cosmetics; technical in medicine and optics; pejorative-political for slander. Dickens’s aesthetic use (light as smear) is literary.
  • Connotation: usually messy, accidental, degrading — except medical (neutral-useful) and culinary (playful-artful).
  • Distinctions: a stain soaks in; a smear sits on top and can be wiped. A spot is round and neat; a smear is elongated by rubbing. A libel is legal; a smear is visceral.

The semantic bridge from grease to slander is contamination: clean ground + out-of-place matter = spoiled clarity, whether window, slide, sky or name. To call candle-glow “smears” is therefore to insist that even light in industrial London is dirtied — ruddy (reddish-healthy) and smeared (soiled) at once. Mastering smears means seeing both the finger-mark and the slur in the same greasy stroke.
