Draught (British spelling; American draft) is a noun whose many senses all radiate from one idea: something that is drawn. In "Could there be a draught from that little hole?" Bilbo feels a current of air moving out of the crack in the cliff — air that is "drawn" through the passage by the difference of pressure. The word's whole family of meanings — a drink, a ship's depth, beer from a cask, a sketch, a haul of fish, a board game — are all "drawings" of one kind or another, and the history of the word is the history of how English reused the noun of drawing for each new thing that gets drawn.

Primary senses.

  • A current of air: air drawn through a chimney, door, window, or hole — "there's a draught," "sit out of the draught." The sense in Tolkien's sentence, and the most common everyday use in Britain today.
  • A single act of drinking; the amount drunk at once: "a draught of ale," "to drink at one draught," "a sleeping draught" (a dose of medicine). What is drawn up into the mouth from a cup or bottle.
  • The depth of water a ship draws: "a draught of twenty feet" — the depth to which the hull is "drawn down" into the water by its own weight; a fundamental measurement of naval architecture.
  • Beer on draught: ale or stout drawn from a cask (not bottled): "draught Guinness," "draught beer." The sense that has kept the British spelling alive in American English ("on draught" at bars).
  • A preliminary version or sketch: "a draught of the letter," a draughtsman's plan — what is drawn with a pen. In modern English this sense is almost always spelled draft (both UK and US for documents), though draught survives in UK bank "draughts" (written orders to pay).

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Draught animals: horses, oxen, and mules that draw (pull) loads — "draught horse," "draught ox"; the sense of animals as living engines of traction.
  • A draught of fish: a haul or catch drawn up in a net — biblical "a great draught of fishes" (Luke 5:6), where the sense is the whole catch drawn to shore.
  • Draughts (plural, UK): the board game known in America as checkers, from the pieces being "drawn" or moved diagonally across the board.
  • Draught (of a chimney, furnace, engine): the flow of air or gas drawn through a fire to feed combustion; "forced draught," "natural draught" in engineering — the air-current sense professionalised.
  • Draught of lots: the drawing of lots for choice (rare, archaic); and, generally, any amount drawn at once — a draught of steam, a draught of water from a well.

The spelling split — draught vs draft.

  • Both spellings descend from the same Middle English word; the -aught spelling reflects the old past-participle pattern of the verb draw (cf. taught, caught, brought), and the modern British pronunciation /drɑːft/ keeps the historical vowel.
  • American English, following Noah Webster's spelling reforms of the early 19th century, standardised on draft for every sense; British English split the two: draft for documents and bank drafts, draught for air, drink, ships, animals, and beer.
  • In the US, draught survives mainly in the beer trade ("on draught"), in "draught horse," and in brand names — otherwise Americans write draft for everything, including a current of air ("drafty room").

Register and usage notes.

  • In Britain draught is an ordinary, homely word — a draughty room is a room with currents of cold air; a pint on draught is the natural way to order beer.
  • In America the word reads as British or archaic, which is exactly the flavour Tolkien wanted in 1937 when Bilbo wonders "Could there be a draught from that little hole?" — the old spelling and the old sense (air moving out of the dark) together make the moment feel ancient and real.
  • Derived words: draughty (adjective, "full of draughts"), draughtsman (one who draws plans), draughtiness; the verb draught "to design, sketch" is rare.

Why the word means all this. Every sense is a "drawing": the air is drawn through a hole; the drink is drawn into the mouth or from a cask; the ship draws its depth of water; the animal draws the plough; the fisherman draws in his net; the draughtsman draws his plan; the player draws his piece. The word is thus a perfect little museum of the verb draw, with each new technology or custom adding one more object to the list of things that get drawn — and the 19th-century spelling reform quietly dividing the collection between two nations.
