Earliest stage (uncertain). The pre-Latin ancestry of Latin floccus "a tuft of wool" is debated. Many dictionaries connect it (via a reconstructed base) to PIE *plek- "to plait, weave, twist" (the root of Latin plectere "to weave/braid" and English ply, complex, flax-family forms), on the logic that a tuft of wool is twisted or woven fiber. Others propose an expressive (sound-symbolic) origin—the word may be imitative of soft, fluffy texture—and note that Germanic cognates such as Old English flocc "a tuft, flock" point to a shared Northwest-European word whose deeper PIE status is unclear. No single reconstruction is certain; the word's status is "uncertain or expressive origin".

Derivation chain:

  • Proto-Indo-European *plek- "to plait, weave, twist" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) — proposed but debated source; a substrate or expressive origin is also possible. Marked uncertain.
  • Latin floccus, "a tuft of wool, a flock of wool, a lock of hair" (attested from c. 1st century BCE; in Cicero and Roman agricultural writers).
  • Latin flocculus, diminutive of floccus with the suffix -ulus, "a little tuft of wool, a small flock" (attested in Imperial Latin, e.g. in Pliny and later botanical/medical writers).
  • New Latin / scientific Latin flocculus (and flocculi), used in anatomy for the lobule of the cerebellum (flocculus cerebelli) and in astronomy for the patches on the sun (from the 19th–20th centuries).
  • English flocculus, borrowed directly from Latin as a scientific and literary word (attested in English from the 19th century onward).
  • FLOCCULUS (present-day English).

Debate note: The only uncertain stage is the pre-Latin one: the connection to PIE *plek- "to weave" is plausible but not proven, and an expressive origin cannot be excluded. From Latin floccus onward the record is clear and unbroken.
