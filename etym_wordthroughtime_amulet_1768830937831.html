- Late 16th century - a learned word - "amulet" enters English directly from Latin amuletum, the term Pliny the Elder used for charms worn against fever and sorcery; it begins as a bookish, quasi-medical word for a bodily protection charm.
- 17th-18th century - occult and travel literature - the word widens from classical remedy to any protective object worn on the person - pendant, ring, band - recorded with wonder in accounts of foreign and ancient religions.
- 19th century - the ethnographer's term - archaeology and anthropology adopt "amulet" as the neutral, scientific word for protective objects across cultures, setting it apart from the more ceremonial "talisman."
- 20th-21st century - technical and metaphorical - the word keeps its matter-of-fact, almost practical register (an amulet is equipment, not ornament), and extends to anything worn or carried as protection; its classical gravity makes it the favored word for the unsentimental magician.
