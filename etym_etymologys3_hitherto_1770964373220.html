The history of hitherto is really the history of two small, ancient words — hither and to — and of the moment when a language turned their spatial compound into a temporal one. The word belongs entirely to English: unlike most of the words in this deck, it was never borrowed from Latin or Greek, but was welded together on English soil out of native Germanic material. Its biography therefore tracks the deep history of the English language itself, from Indo-European deictic particles down to the legal and scholarly prose of the present day.

The first component, hither, descends from a very old demonstrative stem. Indo-European had a set of "this/here" deictic bases — *k̂e-, *k̂i-, *k̂o- — used to point at what is near the speaker. Latin cis "on this side" and hic "this" reflect them; Gothic hita "this" reflects the same family; and English's own pronoun he (originally a demonstrative meaning "that one") comes from it. The Germanic languages built a directional adverb "to here" on this stem with a suffix *-drē: the result was Proto-Germanic *hidrē, "hither, to this place", attested by Gothic hidrē, Old Norse heðra, and Old High German hedera. Old English inherited it as hider, recorded from the earliest Old English texts (eighth–ninth century), where it is the ordinary word for "to this place", used of movement toward the speaker.

In the Middle English period a small but telling sound-shift reshaped the word. Old English hider acquired a -th- spelling and pronunciation — hither — not by any regular sound change but by analogy with its counterpart thither (Old English þider, "to that place") and whither (Old English hwider, "to which place"). The three adverbs had formed a tidy little set — "to here, to there, to where" — and English speakers levelled them into hither, thither, whither. This is a favourite example of analogical levelling, the process by which languages make irregular forms regular because speakers expect symmetry. By the later Middle Ages the trio was fixed, and hither has kept its -th- ever since.

The second component, to, is even older. Its ultimate ancestor is the Indo-European directional particle *do, "to, toward", attested in Greek as the enclitic -de (in oíka-de, "homeward") and in Old Church Slavonic as do, "up to". In Proto-Germanic this became *tō, which surfaces in Old Saxon tō, Old High German zuo (modern zu), and Old Frisian tō. Old English tō is attested from the very beginning of the written record, with the full range of meanings — "to, toward, until" — that it still has. From being a simple preposition of direction, to became one of the most grammatical words in English: the marker of the infinitive ("to run"), of purpose ("to please you"), of comparison ("ten to one"), and of the dative (as in the old "give it to me" construction). English could scarcely form a sentence without it.

The compound hither + to appears in Middle English as hider-to and hither-to, meaning at first "up to this place". A man walked "hitherto" — he walked up to here. But medieval English, like every language, habitually mapped time onto space: events are conceived as places along a road, and to be "at this point" in a narrative is to be at this moment of it. Very quickly the compound crossed from space to time. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, "hitherto" meant "up to this time, until now", and this temporal sense became its only live one; the spatial reading survived only as a transparent relic and then vanished. The word settled into precisely the kind of writing that needed to draw a clean boundary with the past: chronicles, sermons, legal records, parliamentary rolls, and, later, history books and treatises.

The word's golden age was the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when formal prose — historical narrative above all — prized its gravity. A historian writing "the true extent of the damage has hitherto been unknown" was doing something no informal word could match: marking a definitive end to a state of ignorance and, by implication, the beginning of a new knowledge. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, hitherto retreated from speech into the written registers where precision about the past is at a premium — academic monographs, legal judgments, journalism of a certain formality — while casual English made do with "previously", "so far", and "up to now". In the source passage, Yu-cun's ambition had "hitherto been at some pains to conceal": the adverb erects the boundary across his whole past up to the moment the quatrain gives him away. It is exactly the word a formal narrative uses to mark the point at which silence about the past ends.

Summary of the evolution:
  • Formal chain (hither): PIE *k̂e-/*k̂i- → Proto-Germanic *hidrē → Old English hider → Middle English hither (by analogy with thither).
  • Formal chain (to): PIE *do → Proto-Germanic *tō → Old English tō → Middle English to → modern English to.
  • Compound: hither + to → hitherto, spatial "up to here" (Middle English) → temporal "until now" (established by the 14th–15th centuries).
  • Sound and morphology: the -th- of hither is analogical, not phonetic; the compound is written as one word from the medieval period onward.
  • Semantic drift: "to this place" → "up to this time" (the classic time-as-distance metaphor); register rose from ordinary speech to formal, legal, and scholarly prose.
  • Socio-cultural engines: medieval chronicles and legal documents; eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historical narrative; modern academic and legal writing.
  • Literary attestations: Middle English chronicles and devotional prose; Elizabethan and Stuart legal and parliamentary records; Victorian historical prose; modern scholarship and journalism.
