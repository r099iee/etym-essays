Orcrist is an invented word with a perfectly documented "etymology" — but the documentation is of a different kind from that of ordinary English words. Its history is twofold: (1) the history of Tolkien's constructed Elvish languages (roots, sound patterns, and the moment of coinage), and (2) the history of the word within the fiction and within fandom. Both histories are real, verifiable, and worth telling in full — with the honest caveat that no stage of this word precedes its creator.

1. Tolkien's constructed-language method. J. R. R. Tolkien built his Elvish languages genealogically: a small set of primitive roots (functionally like PIE roots, but his own invention) generate, by regular phonetic rules, the attested languages Quenya (High-elven) and Sindarin (Grey-elven). He recorded these roots in working documents — most famously "The Etymologies", written in the late 1930s, in the very years of The Hobbit's success, and published posthumously in The Lost Road (1987). The two elements of Orcrist are set out in that material.

2. The "orc" element. The root is ŌROK, glossed "demon, orc" in Tolkien's etymological notes. From it descend Quenya urko/orco "orc, goblin" and Sindarin orch, plural yrch. The Sindarin word underlies the English "orc" that Tolkien chose for his monsters: in Appendix F of The Lord of the Rings he notes that the modern English word is the Old English orc "demon" (attested in Beowulf), deliberately adopted to translate the Elvish term — a rare case of a real medieval word being drafted into a constructed etymology.

3. The "crist" element. The second element comes from the root KIRIS, "to cut, cleave", which in Tolkien's derived vocabulary yields crist "cleaver (sword)". The compound orch + crist = "orc-cleaver" is formed exactly as a Sindarin noun phrase, with the determinative element first — the same pattern as Glamdring ("Foe-hammer"). Tolkien's own gloss in The Hobbit ("Goblin-cleaver") fixes the intended reading, and later notes confirm the compound analysis. A nuance worth stating: Tolkien's languages evolved over decades — the "Noldorin" of the 1930s was renamed and re-conceived as Sindarin in the 1950s, and some root details were revised — but the meaning of the name is stated twice in the story itself and is not in doubt.

4. The word in the fiction. In-universe, Orcrist is very old: forged in Gondolin before the city's fall in the First Age, when the smiths of Turgon made blades that would shine with pale fire when orcs were near. The sword lay lost through the centuries — survivors of Gondolin probably carried it into the wilds — until Thorin's company found it, with Glamdring and Sting, in the trolls' hoard in 2941 of the Third Age. Elrond names it aloud at Rivendell; in Goblin-town its reputation precedes it ("Biter and Beater!"); Thorin wields it in the escape; Mirkwood's Elves confiscate it when Thorin is captured; and after the Battle of Five Armies the Elvenking lays it on Thorin's tomb, with the songs saying it gleamed ever in the darkness if foes pressed near. The name's in-universe careers are therefore three: Elvish weapon-name, goblin dread-word, and funerary honour.

5. Phonetic record. As constructed Sindarin, Orcrist follows the sound-patterns Tolkien set out (LotR Appendix E): stress falls on the first syllable, the -rc- cluster is pronounced /rk/, and final -t is a crisp stop — hence fandom-standard /ˈɔːrkrɪst/, roughly "OR-krist". This is a pronunciation convention for an invented language, not a historical sound change.

6. Social and cultural life of the word. 1937: The Hobbit publishes the name at Rivendell, and early readers take it simply as a fantasy name. 1950s: The Lord of the Rings and its appendices train a generation of readers in Elvish naming habits, and Glamdring/Orcrist begin to be analyzed as compounds. 1970s–80s: Tolkien's linguistic papers reach print (the Etymologies in 1987), grounding fan philology — "Orcrist" is now read as textbook Sindarin. 2000s–2010s: Peter Jackson's film trilogy (2012–2014) puts Thorin's sword on screen; replicas, merchandising, and licensed games (e.g., MMORPGs that list Orcrist as a found weapon) complete its passage into global pop culture. The word's modern "meaning" is shorthand for Tolkienesque depth: a name that carries its own grammar, its own history, and the smell of a hidden city.

7. Is it etymology? Honest note: reconstructing ŌROK and KIRIS and quoting Elvish forms is not historical linguistics — the roots are Tolkien's invention, documented in his own manuscripts of the 1930s. No stage should be mistaken for an attested ancient form. What makes the word etymologically interesting is precisely that it has a real, checkable "etymology" of the constructed kind: a documented root system, regular derivation, an orthographic tradition, defined stress, and a semantic history inside one book — a case study in how invented names can carry the full apparatus of etymology.

Summary. Coined in 1937 from two Elvish roots concerned with cutting and with goblins; narrated — within the fiction — as a weapon from the First Age; re-analyzed by fandom from the 1970s–80s; and re-circulated globally through film and games since the 2010s: Orcrist is a word whose entire timeline is on the record, from the moment its author wrote it down.
