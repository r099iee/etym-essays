Barred is the past tense and past participle of the verb bar, and an adjective in its own right, with four broad sense-groups: (1) physically fastened or closed with bars (a barred window); (2) blocked, cut off or obstructed (a barred road; “the way was barred” — exactly the sense of the source sentence, where passage is closed); (3) excluded or prohibited by rule, law or authority (barred from office, barred from entering; statute-barred, time-barred); (4) decorated or marked with bars, i.e. transverse stripes or bands (barred feathers, the Barred Owl; in heraldry, a shield “barred” or divided bar-wise). All four descend from the noun bar (a rod, beam or barrier), and the bridges between them are easy to trace once the image of a beam laid across a doorway is kept in mind.

1. Fastened with bars. At its most physical, barred describes a door, gate, window or hatch secured by one or more heavy beams or iron rods slid across it: a barred door, barred windows, a gate barred and bolted. The picture is the medieval door-bar — the horizontal baulk that makes a door impassable from outside and must be drawn before entry. This is the oldest sense of the participle (the verb bar is attested in English from c. 1300 meaning “to fasten with bars”), and it is the sense that keeps the word’s mental picture most alive; in this use barred is close to bolted, locked and secured, but keeps the specific image of the beam.

2. Blocked, obstructed. From “to fasten shut with bars” the verb extended, already in the 14th century, to “to block, hinder, prevent passage”: a road barred by a fallen tree, a river barred by ice, a mountain pass barred by enemies, a way barred by orders — exactly the source sentence’s “the way was barred”. In this sense barred is a near-synonym of blocked, obstructed, impeded and closed, but with a more deliberate colouring: a barred way has been actively shut to you, by barrier or by authority, rather than merely accidentally blocked.

3. Excluded, prohibited. From the barrier-image comes the most productive modern sense: to bar = to prevent or exclude someone from a place, right, office or activity — barred from the profession, barred from the country, barred from competing, barred from entering the courtroom. The bridge runs through courts and law, and it is worth making explicit. The “bar” of a court is the rail that separates the benches of judges and counsel from the body of the hall; to appear “at the bar” is to stand formally before the court; and by extension the whole legal profession is “the Bar”, its members barristers. To “bar” a person from a right or claim is therefore to shut them out at the formal boundary — the same gesture as laying a beam across a door. Legal specialisations: a claim that can no longer be brought because the time limit has expired is time-barred or statute-barred (a claim “barred by the statute of limitations”); a lawyer expelled from the profession is disbarred (literally removed from the Bar). In British informal usage “he’s barred” means he is banned from a pub, club or venue (20th century), and on social media “barred” = banned or suspended.

4. Marked with bars. Independently of fastening and blocking, bar and barred developed the visual sense “a transverse stripe or band”. In heraldry, barred describes a shield bearing one or more horizontal bars (the heraldic bar, distinct from the broader fess; the thin variant is the barrulet, and the placement is “bar-wise”) — regulated in the heraldic treatises of the 15th–16th centuries. In natural history the adjective is purely descriptive of transverse markings: barred feathers, barred wings, barred shells and fish; the Barred Owl (Strix varia) of North America, named in 19th-century ornithology, is the most famous bearer of the word. In modern astronomy the barred spiral galaxy (e.g. the Milky Way itself) is named for the central bar-shaped structure of stars around which the arms wind — a 20th-century technical use. The bridge of all these is simple shape-matching: a barred surface looks like a grate of bars, a straight transverse beam drawn across the field.

5. Grammatical and usage notes. As an adjective barred is identical in form to the participle and past tense; it is not normally graded (we rarely say “more barred”), and its natural opposites are unbarred (of doors, gates, ways), admitted, permitted, allowed (of persons and claims). The participle generates the construction barred from + noun/gerund with great frequency in law, sport, immigration and journalism. Pronunciation is /bɑːd/ — a single syllable in RP, with the long vowel of bar; the double -rr- is purely orthographic (marking the stem’s short syllable in other dialects) and is not pronounced. Register: fully standard across physical, legal and informal senses; the physical sense is vivid and everyday, the legal sense is dignified and exact, the striped sense is specialist (heraldry, ornithology, astronomy) but instantly understandable.
