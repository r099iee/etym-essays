Plumpish is a delicate, hedged adjective: the suffix -ish tells the reader that the girl is not fat, not even plump outright, but merely tending toward a pleasant roundedness. In this passage the first of the three young women introduced beside Dai-yu is “of medium height and slightly plumpish, with cheeks as white and firm as a fresh lychee.” The word is a marker of youthful health and noble beauty, not of excess. Within the aesthetic system of the novel—where beauty is calibrated by jade-pale skin, fine bones, and a fullness that bespeaks good feeding—plumpishness signals that a girl has been raised in comfort, free of want, and cushioned by the soft diet of a great house. It implies a body that has never known famine: cheeks “white and firm,” giving the face a radiant, luminous surface. The tone is gently affectionate and appraising rather than critical; it is the narrator’s shorthand for a young woman who is pleasingly, invitingly soft. The word must be distinguished from its near-synonyms. Fat is blunter and almost always carries censure, suggesting sloppiness or excess; overweight is clinical and modern, measuring the body against medical norms; chubby is cutesy and usually reserved for children or round faces; stout suggests a solid, often middle-aged heaviness; corpulent is an inflated, formal word for pronounced fatness; while portly adds a dignified, almost stately bulk. Plumpish alone keeps the evaluative centre exactly on the threshold: more than slender, less than plump—a soft middle ground whose borders are drawn with kindness. Its suffix -ish performs the same hedging work as in greenish or oldish: it blurs the boundary and softens the verdict. In register, plumpish is colloquial, gently literary, and faintly old-fashioned; it belongs to the descriptive prose of nineteenth- and twentieth-century fiction rather than to medical or scientific writing, where BMI scales and the word overweight have displaced it. It collocates naturally with cheeks, face, build, frame, and figure (“a plumpish face,” “slightly plumpish in build”), and it is almost always softened by degree adverbs—rather, slightly, somewhat—as in the text’s own “slightly plumpish.” In the novel it belongs to the register of the connoisseur of persons: the admiring survey of a matchmaker, an aunt, or a narrator who reads bodily softness as the material evidence of a gentle, well-fed, and virtuous life.
  • Primary sense: Somewhat plump; slightly full and well-rounded in form, whether of a person, an animal, a fruit, or a soft object (“a plumpish face,” “a plumpish cushion”).
  • Degree and hedging: The suffix -ish marks approximation and softens judgement; plumpish is deliberately imprecise, always nearer to plump than to slender, never approaching fat.
  • Register and distribution: Gentle, affectionate, faintly old-fashioned literary description; rare in medical writing, where overweight holds the field.
  • Morphology: plump + the Germanic suffix -ish (Old English -isc, from Proto-Germanic *-iska-, ultimately PIE *-isko- “pertaining to, rather like”), the same suffix as in greenish, oldish, tallish.
Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings: (1) Of persons and bodies—the hedged, kindly word for a full figure, positioned between slender and plump; (2) of fruit, birds, and small animals—pleasingly rounded and well-fed, preserving the older positive sense of thriving abundance; (3) of soft objects—slightly bulging or puffed up (“a plumpish sofa cushion”); (4) as deliberate tact or euphemism—a gentle alternative to overweight or fat in twentieth- and twenty-first-century usage, keeping the old courtly politeness. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: the base plump entered English in the seventeenth century for blunt, massive objects and migrated in the eighteenth century to pleasingly full bodies; adding -ish diluted the claim to an approximation, creating exactly the soft, affectionate, borderline word the novel needs. In the passage it is the narrator’s tactful way of making the girl wholly pleasing—firm, luminous, comfortably nourished, and never coarsely fat.
