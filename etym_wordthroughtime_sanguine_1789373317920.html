- Late 14th Century (Chaucerian Middle English) - Borrowed from Old French *sanguin*; used strictly in medieval Galenic medicine and astrology to denote a physical temperament dominated by the blood humor, characterized by a ruddy complexion, amorousness, and love of luxury.
- 15th to 16th Century (Renaissance & Early Modern English) - Operates across multiple physical registers: used literally for blood-red textiles, heraldic stains, red drawing chalk (*crayon sanguine*), and poetically for blood-drenched battlefields and martial carnage.
- 17th Century - Begins to shed purely physical humoral pathology; writers like Francis Bacon and John Milton apply the word to human dispositions that are warm, cheerful, bold, and enthusiastic without requiring literal medical diagnosis.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era & Enlightenment) - As humoral medicine is dismantled by post-Harveian physiology, the word becomes established in literary and philosophical prose as a refined synonym for cheerful confidence and hopeful expectation.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Solidified in formal novelistic discourse (Jane Austen, Charles Dickens, George Eliot) to characterize an unfailing buoyancy of mind or business confidence, often contrasted with melancholic or bilious temperaments.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains its high literary and intellectual register; ubiquitous in financial journalism, international diplomacy, and political analysis in calibrated idioms like "cautiously sanguine" or "hardly sanguine."
