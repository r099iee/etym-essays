Bedouins (plural noun; also Bedouin as a singular or collective, adjective bedouin) — /ˈbɛdʊɪnz/. The traditionally nomadic, tent-dwelling Arab peoples of the desert and steppe — chiefly of the Arabian Peninsula, the Syrian desert, Sinai and the desert margins of the Levant and North Africa — who historically lived by herding camels, sheep and goats, by caravan trade and by raiding, bound together by tribal law, hospitality and a rich oral poetry. In the source sentence — "A nomad stock, like the Bedouins of Arabia" — the word stands for the classic example of a nomadic people whose vigour and intelligence, lacking the continuity of a settled food supply, could not be banked toward civilization.

I. Who the Bedouins are

  • The Bedouins are the badw ("desert, open country") people — the Arabic word badawī means exactly "dweller in the desert or steppe," contrasted with the settled townsman. The distinction is between two modes of life, pastoral-nomadic and agricultural-urban, within one Arab world.
  • Traditional society was organised in patrilineal tribes and clans (e.g. the ʿAnaza, Shammar, Ruwala and Awlad ʿAli), each with its shaykh, its tribal law (customary ʿurf alongside the sharia applied by qadis), and its institutions of blood-feud and protection.
  • Economic life centred on camel, sheep and goat herding, moving with the rains and pastures (transhumance), on the caravan trade (Bedouin in 19th- and 20th-century Arabia guided and taxed the great pilgrimage caravans), and on raids (ghazw) that, under the old code, were a legitimate sport and a redistributive instrument rather than mere theft.
  • Material culture is distinctive: the black goat-hair tent (bayt shaʿr, "house of hair"), the camel (the "ship of the desert"), the saddle, the sword and rifle, coffee hospitality, and a spectacular oral poetic tradition — qaṣīda and nabati poetry — which medieval Arab grammarians preserved as the purest evidence of the Arabic language.

II. Polysemy and usage notes

  • Number and form. English uses Bedouins as the common plural, but Bedouin also serves as a collective plural (like "the Bedouin of the desert") and occasionally as a singular ("a Bedouin"). The spellings Beduin, Bedoween and Bedawin also occur, mirroring dialectal Arabic and French bédouin.
  • Adjective. Lowercase bedouin is a standard adjective: bedouin camps, bedouin poetry, bedouin hospitality. The capitalised form is reserved for the people.
  • Figurative extension (dated). From the 19th century the word was loosely applied to any wanderer, rover or unsettled person ("city bedouins" in some Victorian journalism). The extension follows the visible surface — mobility and tent-life — and is today avoided by careful writers, who reserve the word for the Arab peoples and their diaspora.
  • Pejorative versus admiring registers. European usage has swung between romanticism and contempt: Orientalist travel writing admired the "noble Bedouin" (free, generous, poetic), while colonial administrators dismissed them as brigands. Both stereotyping poles are worth recognising; modern Bedouin communities often use the name proudly as an ethnonym.

III. The Bedouins and history

  • Bedouin tribes supplied the core of the 7th-century Arab conquest armies and of the early Islamic polity; the contrast between desert and sown has organised Middle Eastern history for millennia, and the historian Ibn Khaldun made the tension between nomadic badw and settled ḥaḍar the engine of his theory of the rise and fall of dynasties.
  • In the modern period, oil wealth, state borders and education have driven mass sedentarization: today most Bedouins are settled or semi-settled citizens of Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Egypt, Israel, Syria, Iraq, Libya, Tunisia, Algeria and the Gulf states, while some continue full nomadism. Bedouin identity — genealogy, poetry, hospitality, tribal memory — remains a powerful living heritage; in 2008 UNESCO inscribed the Bedouin oral poetry of the Arabian Peninsula on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, and related Bedouin traditions of weaving and coffee hospitality have followed.

IV. Why the word fits the sentence

Durant wants a people that is undeniably intelligent, courageous, generous and artistic yet remains, by his argument, below the threshold of civilization because its food supply is discontinuous. The Bedouins are the perfect exhibit: they produced magnificent poetry and some of the finest characters in Arab history, but without agriculture’s stored surplus their energies were consumed by "the perils of the hunt and the tricks of trade." The word also does historical work — it names the most famous nomadic stock on earth, so the example carries its own evidence.
