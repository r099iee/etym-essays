- 15th c. - tapisserye enters English from Anglo-Norman tapisserie; it still names a woven covering that could lie on a floor or hang on a wall, the craft itself being mid-migration from carpet to wall-hanging.
- 16th–17th c. - the great age: tapestries are the pictorial wool-and-silk wall-hangings of noble halls - chronicles of hunts, battles, and heraldry - and the word carries courtly wealth, insulation, and story at once.
- 17th–19th c. - as royal display declines, the word keeps its ceremonial dignity; the Bayeux Tapestry and the great medieval sets become fixed monuments, so tapestry still means a woven record, portable and hangable.
- 19th c. - the figurative sense blooms: the tapestry of history, the rich tapestry of life; the word stops naming only cloth and begins naming any interwoven whole of many threads made one picture.
- 20th–21st c. - two registers run side by side: the concrete art-historical sense (a set of tapestries as museum treasures) and the literary metaphor; the plural tapestries stays the standard form, evoking the woven memory of a lost royal house.
