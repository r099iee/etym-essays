The gesture before the word. The story of groped begins not in a sentence but in a gesture: an open hand reaching out to seize. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *gʰrebʰ-, "to seize, grasp, reach out," preserved in the Indo-Iranian branch in Sanskrit grah- / grabh- (gr̥bhṇā́ti, "he seizes"), the source of Hindi grahaṇ, "seizure, taking, eclipse," and of Avestan grab-, "to seize." The root is a root of the hand — of taking hold, gripping, catching — and it is one of the very oldest items in the Indo-European vocabulary of action, attested on both the eastern and western edges of the family. In the Germanic branch the root, in a form extended with the glide -y- (a variant *gʰreyb-), became the strong verb *grīpaną, "to grasp, seize," which spread across the whole Germanic world: Old Norse grípa (Swedish gripa, Danish gribe), Gothic greipan, Old High German grīfan (German greifen), Dutch grijpen, and Old English grīpan, which survives in modern English grip and gripe. The sense is still active seizure: to grip is to close the hand upon something known and present.

The derived weak verb of repeated reaching. From this strong verb the prehistoric Germanic dialects then formed a derived weak verb, *graipōn-, "to feel about with the hands," built from the same root with the -ōn- verbal suffix and a different vowel grade. The morphology matters: where the strong verb *grīpaną named a single completed act of seizing, the derived weak verb had a repeated, habitual flavour — groping again and again, striking out again and again with the hand in search of something unseen. The distinction is exactly the one English still feels between grip (a firm, single hold) and grope (a repeated, uncertain reaching). This derivative yielded Old English grāpian, "to feel about with the hands, to grope," attested in the earliest English prose, with close cognates in Old Saxon grēpon and Middle Dutch grāpen. The word is native English, inherited from the deepest stratum of the language, and it has kept its plain, homely physicality for over a thousand years.

Middle and early modern English. In Middle English the verb became gropen, pronounced much as today, and its range broadened slightly: it could mean to feel, to search by touch, to test or probe the depth or nature of a thing, and even, in some technical usage, to examine by the hand. By the early modern period the modern shape grope was fixed, and the word began to live the double life it still leads. On one side it remained the plain verb of the dark — groping along a wall, groping in a pocket, groping one's way down a stair. On the other, it became the great metaphor of ignorance and tentative inquiry: to grope after truth, to grope for words, to grope in the dark about a question was to move without light or certainty, the mind's hand stretched into the unknown. This metaphoric sense is attested from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and was loved by the moralists and essayists of the Enlightenment, for whom "groping in the dark" was the standing image of human reason before the torch of science was lit; it is the sense in which Milton speaks of man groping for his way, and in which eighteenth-century writers contrast the gropings of superstition with the clear light of knowledge.

The twentieth-century narrowing. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word extended further into tentative, halting progress — to "grope toward a solution," to "grope through a problem" — the uncertainty of endeavour rather than of panic. But the twentieth century also brought the change that dominates public usage today: the verb narrowed and darkened into the sexual sense. To "grope someone" — to touch them sexually and without consent — is recorded in colloquial and legal use from the later twentieth century, and the noun "a grope" followed. The narrowing is a natural, if grim, application of the old root sense of blind tactile reaching: the unwanted touch is a reaching-out with the hand, and the word's physical core was always present in it. But the effect on the word's public life has been profound: in modern reporting, legal language, and everyday speech, "grope" and "groping" now most often name sexual assault, and the word's old literary innocence — the child fumbling for the light switch, the traveller feeling along the wall — can no longer be assumed.

This is why Tolkien's sentence, "Bilbo groped about in the dark" (1937), reads today with a faint historical distance: the word was still innocent enough in 1937 to be used of a hobbit in a cave, and the plain, homely, physical sense — reaching with the hands through absolute darkness — is exactly the sense the Old English grāpian carried a thousand years earlier. In the whole of its history the word has never needed to travel far from its concrete origin: from the seizing hand of the Indo-European root to the blind hand of the dark tunnel, the gesture is the same, and the word has carried it unchanged in its heart through every shift of register and sense.
