Bedewing is the present participle of bedew, a literary verb meaning “to moisten as with dew” — to cover lightly with drops of water, tears, sweat, or any fine liquid. It is rare in modern prose and carries a distinctly poetic, archaic, or mock-elevated flavour.

1. The literal sense

To bedew something is to sprinkle or moisten it with dew, or with drops resembling dew. The verb implies a light, scattered application rather than soaking: grass is bedewed at dawn, a leaf is bedewed with rain, a surface is bedewed with fine spray. The present participle bedewing describes the ongoing action or the condition produced by it.

  • Light moisture: a film or scattering of drops, not saturation.
  • Natural image: dawn, gardens, petals, and cool air.
  • Agency: dew, rain, spray, or tears can all do the bedewing.

2. The figurative and emotional sense

Because dew is associated with morning freshness and with tears, bedew readily becomes an image of gentle weeping or of a brow wet with sweat. “Bedewed with tears” is a stock poetic phrase; “bedewing my brow” in the source passage describes perspiration gathering on the forehead. The verb lends dignity and softness to bodily moisture, which is why it suits elevated or humorous-pompous registers.

  • Tears: cheeks or eyes bedewed with grief.
  • Sweat: a brow bedewed by exertion or fear.
  • Religious imagery: grace or dew bedewing the soul.

3. Botanical and agricultural uses

In older botanical and agricultural writing, bedew describes the action of dew or irrigation on plants. It can also mean to moisten seed beds or cuttings lightly. The word is largely superseded by sprinkle, mist, and moisten in practical contexts, but it survives in gardening prose that aims at a period flavour.

4. Grammar and word formation

Bedew is formed with the Old English prefix be-, which can mean “around, about” or add a transitive or intensive force, plus the noun dew. The same pattern appears in besprinkle, bedazzle, befriend, and belabour. The present participle bedewing is regular; the past tense and past participle are bedewed. The verb is transitive: one bedews something.

5. Register and connotation

Bedew is a “poetic” or “literary” word in modern dictionaries, and its use often signals a deliberate archaism. In ordinary speech one would say “my brow was sweating” or “the grass was wet with dew.” Bedewing therefore adds a note of mock refinement, which is exactly its effect in the comic passage: the narrator describes perspiration in the language of romance.

6. Semantic bridge

The bridge from dew to tears to sweat is associative rather than logical: all involve small drops of liquid on a surface. Dew supplies the image of gentle, natural moisture; tears supply the emotional charge; sweat supplies the bodily, slightly undignified reality. The verb’s literary softness lets a writer mention sweat without sounding coarse — or, in comedy, makes the sweat sound absurdly genteel.

7. Common collocations

  • bedewed with tears; bedewed with sweat; bedewed with dew.
  • a brow bedewed; cheeks bedewed; petals bedewed.
  • the bedewing mist; the bedewing rain; dawn bedewing the lawn.
  • Figurative: “eyes bedewed,” “a face bedewed with perspiration.”
