- Late 14th c. — Theological sin: enters Middle English as presumpteuous via Old French from Ecclesiastical Latin praesumptuosus, denoting the mortal sin of spiritual hubris—taking God’s mercy for granted while persisting in unrepentant sin.
- 15th–16th c. — Feudal and royal transgression: applied to subjects, vassals, or courtiers who dared to challenge monarchical prerogatives, guild hierarchy, or ecclesiastical authority.
- 17th–18th c. — Intellectual and literary audacity: used in Enlightenment prose and satire (Milton, Swift, Pope) to reprove critics and upstarts who audaciously disputed obvious truths or questioned learned masters.
- 19th c. — Victorian domestic and class etiquette: novelists such as Jane Austen and George Eliot adapted the word to interpersonal boundary-crossing, unwanted romantic familiarity, and social climbing.
- 20th–21st c. — Psychological boundary violation and polite hedge: today marks intrusive workplace assumptions, unearned authority, or entitlement, while fossilizing in polite conversation as a self-deprecating disclaimer (‘if it is not too presumptuous’).
