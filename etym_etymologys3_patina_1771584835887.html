The life of the word patina begins, remarkably, not with bronze at all but with the act of spreading. At the deepest reachable level lies the Proto-Indo-European root *peth₂-, "to spread out, extend, be wide," a root whose Greek reflex, petannymi (πετάννυμι), means "I spread out, unfold, open," as one spreads a cloth, a sail, or a fan. From this spreading came petalon (πέταλον), "that which is spread flat," the thin leaf or petal of a flower, which gave English its word petal through Latin and French. The same root fed Latin pandere "to spread open," whose English descendants include expand and expanse, and patere "to lie open," which produced patent — originally "open letters" of appointment — and the botanical patulous, "spreading open." From the extended form with a suffix came Proto-Germanic *faþmaz, "outstretched arms, embrace, measure," the ancestor of English fathom (the six-foot measure of outstretched arms, and hence, figuratively, "to get to the bottom of").

The branch that matters for patina runs through the flat, spread thing made for food. Greek patane (πατάνη), "a flat dish or plate," passed into Latin as patina, a broad shallow pan or stew-dish — in Latin a perfectly ordinary kitchen noun, the pan in which a Roman cook browned his dish, surviving in that sense in the Romance languages. The real pivot came in Italian, where patina shifted from "shallow dish" to the film or tarnish that forms upon such a vessel, and then to the deposit of age on bronze generally. The precise route of that shift is debated — whether through the glaze or coating on an earthenware dish, or through the oxide skin on a metal one — but by the sixteenth century the Italian word already meant "patina, tarnish." English took the word twice over, from Italian and from French patine, with the specific sense "green film on bronze" firmly recorded from 1765.

The later history of the word is the history of taste. In the eighteenth century the green film was simply the chemical ageing of metal, recorded in antiquarian and trade English. In the nineteenth century collectors and connoisseurs revalued it: the patina ceased to be corrosion and became "honourable antiquity," the admired skin of a treasured object, the very opposite of shiny newness — a bronze without its patina was as unthinkable as a picture without its varnish. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word extended metaphorically to any surface quality or character acquired through time and use: the patina of experience, of history, of an old friendship, even of respectability. What began as a Roman stew-pan, spread flat out of an Indo-European root for spreading, has become the standard English metaphor for the slow, honourable ageing of things and people alike — a word whose whole biography is the story of value accruing with time.
