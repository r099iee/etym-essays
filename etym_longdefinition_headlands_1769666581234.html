headlands — plural noun; narrow promontories or high points of land that jut out into the sea, lake, or ocean. A headland is formed where a harder ridge of rock resists erosion better than the softer rock on either side, leaving a projecting finger of land between two eroded bays. In the source context, the "green, wooded coast" of the island falls away behind the boat and its "little bays and headlands" grow flatter as the travellers move offshore — a precise image of coastal geography seen in receding perspective.

Primary sense — coastal geography. A headland is the landward end of a ridge that has been left standing while the softer rock around it was worn back by wave action. Typical features: steep or vertical sea cliffs on its flanks, a wave-cut platform at its base, and erosion features such as caves, arches, and stacks where the rock is weakened. Headlands are the complement of bays: along an irregular coast, the bays are the recessed, sheltered stretches and the headlands are the projecting, exposed points.

  • Formation: differential erosion — the headland's resistant rock (often granite, chalk, or basalt) survives while adjacent softer strata (clay, shale, sandstone) retreat, so the coast takes on a crenellated saw-tooth outline.
  • Function: headlands shelter the bays on either side from waves, trap sediment to form beaches, and serve as navigation landmarks for sailors because they are visible from far offshore.
  • Synonyms and near-synonyms: cape, promontory, point, foreland, ness, bluff — distinguished by scale and regional usage (a cape is a larger, more prominent headland; a ness is a northern English/Scottish word).
  • In context: the phrase "little bays and headlands" evokes a small, welcoming island coastline — the headlands being the high, woody points between the little bays, exactly as described in the passage.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The word has two well-attested senses and a smaller set of technical extensions:

  • Agricultural sense (older): In farming, a headland is the unploughed strip of land at the end of a field where the plough team turned round between furrows. This is the earliest recorded English sense (Old English hēafodland, c. 900), preserved in modern agricultural terminology and in field archaeology.
  • Geological/geomorphological sense: In modern earth science, headland is the standard term for a projection of resistant rock into a body of water, studied as part of coastal erosion cycles (e.g. the formation of wave-cut platforms, cliffs, caves, arches, stacks).
  • Maritime/navigational sense: A headland is a prominent coastal landmark; lighthouses are historically sited on headlands (e.g. the headlands of the Atlantic coast), and the word appears in sailing directions and pilots' guides.
  • Regional colour: The word is used across the English-speaking world but is especially at home in British, Irish, and Australian coastal vocabulary, where it names the same feature other regions call a cape or promontory.

Historical bridge & semantic reasoning. The compound is body-part metaphor: the head (top, front, or projecting extremity) of the land — land that "sticks its head out" into the sea. In Old English hēafod (head, top) + land (land) named both the field's end strip and the coastal point. The same bodily metaphor recurs across languages (Latin caput "head" gives French cap "cape"; Latin promontorium is related to mons "mountain"). The plural headlands simply generalises: a coast typically has many such projecting points.
