Phalanx is that rare thing: a word whose history is almost entirely visible — marching down the centuries in plain sight — yet whose ultimate origin vanishes into the mist before Greek itself begins.

Homer's word: Phálanx (φάλαγξ) already means ‘battle line, arrayed ranks’ in the Iliad (8th c. BCE), where Achaeans and Trojans clash ‘phalanx against phalanx’. But Homer's word is stranger than mere tactics: the same noun means ‘log, roller, round beam of wood’, and by the classical period it has sprouted still odder senses — a kind of spider, a venomous swelling, the knuckle-bone. A single word spanning battle-lines, logs, spiders, and knuckles is a lexicographer's puzzle, and the likeliest solution is that several originally distinct Pre-Greek words collapsed into one Greek form.

The substrate hypothesis:

  • Beekes' verdict: Robert Beekes, in his Etymological Dictionary of Greek, classes phálanx as probably Pre-Greek — inherited from the non-Indo-European Aegean peoples (Minoans and kin) whose vocabulary survives in Greek words for local flora, tools, and warfare. The -anx cluster and the word's semantic sprawl both point that way.
  • The old PIE guess: earlier scholars (Pokorny among them) derived it from PIE *bhel- (‘beam, plank, tree-trunk’), via the ‘log, roller’ sense: logs laid side by side resemble ranked men. This is ingenious but phonologically strained, and modern scholarship has largely abandoned it.
  • Honest verdict: the chain begins, verifiably, in Greek — whatever came before is beyond recovery.

Rome borrows the enemy's word: Latin takes phalanx wholesale (1st c. BCE) as historians like Livy and tactical writers describe the Macedonian wars — Rome's flexible legion had defeated the phalanx at Cynoscephalae (197 BCE) and Pydna (168 BCE), and the victors kept the vanquished formation's name as a technical term. Through Latin the word sleeps in manuscripts for a thousand years.

Renaissance revival (1550s): English imports phalanx just as pike-warfare — Swiss and Spanish squares that genuinely resemble the Macedonian block — dominates European battlefields. Soldier-scholars reach for the classical term, and it sticks: through the 17th c. it acquires the anatomical sense (finger bones in their ranks), through the 19th the utopian sense (Fourier's communes), and through the 20th the full metaphorical range — phalanxes of police, press, and partisans.

Tolkien's swans: A professor of Anglo-Saxon choosing phalanx for fireworks-birds is a small masterclass: the word's whole weight — Homer, Alexander, the legions — presses behind six syllables of children's-party magic, turning a flock of sparks into an army of myth. In that single choice, three thousand years of ranked warfare — Homer's Achaeans, Alexander's pikemen, the Swiss squares — march inside a children's party in the Shire.
