PIE *bheh2- "to speak, to say, to declare" (the root of Latin fari "to speak") [reconstructed; c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE] > Latin fari "to speak, to say" [Classical Latin, attested from the 1st century BCE] > Latin fatum "that which is spoken (by the gods); destiny, decree of fate" — past participle of fari [Classical Latin, 1st century BCE] > Vulgar Latin *fata "goddess of destiny, fate-woman" — the plural of fatum reanalysed as a singular feminine being [Vulgar Latin, c. 3rd-6th century CE] > Old French fae / fee "fairy, enchantress, woman of fate" [Old French, c. 12th century] > Old French faerie "enchantment, sorcery; the fairy realm" — fae + the abstract/collective suffix -erie [Old French, c. 12th century] > Middle English faerie / faery / fayerye "the realm of fairies; enchantment" [14th century] > Modern English FAERIE — the archaic spelling, fixed in English literature by Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene (1590) and revived by the modern fantasy tradition Notes on structure: FAERIE is not a compound of two independent roots; its two morphemes are the stem fae (from Latin fata < PIE *bheh2-) and the suffix -erie (Old French -erie, from Latin -aria + -ia, the abstract/collective suffix seen in English -ery/-ry, as in knavery, witchery). The modern "fairy" is the same word reanalysed in the 18th century as a singular individual creature.
