The history of strutting is the history of stiffness turned into theatre. The word belongs to a Germanic family built around rigidity: Old High German strozzen meant to swell and bulge outward, the way a stuffed cushion or an angry bird puffs itself up, and its relatives across Germanic describe bristling hair, jutting chins, and rigid posts. The reconstructed thread is PIE *ster-, the widespread root of stiff and staring things — stars that stand fixed, sterns of ships, staring eyes. From this physical matrix Middle English strouten named both the state of being distended and the posture of standing rigidly erect: a garment stuffed out strouts, and so does a proud man drawing himself up. Stiffness, swelling, and self-inflation were one image.

By the sixteenth century the verb had specialised from standing to walking stiffly, and Shakespeare fixed the new sense forever. When Macbeth’s player struts and frets his hour upon the stage (c. 1606), the two verbs divide theatrical vanity between them: fret is inward agitation, strut outward inflation — the actor’s chest-out stalk across the boards. The line’s fame carried strut into every subsequent discussion of pomposity, and the stage sense Licensed the great metaphorical extension to public life: from the 1600s onward, tyrants, fops, aldermen, and coxcombs all strut through satire, sermons, and novels. The engineering noun strut — a rigid brace holding a structure apart — branches from the same stiffness root in the same period, a reminder that the family never lost its mechanical meaning of things held rigidly out.

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries domesticated the barnyard into the word’s permanent illustration. Naturalists describing turkey-cocks, pigeons, and above all peacocks in courtship display reached for strut as the inevitable verb: the bird draws itself erect, fans its train, and promenades. Sporting and agricultural prose is full of strutting roosters, and the image migrated back to humans as the accomplished simile the source sentence uses — strutting around like peacocks needs no explanation because three centuries of fables, cartoons, and nature writing have fused the bird and the walk. Phonetic symbolism helped: the short vowel and final double consonant make strut sound abrupt and jerky, the audible equivalent of the gait it names, which is why poets favour it for comic pomposity.

In twentieth-century English the participle strutting widened once more through African-American dance and music culture’s strut your stuff (attested from the 1920s, popularised through jazz, funk, and disco), converting the walk into a general idiom of confident self-display: to strut one’s stuff is to show what one can do, on the floor or anywhere else. Ethology then returned the compliment, using strut technically for ritualised male promenades in birds and primates, so that popular science now describes both the peacock and the politician with the same verb. The satirical edge, however, never left: to call a walk a strut is to admire the confidence while inviting the audience to smile at the pride — the precise double gesture of the passage, where the neighbours’ whole lives shrink to keeping up and showing off, and we, the sensible, merely watch.

  • c. 800: Germanic strozzen-family — swell, bristle, stand rigid; the stiffness root.
  • c. 1300–1400: Middle English strouten — stand stiffly, swell with pride or stuffing.
  • c. 1606: Shakespeare’s struts and frets — theatrical pomposity canonised.
  • 1700s–1800s: barnyard naturalism — strutting cocks and peacocks fix the bird simile.
  • 1920s–present: strut one’s stuff and ethological strut — dance-floor confidence and courtship display.
