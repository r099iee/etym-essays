ramparts — the broad, high defensive walls or earthworks that enclose a fortress or stronghold; in the narrowest sense, the parapet or embankment of earth raised behind a defensive wall, and by extension the whole circuit of fortification. Etymologically a rampart is a 're-fortification' (see the etymology field), and the word carries within it the idea of preparation against attack: a rampart is what a garrison throws up between itself and the world. In the sentence, the ramparts are the defensive circuit of Gormenghast — the physical boundary that holds back the swarming hovels, the point at which the creeping epidemic of mean dwellings is finally stopped. Peake's ramparts do not merely protect; they define, dividing the clean stronghold from the squalid swarm, and the word's military dignity makes the hovels seem all the more abject by contrast.

Polysemy of ramparts.

  • Fortification (primary): the broad embankment of earth or stone, often topped with a parapet and walkway, enclosing a fortress; the whole defensive circuit.
  • The parapet or breastwork: in narrow military usage, the raised bank or wall behind which defenders shelter while firing — the shoulder-high top of the earthwork.
  • Figurative protection: anything that defends or shields, a bulwark — the ramparts of civilisation, of liberty, of the law. Bridge: the physical wall becomes the image of any barrier raised against a threat.
  • The verb to rampart: to fortify, to defend with ramparts — a rare but genuine verbal use.

Bridge: Old French remparer 'to re-fortify' (re- 'again' + emparer 'to fortify') makes a rampart literally 'a thing re-fortified', built in haste against an advancing enemy. Against its synonyms, rampart is broader than parapet (the low wall atop it), heavier than wall (generic), and less purely metaphorical than bulwark (now mostly figurative).

hovels — small, mean, squalid dwellings; rude huts or sheds barely fit for habitation. The word entered English in the fifteenth century with the sense of a rough shed for cattle, and by the sixteenth century it had settled on the miserable dwellings of the poor. In the passage the hovels are the innermost ring of mean dwellings — those that 'laid hold on the great walls, clamping themselves thereto' — and the word carries the contempt of the stronghold for the swarm at its foot.

Polysemy of hovels.

  • Wretched dwelling (primary): a small, mean, ramshackle house; a squalid habitation of the poor.
  • Rough shelter (historical): a rude shed or shelter, originally for cattle; the source-sense from which the dwelling sense developed.
  • Contemptuous register: the word itself is a verdict — unlike 'cottage' or 'hut', hovel is always a judgment of squalor and sub-human housing.

Bridge: the hovel was first a beast's shelter; transferring the roughness and darkness of the cattle-shed to the houses of the poor made the poor themselves seem animal-grade, and the word has never lost that degradation.

limpets — small marine snails of conical, tent-shaped shell, famous for clinging tightly to tidal rocks with a powerful muscular foot. In the sentence the innermost hovels 'clamping themselves thereto like limpets to a rock' become living creatures of the shore, gripping the castle wall with the same blind, tenacious adhesion.

Polysemy of limpets.

  • The mollusc (primary): a small conical marine gastropod (Patella and related genera) that clamps itself to rocks and is nearly impossible to pry loose; the emblem of stubborn adhesion.
  • Figurative tenacity: a person or thing that clings stubbornly and immovably; a persistent hanger-on. Bridge: the animal's grip becomes the image of unshakeable attachment.
  • Technical jargon — limpet mine: in naval warfare (especially WWII), a small adhesive explosive mine that is attached magnetically or by suction to the hull of a ship, so called because it clings like the mollusc.
  • Any clinging shell (broadened): barnacles and similar adhering molluscs are loosely grouped with limpets in non-technical speech.

Bridge: the limpet is a slow, patient, immovable thing; every derived use of the word keeps that image of grip, from the clinging hovel to the underwater mine.
