- 1701 - the word is coined in English for a settler in a colony; the tone is descriptive and administrative, with little of the later moral charge.
- 18th century - American colonists are British subjects claiming the rights of Englishmen; the word suggests loyalty, property and lawful settlement.
- 1776-1783 - the same people become rebels in British eyes; "colonists" can now be a distancing label used by the metropolis for those it no longer fully controls.
- 19th century - with the great settler empires, "colonists" denotes pioneers building new societies in Australia, New Zealand, Canada, South Africa and Algeria; the connotation is heroic in imperial writing and erased in indigenous accounts.
- 20th century - decolonisation and postcolonial criticism make the word analytical and often accusatory; "settler" is preferred where the writer wants neutrality, "colonist" where the colonial relation is to be stressed.
- 21st century - the term is used for ecological pioneers, space settlers and historical actors alike; in public debate it can be a neutral historical description or a pointed political judgement.
