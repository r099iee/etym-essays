• Cliff: Similar in being a steep rock face; differs because cliff is the general, neutral term for any steep rock exposure, coastal or inland, while precipice stresses near-verticality and the danger of the edge — the cliff you could fall from.
  • Crag: Similar in being a rugged rock projection; differs because crag is a rough, broken, jutting mass of rock, often on a hillside, while precipice is a clean vertical face and emphasizes the drop rather than the projection.
  • Bluff: Similar in being a steep headland; differs because bluff is a broad, rounded, often coastal cliff with a more gently sloping face, while precipice is sharper, higher, and more nearly perpendicular.
  • Escarpment: Similar in being a steep slope; differs because escarpment is a long, continuous cliff formed by erosion or faulting along a geological edge, a landform term, while precipice is any local near-vertical drop, not necessarily geological in scale.
  • Scarp: Similar in being a steep slope; differs because scarp is a technical term for a steepened slope or escarpment face, especially in military fortification (the inner slope of a ditch), while precipice is the general word for a dangerous cliff edge.
  • Sheer drop / cliff-face: Similar in being a vertical descent of rock; differs because these are descriptive phrases naming the vertical surface itself, while precipice names the whole danger — the place of falling, with its brink, its air, and its vertigo.
  • Brink: Similar in being the edge of a dangerous drop; differs because brink names the edge line only — the margin before a fall — while precipice names the entire vertical cliff below and the situation of being at it.
  • Verge: Similar in being an edge; differs because verge is a general word for any boundary or margin, not necessarily dangerous, while precipice is specifically a steep cliff — the passage uses verge of a precipice, the outer lip of the danger.
  • Ledge: Similar in being a rock projection; differs because ledge is a narrow horizontal shelf that might hold a climber, while precipice is the vertical drop itself — opposite functions on the same rock.
  • Declivity: Similar in being a downward slope; differs because declivity is any downward incline, gentle or steep, with no sense of danger, while precipice is a near-vertical fall, the extreme end of declivity.
  • Abyss: Similar in being a great depth; differs because abyss is the bottomless pit, the depth itself conceived as a void, while precipice is the cliff at whose foot the abyss begins — the ledge of the void rather than the void.
  • Chasm: Similar in being a deep gap; differs because chasm is a deep fissure or gorge splitting the rock, a hole you fall into, while precipice is a vertical face you fall from.
  • Ravine: Similar in being a deep narrow cut; differs because ravine is the whole steep-sided valley or gorge, a channel running lengthwise, while precipice is a single near-vertical wall of it.
  • Crisis / danger-point (figurative): Similar in that "on the edge of a precipice" means on the verge of ruin; differs because crisis is the general word for a turning point of danger, while precipice is the concrete image — the brink of collapse from which one may be led back or fall.
  • Absolutely: Similar in expressing completeness; differs because absolutely is the general, everyday intensifier for "completely, totally," while sheerly adds the nuance of purity and unmixedness and, in context, can carry the vertical sense absolutely lacks.
  • Completely / entirely / totally / utterly: Similar in meaning "to the full extent, without exception"; differs because each is a neutral intensifier of degree, while sheerly derives from sheer "pure, clean, unobstructed" and so also hints at a clean, unqualified, sometimes vertical completeness — the others have no geometric charge.
  • Wholly: Similar in meaning "entirely"; differs because wholly stresses undividedness (whole, complete as one piece), while sheerly stresses purity and force — a sheerly impossible task is impossible with nothing to mitigate it.
  • Purely: Similar in meaning "without admixture"; differs because purely emphasizes the unmixed quality of the cause or motive, while sheerly emphasizes the overwhelming force or the vertical/clean character of the action.
  • Vertically: Similar in meaning "straight up and down"; differs because vertically is the plain geometric adverb for perpendicularity, while sheerly (in its vertical sense) adds the sense of an unbroken, unobstructed, clean drop — sheerly vertical is vertical with nothing to break it.
  • Perpendicularly: Similar in meaning "at right angles to the horizontal"; differs because perpendicularly is a precise mathematical term, while sheerly is the poet's and prose-writer's word for the same angle with menace and purity attached.
  • Straight down: Similar in meaning "directly downward"; differs because straight down is plain and conversational, while sheerly is formal and literary, and can mean "purely/absolutely" as well as "vertically."
