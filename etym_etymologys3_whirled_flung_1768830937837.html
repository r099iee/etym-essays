whirled: Whirl is a word of the turning world, and its family tree is rooted in the PIE root *kwer-, “to turn, whirl, move around” — the root of rotation itself. The Germanic branch took it as *hwerbaną, “to turn, to go around, to wander,” and from it the old dialects made verbs of walking, changing, and returning: Gothic hwairban, “to wander”; Old English hweorfan, “to turn, to move about” — a verb the Anglo-Saxons used for everything from the turning of the seasons to the turning of a man’s fate. From hweorfan came the noun hweorfa, “a ring” — specifically the spindle-whorl, the small ring of stone or clay that weights a spindle and makes it spin, which gave English whorl; and from the related hwearf came wharf, “a bank or landing-place,” the turning-place where ships come about to unload. In the northern branch, Old Norse hverfa, “to turn,” produced the iterative verb hvirfla, “to turn around and around” — the -l- suffix marking repeated, continuous rotation — and it is this North Germanic form that Middle English borrowed in the early fourteenth century as whirlen. The word entered English fully armed: from the beginning it meant swift circular motion, and it immediately bred its family of spinning instruments and disasters — the whirlwind, the whirlpool, and the whirligig, the spinning toy that gives its name to any dizzying, giddy motion.

Across the centuries the physical sense held fast while the social and psychological senses accrued. In the 16th to 18th centuries whirl came to mean not merely spinning but being seized by the spin — the whirlpool taking a ship, the whirlwind taking a man — and so the verb acquired its sense of envelopment, of the spinner unwound into the centre of the motion. In the 19th and 20th centuries the metaphor went social: the whirl of society, the whirl of events, the whirl of a ballroom, the giddy round of fashionable life — any rapid, inescapable, dizzying activity. Yet the physical rotation has always remained dominant, and the modern word still names the fastest, most enveloping of all the spin-verbs, the one that carries the body itself into the vortex. In Vance’s sentence the word exercises its oldest power: the cloud whirled down — the storm turning as the Northmen’s wheels turned — and the whirling itself is the snare, the vortex that reaches out and snatches Turjan into the centre of its spin, a human body added to the whirlwind’s machinery. (For the tree-builder, one caution: the similar-looking root *kʷel-, “to revolve,” whence wheel, cycle, and Sanskrit carati, is a separate PIE root and must never be fused with *kwer-.)

flung: Fling is a word of the lash, and its biography begins in violence. The probable root is PIE *plak-, “to strike, beat,” a root well attested in the south of the family — Greek plēssō, “to strike,” plēgḗ, “a blow, a stroke,” Latin plangere, “to beat, to beat the breast in grief” (whence complangere, “to lament aloud,” and through Old French complaindre the English complain and plaint), and Latin plaga, “a blow, a wound,” later “a scourge, a pestilence,” the ancestor of plague. In the northern, Germanic branch the same root appears with an s-extension: Old Norse flengja, “to flog, whip, beat,” and Old English flingan, “to whip, scourge” — a verb so rarely recorded that its existence must be inferred largely from glosses and from the Old Norse form. The earliest English senses are therefore not of throwing at all but of beating: to fling in Middle English was to flog, to lash, to strike.

The great semantic leap came in the late 14th century, when the verb turned from the beating to the throwing: to fling became to throw violently, the way a whip throws out its tip, with sudden, careless, lashing force. The word’s distinct past tense flung (modelled on the native strong pattern of ring–rang–rung, with an earlier variant flang recorded) is unique in the language and reinforces the word’s abrupt, violent character. From the physical throw the senses fanned out: to fling open a door, to fling oneself into a chair, to fling insults and accusations — the contemptuous hurling of words — and, from the 17th century, the noun fling, a brief period of reckless indulgence (a last fling), and the phrases fling oneself at someone, fling away an opportunity. In Vance’s sentence the word is used at its most primitive: Turjan, snatched by the whirling cloud, is flung head over heels into incalculable distance — released like the tip of a cracked whip, with no concern for where or how he lands, the careless violence of the ancient flogger’s stroke made cosmic.
