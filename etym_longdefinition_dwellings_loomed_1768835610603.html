dwellings — places of residence; the noun of to dwell, carrying an old, sober dignity: a dwelling is a place where people live and remain, conceived not with the warmth of 'home' but with the fixedness of a settled habitation. In the sentence, the dwellings are the small, poor houses of Gormenghast's shadow, which 'by ancient law, were granted this chill intimacy with the stronghold' — a closeness to the castle that is itself a form of social and physical servitude, the poor pressed against the wall of power. The word is deliberately flat and legal: a dwelling is a place of residence in the language of statute, survey, and chronicle, and that dry neutrality is exactly right for a sentence about ancient law. The dwellings do not belong to their inhabitants in any warm sense; they are granted, permitted, endured — chill intimacy indeed.

Polysemy of dwellings.

  • Places of residence (primary): houses, flats, shelters where people live; the functional, administrative term for a home.
  • The gerund 'dwelling (on/upon)': the act of lingering or remaining with a thought or feeling — 'dwelling on the past' — from the verb's older sense 'to tarry, delay'. Bridge: Middle English dwellen 'to linger' yields both the place and the act of staying.
  • Legal/statutory register: 'dwelling-house', a building occupied as a residence; the word of surveys, chronicles, and law-courts.
  • Historical depth: the noun is formed from the verb, and the verb once meant to be delayed or misled; the dwelling is thus, etymologically, the place where wandering stopped.

Compared with home (warm, emotional), house (purely physical), residence (official, slightly pompous), abode (literary, temporary), and hovel (contemptuous), dwelling is the sober, administrative word — the word a law or a chronicle would use, which is exactly why Peake chooses it in a sentence about ancient law granting a chill intimacy.

loomed — to appear as a large, threatening, or indistinct shape, especially one that emerges gradually out of darkness, mist, or distance; to tower over something in a way that oppresses or intimidates. In the sentence, the stronghold 'loomed above' the dwellings: the castle rises over the houses of the poor as a great, vague, overwhelming presence, its size magnified by its indistinctness. The verb is comparatively late in English (first recorded in the sixteenth century) and its origin is obscure (see the etymology field); its characteristic tone is menace — whatever looms is huge, dark, and not yet fully seen.

Polysemy of loomed.

  • To appear as a huge indistinct shape (primary): to take shape darkly and largely out of fog, darkness, or distance — a ship looming out of the mist, mountains looming over a valley.
  • To tower oppressively: to rise above something in a way that overhangs and dominates it; a building looming over a street. Bridge: indistinctness magnifies size, and sheer size becomes pressure and intimidation.
  • To impend (of events): of a crisis, threat, or deadline, to be about to happen, to hang over the future — 'a disaster looms'. Bridge: the visible shape in the fog becomes the invisible shape of coming time; what looms is what approaches menacingly.
  • Archaic motion: to move slowly, heavily, and awkwardly (East Frisian and Scandinavian influence), the source-sense from which the visual sense developed.
  • Unrelated noun — the weaver's loom: the frame for weaving (Old English gelōma, 'tool, implement') is a different word entirely, not derived from the verb. Caution: the homograph 'loom' in 'the loom of a weaver' has nothing to do with looming shapes.

Bridge: the modern verb loomed describes the optical and emotional effect of a thing so large and obscure that it fills the horizon and seems to press down on the viewer — exactly the effect of the stronghold on the chill dwellings beneath it.
