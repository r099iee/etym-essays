Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*ḱeh₁s- / *kēys-
"to order, instruct, point out, decree"
(source of Latin cēnsēre "to judge, assess")
↓
Proto-Indo-Iranian (c. 2000 BCE)
*ćās-
"to teach, command, govern, punish"
(cognate with Avestan sāh- "to teach, decree")
↓
Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500 BCE)
śās- (शास्)
"to teach, govern, command, direct"
↓
Sanskrit (morphological derivation) (c. 800–500 BCE)
śāstra (शास्त्र)
"instrument of teaching; manual; code"
(śās- + instrumental suffix -tra [PIE *-trom])
↓
Epic & Classical Sanskrit (c. 500 BCE–500 CE)
śāstrāṇi (शास्त्राणि)
"the authoritative canonical treatises"
↓
Anglo-Indian & English borrowing (late 18th c.)
shaster / shastra
"sacred Hindu law-book or treatise"
↓
Modern English (19th–21st c.)
śāstras
"authoritative Sanskrit texts / treatises"

Note on historical debate: Traditional Sanskrit etymologists (Nirukta and Pāṇinian grammarians) derived śāstra either from the verbal root śās- ("to govern/order/instruct") via the causal formulation śiṣyate anena ("by which instruction is given"), or through a popular folk-etymological doublet combining śās- (governance) and trā- ("to protect/save": śāsanāt trāyate). Comparative Indo-European philology (Mayrhofer, Manfred EWAia) confirms the primary root as PIE *ḱeh₁s- ("to announce, order, teach"), cognate with Latin cēnsēre ("to appraise, formally decree", source of English censor, census) and Old Church Slavonic kazati ("to show, declare, punish"), confirming that the fundamental ancient concept was an authoritative public pronouncement and codification of law.
