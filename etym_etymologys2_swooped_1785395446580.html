Derivation chain for swoop (base of swooped): 1. PIE *sweyb- / *sweybh- 'to bend, swing, throw' (unattested; reconstructed only from Germanic and Baltic evidence; the exact shape is debated - some scholars reconstruct *swep- or *sweip- 'to swing, hurl, strike'). 2. Proto-Germanic *swaipan- (strong verb, class VII) 'to sweep, swing, drive, move swiftly, rush' (reconstructed from Old English swapan, Old Norse sveipa, Old High German sweifan 'to turn, swing, whirl'). 3. Old English swapan (strong verb, 8th-11th c.) 'to sweep, drive, move rapidly, rush, hurry' - past stem swop-, attested in the prose and verse of the Old English corpus. 4. Middle English swopen / swopen (12th-15th c.) 'to sweep along, rush, move violently', strongly reinforced by the inherited strong past-tense form swope (from Old English swop), which began to be treated as a present stem. 5. Early Modern English swoop (verb, c. 1560s) 'to move or walk in a stately manner', then by the 1630s 'to descend rapidly upon' - specialized in falconry for the hawk's kill-dive; the o-vowel of the form preserves the old strong past tense. 6. swooped (Modern English past tense and past participle, 17th c. onward) - formed regularly with the weak past-tense suffix -ed on the base swoop. Note on the debate: the derivation is not fully settled. Some authorities derive swoop directly from Middle English swopen, a dialectal continuation of Old English swapan; others treat swoop as a back-formation from swope, the old strong past tense. Either route passes through Old English swapan 'to sweep, rush'. No intermediate stage between Proto-Germanic *swaipan- and Old English swapan is attested; that step rests on the regular sound correspondences of Germanic strong verbs (PGmc *ai > OE a; PGmc *p > OE p).
