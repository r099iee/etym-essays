Hermits (noun, plural) — people who live in solitude, usually as a religious discipline, withdrawing from society to dwell alone in the wilderness, a cave, a cell, or a hut, in order to devote themselves to prayer, contemplation, and austerity. Here Peter invokes them as proven survivors of the forest — lonely endurance as a human strategy. The word is spoken by Peter in a moment of leadership and boyish confidence: 'Hermits and knights-errant and people like that always manage to live somehow if they're in a forest. They find roots and berries and things.' The tone is romantic and practical at once — fairy-tale resourcefulness borrowed for a survival crisis. A hermit, to Peter, is not a gloomy figure but an expert: a person who has deliberately chosen the wild and, by long practice, knows how to live in it, eating 'roots and berries and things.' The capitalized plural groups them with 'knights-errant' as a recognised class of legendary wanderers — the cast of chivalric romance and saint's legend. The word thus carries a double register: the austere, religious sense of a recluse vowed to solitude, and the storybook sense of the wise solitary in his cave who has learned the forest's secrets. Primary sense. The core religious meaning is precise: a hermit is a person who withdraws from human society to live alone, typically in a wild or desert place, for the sake of spiritual life. The tradition descends from the Christian desert fathers of Egypt, Syria, and Palestine (3rd–5th centuries CE), who left the cities to live as solitaries; the word itself means 'desert-dweller'. Secondary and extended senses.
  • By extension the word is used of any person who lives in deliberate solitude, religious or not — a recluse who prefers isolation: 'he has become a hermit,' 'she lives like a hermit.'
  • In zoology the word appears in transferred names: the 'hermit crab', which lives alone in a borrowed shell; the 'hermit thrush' and 'hermit warbler', reclusive songbirds.
  • In literary and romantic vocabulary the hermit is the stock figure of the wise solitary who shelters and counsels the wandering knight — the romance sense Peter draws on.
  • In modern colloquial speech 'hermit' softens into a playful term for someone who avoids company: 'gone all hermit,' 'a weekend hermit.'
  • In historical and religious studies the word distinguishes the free-roaming solitary (hermit) from the enclosed recluse (anchorite) and from the community-based ascetic (monk).
The semantic bridge. The word enters English from Greek through Latin and Old French, always meaning 'one who lives in the waste (erēmos)'. The religious career was set when the Greek translators of the Bible rendered the Hebrew 'midbār' (wilderness) as 'erēmos' — making the desert the appointed place of trial and encounter, and giving the desert-dweller his enormous spiritual prestige. Nearest companions.
  • A 'recluse' is anyone who shuts himself away from society (Latin recludere, 'to close off'), religious or not, and says nothing about where he lives or why.
  • An 'anchorite' (and its female form 'anchoress') is a specific kind of religious recluse who was often literally enclosed — walled into a cell attached to a church — a stricter, more institutional solitude than the hermit's free wandering.
  • An 'ascetic' is one who practises austerity and self-denial — fasting, celibacy, poverty — but need not be alone at all; monks are ascetics and hermits are ascetics, but the two words name different disciplines.
  • A 'solitary' is the generic word for one who lives alone, with no religious colouring; a lighthouse keeper or a widow may be a solitary, while the hermit's aloneness is his profession.
