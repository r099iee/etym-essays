• Thwacked: Similar because it is the older, nearly identical echoic word for a sharp blow — probably whack's own ancestor; differs because thwacked survives as the more bookish, archaic form, while whacked is the living, multivalent word that also means exhausted.
  • Smacked: Similar because both name a sharp, crisp blow; differs because smacking is lighter and typically a slap of the open hand, while whacking is harder and more forceful, the blow of a stick.
  • Thumped: Similar because both are echoic words for striking; differs because thumping is a heavy, dull, low impact, while whacking is a bright, ringing, sharp crack — the exact acoustic pair in Tolkien's sentence.
  • Pounded: Similar because both mean repeated heavy hitting; differs because pounding is heavier, more regular and rhythmic, while whacking is a single sharp crack.
  • Walloped: Similar because both are vigorous slang for a heavy blow; differs because walloping is more violent, thudding and comic, while whacking is crisper, sharper and far more idiomatically productive.
  • Clouted: Similar because both mean struck with force; differs because clout is an older, blunter word for a blow, now chiefly dialectal or regional, while whacked is standard informal English with a wide modern sense-family.
  • Belted: Similar because both describe hitting hard; differs because belted is stronger, more aggressive and often implies a leather strap, while whacked is a single resounding crack with a stick or flat object.
  • Slapped: Similar because both describe a flat striking blow; differs because a slap is delivered with the open hand and is lighter, while a whack is a harder strike with a stick or flat object.
  • Knocked: Similar because both mean hit; differs because knocking is the general word for a sharp rap or collision, neutral and broad, while whacked is echoic, informal and specifically bright and resounding.
  • Tired / exhausted: Similar because whacked (adjective) means exactly this in British, Australian and New Zealand speech; differs because these are the plain standard adjectives, while whacked is the vivid informal synonym carrying the image of strength knocked out of the body.
  • Shattered: Similar because both, colloquially, mean utterly exhausted; differs because shattered is chiefly British and suggests devastation, while whacked is the more matter-of-fact, everyday word for tiredness.
  • Mad / drugged (whacked out): Similar because whacked out means crazy or under the influence of drugs, so these are sense-relatives; differs because they are standard words, while whacked out is American slang with a specific off-kilter, overloaded image.
