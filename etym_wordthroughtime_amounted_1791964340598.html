- 15th c. — the verb means literally “went up, ascended, mounted”; plain physical motion, with no arithmetical colour at all.
- 16th c. — the financial sense appears: a column of figures “amounts to” its total, and the word becomes the ordinary verb of reckoning in accounts and inventories.
- 17th–18th c. — amount to extends from sums to significance, so that a statement or an action can “amount to” consent, treason or nothing; the form becomes a tool of measured assertion in prose.
- 19th c. — the deflating idioms (amounted to little, amounted to nothing) settle into reviews, biographies and political writing, giving the word a faintly sceptical edge.
- 20th–21st c. — legal and bureaucratic fixed phrases dominate (amounted to a breach, amounted to consent), while the numerical use retreats to finance; the word is now chiefly evaluative rather than arithmetical.
