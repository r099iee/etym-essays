Form and core sense. Straet is a normalized spelling of Old English strǣt, the ancestor of Modern English street. In Old English it meant a paved road, a Roman highway, or a street within a settlement. It is a loanword, taken over from Late Latin strāta, short for via strāta, "a paved road," literally "a spread or laid road," from the past participle of sternere, "to spread, strew, lay flat."

Why a Latin word in Old English. The borrowing happened on the continent, before the Anglo-Saxons crossed to Britain. Roman roads, Roman engineering and Roman trade had carried Latin words for roads, buildings, food and commerce into the Germanic frontier languages, and the ancestors of the English acquired them in the centuries of contact with the empire. When they arrived in England they found the abandoned Roman road network still in use, and strǣt was the word they already had for it.

The Old English evidence.

  • Strǣt glosses Latin strata and platea in Old English glossaries and translations.
  • It appears in place-names and boundary descriptions, often attached to Roman roads: Watling Street, Ermine Street, Fosse Way and many lesser strǣt names.
  • It could mean a Roman road outside a town as well as a paved way inside one, a range of sense that modern street has partly lost.
  • The vowel is long ǣ /ɛː/, written æ in modern editions and often simplified to ae or e in older printed texts; the card's straet is such a normalized spelling.

The modern word and its senses. Modern street has narrowed from "paved road" to "a public road in a town or city, usually lined with buildings." From that core sense a series of extensions has developed:

  • The people in the street: "the street" can mean the general public, popular opinion or ordinary people, as in "the word on the street."
  • Finance: "Wall Street," "Threadneedle Street" and "the Street" name financial districts and, by metonymy, the financial industry.
  • Homelessness and poverty: "on the street," "living on the streets," "street people" describe life without housing.
  • Urban culture: "street style," "street food," "street art," "street smart," "street cred" evoke an authentic, unofficial, urban register.
  • Sport and racing: "street stock," "street race," "street circuit" describe vehicles or courses used on public roads.
  • Publishing and retail: a "street date" is the day a book, film or record goes on sale; "street price" is the typical retail price.

Technical and planning senses. In urban planning a street is distinguished from a road: a road connects places, a street is a public way within a built-up area with frontage and pedestrian life. "Street" in traffic engineering can mean a carriageway with sidewalks; "complete street" is a design ideal balancing cars, pedestrians, cyclists and transit. In typography and design, "street" appears in the names of typefaces and signage systems, and in law "street" has specific meanings in highway and planning statutes.

The doublet. Because strǣt came from Latin strāta, English street is a doublet of stratum, strata and stratus, which were borrowed later and directly from Latin. All go back to the same root, but street travelled through Germanic and stratum through learned Latin, so they look unrelated to most speakers. The same root also underlies strew and prostrate.

Semantic bridges. The sense history is a narrowing followed by a widening. Latin strāta is a paved surface; Germanic *strātā is a paved road; Old English strǣt is a Roman road or a paved way; Middle English strete is a road in a town; modern street is an urban thoroughfare, and then, by metonymy, the life, culture and people of that thoroughfare. The word has moved from engineering to geography to society, which is why it can name both a strip of asphalt and a whole way of life.

Register and nuance. Street is a plain, neutral word, but its compounds carry strong connotations: "street" can suggest authenticity and grit ("streetwise," "street level") or danger and deprivation ("the streets," "street crime"). This emotional range is a modern development, absent from the Old English word, which was a technical term for Roman engineering.
