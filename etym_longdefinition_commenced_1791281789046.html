commenced is the past tense and past participle of commence, meaning began, started, or set in motion, with a formal, official, or ceremonial flavour. To say proceedings commenced, work commenced, or I commenced to don means the action was initiated, often according to procedure, schedule, or ritual. It is the dressed-up equivalent of began, preferred in law, academia, military orders, and narrative prose that wants gravity.

The core legal and procedural sense dominates. Courts commence proceedings, contracts commence on a date, construction commences, academic terms commence. Here commenced marks the exact point at which rights, duties, or operations start. Notices read operations will commence at dawn; histories read hostilities commenced in 1914. The formality signals importance and recordability: what commences is worth dating.

A second literary and slightly archaic sense is inchoative with infinitive: commenced to speak, commenced to don, commenced to rain. In modern speech we would say started to or began to, but commenced to lends mock-heroic or period elevation. In the source fragment I commenced to don, presumably to don clothes, the humour lies in using a grand legalistic verb for the mundane act of getting dressed, as if dressing were a ceremony requiring a herald.

  • Formal beginning (transitive): to start something officially; e.g. to commence proceedings, to commence employment. Bridge: from Latin com- + initiare, to initiate together, preserving official weight.
  • Formal beginning (intransitive): to begin, to come into existence; e.g. the show commenced at eight, hostilities commenced. Bridge: same initiation viewed from the event rather than the agent.
  • Academic ceremonial sense: to graduate, especially in American commencement ceremony; e.g. she commenced from Harvard. Bridge: metonymy where the ceremony of beginning a new life names graduation itself.
  • Literary inchoative + infinitive: commenced to do something; e.g. I commenced to don my coat. Bridge: older syntax where commence mirrors begin to, now felt as stilted and therefore comic.
  • Military / construction jargon: operations commenced; firing commenced. Bridge: need for unambiguous logged start time favours the unambiguous formal term over colloquial start.

Register is the key to use. Commenced is rarely neutral in conversation; to say we commenced dinner sounds pompous or joking. Its natural homes are plaques, timetables, judgments, and historical narrative. Antonyms are concluded, finished, terminated, ceased. Synonyms begin and start are blunter and more versatile. The past form commenced adds completed initiation: by the time we narrate, the start is already history, lending retrospective order to what may have been chaos.

Finally, tense matters. As a past form, commenced views the start from after the fact, giving narrative order and official closure to the beginning itself. Diaries, logs, and comic memoirs favour it for this retrospective neatness: what was messy in the moment becomes a dated commencement in memory, whether a career, a war, or merely the donning of a coat.
