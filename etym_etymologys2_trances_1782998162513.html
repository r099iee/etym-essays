1. PIE *h₁éy- 'to go' (Pokorny *ei-; the fundamental motion-root of the family, source of Latin īre, Greek eîmi 'I go', Sanskrit éti 'he goes', Lithuanian eĩti 'to go'). 2. Latin īre 'to go' (irregular verb: eō, īre, īvī/iī, itum; attested throughout Latin literature). 3. Latin transīre 'to go across, pass over' (trans- 'across, beyond' + īre; attested from Classical Latin onward). 4. Late/Vulgar Latin trānsitāre 'to keep crossing' (frequentative of transīre) → Old French transir 'to go across; to pass away, die; to be chilled or benumbed (with cold or terror)' (attested from Old French texts onward). Note: the noun trance is a back-formation from the verb transir / the past participle transi 'dead, numb, chilled'. 5. Old French noun transe 'the passing of life, death-agony, swoon, extreme dread' (attested in Old French; a back-formation from transir). 6. Middle English traunce 'a swoon, daze, state of fear or deep absorption' (14th c.; the modern spelling trance settled c. 16th c.). 7. English trance 'a state of profound mental absorption, detachment from the external world' (the dread-sense softens into rapture from the 16th–17th c.) → plural trances ('Trances of thought', Wordsworth's exact form in The Prelude, 1805). Debated nuance: the derivation is secure (Latin transīre → Old French transir → transe), but the exact route by which 'to go across' produced 'to die' is a matter of semantic, not formal, inference: dying was experienced as the passage out of life, and intense fear as a freezing of the blood. The modern sense 'deep absorption' is a softening, via religious rapture, of the older 'swoon/fear' sense — a shift documented in English from the sixteenth century onward. The same Old French transir also gave the archaic English verb to trance 'to paralyze, entrance', source of entrance (to put into a trance).
