Section 1 — the whole career of “rigour” and “rigours” English rigours is the plural of rigour (American rigor), a learned and literary noun whose history is almost entirely Latin and Romance until it re-enters English with a double life: bodily hardness on one side, moral and intellectual strictness on the other. The Latin verb rigēre meant “to be stiff, numb, or rigid”—said of limbs cold to the touch, of a body frozen, of things that will not bend—and from it came the noun rigor: physical stiffness, the numbness of cold, and, by the natural Roman habit of turning bodily images to moral uses, the harshness of weather, law, or character. The concrete sense never died in medical Latin; rigor mortis, “stiffness of death,” the stiffening of a corpse after death, passed untranslated into English in the nineteenth century as a forensic and medical term, and rigor still names the chill and shivering of an approaching fever. The handbooks connect Latin rigēre to a Proto-Indo-European root *reig- (or *reik-), “to stretch, to be stiff, to be tight,” and point to the Germanic branch of the same root: Proto-Germanic *raikjaną, “to stretch out,” which gives Old English rǣcan and modern English reach, and German reichen, “to reach, to suffice.” The vowel detail is debated—*reig- with the voiced stop against *reik- with the voiceless stop, and the exact PIE vocalism—but the family idea, “tightness and straightness as tension,” is standard in the handbooks. It is a satisfying root for the word’s career: stiffness and reaching are the same root’s two faces, extension on one side and unbending refusal on the other, so that the same ancient word can describe both the arm stretched out and the rule that will not give. The metaphorical transfer happened early and in several places at once. Ecclesiastical and monastic Latin used rigor for the severity of religious discipline—the unbending rule of a monastery—and scholastic Latin for the exactness of method. Old French carried the word into the vernacular as rigor / rigour, “harshness, severity,” and Middle English took it in the fourteenth century. The English record shows the full spectrum from the start: rigour of the law, rigour of winter, rigour of judgment, and (from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries) the plural rigours as a fixed collective phrase for the harsh demands of a way of life or season. The plural is the real lexical event: where the singular names a quality, the plural turns the quality into a set of things to be endured—a catalogue of cold cells, coarse food, hard hours, and unbroken rule. The modern division of labour is instructive. In the singular, rigour has migrated toward intellectual exactness: scientific rigour, academic rigour, mathematical rigour, the demand for exhaustive, exception-free method and reasoning. In the plural, rigours has kept the old physical and moral harshness: the rigours of winter, of the campaign, of the cloister. Spelling splits the word orthographically: British English keeps the -our of colour, favour, honour, while American English simplified to -or, though rigor mortis remains universal on both sides of the Atlantic. The novice in the passage is a small index of the whole history: he is sick of the rigours—the piled-up hardnesses—of monastic life, and his escape into a yamen post swaps one Latin-born institution for another, leaving the word’s ancient image of unbending stiffness to describe exactly the discipline he flees. Section 2 — sense-by-sense evolution in detail - 14th century: Middle English rigour arrives from Old French with the core senses “stiffness” and “harshness”; early uses are of the law, of winters, of fate, and of persons. - 15th–16th centuries: the figurative range widens into severity of judgment and exactness of rule; rigour of the law becomes a fixed collocation of punishment and enforcement. - 17th century: the plural rigours crystallizes as a stock phrase for the harsh demands of a season or way of life; the religious and military institutions of the age give it constant employment. - 18th–19th centuries: scientific and philosophical writers apply rigour to method, proof, and reasoning; rigor mortis enters medical English; American spelling rigor becomes standard in the US. - 20th–21st centuries: the singular specializes in academic and professional exactness (scientific rigour), while the plural remains the idiomatic word for endured hardship; the two spellings and the two senses coexist without confusion. Section 3 — literary and cultural notes The King James Bible and the Book of Common Prayer do not use rigour, but the word thrived in the legal and devotional prose that shaped English register, and by the eighteenth century it was a standard term for both judicial severity and scholarly exactness. In the present passage the word is doubly apt: monastic rigour is at once the physical hardness of the cloister and the unbending character of its rule, and the novice’s flight to the yamen inverts the usual moral map—he flees severity for a different, more corrupt form of discipline. Rigours, in short, is the plural that keeps a three-thousand-year-old image of stiffness alive in everyday prose, and its career shows how a physical word for “cold hardness” can grow, without ever losing its root, into a term for both the harshest conditions and the most exact thinking.
