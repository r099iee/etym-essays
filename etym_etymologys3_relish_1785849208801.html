FROM THE LOOSE TO THE LEFT-BEHIND

The story of relish begins with an image of slackness and ends with an image of appetite. Its deepest traditional ancestor is a Proto-Indo-European root *sleg-, 'to be slack, to droop, to be languid' — the same root that produced English slack through the Germanic languages — and from it Latin built the adjective laxus, 'loose, slack, lax, not tense', and then the verb laxāre, 'to loosen, to make slack, to release'. Modern scholarship, notably de Vaan, treats the deeper ancestry of laxus as uncertain, so the PIE stage should be marked as traditional rather than proven; what is beyond dispute is the Latin core: the Romans had a whole family of loosening — laxus, laxāre, and the compound relaxāre, 'to relax, to loosen back, to release', the word that gives English its modern relax. The primitive picture is of something held tight being let go.

THE HUNTING FIELD: SCENT AND REMAINDER

Latin relaxāre passed into Old French in several forms — relachier, relasser, relais(s)er — all meaning 'to leave behind, to release, to loosen', and from these the French formed the noun relais, 'that which is left behind, a remainder'. The word was at home in the forest. In the aristocratic hunting culture of medieval France and England, the relais was the scent left behind by the fleeing animal — the trail of smell that the hounds picked up and pursued with frantic eagerness — and by extension anything left in the wake of a passing thing. (The same noun relais produced the English word relay, originally the horses left behind at posting stages to relieve a rider — the two cousins share the idea of something left in place.)

FROM SCENT TO AFTER-TASTE

Middle English borrowed the word in the thirteenth century as reles, and the transfer that made the modern word is beautifully logical: from the scent left by game, the word moved to the taste left behind — the aftertaste, the lingering flavour of food. The dictionaries record the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century senses of reles and relish as a taste, an aftertaste, a flavour, a smack, and from aftertaste the word spread in two directions at once. Toward food, it came to mean flavour and pleasing savour in general — 'a relish of garlic', the seasoning that gives a dish its tang — and from seasoning to the condiment that supplies it, a sense that crystallized in the eighteenth century as the chopped, pickled accompaniment to meat or bread that modern shelves call relish. Toward the mind, it came to mean the appetite and pleasure that flavour excites: to have a relish for something was to have a keen taste or fondness for it, and from the noun's 'taste for' the verb 'to relish' was born in the sixteenth century, meaning to enjoy eagerly, to savour, to take zest in.

THE MODERN WORD

  • The split: the modern word lives in two very different registers — the culinary countable noun (a jar of sweetcorn relish, a spoonful of relish) and the psychological uncountable noun (he ate with relish, he told the story with relish), with the verb (to relish a challenge, to relish the prospect) solidly positive and often anticipatory.
  • Sound and form: the pronunciation /ˈrɛl.ɪʃ/ and the spelling relish settled in the 16th century, replacing the earlier Middle English reles; the shift reflects the French palatal -s- heard as English -sh-.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the rise of the condiment trade in the 18th–19th centuries made the culinary sense universal, while novels and moralists drove the psychological sense — characters relishing pleasures, sometimes sinfully — so that relish carried for a while an edge of appetite that could be suspect; today it is wholly positive.
  • Literary attestations: the psychological sense appears throughout Early Modern and Victorian prose and fiction — characters relish food, company, vengeance, and battle — and the phrase 'with relish' has been a staple of English narrative for four centuries.
  • Register today: 'relish' remains slightly more literary and vivid than 'enjoy' — it implies appetite, savouring, and zest — while the condiment sense is perfectly everyday; both descend from the same hunting image of something left behind that excites the eager chase.
