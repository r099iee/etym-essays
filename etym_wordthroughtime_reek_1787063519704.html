- OE (8th–11th c.) — rēc "smoke, steam, vapor"; a plain, neutral noun of everyday life, used of fires, hearths, and sacrificial smoke; no olfactory meaning at all
- ME (12th–15th c.) — reke "smoke, vapor, steam"; also "to emit smoke"; the visual sense is dominant; used in devotional texts for the smoke of sacrifice and the breath of God
- 16th–17th c. — still primarily "smoke/steam"; begins to appear with bad smells in the same breath (a foul reek); Scots usage flourishes — the "reek" of Edinburgh's chimneys becomes proverbial
- 18th–19th c. — the great shift: the olfactory sense overtakes the visual; "reek" as "strong unpleasant smell" is standard by the 1830s–1860s; the "smoke" sense retreats to dialect, poetry, and Scotland, where "Auld Reekie" is fixed in literature by Burns, Scott, and later Stevenson
- 20th c. — the verb "reek of" becomes the dominant construction; figurative journalism: things "reek of" corruption, hypocrisy, danger; the word's emotional value turns thoroughly negative
- Present — a two-layered word: visible smoke (archaic, literary, Scots) + foul smell (everyday); "reeking of" is the living idiom, always pejorative
