- [13th–14th century] - "Pliant" enters English via Old French ploier ("to bend", from Latin plicare, "to fold"), first describing physical matter — willow, wax, or leather that bends readily under pressure without snapping.
- [15th–17th century] - The sense extends from the body to the mind: a "pliant" or "pliable" disposition yields to influence, persuasion, or authority; valued as adaptability, yet increasingly eyed with suspicion as a lack of firm principle.
- [18th–19th century] - In Austen's moral vocabulary, "pliancy of temper" names Jane Bennet's sweet, accommodating flexibility of judgment — loveable and harmonious, yet dangerously open to manipulation by stronger, selfish wills.
- [Contemporary sense] - "Pliancy" today is mostly neutral-to-critical: used of policy, materials, or personalities, it signals easy bending and compliance, shading toward submissiveness when applied to people rather than things.
