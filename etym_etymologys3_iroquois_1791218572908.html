The name Iroquois is not what the people so named call themselves, and that single fact governs its entire evolution. The endonym is Haudenosaunee, commonly translated as People of the Longhouse, a political and architectural self-description referring to the confederacy and its bark longhouses. Iroquois is a French-colonial exonym taken into English, and its history is therefore a history of outside perception, fur-trade alliance, colonial war, anthropology, and finally decolonial renaming, rather than a normal phonetic descent from Latin or PIE.

First contact and French fixation: Samuel de Champlain encountered confederacy members and their Huron, Algonquin and Montagnais enemies in 1603–1609. French texts of 1603–1615 already write Irocois, Yrocois, Iroquois. The French needed a collective label for the five, later six, nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, later Tuscarora — who acted diplomatically and militarily as a league. French missionaries, above all the Jesuits of the Relations, standardised the spelling Iroquois by the 1640s–1660s. English traders and officials borrowed it wholesale in the 1660s as Iroquois, Iroquoys, Irroquois during Anglo-French rivalry for the beaver trade around Albany and Montreal.

  • Proposed Algonquian source: the traditional account derives French Iroquois from an Algonquian insult meaning real snakes or rattlesnakes — e.g. reconstructed Massachusett / Narragansett forms akin to irinakhoiw. Huron and Algonquin speakers were hostile to the Five Nations, and exonyms meaning snakes, vipers or man-eaters are typologically common. No single Algonquian form has been securely attested as the direct donor, and phonological fit is loose.
  • Competing Basque theory: some scholars noted Basque fishermen in the St Lawrence in the 16th century and proposed Basque hil dead + roko? or iroqu- killer of men. Evidence is circumstantial and rejected by most Algonquianists.
  • Huron intermediary theory: early spellings Yrocois may transcribe a Huron form. Jesuit dictionaries record Huron words for the confederacy distinct from Algonquian ones, suggesting French blended multiple native pronunciations.
  • No PIE root: as a 17th-century North American ethnonym mediated through French, Iroquois has no Indo-European etymon. Attempts to attach it to PIE are spurious.

Semantic expansion in English: once borrowed, Iroquois widened from tribe to family. By the 18th–19th centuries it denoted:

  • The League: the Haudenosaunee Confederacy as a political entity, celebrated by Lewis Henry Morgan in League of the Ho-dé-no-sau-nee, Iroquois (1851) as a model of democratic federation.
  • The language family: Northern Iroquoian languages including Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, Tuscarora, Huron-Wyandot and Cherokee as Southern Iroquoian. Linguists still use Iroquoian as a technical family name even where Iroquois as an ethnic label is declined.
  • The architectural type: Iroquois longhouse, Iroquois village, as in the source context — sprawling bark edifices five hundred feet long sheltering many matrilineal families.
  • The hairstyle: in British English, an iroquois or mohican / mohawk can denote the shaved-sides crest, though the historical connection to actual Haudenosaunee hairdress is loose and stereotyped.

Phonetics and orthography: French Iroquois is pronounced /iʁɔkwa/ with silent final -s in the singular. English reanalysed the -s as a plural marker and created a singular Iroquois pronounced alike for one and many, with variant pronunciations /ˈɪrəkwɔɪ/ and American /ˈɪrəkwɔɪ, ˈɪrəkoɪ/. The plural is invariant: one Iroquois, two Iroquois. Adjectival forms are Iroquois, Iroquoian, Iroquois-speaking. Capitalisation is obligatory as an ethnonym; lowercasing is considered pejorative.

Modern perception and replacement: since the late 20th century, Haudenosaunee has increasingly replaced Iroquois in Canadian and US official, museum and educational usage as more accurate and self-chosen. Iroquois survives in historical titles, place names — Iroquois County, Iroquois Falls, Iroquois Point — and linguistic classification, but style guides now advise Haudenosaunee for the living people and Iroquois only in historical quotation or when citing older literature. That trajectory — from enemy insult to colonial administrative term to romantic literary Indian to contested exonym yielding to an endonym — is the full evolution of the word.
