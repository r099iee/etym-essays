- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Enters the language via Anglo-Norman from Old French; denotes a portable covered bed or stretcher (*litere*) as well as clean straw bedding spread out for domestic animals and beasts of burden.
- 15th to 16th Century - The verb emerges in agricultural husbandry, signifying the act of providing livestock with straw beds, and by extension, domestic animals giving birth to a brood ("litter") of offspring in their straw.
- 17th Century - Undergoes profound semantic pejoration; as used, soiled straw bedding was swept into stable yards and city streets, "litter" began to denote foul straw, and subsequently any scattered waste, rubbish, or disordered refuse.
- 18th Century (Augustan Age) - Expands into household and intellectual life; first attestations of desks, libraries, and streets being "littered" with waste papers, discarded pamphlets, and household detritus, establishing the participial adjective.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Strongly moralized in municipal sanitation campaigns and widely deployed in literary criticism to censure careless, undisciplined writing ("a novel littered with solecisms and blunders").
- 20th to 21st Century - Enshrined in statutory environmental law ("no littering") and universally deployed in textual genetics to describe the volatile, correction-packed working manuscripts of modernist geniuses (Proust, Joyce, Woolf).
