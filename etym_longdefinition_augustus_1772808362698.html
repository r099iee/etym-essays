AUGUSTUS is both a proper name and a title: it names Gaius Octavius (63 BC - AD 14), the first Roman emperor, and it is also the sacral honorific that the Roman Senate conferred upon him on 16 January 27 BC, by which he and all subsequent Roman emperors were styled. In the source passage from Edward Gibbon's The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the name is used historically: it was "reserved for Augustus to relinquish the ambitious design of subduing the whole earth, and to introduce a spirit of moderation into the public councils". Gibbon here uses the name to evoke the historical pivot in Rome's history - the moment when the Republic's endless expansion gave way to the settled, moderated rule of the Principate. The word thus carries layers of historical, political, and sacral meaning. PRIMARY SENSE (the historical person)
  • The first Roman emperor: Born Gaius Octavius, later styled Octavian, he was the adopted heir of Julius Caesar; after defeating Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium (31 BC), he became sole ruler of the Roman world and was granted the title "Augustus" by the Senate in 27 BC, reigning until his death in AD 14. This is the primary referent of the word in historical writing.
  • The title of subsequent emperors: "Augustus" was adopted as the senior imperial title by all later Roman emperors (often alongside "Caesar" and "Princeps"). An empress could hold the feminine form "Augusta". In this sense the word is a rank, not a personal name.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • The Augustan age (period name): By extension the adjective "Augustan" and the phrase "the age of Augustus" name the golden age of Latin literature (Vergil, Horace, Ovid, Livy) and the period of Roman peace, prosperity, and cultural consolidation under Augustus's rule. The word "Augustan" then travels further and names any analogous golden age of literature, above all the "Augustan Age" of English letters in the early eighteenth century (Pope, Swift, Addison).
  • The adjective "august" (venerable, majestic): English has independently borrowed the Latin adjective "augustus" as "august", meaning "inspiring reverence or admiration; majestic, venerable". The semantic bridge is the Latin meaning of the title itself: "consecrated, majestic, venerable" - the qualities the Senate wished to attribute to Octavian.
  • Proper-name use in modern English: "Augustus" survives as a given name (chiefly British and American usage, often shortened to "Gus" or "Augie") and is a stock name in fiction for dignified, pompous, or grand characters. It is also the name of several royal personages in British history (e.g. Prince Augustus Frederick, Duke of Sussex).
  • Institutional and historical-technical use: In scholarship the word anchors a dense vocabulary: "Augustan settlement", "Augustan constitution", "Augustan propaganda", "Augustan classicism". Each of these uses the name as shorthand for a specific historical arrangement and its cultural ideology, making "Augustus" a technical term of ancient history and literary history as much as a personal name.
  • Metaphoric and journalistic use: In modern political and cultural journalism "an Augustus" can be invoked as a type - the ruler who consolidates, moderates, and ends expansion; the figure who transforms chaos into order. The metaphor exploits the historical association between Augustus and the transition from civil war to stable peace.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES The title "Augustus" was chosen deliberately in 27 BC, on the proposal of Munatius Plancus, because it was not a political office but a sacral designation. Latin "augustus" meant "consecrated, venerable, majestic", and it derived from the language of augury and divine favour: it was related to "augur" (a priest who read the will of the gods) and to "augeo" (to increase), so that "augustus" carried the sense of being increased, blessed, and favoured by the gods - a man whose power had been augmented by divine will. Unlike "rex" (king) or "dictator", which were politically charged and potentially offensive, "augustus" sounded religious and reverent, and it could be claimed as a title without seeming to usurp the Republic's offices. The name thus bridges divine favour, moral authority, and political power: it says that the ruler's supremacy is not a job but a state of being, sanctified by heaven. CONTEXT IN THE PASSAGE Gibbon's sentence uses "Augustus" at the moment of transition: Rome's first seven centuries are "a rapid succession of triumphs", and it is reserved for Augustus to give up the ambition of world conquest and bring moderation into public councils. The name does rhetorical work: it points to the historical emperor, but also to everything the title means - consolidation, sacral majesty, and the end of restless expansion. Gibbon's Augustus is the founder of the settled empire, and the passage's contrast between "subduing the whole earth" and "a spirit of moderation" is the contrast between the conquering Republic and the Augustan Principate. PRONUNCIATION AND USAGE NOTES In English the name is pronounced with stress on the second syllable, reflecting the Latin stress pattern Au-GUS-tus, while the adjective "august" shifts the stress to the first syllable. The word is normally capitalized as a proper noun; used generically of the title it may appear lower-case in Latin ("princeps augustus") but in English the capitalized form is standard for the emperor and the title.
