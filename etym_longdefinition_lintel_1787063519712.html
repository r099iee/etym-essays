A lintel is the horizontal structural member — a beam of timber, stone, steel, or concrete — that spans the top of an opening such as a doorway, a gate, or a window, and carries the weight of the wall, masonry, or structure above it down to the two upright sides (the jambs). It is one of the most ancient building devices in human history, the horizontal counterpart of the arch: where an arch springs in a curve, a lintel runs straight across.

Primary sense (structural spanning member)

  • Load-bearing: the lintel’s whole job is to redirect the downward load of the masonry above an opening sideways into the jambs or supporting columns.
  • Materials: timber lintels (medieval and vernacular building), stone lintels (ancient megalithic and classical work), and steel or reinforced-concrete lintels (modern construction, often precast).
  • It is the top member of a “post-and-lintel” (trabeated) system — the two uprights plus one horizontal that frame an opening.

Secondary and extended senses

  • In classical architecture: the lintel is the ancestor and functional equal of the Greek epistyle / Latin architrave, the lowest horizontal band resting on the columns.
  • In archaeology: the trilithon (two posts plus a lintel, as at Stonehenge) and the great Egyptian temple lintels are the standard textbook cases of megalithic construction.
  • Decorative lintels: many historic doorways are crowned by elaborately carved lintels, so the word also carries an ornamental sense — the beam as a canvas for carving, inscription, or relief.
  • Figurative extension (rare): “the lintel” of a building can stand, by synecdoche, for the threshold or entrance itself — a literary usage found in poetry and gothic prose (“beneath that lintel no foot passed”).

Technical distinctions (modern building)

  • A lintel spans an opening; a beam or girder is the generic horizontal member in a structure; a header is the equivalent member in timber-frame construction over a rough opening.
  • A cill/sill is the bottom member of a window; a threshold is the bottom member of a door — the lintel is always the top.
  • A transom is a horizontal crossbar within or below the top of an opening (over a door or dividing a window), often not structural; the lintel is structural.
  • In an arch, no lintel is needed — the arch itself carries the load; hence “arched doorway” versus “lintelled doorway” is a real architectural contrast.

Register and history of use

  • Register: technical, architectural, and antiquarian; standard in building trades, archaeology, and art history; uncommon in casual speech but fully understood.
  • The word is centuries old in English (14th c.) and has never shifted meaning — a rare stability, since its building function is unchanging.
  • The phrase “post-and-lintel” has escaped architecture into general discussion of any system built from simple supports and spans.

Common pitfalls

  • Spelling: lintel, not “lintle” or “lintall”; pronunciation /ˈlɪntəl/.
  • Not to be confused with threshold (bottom) or jamb (sides) — a lintel is specifically the top horizontal member.
  • In the source context, the phrase “post or lintel or threshold” names the three framing members of a doorway in order: the side posts, the top beam, and the sill.
