- c. 1300–1350 — Scandinavian entrance: The noun nygard enters Middle English from Old Norse roots (*nyggia*, *hnøggur*), naming a contemptible miser or hoarder.
- Late 14th c. — Adjectival formation: Chaucer and his contemporaries append -ly, producing nygardly to describe stingy conduct, ungenerous meals, and tightfisted masters.
- 16th c. — Renaissance drama and humanist prose: Shakespeare, Marlowe, and Spenser use niggardly to dramatize the unnatural moral deformity of hoarding wealth while fellow humans starve.
- 18th c. — Augustan essays and moral philosophy: Samuel Johnson, Jonathan Swift, and David Hume apply niggardly metaphorically to social vices, such as withholding reputation, praise, or civility from rivals.
- 19th c. — Victorian fiction and political economy: Dickens and Thackeray wield the word against workhouse guardians, grasping landlords, and niggardly government budgets.
- Late 20th to 21st c. — Sociolinguistic retreat: Due to accidental phonetic collision with a phonologically similar racial slur, the word encounters intense public controversy and has largely vanished from spoken discourse into historical texts.
