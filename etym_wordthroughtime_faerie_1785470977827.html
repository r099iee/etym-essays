- [Old French origin: enchantment, not a creature] - Faerie (fae + -erie) named enchantment itself — the sorcery of the fairy-woman, the condition of being fey — a power and a place rather than a person, charged with magic and peril.
- [Middle English borrowing (14th c.)] - English took the word as faerie/faery, keeping the collective, environmental sense: faerie was the realm and the otherworld, beautiful, glamorous, and dangerous, not an individual being.
- [Spenserian elevation (1590)] - The Faerie Queene fixed the archaic spelling in the English literary imagination — a consciously elevated, poetic, medieval-romance register in which the fairy realm carries menace, majesty, and allegorical weight.
- [Fracture in the 18th century] - The collective was reanalyzed as a singular individual — fairy, a small winged creature of children's books — and the realm-sense faded from ordinary speech, the modern spelling carrying the nursery connotation.
- [Modern revival in high fantasy] - Faerie (with its -ie spelling) survives as a deliberately archaic, literary marker — Tolkien and the fantasy tradition use it to recover the mystery and menace of the older folklore, set against the tinsel-winged modern fairy.
