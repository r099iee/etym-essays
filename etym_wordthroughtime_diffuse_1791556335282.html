- Late 14th to 15th Century: Described speech, scholastic treatises, or writing that was expansive, elaborate, or overly copious - Regarded with mixed reception; often admired for richness and learning, but occasionally criticized for tedious lack of brevity.
- 16th to 17th Century: Emergence of the verb form in English ("to diffuse"); application to the physical spreading of liquids, light, scents, and heat across space - Connoted gentle, natural dispersal, radiant illumination, and life-giving warmth.
- 18th to 19th Century: Solidification of the scientific senses in physics, chemistry, and optics (diffuse light, molecular diffusion); in literary criticism, increasingly deployed pejoratively against sprawling, undisciplined prose - Perceived stylistically as an intellectual defect, conveying flaccidity, weakness, and lack of analytical vigor.
- 20th Century: Formalized across medicine and pathology (diffuse disease, diffuse pain) to signify non-localized, systemic conditions; adopted into cybernetics and sociology (diffusion of responsibility, diffusion of power) - Associated with elusive, decentralized phenomena that resist containment or pinpoint focus.
- 21st Century: Prominent in cryptography, neural network architectures (diffusion models in generative artificial intelligence), and modern geopolitical strategy - Evokes sophisticated mathematical dispersion, probabilistic generation, and ambient ubiquity.
