Perused is the past tense and past participle of the transitive verb peruse, signifying the thorough, attentive, and systematic reading, examination, or scrutiny of a written text, document, or set of physical objects from beginning to end. Historically and in formal literary and legal discourse, to have perused something means to have inspected it with rigorous diligence, weighing every clause, word, and detail with critical care. In the source passage from Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726), the narrator's publisher, Richard Sympson, remarks: ‘I have carefully perused them three times.’ Here, the adverb carefully paired with perused reinforces the traditional, intensive sense: Sympson did not merely glance over Lemuel Gulliver's travel memoirs; he subjected the entire manuscript to repeated, meticulous scrutiny before preparing it for the press.

Semantic Architecture and the Contronymic Shift: In contemporary English, peruse occupies a unique and controversial lexicographical position as an auto-antonym (or contronym)—a word that has developed two contradictory meanings depending on register and context:

  • Primary Historical and Formal Sense (Exhaustive Scrutiny): To read or examine something in its entirety with focused care, searching analysis, and profound attention to detail. This sense governs formal, academic, archival, and legal writing. Lawyers peruse contracts, deeds, and statutory instruments; scholars peruse ancient manuscripts and codices; editors peruse proofs before publication; and scientists peruse experimental datasets. The historical bridge originates from the intensive prefix per- (‘thoroughly, completely’) fused with use (‘to put to purpose or consume’), originally conveying the idea of exhausting a subject or going through an entire inventory item by item.
  • Secondary Colloquial Sense (Casual Browsing or Skimming): In mid-to-late twentieth-century informal spoken English, peruse underwent a dramatic semantic drift, coming to mean the exact opposite: to glance over casually, browse superficially, or skim through without rigorous concentration (e.g., ‘she casually perused the magazines in the waiting room’ or ‘shoppers perused the supermarket aisles’). The semantic bridge behind this reversal is pragmatic and psychological: speakers observing someone turning pages at leisure in a bookstore or library adopted the high-register word peruse either out of humorous hyperbole, mistaken gentility, or the vague impression that looking over a text leisurely is a form of perusal. While traditional usage guides and style manuals (such as Bryan Garner and Fowler's) historically condemned this casual sense as an erroneous loose usage, major dictionaries (including the OED, Merriam-Webster, and American Heritage) now document it as a fully established secondary sense, advising writers to be aware of potential ambiguity.
  • Domain Usages and Metaphorical Extensions:
    • Legal and Jurisprudential Review: In legal practice, ‘perusal’ is a billable action representing the professional, legally binding reading of affidavits, interrogatories, deeds, and discovery materials. An attorney who certifies having perused a document affirms that its legal ramifications have been thoroughly evaluated.
    • Literary and Bibliographical Inspection: Describing the systematic reading of texts, collections of letters, or archival bundles, emphasizing linear completeness rather than selective sampling.
    • Visual and Physical Scrutiny (Non-Textual): Extended metaphorically to inspecting physical features, faces, works of art, or landscapes (e.g., ‘the detective perused the crime scene for subtle impressions’, or Shakespeare's Hamlet perusing Ophelia's face: ‘he falls to such perusal of my face / As he would draw it’).
    • Retail and Commercial Browsing: Modern commercial marketing frequently invites customers to ‘peruse our catalog’ or ‘peruse the menu’, striking an inviting, slightly elevated conversational tone between casual looking and deliberate selection.

Register and Connotative Value: Perused is an elevated, Latinate-influenced term that lends solemnity, intellectual gravity, and deliberateness to prose. When employed in its traditional sense, it conveys scholarly rigor, administrative responsibility, and an unhurried, thorough mind. However, because of its modern contronymic bifurcation, precise writers often qualify it with adverbs—such as carefully perused, meticulously perused, or conversely leisurely perused—to signal immediately to the reader which end of the semantic spectrum is intended.
