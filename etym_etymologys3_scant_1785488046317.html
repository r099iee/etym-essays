Scant is a word of the Scandinavian settlement, a loan from the Norse speech of the vikings who colonised northern and eastern England from the ninth century onward. Its ancestor is the Proto-Germanic adjective *skamma-, 'short, brief', which survives in Old Norse as skammr, meaning short of space, short of time, or short of measure. The word is confined to the Germanic North: it has no far-flung family in Sanskrit or Greek, and no convincing Proto-Indo-European ancestor has ever been agreed upon. Older etymologists reached hopefully toward roots meaning 'to cut short' or toward Latin and Greek words for 'bare, clipped', but modern scholarship treats the pre-Germanic ancestry as simply unknown — a state of affairs the word shares with many ordinary Germanic adjectives. What is securely attested is the Germanic form *skamma- and its North Germanic daughter skammr, and it is from this stock that English received its word.

English took the word not from the nominative skammr but from its neuter form, skamt, which functioned in Old Norse as both adjective and adverb meaning 'short of measure, a short while, barely'. The borrowing belongs to the deep layer of Norse loanwords in Middle English — the same contact that gave English skin, sky, egg, and the pronoun they — and the first records of scant appear in the fourteenth century, chiefly in northern texts where Scandinavian influence was strongest. The meaning at entry was 'short in measure': a scant portion, a scant hour, anything that fell short of the full amount. For two centuries the word stayed close to this neutral sense of measure and quantity, describing weights, time, and space.

Sound and form:
  • Old Norse skamt was simply anglicised: the initial sk- was kept (as in skin, sky), the final -t was preserved, and the vowel /a/ remained, giving Middle English scant.
  • English treated the borrowed adjective as a regular -t final, and it inflected normally (scant, scantly).
  • Unlike the French loanwords of the same period, scant shows no palatalisation or voicing changes; it is phonologically a plain Scandinavian inheritance.

Semantic drift:
  • 14th century: 'short in measure' — a neutral word for anything falling short of the full amount, in space, time, or quantity.
  • 16th–17th centuries: the meaning hardened from 'short' to 'not enough' — a scant measure, scant supplies — acquiring the flavour of a shortfall that is noticed and regretted.
  • 17th–18th centuries: the word proved strikingly flexible, working as an adverb ('scant two years', barely) and as a verb (to scant someone, to give short measure, to stint), the sense of withholding taking over from mere shortness.
  • Modern usage: scant is the slightly formal, bookish word for a portion deliberately kept short — scant attention, scant regard, scant consolation — shifting from neutral description of small size to a measured, almost courteous insufficiency.

The literary life of the word has followed this drift. In the seventeenth century it appears in the prose of the Authorised Version and in sermons as a word of honest deficiency; the verb to scant is used by the dramatists of giving short measure; and in the nineteenth century the adjective is a favourite of the novelists for describing rationed hopes and meagre circumstances. The final form, SCANT, is a thousand-year-old Norse adjective that has kept its form almost unchanged while its meaning moved from the measurement of goods to the rationing of attention.
