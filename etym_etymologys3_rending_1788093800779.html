A word without a pedigree. Rending descends from a verb, Old English rendan, that etymologists have never been able to explain. English shares it with no other Germanic language in a secure way; there is no Indo-European root that plausibly yields it; and even the proposed Germanic cognates are shaky. The honest reconstruction therefore starts where the attestation starts — with Old English — and the deep prehistory (before the later Anglo-Saxon period, where glossarial texts first show the verb) is simply unknown. That gap is flagged in etymologyS2 rather than papered over with an invented root, in line with the protocol's zero-guesswork rule.

Old English: glosses and violence. In the late Old English period rendan appears chiefly in glosses and translations rendering Latin scindere and discerpere, 'to cut apart, tear to pieces'. The verb begins life as an explicitly violent word: what rends is a beast, a storm, or an enemy; what is rent is flesh, cloth, or a body of men. The register is fixed within a generation of the verb's first recorded use, and it never softens.

Middle English: devotional and romance rending. Middle English renden (from c. 1200) enters the two great streams of the period's literature. In devotional prose and lyrics, rending is the gesture of penitence and grief — rending one's garments, rending one's heart before God — images that descend from biblical prophecy and give the verb its lasting emotional colour. In romances and chronicles, rending is the act of war and appetite: shields and mail are rent, flesh is rent by sword and tooth. The verb is thus bivalent from the start: holy violence against the self, and brutal violence against the world.

The King James Bible (1611): the moment of canonisation. The Authorised Version fixed rend and its strong-style past participle rent in the English collective memory with a series of unforgettable verses: 'Rend your heart, and not your garments' (Joel 2:13); 'the veil of the temple was rent in twain' (Matthew 27:51); 'I will rend the kingdom out of the hand of Solomon' (1 Kings 11:11). These verses did three things at once. They preserved the archaic strong form rent as a living literary form centuries after the verb had otherwise regularised to rended. They anchored the garment-rending gesture in the culture of mourning, penitence, and horror. And they made rend and rent the natural English words for catastrophic division — of veils, covenants, kingdoms, and ultimately of the heart itself.

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: gothic and sentimental. The gothic novel and Romantic poetry took the verb and its participle to their hearts. Screams rend the air and the night in gothic melodrama; storms rend sails, roofs, and skies in Romantic verse; and the sentimental lexicon crystallises the crucial derivative, heart-rending (late eighteenth century), for sights and stories that are agonisingly pitiful. The participle's fate is sealed: rending becomes the property of high drama, of journalism reaching for purple, and of poets reaching for finality.

Twentieth-century drift and the present. Spoken English quietly abandoned rend in the twentieth century: everyday speakers say tear, rip, or split. Rending survives in a ring of fixed phrases — heart-rending, earth-rending, rend the veil, rend the silence, rent asunder — and as a deliberate archaism in fantasy and historical fiction, including the very context of this card, Tolkien's The Hobbit, where wolves are 'rending the dead and the wounded'. The perception shift is complete: from an ordinary violent verb in Old English to a flagged, dramatic, biblically resonant shell-word in modern English, whose very rarity is now its power.

Phonetics and form. Phonologically the word has been stable: Old English rendan to Middle English renden to modern rend /rɛnd/, with the participle rending formed by the standard participial suffix that continued Middle English -ende. The interest lies entirely in morphology and semantics: the double past (rended beside strong-style rent) and the drift of the participle from literal to metaphorical to stylistically marked — three quiet centuries of semantic stratification that left the word a literary antique with perfect emotional pitch.
