• Clip someone round the ear: similar because it is the same idiom in a slightly different prepositional form; differs because it is more common and less explicitly jocular than “ear-hole.”
  • Box someone’s ears: similar because it means to strike someone on the ears; differs because it is a fixed idiom with a longer literary history and usually implies both ears or a cuffing of the head.
  • Cuff: similar because it means to strike with the open hand; differs because it is a single verb and does not specify the ear, and it can also mean to manacle.
  • Smack: similar because it denotes a sharp blow with the hand; differs because it is broader and can be playful or violent, and it does not carry the dated upper-class register.
  • Slap: similar because it is a flat-handed blow; differs because it is more general and can be to the face or body, and it is not specific to the ear.
  • Thwack: similar because it is a sharp, resounding blow; differs because it is noisier and more comic-book, often implying an instrument rather than a hand.
  • Clout: similar because it means to hit hard; differs because it is stronger and more dialectal, and it can also mean “influence.”
  • Wallop: similar because it means to strike soundly; differs because it is more violent and emphatic, often suggesting a beating rather than a single cuff.
  • Biff: similar because it is a comic slang verb for hitting; differs because it is twentieth-century American, not British upper-class.
  • Sock: similar because it means to punch; differs because it implies a closed fist and American slang, not an open-handed clip.
  • Belt: similar because it means to hit hard; differs because it is more violent and often implies a belt or a heavy blow.
  • Hit: similar because it is the general verb for striking; differs because it is neutral and lacks the specific ear target and the dated comic tone.
  • Strike: similar because it is the formal verb for delivering a blow; differs because it is elevated and unspecific, and cannot carry the idiom’s jokiness.
  • Buffet: similar because it means to strike repeatedly; differs because it is literary and implies a series of blows, not a single clip.
