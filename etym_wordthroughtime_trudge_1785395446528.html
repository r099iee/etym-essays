- [Mid-16th century: first recorded senses] - 'Trudge' appears (c. 1550s) meaning to walk wearily or with difficulty — tramping, dragging oneself along — a coarse, everyday verb for travelling on foot in bad conditions.
- [17th-18th century: weary travel verb] - Settles into the sense of travelling laboriously on foot, often over mud or long distance; 'trudge' also becomes a noun, 'a trudge' = a long, tiring walk.
- [19th century: dogged endurance] - Deepens the note of grim perseverance: the trudge is walked not in comfort but under fatigue or obligation, the step heavy but resolute.
- [20th century: metaphorical extension] - Extends beyond walking to any grinding slog — trudging through chores, paperwork, a dull day — the effort felt out of proportion to the ground covered.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still informal and vivid: slow, heavy, effortful gait implying effort disproportionate to distance, carrying an undertone of unbroken will — exactly the 'tighten our belts and trudge on' of Tolkien's dwarves.
