- Old English — the parts exist apart: under for beneath and growan for growing green, not yet joined, woods described as wudu and thicket instead.
- Middle English — under joins freely: underwood appears for coppice and low timber, the working wood beneath great trees, cut for fuel and fences.
- 18th c. — the modern compound is born: undergrowth enters gardening and forestry books for the low tangle beneath canopy, distinct from managed underwood.
- Victorian era — explorers thicken it: tropical and colonial narratives fill jungles with impenetrable undergrowth, the word becoming shorthand for green peril and adventure.
- 20th c. — ecology adopts it: textbooks map canopy, understorey, and undergrowth as layered habitat, gamekeepers manage it for pheasants, armies fear it for ambush.
- Today — presence and absence speak: gardeners clear it, rewilders praise it, and Tolkien readers shiver when there is none, the clean floor more ominous than the tangle.
