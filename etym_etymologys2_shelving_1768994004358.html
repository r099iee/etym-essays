CHAIN — SHELVING (built on SHELF): Stage 1 — PIE root *skel- "to cut, split, cleave" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the family of the slice: Greek skállō "I hoe, dig, scrape"; Proto-Germanic *skaljō "shell, husk (what is split off)" → Old English sciell, English shell, German Schale; probably also the mysterious Germanic skull ("the shell of the head").
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *skelf- "shelf, ledge (something split off as a flat board)" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE) — a Germanic derivative of the split-root; cf. Middle Low German schelf, Old Norse skjálf "bench, ledge".
Stage 3 — Old English scylfe "shelf; ledge of rock" (attested c. 9th–11th c.).
Stage 4 — Middle English schelf, shelf "flat board; ledge" (12th–15th c.).
Stage 5 — Modern English shelf "flat board for holding things; ledge of rock" → verb shelve in two senses: "to place on shelves" (16th c.) and, transferred, "to slope gradually" of shores and seabeds (16th c.).
Stage 6 — present participle / adjective shelving "sloping stepwise like a shelf" (16th–17th c.).
NOTE: The *skel- connection for shelf is the standard etymology, but the exact shape of the Germanic base (*skelf- vs. *skalb-/skelb-) is debated; some scholars group shelf with the *skel- "to bend, incline" root rather than the "split" root. The step from shelf "ledge of rock" to shelve "to slope" (16th c.) is well attested, though its birth is assumed to lie in sailor and hydrographer speech.
