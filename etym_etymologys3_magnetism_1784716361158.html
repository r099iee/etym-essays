Magnetism is one of the few scientific words whose history begins not with a concept but with a place. Magnesia (Μαγνησία) was the name of a district in Thessaly, in northern Greece, the land of the Magnetes (Μάγνητες), a people whose own name — and therefore the name of their territory — is of uncertain, almost certainly pre-Greek origin, preserved from the Aegean substrate languages that predated the arrival of the Indo-European speakers. The name is thus a fossil: no Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it, and scholars treat it as a toponym of the "Pelasgian" or Anatolian substratum, though the exact source remains unknown.

Near this district, and near the Lydian town of Magnesia ad Sipylum on the river Maeander (modern Manisa in western Turkey), ancient miners and naturalists found a dark iron ore that exerted a strange power: it attracted iron. The Greeks called it Magnetis lithos (Μαγνῆτις λίθος), "the Magnesian stone," and by the time of Plato's Ion (c. 380 BCE) the stone was already a metaphor. The poet, says Socrates, is like the lodestone: the Muse gives the poet his power, the poet passes it to the rhapsode, and the rhapsode passes it to the audience — "a chain of iron rings hung from the stone." It was thus from the very start a word about attraction between persons, not merely about iron.

Ancient and medieval transmission.

  • Greek natural philosophy: Thales of Miletus (6th century BCE) is credited with observing the lodestone's attraction; Aristotle and later writers repeat the observation.
  • Lucretius, De rerum natura (1st century BCE): a celebrated passage describes the lodestone's power in verse, calling it by the name the Latin world used, magnes.
  • Pliny the Elder, Natural History (1st century CE): records the name magnes and the story that the stone was found in the territory of the Magnetes.
  • Isidore of Seville, Etymologiae (7th century CE): gives the Latin adjective magneticus a place in the encyclopaedic tradition, keeping the ancient lore alive through the Middle Ages.
  • Medieval Latin: magneticus and the lore of the compass needle develop as the lodestone becomes the navigator's instrument; the word magneticus travels with the newly practical science of the needle.

The scientific and figurative revolutions.

  • 1600: William Gilbert, physician to Queen Elizabeth, publishes De Magnete ("On the Magnet"), founding the modern science of magnetism; he demonstrates that the Earth itself is a great magnet. Neo-Latin magnetismus names the phenomenon under study.
  • 1610s: English adopts magnetism in the physical sense, alongside the older noun magnet (from Middle English magnete, 14th century, via Old French) and the adjective magnetic (1630s, via French magnétique).
  • 1770s–1780s: Franz Mesmer popularises "animal magnetism" — the theory that a subtle fluid in living bodies can be directed to heal and to influence others. The word leaves the laboratory for the parlour and the medical controversy, and mesmerism becomes a European craze.
  • 19th century: as mesmerism is discredited and replaced by hypnotism, the figurative sense stabilises: "personal magnetism" becomes the standard phrase for an inexplicable, quasi-physical power of attraction that an orator, performer, or leader exercises over a crowd. Men are pulled toward the speaker, not merely charmed.
  • 19th–20th centuries physics: Oersted (1820) links electricity and magnetism; Faraday introduces the field concept and electromagnetic induction; Maxwell unifies electricity and magnetism in his equations (1865); Einstein's special relativity (1905) explains magnetism as a relativistic effect of electricity. The physical sense becomes enormously technical.
  • 20th–21st centuries: magnetism remains firmly figurative (stage presence, aura of command) while its physical sense retreats into physics; in modern speech it now suggests a forceful, irresistible pull of personality — more earthy and physical than the divine halo implied by charisma.

Cultural and literary life. The magnet was one of the great wonders of the ancient and medieval world, an object of wonder treated in natural histories, bestiaries, and encyclopaedias, and the lodestone and compass needle revolutionised navigation from the twelfth century. In rhetoric the magnetic metaphor has served orators and their critics ever since Plato; the House of Commons sentence in which a speaker "lacks magnetism" stands in a long tradition that runs from the rhapsode's chain of inspiration through the pulpit orator and the music-hall star to the modern televised leader. The word's double life — hard physics and the mystery of personal attraction — is exactly what makes it so useful, and so precise, in describing a failure not of argument but of presence.
