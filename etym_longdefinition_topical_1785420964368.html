TOPICAL (adjective) has two principal and at first surprising families of meaning: "relating to subjects of immediate or current interest" (the news-value sense) and "relating to a particular place or local area" (the locative sense). In the Empson passage, "the topical interest of Freud" means the fashionable, zeitgeisty appeal of psychoanalysis in the early twentieth century — the intellectual trendiness that drew readers and critics to Freud precisely because he was current. Empson regrets that this modish relevance distracted him from giving adequate representation in the seventh chapter to the poetry of straightforward mental conflict; the implication is that topical interest is ephemeral, a matter of the moment, and a distraction from durable literary value. The tone is rueful and self-correcting.

Polysemy and the full range of senses. The word has a richer range than the two headline senses, and all of them trace back to the Greek noun topos, "place":
  • Of current interest / newsworthy: belonging to the subjects under active public discussion at the present moment — "topical jokes," "topical issues," "topical satire," "a highly topical article." This is the dominant modern sense.
  • Relating to a place / local: connected with a particular place, region, or locality; of or situated in one spot. In geography and general prose this sense survives (a "topical" map of a district), though it is now chiefly literary.
  • Medical (applied to a part of the body): applied to or affecting a particular spot or surface of the body rather than the whole system — "topical ointment," "topical anaesthesia," "topical application." This sense is among the earliest recorded in English.
  • Rhetorical / logical (relating to "topics" or commonplaces): in older usage, concerned with the "topics" — the standard seats or categories of argument inherited from Aristotle's Topica; a "topical" treatment was one proceeding by commonplaces. This sense is now largely historical but explains the bridge.
  • Of a subject / relating to a theme: by easy extension, "belonging to a topic or subject of discourse" — the adjectival shadow of the noun "topic."
  • In topical allusion: in literary criticism, "topical allusion" means a reference to contemporary persons or events, the engine of much satire.

Semantic bridges. The bridge is the ancient Greek metaphor of the topos as a "place" in the mind. Aristotle's Topics treated a "place" (topos) as a standard seat or shelf where a speaker could find arguments on any subject, a rhetorical commonplace. From "place" the word thus grew two branches: the physical/local sense (a spot on a map or body) and the discursive sense (a place where arguments sit, hence a "topic" of discussion). Something "topical" in the rhetorical sense pertained to such commonplaces; and since commonplaces are what the public is talking about, the sense drifted from "pertaining to topics" to "pertaining to the topics of the day" — the newsworthy sense that dominates today. The medical sense is the purest survival of the original "spot": an ointment applied "topically" is applied to a place, the skin's surface, not swallowed.

Register, collocations, and usage notes. The newsworthy sense is neutral-to-formal and extremely productive: "topical issue," "topical subject," "topical interest," "topical humour," "topical allusion," "highly topical," "less topical now," "to become topical again." The medical sense is technical. The locative sense is now rare outside specialized prose. The antonym "untopical" or "of no topical relevance" is used, and the near-synonyms "timely" and "current" do not carry the word's implication of active public debate. The stress falls on the first syllable, /ˈtɒpɪkəl/, and the word is not to be confused with "tropical" (of the tropics), a different adjective entirely.
