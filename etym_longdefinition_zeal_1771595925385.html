zeal — great energy or enthusiasm in the pursuit of a cause or objective; passionate, sustained, and often partisan devotion. In Ruskin's sentence — "No zeal for the reputation of any individual, no personal feeling of any kind, has the slightest weight with me" — the word names exactly what he is disclaiming: passionate partisan commitment to a person's fame. Ruskin's point is scrupulous and almost legalistic: his defence of Turner is not the hot advocacy of a partisan who loves the man, but the cool judgment of a critic committed to the truth of art; zeal is precisely the emotion that would compromise that judgment. The nuance is that zeal is the hot word in the sentence — eager, heated, consuming, almost bodily — and Ruskin's "no ... has the slightest weight with me" is the voice of reason standing outside the furnace. The tone is austere, principled, self-dramatising in its very disavowal of drama. Polysemy in historical order. Zeal entered English in the late fourteenth century through Bible translation and has kept a double temperature ever since:
  • Sense 1 (late 14th c., the religious core): "fervent ardour in the service of God or a holy cause" — the zeal of the Psalmist, the zeal of the apostles; the sense in which the word was first borrowed.
  • Sense 2 (16th c. onward, secularized): "eager, sustained energy in pursuit of any cause or objective" — zeal for reform, zeal for learning, zeal for work; the modern default sense.
  • Sense 3 (the shadow sense, always present): excessive, immoderate ardour that tips into fanaticism — the zeal that hardens into zealotry; the word is never far from its opposite, the fanatic's excess.
  • Sense 4 (archaic and biblical): "jealous or possessive ardour" — the "zeal" (i.e. jealousy) of God, who will tolerate no rival; this is the original ambivalence of the Greek source word zēlos, which meant both emulation and jealousy.
  • Sense 5 (theology): "the zeal of the LORD," the divine ardour for righteousness — "Zeal for your house consumes me" (Psalm 69:9, quoted in John 2:17).
  • Related formations: zealous (adj., 1520s), zealously, zealousness; zealot (n., early 14th c., from Greek zēlōtēs "one who is a zealous follower"), zealotry, zealotism; the namesake sect of the Zealots, first-century Judean resisters of Rome.
The semantic bridge. The Greek noun zēlos had a built-in ambivalence that the whole family has never lost. To the Greeks it meant both "eager rivalry, emulation — the burning ambition to excel" and "jealousy — the resentful envy of another's possession or success." The same heat of the heart could be admiration or envy, and the word preserved both fires. From zēlos came zēlōtēs, "one who is a zealous follower," the name of the Jewish sect that fiercely resisted Rome in the first century — so that even before the word reached English, it already carried the double meaning of holy devotion and dangerous extremism. A modern reader cannot hear "zeal" without hearing its shadow, the zealot. Boundaries against near-synonyms.
  • Enthusiasm is lively interest and may be casual or passing; zeal implies deeper, sustained commitment that drives action.
  • Ardor is the heat of passion, especially of love or desire, and need not be purposeful; zeal is goal-directed, striding toward an objective.
  • Fervor, burning intensity especially religious, is nearly synonymous, but zeal adds the distinctive element of rivalry and striving that its Greek source names — emulation, the urge to excel in a cause.
  • Passion is any strong emotion, not necessarily productive; zeal is energy in pursuit of a cause.
  • Devotion is loyal attachment to a person or cause; zeal adds the energetic, almost aggressive activity.
  • Fanaticism is zeal carried past reason; zeal is the praiseworthy or neutral form of the same fire, fanaticism its excess.
  • Eagerness is lighter, more immediate desire; zeal is weightier, more durable, more consuming.
  • Earnestness is seriousness of purpose without heat; zeal has the temperature that earnestness lacks.
Grammar and register. Zeal is an uncountable abstract noun, used with for or in ("zeal for the work," "zeal in the cause"); the adjective zealous governs in or for ("zealous in pursuit," "zealous for truth"). It is a word of formal and literary registers — a "zealous" worker is praised, but the praise is a little ceremonial. The word remains double-edged: "zeal for the work" is admiring, "zealotry" is a warning, and the two poles are always in play. The Hindi hint on the card — जोश (josh, "fiery fervour") — catches the word's temperature well: zeal is hot, eager energy pushing toward a goal. Modern use. In contemporary English zeal appears most often in (1) religious and moral discourse ("zeal for God," "missionary zeal"), (2) work and public life ("reformist zeal," "zeal for the cause"), (3) sport and enthusiasm ("the zeal of the new convert" applied to converts to any activity), and (4) critical descriptions of excess ("zeal that borders on fanaticism"). The adjective zealous is far more frequent than the noun in ordinary speech. The word never fully lost its religious frame, and even the most secular use of zeal carries a faint echo of the fervour of the saints — which is precisely why Ruskin, a critic who wanted to be judged by reason rather than passion, reaches for it when he swears that no one's reputation moves him.
