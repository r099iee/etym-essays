Origins: a word of the luxury trade. Unlike the native Germanic colour words of English, scarlet has no homeland root in the British Isles: it is a prestige loanword of the Mediterranean silk-and-wool trade, entering the English record only in the thirteenth century. The generally accepted chain runs from Byzantine Greek σιγιλλάτος (sigillátos) "sealed, stamped", a technical term for cloth stamped or certified with a seal, through Arabic siqillāṭ and Persian saqirlāt "costly red cloth", into Medieval Latin scarlata / scarlatum, Old French escarlate, and so to Middle English scarlat and modern scarlet. The "sealed" idea plausibly refers to cloth officially stamped with an authenticating mark, the medieval equivalent of a brand guarantee of quality — and the word's journey from Constantinople through Arab and Persian markets to the fairs of Latin Christendom maps the actual trade routes of Europe's most expensive textile.

The Latin and PIE base. The Greek form itself is a loan from Latin: sigillātus is the past participle of sigillāre "to stamp with a seal", built on sigillum "a seal, a small figure", the diminutive of signum "a mark, a sign, a token", and signum continues the Proto-Indo-European root *sek- "to cut, sever, mark" — the same root that gives Latin secare "to cut" and English saw and section. The semantic logic is visible at every step: to sign is to make a cut-mark that guarantees identity, and scarlet cloth was the cloth that carried the mark of its makers, cut, sealed and certified as the finest red money could buy.

Phonological evolution. Passing through Arabic, Persian and the Romance vernaculars, the word underwent predictable reshaping: the Greek -átos suffix weakened to -āṭ / -āt, the lateral and sibilant clusters simplified, and the vowels settled into the Romance -ata / -atus pattern. In French, escarlate lost its unstressed initial e- when borrowed into English, the same aphesis visible in esquire becoming squire, and the final element settled as modern -et as the word was reanalysed as a colour word. The Middle English form scarlat, attested from the late thirteenth century, had settled by the fourteenth century into the spelling scarlet, pronounced with initial stress on a long "a" — a form unchanged for seven hundred years.

The kermes and cochineal economy. Behind the word stood a real commodity economy. Kermes, the dried bodies of the oak-scale insect Kermes vermilio, yielded a crimson dye of extraordinary fastness; from about the sixteenth century the New World cochineal insect (Dactylopius coccus) supplied still stronger and more abundant scarlets, and the Spanish export of cochineal became one of the great cash trades of the colonial age. Scarlet-dyed cloth from Flanders, Florence and Lucca was traded across Europe as the most expensive broadcloth available, regulated by guild statutes that specified the dyestuffs, mordants and vats; the colour's prestige was underwritten by chemistry long before chemistry had a name.

Semantic drift: from cloth to colour. In Old French and early Middle English, escarlate and scarlat still denote the cloth or a garment of it: when Chaucer's pilgrims and Langland's Englishmen wear "scarlet", they are wearing luxury wool, not a hue. The colour sense emerges by metonymy in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, exactly as the dye's famously vivid red became the cloth's most salient attribute: the object gives its name to its property, the way "orange" would later migrate from fruit to hue. By the sixteenth century scarlet is unquestionably a colour word, ready to attach to anything hot and red. The King James Bible of 1611 fixed the biblical associations in English ears, "arrayed in purple and scarlet colour" (Revelation 17:4); medicine, natural history and fashion followed, with scarlet fever so styled in the early modern period, the scarlet tanager and scarlet pimpernel receiving their names in the age of scientific naturalism, and the scarlet tunics of the British Army making the word a nineteenth-century byword for empire and courage.

From prestige to sin, and back. The medieval cloth's associations of wealth, display and the court coloured the word's moral uses. Scarlet belongs to the Whore of Babylon in Revelation 17, and the scarlet woman of later moralising inherits that gilded sinfulness; in 1850 Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter seared the colour into the American imagination as the emblem of adultery publicly branded, and the scarlet letter itself became a figure for any visible stigma. Yet the same vocabulary of luxury fuels modern positive usage: the scarlet lipstick, the scarlet sports car, the scarlet sash of ceremony. The word today carries a double valence — glamour and shame, heat and alarm — and both halves descend directly from the medieval cloth's double status as the most coveted and the most ostentatious textile in Europe.

  • Key stages: Byzantine Greek sigillátos → Arabic siqillāṭ → Persian saqirlāt → Medieval Latin scarlata → Old French escarlate → Middle English scarlat → Modern English scarlet.
  • Debated point: the Persian-Arabic side is not universally accepted as Greek in origin; several handbooks label Persian saqirlāt "of obscure origin" and caution that the sigillátos derivation, though the most cited, rests on inference about a stamped-cloth trade rather than on a surviving Greek textile document.
  • Attestation anchors: Medieval Latin scarlata (11th-12th centuries), Old French escarlate (12th century), Middle English scarlat (c. 1300), King James Bible (1611), Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter (1850), and the note's own source, Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888), where the Charity Children wear scarlet cloaks.
