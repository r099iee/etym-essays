panting (present participle of pant; RP /ˈpæn.tɪŋ/) — breathing rapidly, audibly, and in short shallow gasps, typically as the body's automatic response to exertion, shock, or sudden emotion. The present participle frames the state as ongoing: the children arrive in Narnia still mid-breath, the action of breathing caught in progress. 1. The word in this literary context. In Prince Caspian, the four children are yanked out of an ordinary station and dropped, "holding hands and panting," into a crowded, branch-ridden wood. The participle compresses several physical causes into one breathless image: the terror and astonishment of the vanishings, the cramping pressure of the surrounding branches, and the scrambling effort of getting their bearings. Panting here is involuntary and pre-verbal — it is the sound of the body speaking before the mind does, the audible signature of shock. The word also glances forward: once the children have "rubbed their eyes and taken a deep breath," the panting is over and the scene can become wonder rather than disorientation. It marks the physiological cost of magic, and its short, closed, plosive syllables mimic the short stabbing breaths it names. Nothing in the word is heroic; it is pure bodily honesty. 2. Polysemy — the senses of pant and panting. The word spreads across four registers in modern English:
  • Literal breathing (primary sense). Rapid, shallow, audible breathing from exertion, heat, or emotion: the panting dog, the runner at the finish line. In dogs and other animals panting is also a vital cooling mechanism — thermoregulation by evaporation from the mouth and tongue, the animal's substitute for sweating.
  • The noun pant. A single short breath or gasp — "a pant of exertion" — and, in dialect, the heaving chest itself. (Anti-merger note: the garment word pants is an unrelated shortening of pantaloons, named from the Venetian comic character Pantalone, and has nothing to do with breathing.)
  • The figurative verb pant for / after. To yearn, long, or crave for something: "panting for revenge," "panting after fame" — breathless eagerness pictured as eager gasping. This sense is as old as the 14th century and carries a strong biblical and literary heritage (Psalm 42: "my soul panteth after thee").
  • Technical and scientific jargon. In zoology, pant-hoot names the loud, rhythmical vocalisation of chimpanzees; in engineering, a panting action describes the rhythmic inward-and-outward flexing of a ship's hull plates under heavy seas; in medicine, panting breathing is a diagnostic feature of certain anxiety, respiratory, and acid-base states.
3. Synonym boundaries. Pant, gasp, huff, puff, and wheeze all describe laborious breathing but differ in mechanism and cause. To gasp is to take a single sharp convulsive inhalation — usually after surprise, pain, or a blow — a one-off event, not a rhythm. Huff and puff (often the reduplication huffing and puffing) are louder, more theatrical, almost indignant: the breath of the grumpy or the show-off, whereas pant is genuinely effortful and unglamorous. Wheeze adds an abnormal whistling quality from narrowed airways, implying illness or age. Breathless describes the resulting state rather than the act, and blow (as in "blowing hard") is broader, covering any forceful expulsion of air. Only pant combines rapidity, shallowness, and audible effort with a complete absence of affectation — the honest breath of real exertion or shock. 4. The historical semantic bridge. The modern verb has travelled far from its origin. English pant descends, through Old French pantaisier, from a Vulgar Latin verb that meant "to be oppressed with visions; to gasp like one who faints and sees apparitions" — itself a development of Greek phantasía, "appearance, apparition." The oldest recorded English sense is bound up with nightmare and suffocation: a panting person in Middle English was a person gasping under a vision or a dream. Only gradually did the word shed its supernatural colouring and settle into the plain physiological sense we know today — a shift completed by the 16th and 17th centuries, when "to pant" simply meant to breathe short and quick, and "panting" named the ongoing breath of effort. The figurative "pant for" (to yearn) preserves a ghost of the old intensity: to pant for something is to gasp toward it with the whole body.
