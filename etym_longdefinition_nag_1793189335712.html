Nag is one of English's compact double-lives: as a noun it names a horse — originally a small riding horse or pony, very often a worn, sorry, broken-down one — and as a verb it names the human habit of gnawing at another person with a stream of small scoldings, reminders, and complaints. The two lives may or may not share blood, and the dictionaries argue about it still; in use they are unmistakable and both are everyday.

In the source sentence the noun stands in its classic sorry sense: the rider rates his stumbling horse as a sorry nag — a poor, wretched, good-for-little beast, the word itself half the scolding. Paired with stumblest in the line before, nag completes a small portrait of contempt: not a steed, not a horse, but a nag, the animal as disappointment.

Core meaning in detail: The noun's centre is smallness with disparagement. A pony is small and neutral; a nag is small and looked down on — aged, tired, cheap, fit only for drudgery. From this stable the verb takes its colour whether or not it takes its origin: to nag is to be the burr under the saddle of conversation, the constant small irritation that wears patience down exactly as a hard-mouthed rider wears a horse down. The nagging pain that will not leave the tooth and the nagging doubt that will not leave the mind extend the same gnawing image inward.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A small horse or pony, especially a worn-out one (primary noun sense, from c. 1400): the sorry nag of the source sentence — aged, cheap, tired. The bridge from neutral smallness to contempt is use: horses kept past their prime for hire and hack-work earned the sneer now baked into the word.
  • An old or inferior horse generally (extended noun sense): any horse spoken of dismissively, whatever its size — the loser's mount, the plough-team veteran. The bridge is pure pejoration: the word slid from describing stature to delivering judgment.
  • A racehorse in sporting slang (ironic reversal, 19th c.): tipsters and owners call even fine thoroughbreds nags with rough affection. The bridge is the stable-yard's irony — professionals nickname all horseflesh by the humblest name, the way sailors call the sea the pond.
  • To scold, pester, or complain persistently (primary verb sense, from the early 1800s): to nag someone about chores, bills, or habits. The bridge most scholars favour is gnawing: compare dialectal Scandinavian words for gnawing and grumbling — the complaint worries its victim the way teeth worry a bone.
  • To ache or trouble persistently — a nagging pain or doubt (figurative extension): the pain that nags the shoulder, the suspicion that nags the mind. The bridge carries the verb's persistence inward: what will not stop complaining at us, whether nerve or conscience, nags.
  • To ride or urge a horse on (minor, now obsolete overlap sense): in older usage a rider could nag a horse — keep after it, spur its effort. The bridge, if the noun and verb are truly kin, is the stable itself: the worn beast and the wearing tongue meet in the image of endless prodding.

Register and connotation: The noun is dismissive and the verb is wearying — neither is a compliment. Calling a horse a nag ends the argument about its quality; calling a person a nag ends the argument about their company. Yet both words are democratic and vivid rather than obscene: folktales, racing pages, sitcoms, and proverbs all admit them. The verb carries a faint traditional association with domestic scolding that modern usage is slowly unpicking, applying it to managers, creditors, and consciences of every description.
