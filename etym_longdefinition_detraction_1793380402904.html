Detraction is a formal noun referring to the act, habit, or utterance of maliciously taking away from, belittling, disparaging, or subtracting from a person's reputation, character, merit, or achievements. Morphologically derived from the Latin verb detrahere—compounded of de- ("down from, away from") and trahere ("to draw or drag")—the word literally signifies "the act of dragging down or pulling away."

In the source passage from John Ruskin's Modern Painters (1843), the word is deployed with acute philosophical and psychological precision: "Be it remembered, that the spirit of detraction is detected only when unsuccessful, and receives least punishment where it effects the greatest injury..." Ruskin observes that when malicious criticism attacks an established, revered master ("those throned in heaven"), the detraction is immediately exposed as futile envy, and the critic is disgraced. However, when detraction is directed against an emerging, vulnerable young genius ("the rising of new stars"), the poisonous mists of disparagement quietly choke the budding talent before the public even realizes what has been lost. In such cases, detraction achieves its most destructive triumph precisely because it escapes detection and punishment.

Moral theology, ethics, and legal distinctions: In Christian moral theology, particularly in the scholastic ethics of Saint Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 73), detractio is analyzed as a mortal sin against justice and charity, defined as the unjust blackening of an absent neighbor's good name. Aquinas draws a crucial boundary between detraction and related verbal sins: while calumny (or slander) consists of inventing outright falsehoods, detraction often operates by uncharitably disclosing true but secret faults, magnifying hidden defects, or putting the most malicious possible interpretation on innocent or virtuous deeds. In secular philosophy and literature (from Chaucer and Shakespeare to Bacon and Swift), detraction is recognized as the quintessential weapon of envy, through which mediocrity attempts to drag superior talent down to its own level.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Malicious disparagement of reputation (primary modern sense): The unjust belittling or subtraction from someone's character, honor, or accomplishments; verbal derogation designed to diminish public esteem.
  • Theological category of verbal sin (scholastic ethics): The sin of secretly revealing another person's hidden faults without a legitimate moral reason, thereby destroying their deserved good standing in the community.
  • Literal and physical subtraction (archaic/etymological sense): The act of withdrawing, taking away, subtracting, or drawing off a physical portion from a larger whole (e.g., historical medical texts referring to "the detraction of blood" during phlebotomy, or mechanical descriptions of drawing away weight).
  • Legal and financial derogation (civil and Scots law): The deduction or subtraction of a portion of property or estate, notably preserved in the historical civil-law duty known as the tax on detraction (French: droit de détraction), a tax levied on the removal of inherited wealth or property out of a state's jurisdiction.
  • Rhetorical and critical devaluation (aesthetic sense): The systematic ungenerous discounting of an artistic or intellectual creation through dismissive framing, backhanded compliments, or hypercritical fault-finding.

Register and connotation: Detraction occupies a formal, literary, and moral register. Unlike common words like bad-mouthing or gossip, it conveys a profound ethical gravity and diagnostic weight. It suggests not merely careless chatter, but a corrosive, calculating spirit that resents excellence and actively seeks to erode merit. Writers use it when they wish to expose the dark psychological undercurrents of envy, censorship, and bad-faith criticism.
