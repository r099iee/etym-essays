Single root word; one linear chain: PIE *sker- "to cut, separate, divide" (reconstructed; unattested; the great cutting root, source of words for shearing, sharing, and severing across the family) → Latin scrūta "rubbish, broken bits, cast-off rags" (things cut off and thrown away; the root's derived noun) → Latin scrūtārī "to search through rubbish, to ransack, to examine minutely" (attested c. 1st century BCE) → Late Latin scrūtīnium "a search, an examination, an investigation" (also the early church's pre-baptismal inquiry into candidates) → Medieval Latin scrutinium → English scrutiny "a close search or examination; the official counting of votes" (borrowed c. 1400) → English scrutinize "to examine minutely and searchingly" (c. 1671; formed on Latin scrūtārī and/or French scruter, with the suffix -ize) → past participle scrutinized. DEBATED POINTS: - The link from PIE *sker- "to cut" to Latin scrūta "rubbish" rests on the idea of broken, cut-off bits; it is the standard etymology and is accepted by the handbooks, but the exact derivational path within Italic is not fully attested, and the noun is first securely documented only in the Classical period. - The verb scrutinize (c. 1671) was formed in English on the model of the Latin scrūtārī, with the possibility of influence from French scruter; the two sources are so close that handbooks do not always decide between them.
