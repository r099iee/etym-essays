TRUDGE, verb (intransitive, with an object in transitive use): to walk slowly, heavily, and with dogged effort — dragging oneself forward step by step under fatigue, bad weather, or discouraging terrain; also, to traverse laboriously, as to trudge a road. In the passage from The Hobbit, Gandalf's 'we must just tighten our belts and trudge on' names the dwarves' grim persistence through the Misty Mountains: not mere travelling, but pressing on with sore feet and empty stomachs because stopping means death — they would otherwise 'be made into supper'. The nuance is laborious, unglamorous endurance: the body becomes a burden to be hauled, the spirit worn but unbroken. TRUDGE always implies effort disproportionate to the distance — ground that would be a stroll to a rested man becomes a trudge to a hungry one — and the word's dull vowel and jolting consonants enact the thud of heavy boots. Gandalf's phrasing seals it: 'tighten our belts and trudge on' sets hunger and motion side by side, and the trudge is the physical form that tightening takes — the very gait of a company refusing, mile by mile, to become supper. The verb's full range:
  • Core gait — to walk slowly and heavily, with laborious steps, under fatigue, weight, mud, snow, or discouragement; the basic sense of plodding endurance.
  • Transitive traverse — to trudge a road, a mile, a moor; to cross or cover ground laboriously, the ground become an object of the verb.
  • Noun use — 'a trudge' is a long, tiring walk, the wearisome journey itself (an early 19th-century nominalisation).
  • Figurative extension — to trudge through work, paperwork, a dull day, or a bureaucratic process: to get through something grindingly, with effort disproportionate to progress.
  • Register — informal, vivid, and unglamorous; the word of muddy boots and weary feet, at home in narrative, reportage, and plain speech.
Set against its neighbours, TRUDGE fuses three elements — slowness, heaviness of step, and a persevering will. 'Walk' is the neutral term for any ambulation, free of strain. 'Plod' is slower, flatter, and almost mechanical, lacking trudge's note of resolution. 'Slog' emphasizes grinding effort and resistance — mud, work — more than the gait itself. 'March' and 'stomp' are purposeful, even proud. 'Stumble' is loss of control. 'Tramp' is heavy walking with a sense of the ground beaten down, 'shuffle' a dragging of the feet, 'trek' a long slow journey (from the Afrikaans treck). Where 'plod' is the sound a tired man makes, 'trudge' is the decision a resolute one keeps: the trudger is not merely moving but enduring, each step a small act of refusal aimed at the horizon rather than at the pain. In collocation, trudge governs prepositions of hostile ground — through mud, snow, rain, uphill, across a field, back home at night — and its nouns are the weary traveller, the refugee, the soldier, the exile, all of whom trudge on because stopping is worse. The grammatical range is that of a fully regular English verb: trudge (base), trudging (present participle and gerund), trudged (preterite and participle), with the noun 'a trudge' and the agent noun 'trudger' completing the set. It admits both transitive and intransitive construction — 'they trudged the last mile' beside 'they trudged on' — and it tolerates the adverbial particles of endurance (trudge on, trudge along, trudge homeward) that turn a verb of motion into a verb of resolve.
