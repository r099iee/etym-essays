CANDOUR (noun; British spelling; US spelling candor): the quality of being open and honest in expression; frankness. Pronounced /ˈkandə/. In its classical English sense (17th-18th centuries) it also meant fair-mindedness and freedom from prejudice - a generous purity of judgement; the modern sense is straightforward truthfulness. Sense 1 - Modern: frank, open, honest expression. Today candour means speaking plainly and truthfully, saying what one actually thinks without evasion, flattery, or concealment. "Let me speak with candour" = "I will be blunt with you." It is the noun of candid (candid camera, a candid interview). Modern candour is about the mouth: what comes out is honest. Sense 2 - The 18th-century sense: generous fairness of judgement (the Austen sense). In Pride and Prejudice (1813, Chapter 4), Austen's candour is quite different - it is a disposition of the heart and mind toward others: a sweet, generous, blameless benevolence that seeks the good in everyone's character, refuses to impute bad motives, and passes over faults. Elizabeth marvels that Jane "takes the good of everybody's character and makes it still better, and says nothing of the bad". Genuine candour here is purity of judgement - "candid" in the older sense of "white, spotless, free from malice" (Latin candidus "white"). The "affectation of candour" is the counterfeit: pretending universal good opinion in order to appear virtuous. Austen's candour is the emotional opposite of modern bluntness; it is warmth, not sharpness. Sense 3 - The historical core: whiteness and purity. Etymologically candour is "brilliant whiteness" (Latin candor, from candere "to shine, be white-hot"). Its moral career traces an arc: physical whiteness, then spotlessness, then purity of reputation, then freedom from bias, then openness of expression. This is why candid means both "white" (archaic, of light and garments) and "honest". Sense 4 - Legal and institutional uses.
  • Law: "candour" in legal writing describes the duty of full and frank disclosure owed to a court - the duty of candour - under which lawyers must reveal adverse authorities and public bodies must disclose relevant material.
  • Government and management: "candour" in official discourse means honest reporting of problems without blame-shifting ("a culture of candour"); UK statutory inquiries and health-care reviews recommend a statutory "duty of candour" for professionals.
  • Journalism: candour is prized as the opposite of spin, evasion, and official secrecy.
Sense 5 - Slightly negative modern shading. In some usage candour shades toward "undiplomatic bluntness": "her candour was refreshing but rude." It can be a euphemism for saying harsh things plainly. This is the modern descendant of frankness, and the register is neutral-to-approving, sometimes wry. Grammar and formation. candour (noun), candid (adjective), candidly (adverb), candidness (noun). The British -our spelling preserves French candeur; the US -or spelling restores the Latin candor. Candid "white" is now rare, surviving in formal and literary contexts (candid light). Register and nuance. Candour is more formal and morally weightier than frankness. In Austen's world it is a virtue of judgement (generous purity); in ours it is a virtue of speech (honest openness). This shift from fairness-of-heart to frankness-of-tongue is the single most important fact about the word. Common collocations. speak with candour; perfect/utmost candour; the candour of her judgement; a duty of candour; with refreshing candour; candour and simplicity; affectation of candour. Metaphoric logic. The word is literally about light: a candid judgement is "white-hot clean", unclouded by the smoke of malice or prejudice - hence unspotted, hence impartial, hence today simply honest.
