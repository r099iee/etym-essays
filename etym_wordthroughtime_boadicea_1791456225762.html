AD 60/61 — The historical Boudica leads the Iceni and their allies against Rome; Roman writers depict her as a barbarian rebel and a warning against imperial misgovernment.
Medieval period — The story survives in fragments; Gildas remembers a “treacherous lioness” without naming her, and the classical name is largely lost.
16th c. — Renaissance antiquarians recover Tacitus; she appears as Voadicia, Voada and Bunduca in histories and plays, often as a Tudor emblem of British resistance.
17th c. — The spelling Boadicea appears as a misprint and is adopted by Thomas Browne; Fletcher’s Bonduca and Purcell’s music keep the story on the stage.
18th c. — Cowper’s ode (1782) turns her into a patriotic prophetess of British liberty; the name becomes a literary and political symbol.
19th c. — Tennyson, the Thornycroft statue and Victorian pageants fix her as a bloodthirsty warrior and imperial icon; Boadicea is the standard spelling.
20th c. — Scholarship restores Boudica; feminists and postcolonial writers reinterpret her as a resistor against empire; Wodehouse uses “as Boadicea” for comic domestic tyranny.
21st c. — Boudica is preferred in serious usage; Boadicea survives as an archaic, literary and allusive form, and as the name of the London statue.
