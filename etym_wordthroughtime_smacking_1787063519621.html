- c. 9th-10th c. - Old English smæccan / smæc functioned purely as a gustatory verb and noun meaning "to taste, savour, enjoy flavor, or emit an odor."
- 13th-14th c. - Middle English incorporated the Low German / Dutch onomatopoeic verb smacken ("to strike with a noise, slap, kiss loudly"), establishing the acoustic and kinetic senses alongside the older gustatory meaning.
- 16th c. - Early Modern English (Shakespeare, Spenser) fully embraced both senses: dramatic texts used smack for sharp blows and hearty kisses, while moral prose used smacking of to denote ideological taint or lingering flavor of sin.
- 17th-18th c. - Maritime and vernacular English developed the adjectival sense of "brisk, vigorous, lively" (e.g. a smacking breeze), while comedic literature popularized smacking lips for culinary delight.
- 19th c. - Victorian fiction (Dickens, Melville, Stevenson) cemented smacking as an energetic descriptive term for waves striking hulls, disciplinary palms, and rapid travel speeds.
- 20th-21st c. - Broadly utilized across descriptive narrative, colloquial idioms ("smack-dab in the middle"), culinary commentary, and kinetic action descriptions.
