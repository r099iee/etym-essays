The word INHABITANTS hides a philosophical history in its roots: an inhabitant is, at bottom, a "holder." The chain runs from an Indo-European root meaning to grasp and hold, through the Latin verb for having, to a frequentative verb meaning to dwell, and finally to a participle that became the English noun. Tracing that chain shows how a physical act — holding something in the hand — came to describe the entire civilian population of an empire. The deepest reconstructed layer is the Proto-Indo-European root *ghabh- (also written *gʰebʰ-), whose meaning is generally given as "to give, to take, to grasp." The family is striking for combining giving and taking in one root — a reminder that in Indo-European terms, exchange and possession were two sides of the same gesture. Germanic developed the "give" side into the verb that became English give and gift (Proto-Germanic *geban-), while Latin developed the "take/hold" side into habēre, "to have, to hold, to possess." The Latin family became enormously productive in English: habit, habitation, habitat, inhabit, exhibit, prohibit, behave, and, through French, forms descended from the related habilis "that can be held or handled" (giving English able and ability). The exact reconstruction of the PIE root is debated — some scholars would set a laryngeal or a different final consonant — but the existence of a "grasp/have" root linking the Germanic and Latin families is well established. From habēre "to have" Latin formed the frequentative verb habitāre, "to dwell, to reside." The frequentative suffix -itāre (with the same -it- seen in volvitare, etc.) adds the sense of repeated or continued action: where habēre is simply "to have," habitāre is "to keep having" or "to keep occupying" — and from that continuous holding comes "to dwell, to live in, to inhabit." The semantic step is the crucial one: to inhabit is not to possess once but to hold continuously, to remain in. The verb was then strengthened with the prefix in-, "in," giving inhabitāre, "to dwell in" — the exact verb from which our word comes. In post-classical Latin the present participle inhabitāns (genitive inhabitantis) served as a noun: "one who dwells in," "an inhabitant." The participle is the agent-form of the verb — literally "in-dwelling one" — and it is this form, not the plain verb, that became the English noun. The word passed into English through French. Latin inhabitans gave Old French inhabitant / inhabitans, which Anglo-Norman and Middle English borrowed. English records inhabitaunt / inhabitant from the 15th century onward, in legal documents, chronicles, and administrative records, meaning "a person who lives in a specified place." The spelling stabilized as inhabitant, and the plural inhabitants became the standard collective term for the residents of a country, city, or territory. It is worth noting the word entered English as an administrative-legal term — the same context in which Gibbon, three centuries later, would use it in the grand prose of The Decline and Fall. The -ant ending (from the Latin -ans) marks the word as a borrowing from the learned/Romance stratum, not a native Germanic formation. The semantic career of the word in English has been remarkably stable, which is itself telling. Because inhabitant means simply "one who dwells in," it never carried the legal charge of citizen (Latin civis, someone with civic rights and duties) or the emotional charge of native (someone born in a place). It has always been the neutral, demographic, ethnographic term: the inhabitants of a place are its human content, whoever they are and whatever their legal status. This neutrality is exactly what Gibbon needs. When he writes "Their peaceful inhabitants enjoyed and abused the advantages of wealth and luxury," the word does not make a legal or patriotic claim — it simply gathers the whole population of the empire, citizens and non-citizens alike, as the people who live there. The moral verdict (enjoyed and abused) is carried entirely by the verbs, and the demographic flatness of "inhabitants" lets the sentence describe a whole civilization in the passive key: they hold the land, they hold its wealth, they enjoy and abuse it. Modern English has kept all these layers in place. Inhabitants is used of the residents of cities and countries ("the inhabitants of London," "per inhabitant" in economic statistics), of animals and plants in ecology ("the inhabitants of the coral reef," "the inhabitants of the forest floor"), and of the beings of a whole planet ("the inhabitants of Earth," even "the inhabitants of Mars"). The verb inhabit and the nouns habitation and habitat (a later scientific borrowing of the Latin habitare stem) complete the family, all carrying the original "holding" image. In every case, the "inhabitant" is the one who holds a place by dwelling in it — and in Gibbon's sentence, the peaceful inhabitants hold the wealth and luxury of the Roman empire without the martial strain that once secured that holding. The word's deep etymology from "grasp, have, hold" is thus not a mere curiosity but the semantic skeleton of the whole passage: the empire's people are its holders, and their peaceful holding of wealth is at once the happiness of the age and the seed of its decline.
