The biography of VENERABLE begins deep in an Indo-European root about desire. The Proto-Indo-European root *wen- meant 'to desire, strive for, love,' and its children across the family show how a word for wanting could ripen into a word for worshipping. In Indo-Iranian the root produced Sanskrit vanati, 'he desires, loves, wins,' and vanas-, 'desire,' whose derivative vanita, 'beloved, cherished woman,' descended into Hindi as vanitā, a literary word for 'woman, wife'; the closely related Avestan vanaiti means 'he wishes, is victorious.' In the Germanic branch the same root gave Old English wynn, 'joy, delight,' which lives on in English win, winsome, wish, wean, and wont. Everywhere the root points to striving, longing, and the delight that attends them — but it was in the Italic branch that the word took its decisive turn toward reverence. Latin built from *wen- the noun venus, 'love, desire, loveliness, beauty, charm.' The goddess Venus herself bore the name, and from the same stem came venenum, originally 'a love-potion' — whence English venom — and the verb venerari, 'to worship, revere, hold in awe.' The semantics are the bridge: to love and desire a thing so strongly that one stands in awe of it is, in the Latin mind, the beginning of worship. From venerari Latin formed the adjective venerabilis, 'worthy of reverence,' by adding the suffix -bilis, which marks capacity or worthiness: the word meant, literally, 'that can or should be venerated.' In the Christian West, venerabilis was applied from late antiquity to church fathers, saints, relics, and to Christ himself, and the Venerable Bede carried the title in the eighth century — a monk whose sanctity and learning made him 'worthy of veneration' to his own age and to every age since. English took the word twice over. It entered through Old French venerable, a learned borrowing from Latin, and also directly from the Latin adjective, around 1400, with the twin senses of 'worthy of respect' and 'worthy of religious worship.' For a century the word moved in clerical circles: it described churchmen, holy relics, and the saints. But in the late fifteenth century it broadened decisively. Anything hallowed by a combination of religious, historic, or other lofty associations could now be called venerable — an ancient law, a great institution, a time-honoured custom — and the word stepped from the altar into the world of courts, universities, and public life. In Anglican usage it became the official title of an archdeacon, and in Roman Catholic canonization procedure it designates a Servant of God declared to have lived a life of heroic virtue, one step short of beatification. The socio-cultural drift is worth pausing over. In every century, venerable has been a word about the authority of the past — a claim that age, when joined with worth, has the right to command our respect. In the Renaissance and Reformation, when old institutions were under attack, venerable became a fighting word on both sides: reformers denied that venerable traditions were worth preserving, while defenders argued that their very survival proved their value. In the nineteenth century, as the modern world accelerated, the word found new homes in the language of universities, learned societies, and the law — venerable colleges, venerable judgements — where institutional memory is itself a kind of sanctity. And in contemporary English the word has thinned in two directions at once: toward the merely impressive ('a venerable institution') and toward the comically grand ('a venerable armchair'), with the old liturgical glow still faintly warming both. Tolkien's use in The Hobbit gathers all of this history into a single simile. Elrond is 'as venerable as a king of dwarves': the phrase crowns the Elf-lord with the double dignity of sacred awe and immemorial age — the very registers that *wen-, a root of desire, had accumulated across three thousand years. What began as longing became love, love became reverence, reverence became an honorific of the church, and the honorific returned to secular life to dignify an immortal elf in a children's story. Few adjectives carry so long a chain of ascents, and fewer still manage, at the end of it, to sound as fresh as on the day a Roman first stooped to call an elder venerabilis.
