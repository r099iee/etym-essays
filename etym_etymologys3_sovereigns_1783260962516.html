The deepest root of sovereigns is the Proto-Indo-European word *upér, "over, above," the "above" twin of the root *upó, "up from under." This prehistoric pair — something rising from below and something positioned over it — is preserved across the whole Indo-European family with astonishing regularity: Latin super, Greek hupér, Sanskrit upári, and the Germanic uber that became English over and German über are all the same word, and Hindi still says ऊपर ūpar "above" from Sanskrit upari. The meaning at every stage is purely spatial — "higher than, on top of" — and it is from this simple vertical idea that the whole political vocabulary of sovereignty descends: to be above everyone else is to be supreme. Greek pushed the word into the prefix hyper- (our hyperbole, "an over-throwing"), while the sibling root *upó gave Latin sub "under," the source of subject and submarine — so that the two roots even preserve, in the words sovereign and subject, the vertical relation of ruler and ruled.

Latin built on super a rich family. The simple preposition governed a host of derivatives — superior "higher," supremus and summus "highest" (our supreme, summit, sum), superbus "proud," literally "rising above" (our superb), supercilium "eyebrow," the brow arched in disdain (our supercilious) — and in popular speech speakers created the adjective *superānus, "situated above, topmost, chief," by adding the ordinary suffix -ānus. It was this Vulgar Latin *superānus, not the classical word, that passed into the Romance vernaculars. In Old French it became soverain — the clumsy cluster super- was eased and weakened to sove-/sou- — and the word carried a double life from the start: as an adjective meaning "supreme, highest, above all others," and as a noun meaning "a supreme ruler." The Romance sisters show the same development: Italian sovrano, Spanish and Portuguese soberano.

  • Sound change — Vulgar Latin to Old French: *superānus → soverain: the medial -p- weakened and the cluster eased to -ve- (dissimilation of the p against the following vowel), and the vowel quality shifted, yielding the characteristic sove-/sou- opening.
  • Spelling re-cast in English (15th–16th c.): sovereyn was respelled sovereign with -ei- by false association with reign (Latin rēgnāre); the two words are etymologically unrelated — the Romance cognates (sovrano, soberano) show the true development of *superānus.
  • Semantic drift — from spatial "above" to political "supreme": the vertical image of standing over everyone becomes the definition of final authority; by the 17th century the word detached from persons to name the supreme power itself — the sovereignty of the state, of Parliament, or of the people.
  • Socio-cultural factor — political theory: Jean Bodin's République (1576) made souveraineté the central term of political science, and English constitutional law contrasted the sovereign with the subject — the two words still preserving, from PIE, the relation of "over" to "under."
  • Numismatic sense (from 1489): the gold sovereign of twenty shillings, stamped with the monarch enthroned, became the great standard coin of the British Empire — the ruler's supremacy projected into currency.

English took the word in the thirteenth century from Anglo-French as sovereyn or souerein, at first chiefly as an adjective of address ("my sovereign lord") and then as the noun for a supreme ruler. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the spelling was recast as sovereign — the -ei- inserted by false association with the unrelated word reign, which lent the noun a spurious air of royal ancestry. The semantic history is the rich one: sovereign named the king, but it also named the supreme power itself, so that political theory could speak of the sovereignty of the state or of Parliament (Jean Bodin's République of 1576 made souveraineté the central term of political science), and English constitutional law could contrast the sovereign with the subject. From 1489 the word also named the gold coin of twenty shillings, stamped with the monarch enthroned, that became the great standard of the British Empire. In Macaulay's plural, sovereigns, all these strata meet: the Stuart kings who claimed to be supreme above the law, the constitutional revolution that bound their title to the rights of the people, and the word whose own root, "over," had always contained the entire quarrel about who stands above whom.
