From a city to a bun: the toponym becomes a commodity. The English fruit-name currant is a textbook case of a place-name becoming a common noun. It begins not with a plant but with a city: Ancient Greek Κόρινθος (Kórinthos), Corinth, the wealthy isthmian city-state whose port shipped small, sweet, seedless dried grapes around the Mediterranean. The city-name itself is Pre-Greek: the suffix -nthos is a hallmark of the lost non-Indo-European substrate of the Aegean (shared by Zákinthos, Tiryns, and the legendary Labýrinthos of Crete), and a Pelasgian *kar- "point, peak" has been suggested, though not proven, for its first element. Corinth the city is therefore outside Proto-Indo-European ancestry, and the fruit-name inherits that substrate geography rather than a PIE plant word — an important honesty check against the temptation to reconstruct a root for "currant."

The medieval trade phrase and its shortening. In medieval trade French the dried grapes were raisins de Corinthe — "raisins of Corinth." Anglo-French and Middle English rendered the phrase as reisin de Corauntz, raysyns of Coraunte, and similar spellings (late fourteenth century). Speakers then did what speakers always do with long names: they shortened the phrase, dropping raisins of and keeping only the place — corantes, currants. A crucial reanalysis followed: the final -s of the French place-form was understood as an English plural, and a singular currant was back-formed from it. By about 1500 English currant meant specifically those tiny, dark, seedless dried grapes used in cakes, buns, and puddings — the sense still alive in Potter's "currant buns."

The sound changes. The path from Corinthe to currant shows classic reductions of a borrowed foreign phrase: loss of the preposition and head noun (raisins de), the stress falling onto the place element, and the working of English phonology on the unfamiliar cluster — Corauntz → corans → currant — with the vowel settling to the short u /ʌ/ of modern RP. The word thus experienced, in miniature, the same fate as many trade loans: a foreign geographical name worn down into a short, everyday commodity noun.

The second currant: a visual transfer (1570s). A second, almost accidental sense arrived in the 1570s. English gardeners began cultivating the small, tart berries of the northern genus Ribes, and because the clustered round berries looked rather like the imported dried Corinth grapes, speakers extended the name currant to them — yielding blackcurrant, redcurrant, and whitecurrant. Botanically the plants are unrelated to the grapevine; the link is pure visual analogy. The consequences ripple through the modern language:

  • Baking English uses currant for the dried grape; horticulture uses it for the fresh berry — one word, two plants, since the sixteenth century.
  • The transfer is not unique: similar name-borrowings by resemblance (like "gooseberry" itself) show how everyday taxonomy often leans on looks rather than botany.
  • Collocations split accordingly: currant bun, currant cake, dried currants belong to the kitchen; blackcurrant cordial, redcurrant jelly, flowering currant belong to the garden and the pantry of preserves.

Cultural life: buns, slang, and the tea table. The word gained a distinctly British cultural flavor. In Cockney rhyming slang, "currant bun" is the traditional rhyme for "sun" (as in "a bright currant bun"), carrying the bakery word into London street idiom. In Edwardian England, where Potter wrote, the "currant bun" was a standard bakery staple — cheap, sweet, and comforting — which is why Mrs. Rabbit's purchase of "a loaf of brown bread and five currant buns" reads as the very image of homely, motherly provision. The word's warmth, its double botanical identity, and its place-name ancestry together make currant one of the most instructive little words in English vocabulary: a city, a raisin, and a berry folded into a single syllable.
