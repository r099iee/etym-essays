— CONSTITUENT ROOT 1: OVER —
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE)
*upér
‘over, above, beyond’ (comparative of *upo)
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*uberi / *uber
‘over, across, above’
↓
Old English (c. 8th–9th c.)
ofer
‘over, across, beyond, past balance’
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.)
over-
‘over, excessively, toppling across’

— CONSTITUENT ROOT 2: SET —
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE)
*sed-
‘to sit’
↓
PIE Causative Formation (c. 3000 BCE)
*sod-éye-ti
‘to cause to sit, make seated, place’
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*satjaną
‘to cause to sit, set, place, establish’
↓
Old English (c. 8th–9th c.)
settan
‘to seat, place firmly, set, fix’
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.)
setten
‘to place, position, establish’

— COMPOUND SYNTHESIS —
Middle English Verbal Compound (c. early 14th c.)
oversetten
‘to overburden; to topple over, overturn, capsize’
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th–18th c.)
overset
‘to capsize a vessel; to overthrow or mentally unhinge’
↓
Modern English (current)
overset
‘to capsize, overturn, subvert, or derange’

Note: In Middle English, oversetten initially functioned as an inseparable prefix compound meaning ‘to oppress or overcome with superior force’ before developing its physical directional sense of displacement past the center of gravity. Both components—over and set—are quintessential native Germanic vocabulary, descending directly through Old English without Romance intermediation.
