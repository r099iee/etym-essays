- Early modern entry - promontory, from Latin promunturium (from prominere, to jut forth), entered English in the 17th century as a formal geographical term for a high point of land projecting into the sea.
- Shakespeare's figurative turn - in Hamlet (What a piece of work is a man) the word became an emblem of barrenness and isolation: the goodly frame of the earth seems a sterile promontory, a towering shelf of rock thrust uselessly into a void.
- Contemporary sense - promontory remains a formal, literary or geographical word, standard in geomorphology for a rock headland, and retaining in literary use the melancholy grandeur Shakespeare gave it.
