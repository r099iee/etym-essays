- Classical Antiquity (5th–3rd c. BCE) — Emerged in Ancient Greek as didaktikós (apt at teaching), celebrated by Aristotle and Plato as the essential attribute of skilled philosophers and educational poetry.
- Renaissance & Early Modern Humanism (c. 1550–1650) — Borrowed into French and English as an elevated, unreservedly positive scholastic adjective signifying works constructed for the noble illumination of the human mind.
- Enlightenment Golden Age (c. 1700–1780) — Reached peak literary prestige; poets like Alexander Pope and Voltaire viewed didactic verse as the supreme synthesis of reasoned philosophy, scientific clarity, and poetic wit.
- 19th-Century Romantic Revolt (c. 1790–1880) — Underwent severe aesthetic devaluation; Romantic poets like Keats, Shelley, and Edgar Allan Poe denounced the 'didactic heresy,' arguing that true art must seek pure beauty rather than pedantic moral instruction.
- 20th-Century Sociological & Clinical Splitting (c. 1900–1990) — Split into two distinct registers: a neutral technical descriptor in medical and educational curricula ('didactic lectures'), and a sharp pejorative in literary criticism for preachy, heavy-handed propaganda.
- 21st-Century Digital Era (Present Day) — Widely applied in media analysis to criticize condescending moral lectures in films and gaming dialogue, while remaining indispensable in cognitive science and instructional design.
