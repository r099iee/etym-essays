Antony is the English form of the Latin family name Antonius, borne by a plebeian Roman gens and later by one of the most famous Romans of all. In this reading it denotes Marcus Antonius (143–87 BCE), known to modern scholarship as Antonius the Orator to distinguish him from his grandson, the triumvir Mark Antony. He was consul in 99 BCE, censor in 97 BCE, and — with Lucius Licinius Crassus — one of the two greatest Roman speakers of the generation before Cicero. He is the second principal speaker of Cicero's De Oratore, and the quoted passage names him as one of the characters through whom Cicero's own opinions may at times be heard.

The bearer of the name in this passage

  • Marcus Antonius (143–87 BCE) sprang from a respectable but not recently consular branch of the plebeian Antonii; his own consulship in 99 BCE established the family among the Roman elite.
  • A public career: quaestor in Asia, praetor with a naval command against the pirates, consul in 99, censor in 97. He was also a senior senator whose authority in the courts was immense.
  • An orator of the old school: Cicero praises his force, resourcefulness, memory and delivery rather than his polish. Antonius was famous for speaking without a written text, and Cicero notes that he published almost nothing, so that his performances survived only in the memory of those who heard them.
  • A violent death: in 87 BCE, when Marius and Cinna seized Rome, Antonius was hunted down and killed; ancient sources report that his head was displayed on the rostra, the very platform from which he had so often spoken.

Antony in De Oratore

  • The dialogue is set in 91 BCE, four years before his death, at the Tusculan villa of Crassus. Antonius and Crassus are the two senior speakers; younger men such as Sulpicius and Cotta listen and prompt.
  • In Book I Antonius defends a practical, experience-based conception of oratory against Crassus's ideal of universal philosophical culture. He doubts that an orator needs to master every branch of knowledge, and he speaks for native talent, imitation and hard practice.
  • In Book II Antonius carries the technical weight of the dialogue: he treats inventio (the finding of arguments), arrangement, memory, delivery, and the use of humour. The passage in question refers precisely to these comments on inventio, which the author is said to endorse.
  • His portrait is warm and worldly: affable, ironic, pragmatic, a man of the forum rather than the study. Cicero gives him some of the dialogue's best anecdotes and much of its practical wisdom, which is why critics debate how far Antonius, and not Crassus, speaks for Cicero.
  • He is not a mere foil. The dialogue's design requires two complementary masters — Crassus the theorist of the complete orator, Antonius the master of forensic technique — and Cicero's own position emerges from the tension between them.

The other Antony — and why the distinction matters

  • Mark Antony is Marcus Antonius (83–30 BCE), the orator's grandson through his son Marcus Antonius Creticus: Caesar's lieutenant, consul in 44 BCE, triumvir with Octavian and Lepidus, lover of Cleopatra, defeated at Actium in 31 BCE, dead by his own hand in Alexandria in 30 BCE.
  • He is the Antony of Shakespeare's Antony and Cleopatra and of modern cinema and biography, and he is the Antony most readers unconsciously supply when they see the name.
  • The orator, by contrast, is a figure of the Republic's last free generation: no empire, no Cleopatra, no Actium — only the courts, the Senate and a style of eloquence that Cicero revered.
  • Because grandfather and grandson share the same three names, historians use "Antonius Orator" or "the orator Antonius"; in English, "Antony" without qualification usually means the triumvir, so context must decide.

The name and its spelling

  • Antonius was the nomen of the gens Antonia, a plebeian clan with consular ambitions in the late Republic; its deeper origin is not securely known and may be Etruscan, like many Roman family names.
  • English Antony comes through Old French Antoine from Latin Antonius. The variant Anthony, with an intrusive h, spread in the sixteenth century through a false association with Greek anthos "flower"; Antony is the older and historically tidier English spelling.
  • The name is common as a given name and as the name of saints — Antony of Egypt, the desert father, and Antony of Padua, the Franciscan preacher — so its religious associations are strong.
  • As an English proper noun it carries no descriptive meaning: unlike Crassus it is not a transparent Latin adjective, and nothing in the name predicts the character of its bearer.

Register and reference today: in classical prose Antony is neutral and formal, a bookish name that signals the world of Cicero and Plutarch. In general English it is a familiar personal name with a slightly old-fashioned, literary flavour. In this passage the name functions as a marker of authorial voice: Cicero distributes his own doubts among Crassus, Antony and the aged Scaevola, so identifying which Antony is meant — the orator of 91 BCE, not the triumvir of Actium — is the first step in reading the argument about eloquence and society.
