TWO ROOT WORDS — separate chains for each element, joined in the English compound. CHAIN 1 — MAN: PIE *mon- / *man- 'man, human being' (reconstructed; the Germanic reflexes are secure but the PIE root itself is debated—some scholars link it to PIE *men- 'to think', i.e. 'the thinking being'; this link is plausible but unproven) → Proto-Germanic *mann- 'person, human being' (c. 500 BCE–200 CE, reconstructed) → Old English mann / monn 'person, human being; later adult male' (attested 8th c.) → Middle English man (12th–15th c.) → English man 'human being' (in the compound manslaughter the victim is any human person, not specifically a male). CHAIN 2 — SLAUGHTER: Proto-Germanic *slahaną 'to strike, hit, kill' (reconstructed; cognates Gothic slahan, Old Norse slá) → Old English slēan 'to strike, slay, kill' (attested 9th c.; source of modern slay, slew, slain) → Old English slieht / sleaht 'striking, killing, slaughter' (attested) → Middle English slaughter / slaghter 'killing, butchery, the killing of livestock for food' (14th c.; the form is reinforced and shaped by Old Norse slátr 'butcher's meat, slaughtered carcass') → English slaughter 'wholesale or violent killing; the butchering of animals'. COMPOUND (joined in English): man + slaughter → Middle English man-slaughtre / manslaughre 'the killing of a man' (attested 13th c.) → legal specialization in English common law: unlawful killing of a human being without malice aforethought, contrasted with murder (14th–17th c., crystallized in later medieval and early modern legal texts) → Modern English MANSLAUGHTER 'the crime of killing a human being without malice aforethought'. Debate note: The PIE link for *mann- to *men- 'to think' (the human as 'the thinking one') is attractive but uncertain; several handbooks label the ultimate origin of Germanic *mann- as obscure. The compound itself is native English formation, not a Latin calque; French meurtre and homicide are parallel labels, not the source of manslaughter.
