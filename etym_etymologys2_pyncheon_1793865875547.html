Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*peyk- / *peyg-
"to prick, cut, pierce; or imitative *pi- for birdsong"
[Source of Latin pungere "to prick", picus "woodpecker"]
↓
Vulgar Latin (c. 4th–6th c. CE)
*pincio / *pinctio
"to pinch, nip, squeeze; or chaffinch (from its call)"
[Blend of punctio and onomatopoeic bird-call *pink]
↓
Old French (c. 11th–12th c.)
pinczon / pinçon
"chaffinch (bird); or small pincer / pinch"
[Modern French pinson; used as a lively personal nickname]
↓
Anglo-Norman French (c. 12th–13th c.)
Pinchon / Pynchon
"hereditary family surname derived from nickname"
[Recorded in Pipe Rolls of Norfolk and Essex]
↓
Middle English to Colonial American English (c. 14th–17th c.)
Pynchon / Pyncheon
"prominent English and Massachusetts family name"
[William Pynchon, founder of Springfield, MA, 1636]
↓
19th c. American Literature (1851)
Pyncheon
"Hawthorne's fictional dynasty in Seven Gables"
[Eponym of Pyncheon Elm, Colonel & Judge Pyncheon]
