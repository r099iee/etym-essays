Travail, pronounced /trəˈveɪl/, is a formal, now somewhat literary noun (and rarer verb) meaning painful or laborious effort, toil, hardship, or suffering endured in doing something difficult. In the source sentence, "his hour of travail" means his time of trouble and hard struggle, when the in-the-soup friend needs a rescue scheme. The word carries biblical and archaic dignity: it elevates ordinary work into affliction nobly borne.

Historically travail and travel were the same word. In Middle English, travail meant labour, toil, and painful journey, because journeys were labour. Over time spelling split the senses: travel for going, travail for suffering work, though the shared root still explains phrases like "travail of travel." Modern travail retains three intertwined strands: general toil, tribulation or suffering, and specifically the pangs of childbirth ("in travail"). All share the idea of productive pain — effort that may bring forth something, whether a crop, a book, or a child.

The register is elevated and slightly archaic; everyday speech prefers work, toil, grind, or labour, while travail appears in sermons, literature, and solemn journalism. To call effort travail ennobles it and invites sympathy: it says the sufferer is not merely busy but burdened. It collocates with hour of, years of, life of, and verbs like endure, spare, and reward — "spare me further travail," "his travails were rewarded."

  • Arduous toil sense (core): Hard, grinding work — e.g., "the travails of writing a thesis." The bridge from Latin tripalium (torture device) to toil preserves the idea that labour hurts. This sense stresses duration and weariness rather than momentary exertion.
  • Suffering / tribulation sense: Painful experience or hardship generally — e.g., "his hour of travail," "the travails of exile." The bridge is generalisation from work-pain to life-pain. It overlaps with trial, tribulation, and ordeal but retains a labour overtone: suffering as work to be got through.
  • Childbirth sense (technical): Labour pains, parturition — e.g., "she was in travail." This is the oldest specialised medical sense, attested since Middle English. The bridge is direct: childbirth as the prototypical painful productive labour. Though now largely replaced by labour itself, it survives in hymns and older translations.
  • Journey-anguish sense (archaic, shared with travel): Wearisome journeying — e.g., Chaucer's "travail" for pilgrimage hardship. The bridge is historical identity: medieval travel was travail. This explains why travel still carries faint labour overtones and why travail can sound like a voyage through suffering.

Mastering travail means hearing both its weight and its promise. Unlike torment, which is pain without purpose, travail implies pain with issue: after travail comes delivery, harvest, or rescue. That is why Wodehouse can use it half-mockingly — "his hour of travail" dignifies a silly upper-class scrape with biblical solemnity, making the comedy affectionate rather than cruel.
