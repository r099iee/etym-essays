- Late 16th c. "frayed cloth and rope edge" - Coined in Elizabethan England within the cloth-weaving guild for the coarse, uneven terminal end of a web of fabric, rapidly adopted by naval mariners for untwisted, unwhipped rope ends.
- 17th c. "metaphorical dregs and remnants" - Expands into wider literary usage (Francis Bacon, John Milton) for the inferior, spent, or corrupted remainder of human institutions, councils, arguments, and lives.
- 18th–19th c. "temporal exhaustion and scenic decay" - Established across classic British prose (Jonathan Swift, Walter Scott, Charles Dickens) as an atmospheric idiom for the tired close of an afternoon, a dying season, or the fading embers of twilight.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c. "tobacco butts and satirical deflation" - British colloquial parlance adopts 'fag' for a cigarette, giving fag-end a concrete domestic life in ashtrays; humorous writers like P. G. Wodehouse exploit the phrase to humorously deflate grand nature descriptions.
- Late 20th c.–Present "British and Commonwealth idiom" - Retains its colorful dual identity in British, Irish, and Australasian English as both a cigarette butt and a world-weary idiom for the unglamorous tail-end of an event or holiday.
