teamsters — The man wears "long moustaches after the fashion of teamsters": the word is an occupational label that places him, at a glance, in the freighting class of the mid-nineteenth-century frontier. A teamster was the driver of a team of draft animals — horses or mules yoked in harness and hitched to a wagon — whose business was hauling freight over the long roads of the American West. The term stamps him with a trade identity: rough, practical, weather-beaten, a man of the trace and the rein rather than the parlor. In the context the word works as social portraiture — the moustaches are not decoration but uniform, the sign of a class who lived with animals and roads. The tone is ethnographic and appraising: McCarthy names the fashion as a code, and the reader is meant to read the man's whole condition from it.

Polysemy of "teamster."

  • Original sense (1777): driver of a team of draft animals. The man who managed the yoked horses, mules, or oxen pulling a wagon or plow — the freight driver of the pre-railroad world.
  • Modern labor sense (from 1903): a truck driver and union member. The International Brotherhood of Teamsters, the great American freight union, carried the name over from the animal to the engine; today a "teamster" commonly drives a motor vehicle, and many members never drove an animal at all.
  • Transferred occupational sense. Any driver of a hauling vehicle or rig — "teamster" can apply to a trucker, a hauler, or a logistics worker, the word following the profession as transportation mechanized.

Boundary comparisons. A driver is the generic term, with no trade color; a wagoner drives a wagon and is relatively neutral, even old-fashioned; a carter hauls with a cart and carries a low, workaday register; a drayman drives a dray, the heavy brewery cart, and so belongs to urban commerce; a muleteer (borrowed from French muletier or Spanish mulero) drives mules specifically and has an older, more international sound; a carman drives a tram or cart and is chiefly British; a trucker is the modern successor, driving motor vehicles, and the word today often means a member of the Teamsters Union who is not an animal-driver at all. "Teamster" is distinctive because it names the harnessed team — the animal unit, yoked and pulling together — and the man who manages it; it is the only one of the set that institutionalized itself, in the International Brotherhood of Teamsters (founded 1903), the great American trucking union.

Collocations and register. The noun collocates with its world: "teamster's wagon," "teamster's union," "freight teamster," "pack team," "team of horses," "draft team," "six-mule team." Register is American, occupational, and concrete; the word belongs to freight yards, wagon roads, and labor history. Its suffix, -ster, is an agent-ending that survives from Old English -estre (originally feminine — spinster, songster) and was generalized in Middle and Early Modern English to masculine agents (gamester, punster, trickster), so that "teamster" literally means "one who is the team's man." In the passage the plural "teamsters" describes the class — the men of the mule and the wagon whose fashion the moustached stranger follows, placing him, even without a wagon in sight, in the fraternity of the road.
