- [13th century the language of romance] - [Enters from Old French as the adjective of peril — exposed to danger; connotation of high romance, the 'perilous seas' and 'perilous pass' of chivalric story]
- [16th century physical risk] - [Settles as the grave, formal adjective for exposure to danger — the perilous journey, siege, or cliff path; connotation earnest and old-fashioned]
- [19th century moral and metaphorical] - [Ruskin calls false criticism 'perilous' in its tendency as a guide of public feeling — a moral hazard borrowing the gravity of physical danger, as if bad taste could shipwreck a nation's judgment]
- [20th-21st century literary retreat] - [Yields everyday currency to the plain 'dangerous'; perilous survives in elevated, prophetic, or slightly archaic registers, keeping its sense of an imminent, possibly fatal threat]
