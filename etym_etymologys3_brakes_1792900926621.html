The thicket-brake enters English in the late 1300s already fully formed and already mysterious: a clump of bushes, a patch of briars, a tangle on the waste. No ancestor steps forward to claim it. It may be kin to Middle English braken, fern or bracken — itself a northern word with Scandinavian relatives like Swedish bräken and Danish bregne — on the theory that fern-choked ground lent its name to every other kind of tangle. Or the bush-word may be older than the fern-word, and the resemblance an accident of scrubland. The OED, weighing both, declines to decide, and honest etymology reports the draw rather than inventing a winner.

What is certain is the word’s long double life. From the start there were (at least) two brakes in English: the green one of fern and thicket, and the grey iron one of the flax-crushing mill — Middle Dutch braeke, a frame for breaking stalks — which gave, by way of the carriage-maker’s curb and the railwayman’s lever, the modern stopping-device. For centuries context kept them apart without effort: no reader of Shakespeare’s ‘bank whereon the wild thyme blows’ ever reached for a handbrake. Only the motor age, filling every street with the screech of the mechanical sense, pushed the thicket into the shade, until today the green brake survives mainly in novels, nature writing, and place-names.

Stages of the tangle:

  • Late 14th c. — first thickets: brake is attested for clumps of bushes and brushwood on marginal land; alongside it stands braken, the northern word for fern, and the two begin their long association.
  • 15th–16th c. — fern and thicket wed: writers use brake for bracken-beds and bracken-beds for brakes; the plant and the place become one idea — low, dense, rustling cover.
  • 1590s — Shakespeare’s bower: A Midsummer Night’s Dream plants English poetry’s most famous brake — the bank of thyme, oxlips, and nodding violets where Titania sleeps — fixing the word’s pastoral, enchanted colour for four centuries.
  • 17th–18th c. — emigration: settlers carry brake to America, where it takes root as the cane-brake, vast river-cane jungles of the southern bottomlands — the same old word grown gigantic in new soil.
  • 19th c. — the sporting thicket: game-books standardise brakes and coverts as the pheasant’s address; beating the brakes becomes an autumn ritual, and the plural in Tolkien’s sentence carries exactly this countryman’s ring.
  • 20th c. — Tolkien and the motor car: the Shire’s lanes run between deep brakes of hazel while, outside the book, brakes increasingly means what stops the car — the thicket surviving as a deliberate archaism, all the greener for its rarity.

Grammatically the plural -s is the ordinary Old-English-derived plural marker, and the noun compounds freely — brake-fern, cane-brake, fern-brake — each compound testifying to the parent plant. To meet brakes in Tolkien’s lane is therefore to meet a small conservative miracle: a word whose origin scholars honestly confess unknown, whose spelling it shares with an unrelated machine, and whose meaning — dense, deep, green, hiding — has nonetheless survived six hundred years without fading, like bracken itself, copper-gold every autumn on the slopes it names.
