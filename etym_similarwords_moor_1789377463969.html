• Heath: Similar because both denote open, uncultivated land dominated by low ericaceous shrubs like heather; differs because a heath typically occupies well-drained, dry, sandy or gravelly soils in warmer lowland areas, whereas a moor is characteristically cooler, upland, wet, and underlain by deep, acidic peat bog.
  • Bog: Similar because both feature waterlogged, acidic peat soil and sphagnum moss; differs because a bog refers specifically to the water-saturated peat wetland itself (a mire), whereas a moor encompasses the entire rolling upland landscape, including drier heather slopes and rocky crags as well as peat pockets.
  • Fen: Similar because both are wetland ecosystems; differs because a fen is fed by mineral-rich groundwater rather than just rainwater, resulting in alkaline or neutral water that supports alkaline grasses, reeds, and rushes, whereas a moor is rain-fed, extremely acidic, and dominated by heather.
  • Marsh: Similar because both are wet, treeless natural landscapes; differs because a marsh is a mineral-soil wetland characterized by reeds, cattails, and open water, lacking the deep peaty deposits and woody heather characteristic of a moor.
  • Mire: Similar because both evoke treacherous, waterlogged, soft earth that can trap travelers; differs because mire is a general, visceral term for deep mud, slime, or quagmire, whereas moor is a formal geographic and ecological classification for a broad landscape.
  • Fell: Similar because both refer to wild, uncultivated upland areas in Northern England and Scandinavia; differs because a fell (from Old Norse fjall, "mountain") specifically denotes a high mountain plateau, bare rocky fellside, or ridge, whereas a moor denotes the peat-and-heather blanket ecosystem.
