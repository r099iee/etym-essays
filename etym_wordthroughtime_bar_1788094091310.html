- 12th–13th centuries: "barre" enters Middle English from French — a rod, rail or gate-bar; the practical word that fastens doors, gates and city bars, plain and sturdy in register.
- 13th–14th centuries: in Anglo-Norman courts the barre is the railing fencing off judges and advocates; "the bar" begins its legal career; heraldry borrows the stripe.
- 16th–17th centuries: "called to the bar" inaugurates the barrister; taverns serve over a bar-counter; sailors and geographers meet the sandbar; the word is now a barrier of every kind — "bar none" and "barring" fix the excluding force.
- 17th–19th centuries: musicians borrow the bar-line; "the bar" begins to mean the whole drinking-room; bars of gold, soap and (later) chocolate are the rod-shape measured out.
- 20th–21st centuries: meteorology adds the pressure bar (1911, from Greek baros); barcodes (1970s), space bars, progress bars and address bars make the divider universal; BAR today is at once a legal institution, a place to drink, a strip of music, a ridge of sand and a unit of force.
