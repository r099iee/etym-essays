1. From ‘speaking’ to ‘speech-story’. The word fabliau carries in its bones the oldest Indo-European verb of speaking. PIE *bʰeh₂- ‘to speak’ produced Latin fārī ‘to speak, say’, and from fārī came fābula, ‘a story, tale, play’ — the word behind English fable. In Late Latin an affectionate diminutive, *fābellum (fābula + -ellum), softened fābula into ‘a little tale’; in the Gallo-Romance of the north that diminutive emerged as Old French fablel in the royal Parisian dialect and fabliau in Picard. The first great fabliau poet, Jean Bodel of Arras, used the word in his Chanson des Saisnes (c. 1199), and with that the ‘little fable’ became, for the first time, a genre-name.

2. The urban century. The fabliau flourished where the money was: the towns of Picardy, Artois and Flanders — Arras, Douai, Lille — and then along the trade roads into Champagne and Normandy and down to Paris. This was the world of fairs, guilds, taverns and legal fights, and the fabliaux are its paperwork: transactions, contracts, lawsuits and bets turned into plot. Their typical characters — the upstart peasant, the shrewd wife, the merchant deceived, the clerk on the make, the priest at the wrong door — mirror the social mobility and anti-clerical satire of the thirteenth-century town. The tone is one of complete, delighted cynicism: nobody is innocent, and the joke is always on someone.

3. Form and craft. The genre is metrically tight: octosyllabic rhyming couplets, a rapid pace, direct address to the audience (‘Seignor oïes…’, ‘lords, listen’), and usually a pointed ending with an application to the listeners. Length is modest — a hundred to a few hundred lines — so the fabliau is a performance piece, recited in the hall or the tavern like a stand-up turn. Named authors (Jean Bodel, Rutebeuf, Henri d’Andeli, Gautier le Leu, Jean de Condé) are professionals of this verbal economy, and their surviving signatures prove the fabliau was a marketable art, not folklore.

  • Stock plots: the duped husband (La Borgoise d’Orliens), the trickster priest, the student’s revenge, the false miracle, the talking animal, the bargain with the devil — comedy of deception with the deceiver as hero.
  • Anti-courtly polemic: several fabliaux parody the conventions of courtly love, showing what ‘love’ looks like from the kitchen and the marriage bed; this polemic is exactly the realistic counterweight Pater invokes in his essay when he links the French fabliaux to the Renaissance.
  • Obscenity and censorship: many fabliaux are graphic; scribes censored or completed them differently, and modern editions must reconstruct the doublings — the obscenity is structural (it is the joke), not ornamental.

4. Afterlife: Boccaccio, Chaucer, the novel. The 14th century saw the genre’s decline as verse comedy yielded to prose and to the theatre. But its matter migrated: Boccaccio, as Pater notes, ‘borrowed the outlines of his stories from the old French fabliaux’ for the Decameron; Chaucer re-set the fabliau in Middle English (Miller’s, Reeve’s, Shipman’s Tales); La Fontaine retold their plots in his contes. Modern scholarship — from the 18th-century antiquaries through Bédier’s still-fundamental Les Fabliaux (1893) to the new editions of the last fifty years — made ‘fabliau’ the fixed generic term and rescued the corpus’s laughter, its irreverence and its documentary value for the history of the medieval town.

5. The word today. Fabliau/fabliaux is one of the few genre-words that English borrowed straight from Old French in its scholarly form, keeping the plural -x as a badge of origin. It designates both a historical corpus (the 150-odd Old French poems) and a portable critical category (bawdy short comic verse anytime, anywhere). Its journey — from an Indo-European verb ‘to speak’, through Latin ‘story’, to a Picard tavern-name for a dirty joke, to a philological term — is itself a fabliau of language: the word who told tales became the tale it told.
