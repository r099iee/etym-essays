- Classical Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — Attested as sollicitūdō, denoting acute mental agitation, agonizing anxiety, nervous trouble, or civic apprehension (e.g. Cicero describing the tormenting anxieties of political life during civil war).
- Late Latin & Medieval Scholasticism (4th–13th c.) — Reinterpreted in the Christian Vulgate and monastic rules as pastoral care and vigilant spiritual oversight over the souls of the faithful (sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum, "care of all the churches").
- Middle English (c. late 14th c.) — Borrowed via Old French into Middle English; featured in Chaucer's translation of Boethius (Boece) and John Wycliffe's writings, denoting anxious trouble, spiritual diligence, or worldly cares distracting the soul.
- 17th–18th c. (Enlightenment & Age of Sensibility) — Softened into a prized interpersonal virtue of polite society and domestic affection, signifying tender, compassionate concern for family, friends, or the suffering.
- 19th c. (Victorian era) — Pervasive in 19th-century domestic fiction (Jane Austen, the Brontës, George Eliot) to characterize romantic devotion, parental duty, or smothering overprotection; simultaneously adopted into natural history for parental investment.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains an elevated, formal literary register denoting refined, tender protectiveness or anxious attention, prominently utilized in evolutionary ethology, medical ethics, and high-register literature.
