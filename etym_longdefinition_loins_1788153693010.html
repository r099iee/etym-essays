loins (plural noun) — in its primary anatomical sense, the parts of the body between the lowest ribs and the hips, especially the lower back and sides; in older and biblical usage, more broadly the lower abdomen and groin, regarded as the seat of physical strength and of procreative power. In Walter Pater's sentence "the austere and serious girding of the loins in youth" the word is part of the great Scriptural idiom: the youth braces himself as a runner or soldier hitches up his robe — the body gathered, disciplined, ready.

Primary sense — anatomy. Strictly, the loins (singular loin) are the regions on each side of the vertebral column between the lower ribs and the hip bones — the lumbar region. Medical and anatomical English still uses the term (the lumbar vertebrae guard the loins; a "loin strain"; "loins" in veterinary anatomy). In ordinary older idiom loins covers the whole lower trunk — waist, hips, and groin — and it is in that broad sense that the Bible uses it ("gird up thy loins").

Second sense — the seat of strength and vigour. Because the loins carry the body's weight and contain its muscular power, the word became, especially in biblical and Shakespearean diction, the poetic seat of strength, courage, and virility: "loins girt about with strength" (cf. the KJV rendering of Job 40:16, "his strength is in his loins" — of the behemoth), "the weak in spirit... strong in the loins" (Milton's Samson Agonistes: "a spirit and pulse ill-governed... in the loins strong"? — the safe gloss is that Milton regularly uses loins for bodily vigour).

Third sense — procreative descent. By metonymy the loins stand for the generative organs and hence for descent and offspring: "the fruit of my loins" (children), "come forth of his loins" (the KJV of Hebrews 7:5, of Abraham's seed). In genealogical and legal-biblical prose "issue of the loins" is a formal expression for legitimate descendants. This sense grew directly from the anatomical one: where the child is begotten, there the family line resides.

Fourth sense — the culinary cut. In modern English the singular loin denotes the choicest cut of meat from this region of an animal — pork loin, lamb loin chops, sirloin (from Old French surloigne, "above the loin"); the related tenderloin names the prized strip beneath the loin. The word travelled from the anatomy of men and beasts to the butcher's block and the menu, a narrowing familiar in meat vocabulary (cf. ham, flank, rib).

Fifth sense — euphemism. In restrained or archaic prose "loins" also served as a polite stand-in for the genitals and for coition ("the issues of the loins"), a usage that flourished in Victorian editing of classical texts and in religious language, and survives in prudish or jocular residual forms.

Register. Anatomical and culinary loin are plain modern English; the plural loins has shifted decisively to the biblical, literary, or euphemistic register, anchored by the fixed phrase "gird up one's loins" and by scriptural quotation. A speaker today who says "loins" rather than "lower back" is deliberately invoking that heritage.

Polysemy summary.

  • lower trunk: the lumbar region and hips — anatomical, literal;
  • the seat of strength and vigour — biblical and poetic;
  • the seat of procreation, hence descendants ("fruit of the loins");
  • the meat cut (loin) — culinary narrowing of the same body region,
  • euphemism for the genitals — Victorian and religious reticence.

The semantic bridge. A single metonymy — the part of the body where strength and begetting reside — explains every transfer: clothes are girded there, swords are belted there, the child is formed there, the choicest meat lies there. The word has simply wandered its own anatomy outward into nutrition, genealogy, and morality.
