Wilful (British spelling; American willful) describes a person who deliberately and stubbornly follows their own will rather than external advice, rules, or convention. At its root the word is literal: wilful = will + -ful, "full of will". It fuses two ideas — intentionality and obstinacy — in one adjective, and the balance between them shifts with context. In the passage from The Story of the Stone, Dai-yu recalls her mother's words about Bao-yu: "though he was very wilful, he always behaved very nicely to girls." The word here is doing delicate work: on the surface it carries the mild disapproval attached to a headstrong boy, but the narrative immediately redirects that judgment — the same wilfulness, which in a son of the house might be feared as dangerous, is shown to be directed toward "behaving very nicely to girls", so that his stubborn independence is revealed as a kind of chivalrous eccentricity rather than malice. Primary senses
  • Self-willed, headstrong, obstinate — of a person, determined to have one's own way against advice, authority, or reason: "a wilful child".
  • Intentional, deliberate — of an act or attitude, done on purpose rather than by accident or negligence: "wilful disregard", "wilful blindness". This is the dominant sense in legal English.
  • Of things and abstractions — wilful can attach to attitudes rather than persons: wilful ignorance, wilful neglect, wilful misinterpretation, where it means "knowing, deliberate, and culpable".
Polysemy and the historical bridge The two senses — obstinate and intentional — are two readings of the same compound: being full of one's own will. In ordinary use the emphasis falls on stubbornness (a wilful child insisting "I will!"); in legal use the emphasis falls on deliberate intent (wilful misconduct = harm done knowingly, in defiance of duty). The historical bridge is the shift from describing a strong self-directed character to condemning a disobedient one. Early English wilful could be neutral or even admiring (abundant in will, determined); from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries it darkened into firm disapproval; and in modern legal language it crystallized into a precise term for knowing, intentional wrongdoing, while the British/American spelling split (wilful vs willful) settled in the nineteenth century. Register and use In register, wilful ranges from the everyday family complaint (a wilful toddler) to formal and legal usage (wilful misconduct, wilful murder, wilful default, wilful neglect). Its characteristic collocations are a wilful child, wilful disobedience, wilful blindness, wilful disregard. The word is double-edged: it condemns the defiance even as it concedes the strength of will behind it. It must be weighed against its near-synonyms: headstrong stresses rash, impulsive self-will and heedless action, whereas wilful adds the note of deliberation — the wilful person knows what they are doing and does it anyway; obstinate and stubborn describe an immovable refusal to change one's mind even against reason, while wilful emphasises the positive act of choosing to follow one's own desire; wayward implies perverse, unruly turning aside from the proper path; capricious suggests whim and changeability, while wilful is steady and self-directed. In the novel the word quietly anchors the portrait of Bao-yu, whose self-willed nature — at once disruptive and tender — is the engine of his character, and Lady Wang's rueful smile shows precisely how the household has learned to manage it.
