Signification is a formal noun referring to the established meaning, semantic sense, or communicative import conveyed by a word, phrase, symbol, gesture, or natural phenomenon. At its core, the word denotes the relationship between an outward, perceptible sign and the mental concept or objective reality that the sign represents. Unlike casual synonyms such as meaning or sense, signification carries an analytical, scholarly weight, emphasizing the deliberate act of conveying meaning or the systematic property of possessing a defined referent within a semiotic or linguistic structure.

In the source passage from John Ruskin’s Modern Painters, the term performs a crucial philosophical and aesthetic function. Ruskin contrasts mere physical description with spiritual interpretation: he notes that in his early introductory volumes he was compelled to describe natural phenomena (clouds, mountains, moving water, foliage) primarily through their physical and technical characteristics, but that his subsequent essays would endeavor to reveal both the ‘signification and the value’ of those external phenomena. Here, signification is paired with value to denote the intrinsic divine message, moral lesson, and symbolic truth legible within the natural world. For Ruskin, nature is not a collection of mute material objects but a legible scripture authored by the Creator; thus, the signification of a thundercloud or an alpine precipice is its capacity to signify moral majesty, power, and divine purpose to the receptive human soul.

Core grammatical and morphological profile: Morphologically, the word descends from Latin significātiō, formed from the compound verb significāre (from signum, ‘mark, sign’ + facere, ‘to make’) combined with the Latin abstract action suffix -tiō (English -tion). Syntactically, it functions as a countable or uncountable noun: one may speak of ‘the multiple significations of a term’ (countable senses) or ‘the signification of an event’ (the overall import). It regularly takes prepositional complements headed by of (‘the signification of the ritual’) or functions as a direct object of verbs of decipherment such as determine, expound, elucidate, convey, or alter.

All senses, primary, secondary, and technical:

  • The established semantic meaning or definition of a linguistic unit (primary semantic sense): The specific concept or sense attached to a word, morpheme, or idiom within a language system. Semantic bridge: Direct descent from scholastic grammar, where words are vocal tokens whose primary utility is their assigned signification.
  • Semiotic representation and meaning-generation (theoretical linguistics and semiotics): In Saussurean linguistics and structuralist semiotics, the cognitive relationship established between the signifier (signifiant, the sound-image or graphic form) and the signified (signifié, the mental concept). Semantic bridge: Modern theoretical formalization of the ancient principle that a sign functions by producing an intelligible mental effect.
  • Symptomatic indication or diagnostic omen (indicative / natural sense): An outward manifestation, omen, or symptom that points reliably to an underlying physical condition, psychological state, or imminent event (e.g., ‘the sudden drop in barometric pressure was a clear signification of the approaching hurricane’). Semantic bridge: Rooted in the classical medical and augural understanding of natural signs (Latin signa) indicating concealed causes.
  • Moral, spiritual, or allegorical meaning (theological and aesthetic sense): The deeper spiritual lesson, divine design, or metaphysical truth embodied in an earthly object, sacrament, or historical occurrence (e.g., the typological signification of Old Testament events in Christian theology, or Ruskin’s moral reading of landscape). Semantic bridge: Extension of verbal meaning to the universe understood as the ‘Book of Nature’.
  • Formal declaration or notification (legal and diplomatic jargon): In civil law traditions (particularly French and Scots jurisprudence), the official service or formal legal notification of an act, judgment, or summons to an opposing party. Semantic bridge: Preservation of classical Latin legal usage, where significātiō meant an official public notice or formal declaration.
  • Expressive conveyance of emotion or intention (rhetorical / performative sense): The deliberate demonstration of a sentiment, approval, or dissent through applause, gestures, or facial expressions (e.g., ‘a warm signification of gratitude from the assembled crowd’). Semantic bridge: Direct continuation of Roman oratorical usage, where significātiō referred to an audience’s audible or visual response to a speaker.

Register, connotations, and distinctions: Signification belongs strictly to educated, literary, philosophical, and academic registers. It should not be confused with significance, though both share the same Latin root. In modern standard English, significance predominantly denotes ‘importance, weight, or consequence’ (‘a discovery of great significance’), whereas signification denotes ‘meaning, definition, or sense’ (‘the exact signification of the Latin noun’). To confuse the two is a frequent stylistic error; careful writers reserve signification when analyzing what something signifies or means, and significance when evaluating how much it matters.
