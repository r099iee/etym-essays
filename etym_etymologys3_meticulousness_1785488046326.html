METICULOUSNESS begins, astonishingly, not in care but in fear. Its Latin ancestor metus meant "fear, dread, anxiety", a word of unknown — possibly pre-Indo-European — origin, with no securely established cognates anywhere in the Indo-European family. Some scholars have tentatively connected it to a root *mei-, "to avoid, to shrink back", but the link is unproven and generally rejected; the word stands almost alone, an isolate in the Latin vocabulary of fear. Latin, in fact, kept several kinds of fear in careful distinction — timor, "timidity", pavor, "terror", formido, "dread", and metus, the deep, anxious dread that keeps a man watchful. It was precisely this watchfulness that made the word fertile: the fearful man cannot relax his attention; he watches everything, lest danger arrive. That was to become, nineteen centuries later, the perfect proofreader.

Formation. Latin built from metus the verb metuō, "to fear, to dread, to shrink in fear", and from the noun and verb the adjective meticulōsus, "full of fear, timid, faint-hearted" — the base metu-, an interstitial -cul-, and the possessive suffix -ōsus, "full of", the same suffix that gives English -ous in words like anxious, curious, and gorgeous. The rare adverb meticulōsē, "timidly", shows the adjective was fully productive. The man the adjective described trembled — he was literally "full of fear".

Entry into English and three centuries of "fearful". English borrowed the word in the 1530s as meticulous, in the era of Shakespeare and the King James Bible, and for nearly three centuries it meant "fearful, faint-hearted, timid" — a purely negative word. French méticuleux preserved the same timid sense, and even today the older meaning lingers in etymological handbooks. During these centuries the word lived a quiet, unremarkable life in the language of moral and devotional writing, describing the timid soul, the man of little courage.

The great inversion. The change came in the nineteenth century, and it came out of the old psychology of the anxious person. Eighteenth-century novelists and essayists had begun to picture the anxious, fearful temperament as one that cannot relax its attention — the timid man watches everything, checks everything, fusses over details as if a single error were a catastrophe. From "excessive care arising from fear of error" the sense shaded into "excessive care", and from "excessive care" into simply "very great care". By the 1850s the adjective had settled into its modern meaning, "scrupulously, painfully careful"; in the 1860s the abstract noun meticulousness entered the language for "extreme, scrupulous, detail-loving carefulness". The word thus embodies a full circle: the trembling vigilance of fear became the trembling vigilance of the perfect proofreader. The fear did not disappear; it was re-read as the anxiety that drives exactness — the same watchfulness, redirected from danger to detail.

Modern career and literary register. In the twentieth century meticulousness became a professional virtue of the exact trades — the editor, the proofreader, the surgeon, the watchmaker, the archivist — and entered the vocabulary of scholarship and administration. In the dedication to Randolph Quirk it stands between despatch and humour as the editor's cardinal virtue: speed balanced by patience, business balanced by exactness, exactness redeemed by humanity. The word still carries its history in its tone: meticulousness is not merely carefulness but a slightly obsessive, almost tender exactness, as though the meticulous editor were still, deep down, a timid soul watching everything lest one slip be fatal. It is the rare word whose whole biography — from dread to diligence — is still legible in a single adjective and its noun.
