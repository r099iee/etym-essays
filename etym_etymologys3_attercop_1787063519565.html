The word attercop is a quintessential Old English compound noun that encapsulates early Germanic conceptions of the natural world, combining two ancient Proto-Indo-European roots into a descriptive compound that directly reflects how Anglo-Saxon speakers perceived the biology and danger of arachnids.

1. Etymological Evolution of Constituent 1 (Atter - Poison):

The initial element atter- traces to the Proto-Indo-European root *h₁edʰ- (or *ait-), conveying ideas of swelling, burning heat, and bitter toxicity. In Proto-Germanic, this formed the strong neuter noun *aitrą ("poison, venom, festering pus, bodily corruption"). This root was ubiquitous across early Germanic languages: in Old High German it became eitar (whence Modern German Eiter, "pus"), in Old Norse it surfaced as eitr ("poison, venom, bitter cold"), and in Old English it yielded ātor (or ǣttor), meaning "snake venom, poison, or moral corruption." The word gave rise to the Old English adjective ātriġ / attriġ ("poisonous, venomous," source of the archaic English adjective attry).

2. Etymological Evolution of Constituent 2 (Cop - Head/Spider):

The second constituent -cop derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *gewb- / *kewp- ("round object, head, vessel, arch"). In Proto-Germanic, this produced *kuppaz ("round vessel, crown of the head, tuft, bowl"), which yielded Old English copp ("top, summit, head," as preserved in coping stone) and the feminine variant coppe ("spider," literally "small round-bodied creature" or "head-like creature").

3. Synthesis in Old English & Middle English Transformation:

In Late Old English, these two roots were forged into the compound ātorcoppe (literally "poison-head" or "venomous spider"). Anglo-Saxon medical texts, such as the Bald's Leechbook (10th century), frequently listed ātorcoppe bites alongside serpent stings, prescribing herbal remedies against their toxic venom. As Old English transitioned into Middle English, unstressed vowels weakened, producing attercop or attercoppe by the 14th century.

During the Middle English period, however, a semantic and dialectal split occurred. In southern England, speakers began preferring the newly coined agent noun spider (derived from Old English spīþra, from the verb spinnan, "to spin"). While standard London English gradually abandoned attercop in favor of spider, northern English dialects and Scots retained attercop with ferocious loyalty. Furthermore, while the independent word coppe vanished from standard English, it survived permanently within the compound cobweb (Middle English coppeweb).

4. Cultural History & Modern Literary Immortalization:

In the 16th and 17th centuries, as attercop receded into regional dialect, it took on a sharp pejorative meaning, denoting a bitter, venomous-tongued person or a malicious scold. It was this rich dialectal pedigree that attracted the Oxford philologist J.R.R. Tolkien. In The Hobbit (1937), Bilbo Baggins uses attercop as an ancient, stinging insult against the giant spiders of Mirkwood, permanently embedding this venerable Anglo-Saxon compound into modern world literature.
