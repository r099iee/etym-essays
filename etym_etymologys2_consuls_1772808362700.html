CONSULS is the plural of English "consul", a word that descends from Latin and whose Latin root is itself debated at its deepest level. The attested chain is as follows. - PIE root (debated) - the base from which Latin "consulere" derives is not securely established. The most common proposal connects it with PIE *sel- "to take, seize, grasp" (cf. Greek "helein" "to take"), with "con-" "together", giving the sense "to take (counsel) together"; an alternative connects it with the root of "salus" "health, well-being" ("to take care for"). Neither proposal is certain, and the exact PIE etymon of "consulere" is unknown. - to Latin "consulo, consulere" "to deliberate, take counsel, consult; to look after, take care of" (verb, attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus; formed with the prefix "con-" "together" and a base of the same disputed root). - to Latin "consul" "the consul, the annual chief magistrate of the Roman Republic; one who deliberates/counsels" (noun, attested from the 3rd c. BCE in Plautus and Ennius; the office itself was traditionally founded in 509 BCE at the establishment of the Republic). - to Old French "consul" / "conseil" "consul, chief magistrate of a commune" (attested from the 11th-12th c., especially of the magistrates of Italian and Provencal communes) - the medieval revival of the republican title. - to Middle English "consul" "the chief magistrate of ancient Rome; a civic magistrate" (attested from the 14th c., e.g. in Gower and Trevisa). - to English "consul" "the Roman chief magistrate; (later) an official appointed to represent a state's commercial interests abroad" (attested from the 14th-16th c.) to English "consuls" (plural form, regular -s plural) to CONSULS (modern English form). DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The derivation of "consul" from "consulere" "to deliberate" is traditional and is reflected in the ancient sources themselves (Cicero and Livy explain the name from "consulting" the people and senate), but the ultimate PIE root of "consulere" is not known with certainty. The connection with *sel- "to take" is plausible but contested, and some scholars have suggested a link with "consilescere" or other forms; the safest statement is that "consul" is securely derived within Latin from "consulere", while the pre-Latin history of that verb remains debated. The modern diplomatic sense develops from the medieval civic magistracy, not directly from the Roman office.
