mule — (1) The hybrid offspring of a male donkey (a jack) and a female horse (a mare), bred for endurance, sure-footedness, and hybrid vigor, and used for transport and hauling; the classic work-animal of agriculture, armies, and the American frontier. (2) By extension, any hybrid thing, a stubborn person, or various objects named after the animal — the spinning mule, the mule as a backless slipper, the mule of smuggling slang.

The literary moment. In the sentence "A lone dark husbandman pursuing mule and harrow down the rain-blown bottomland toward night," the mule is the living engine of a solitary agriculture. The husbandman does not ride or lead the mule in the usual sense; he pursues it, and the two — animal and tool — are driven together through mud toward the dark. The word mule rather than horse is the whole poem in miniature: the horse was the animal of status and speed, the mule the animal of endurance, hybrid vigor, and hardscrabble survival, the work-animal of the poor farmer across the American South and frontier. The tone is bleak, hushed, and nearly anonymous — the figure is "lone" and "dark," and the mule, like the man, is a creature of the bottomland, soaked by rain, headed for night.

Primary sense — the hybrid animal. Strictly, the mule is the offspring of a jack donkey and a mare; the reverse cross (stallion horse × jenny donkey) is the hinny. Because the hybrid is almost always sterile, it must be bred deliberately, which is why the animal — and the word — spread as a cultural loan across the Mediterranean rather than growing a native Indo-European name. The mule combines the donkey's sure-footedness and endurance with the horse's size and strength, and for centuries it was the standard draft and pack animal of armies, mines, plantations, and frontier roads.

Secondary senses — the word's extensions. The animal's stubbornness became proverbial: "stubborn as a mule," "mule-headed," and the adjective mulish. In industry, the spinning mule was a cotton-spinning machine that drew and twisted thread in a hybrid imitation of hand-spinning; the mule also named a backless slipper and, in smuggling slang, a person who carries contraband across a border. All of these reach back to the animal as the figure of patient, hybrid, burdened labor.

Register. Mule is a plain, workaday noun — deliberately unglamorous, the vocabulary of the barnyard, the pack-train, and the ledger. That homeliness is precisely its power in the card, where it anchors the poem in the economics of the poor: mule and harrow are not images of romance but of survival.



harrow — (1) A farm implement: a heavy frame fitted with iron teeth, spikes, or disks, drawn over ploughed land to break up clods, level the soil, and cover seed. (2) As a verb, to break and level soil with such a tool; and, far more common in ordinary English, figuratively "to wound, lacerate, or torment," as in the Harrowing of Hell and "a harrowing experience."

The literary moment. The husbandman is "pursuing mule and harrow," and the harrow is the second half of the pair — the tool that tears the earth as the mule carries the burden. The word holds two lives at once in the line: literally, the frame of iron tines dragged across the rain-blown field; figuratively, the harrowing solitude of the scene, which tears at the reader exactly as the tines tear at the soil. One word, two ancestries, one exhausted field.

Primary sense — the tool. The harrow follows the plough in the old cycle of tillage: the plough turns the soil over in furrows, the harrow breaks the clods and levels the seedbed so seed can be sown. The tool is ancient and simple — a wooden or iron frame with teeth — and its name travelled with the technology itself into English, most probably from a North Germanic source related to Old Norse herfi, rather than through a native English root.

Secondary sense — the figurative verb. The striking thing about harrow is that its most famous modern use is not agricultural at all. "The Harrowing of Hell" is the medieval doctrine and mystery-play subject of Christ's descent to free the souls of the dead; "a harrowing experience" is one that wounds, lacerates, or distresses the heart. This figurative verb was born of a Middle English collision: the tool-word merged with the Old English verb hergian, "to harry, ravage, plunder," so that to harrow came to mean "to wound and torment" while the tool kept its own literal life.

  • The literal tool: a toothed or disked frame drawn across plough-land to break clods and level the seedbed; the workaday second stage of tillage.
  • The agricultural verb: "to harrow the field" — to work the soil with the implement; a rural, technical, now rare use.
  • The figurative verb: "to harrow the heart/soul" — to wound, distress, or horrify; the dominant modern sense, fixed by the Harrowing of Hell.
  • The homonymic heritage: the figurative sense flows from Old English hergian "to harry, ravage," which collided with the tool-word in Middle English; harrow and harry are thus historically entangled in spelling and meaning.
  • Derived forms: harrowing (adj.) "extremely distressing," harrowingly (adv.), and the now rare noun harrower "one who harrows."

Register. As a tool-word, harrow is rural, technical, and nearly obsolete outside farming; as a figurative word, it is strong, literary, and emotionally violent — "harrowing" is one of the standard English adjectives for an ordeal. The card's line draws on both registers at once, which is what makes the husbandman's pursuit so quietly dreadful.
