ERRANT — "wandering or straying from the proper course or standards; roving, travelling in search of adventure (especially of knights); deviating from what is right, correct, or expected; going astray." In the supplied literary context, an "errant flocculus" of humour escapes a heated alchemical crucible: the word describes a small particle that has slipped free of containment and now wanders unpredictably — a rogue element off its intended path.
  • Sense 1 — wandering, roving, travelling (the chivalric sense): "a knight-errant," "errant adventurers" — a person or force that roams in search of adventure; historically the dominant and admiring sense of medieval romance (from Old French "errant," the present participle of "errer," to travel).
  • Sense 2 — straying from the proper course (spatial): "an errant arrow," "an errant ball," "errant clouds" — an object that has deviated from its intended path, a sense now common in sports and mechanics.
  • Sense 3 — straying from standards, going wrong (moral / intellectual): "errant behaviour," "an errant son," "errant logic" — conduct or thought that has strayed from what is right, correct, or expected; the judgmental extension of the spatial sense.
  • Sense 4 — escaping containment, unruly, rogue (scientific / technical): an "errant" particle, an "errant" current, an "errant" gene — something that has broken free of a system and behaves unpredictably; the sense required by the alchemical context, and common in modern physics, biology, and engineering prose.
  • Sense 5 — (as a noun, chiefly archaic) a wanderer or knight-errant: "the errant and the exiled" — substantival use is rare and literary.
Polysemy and historical bridges. Errant has a double genealogy that explains its two faces. From Latin "errare" (to wander, to stray, to make a mistake) came the present participle "errans, errant-," "wandering"; via Old French this produced the chivalric "errant" of the "knight-errant," the roving adventurer — and also the moral "errant," the one who strays from truth or right (the same Latin root that gave "error," "erroneous," "erratic," "aberration"). A second, homonymous Old French verb, "errer" (from Latin "iterare," to journey, from "iter," a journey, a road), also gave "errant" with the sense "travelling, journeying"; the two words — Latin "errare" (wander/stray) and "iter" (journey) — collided and blended in medieval "errant," a fact scholars debate but that securely underlies the dual meaning "wandering deviantly" and "travelling adventurously." The modern sense range runs from the admiring ("knight-errant") through the neutral ("errant arrow") to the judgmental ("errant behaviour") and the technical ("errant particle"). Register and related words. Errant is formal and now slightly literary; in speech it survives chiefly in fixed contexts ("knight-errant," "errant baseball," "errant behaviour"). Related forms: "error" (a mistake), "err" (to make a mistake, to go astray), "erroneous" (wrong), "erratic" (irregular, unpredictable), "aberration" (a deviation from the normal), "errant" itself. Distinguish "errant" from "wandering" (neutral, no rule broken), "stray" (accidental or domestic, like a lost dog), "wayward" (deliberately perverse or unruly), and "deviant" (clinically or socially disapproved). The mnemonic "ERR-ant = making an ERR-or and wandering" captures the moral-spatial doublet perfectly: the word means both to wander and to go wrong, because Latin long ago fused the idea of leaving the road with the idea of leaving the truth.
