- 17th c. (c. 1600) — Verb verge enters English from Latin vergere, primarily meaning to bend, slope, incline downwards, or decline (e.g. the verging sun sinking toward evening).
- Mid-to-late 17th c. — Shifts from literal physical declination to directional orientation and spatial adjacency, influenced by the homophonic noun verge (boundary, perimeter of royal authority).
- 18th c. — Rapidly expands into British moral philosophy, legal rhetoric, and political prose to indicate approaching a precarious moral or institutional threshold (e.g. verging on folly, verging on madness).
- 19th c. — Widely adopted in Victorian social reform, historiography (Macaulay, Carlyle, Durant), and economic commentary to articulate conditions bordering upon poverty, serfdom, or revolution.
- 20th–21st c. — Firmly cemented in high-register political discourse, diplomatic exchanges, and clinical psychology as the definitive term for systems or minds hovering upon the precipice of catastrophe.
