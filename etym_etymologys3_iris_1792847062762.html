The history of iris opens in the bright upper air of Greek epic, where îris is first of all the rainbow, the shining arc the storm leaves behind, and second the goddess who wears it as her road. In Homer she is the golden-winged messenger darting between Olympus and earth, and her name already blends phenomenon and person: the rainbow is Iris travelling. This double sense, thing and being at once, sets the pattern for everything the word will later absorb.

From sky to flower-bed: Greek physicians and plant-lovers, above all Theophrastus and later Dioscorides, bestowed the goddess's name on the flag flower because no other bloom parades so many heavenly colours: violet, sky-blue, gold, white, and rust-brown on neighbouring plants. The naming bridge is pure admiration, and it stuck so firmly that Latin handbooks took īris over wholesale, listing rainbow, plant, and even the rainbow-sheened gemstone under one headword. Pliny describes iris roots ground for perfumes and medicines, so the flower entered Roman gardens as both ornament and drug chest.

Through Rome to England: the Latin form passed through monastic herbals into Middle English, where iris named the flag flowers of physic gardens and wet meadows. Old English had its own native word, glædene, for the yellow flag, the very word fossilised in Tolkien's Gladden Fields, and for centuries the learned iris and the homely gladdon lived side by side, one for scholars and one for countrymen. By the early modern period the classical form had won the garden while the dialect word lingered in hedgerows.

  • Anatomy borrows the rainbow (16th–18th c.): Renaissance anatomists, dissecting the eye and marvelling at its painted ring, named it the iris, since blue, hazel, and brown eyes repeat the flower's palette in miniature. Medical Latin kept the Greek plural irides, still heard in clinics today.
  • Optics copies the eye (19th c.): instrument-makers building adjustable light-stops of overlapping leaves for microscopes, then cameras, called them iris diaphragms, completing a charming circle: sky to flower to eye to machine, each new shutter honouring the last.
  • Poetry never let go: from Milton's rainbow robes to Victorian flower-names for daughters, literary English kept the goddess audible inside the botany, which is why the word still sounds half-mythical on a seed packet.

Debated depths: beyond Homeric Greek the trail goes cold, and honest lexicography says so. Etymologists note that the word's shape fits neither tidy Indo-European pattern securely nor any certain foreign loan, and Beekes lists it among probable Pre-Greek survivals, words the Greeks inherited from the Aegean peoples before them. Older guesses connecting it with verbs of speaking, after the messenger's announcements, are now set aside as folk reasoning.

Modern standing: today iris lives three full lives at once without confusion: gardeners divide rhizomes, lovers gaze into one another's irises, and photographers stop the iris down for depth of field. Tolkien's single use summons the oldest botanical layer, beds of wild flags along an ancient river, and reminds readers that behind the flower lies the rainbow, and behind the rainbow a goddess with wings of storm-light.
