cynicism is, in its modern and dominant sense, an attitude of jaded, scornful distrust of human motives, grounded in the belief that people act ultimately only out of self-interest; a sneering readiness to doubt the sincerity of lofty sentiment, idealism, public virtue, or profession of principle. In the present context the word names the worldly reader who would “curl a sceptical lip” over Mill's passionate sentences of homage to his wife in the dedication of On Liberty: the cynic, the passage implies, cannot believe such praise is genuine, and so reads Mill's panegyric as sentimental naïveté. The nuance is the hard, knowing laughter of the marketplace aimed at the language of the heart.

Historically, however, cynicism names something very different: the ancient Greek philosophical school of the Cynics (Greek Kynikoí, “the dog-men”), followers of Antisthenes and Diogenes of Sinope, who practised an ascetic, shamelessly plain way of life in defiance of convention and sought virtue through self-sufficiency and the rejection of luxury, pleasure, and social pretence. The modern “distrust of motives” sense grows out of the Cynics' aggressive unmasking of sham and their contempt for conventional values — the ancient Cynic's bark was a philosophical method before it became a modern mood.

  • Sense 1 (philosophical, historical): “the doctrine or practice of the ancient Cynic school”; asceticism, contempt for luxury, shameless plainness of speech and behaviour, self-sufficiency (autarkeia), freedom (eleutheria), and a cosmopolitan indifference to social rank. This sense is fully alive in histories of philosophy and in the phrase “Cynic philosophy.”
  • Sense 2 (moral-psychological, modern): “a disposition to distrust the sincerity or goodness of human motives; a jaded expectation that everyone acts from self-interest.” This is the ordinary modern sense, dominant since the 19th century, and the sense in the present passage (“ordinary worldly cynicism”).
  • Sense 3 (institutional / political / cultural): “a generalised distrust of institutions, promises, and public rhetoric,” e.g. political cynicism, institutional cynicism, voter cynicism — a refusal to credit official claims as anything but cover for interest.
  • Sense 4 (rhetorical / defensive posture): “a cynical manner or remark,” a pose of knowing disbelief adopted as a shield, as in “He hid his insecurity behind a shield of sharp cynicism.”
  • Historical bridge: the bridge from “dog-philosophy” to “distrust of motives” is the Cynics' famous barking unmasking of pretence: Diogenes “sought a man” with a lamp in daylight, mocked Alexander the Great, and proclaimed that he was “a citizen of the world.” Because the Cynics specialised in exposing conventional virtue as self-interest and hypocrisy, their name came, from the 18th–19th centuries, to denote the general habit of reading every lofty motive as secretly selfish — the school's method detached from its ascetic doctrine and frozen into an attitude.
  • Distinction from near-synonyms: skepticism is doubt about knowledge and evidence, not about motives (the skeptic withholds belief until proof arrives; the cynic assumes the worst from the start); pessimism is a general expectation that things will go badly, and may be wholly benign in character, whereas cynicism is specifically about human motive and carries a flavour of contempt; irony is a rhetorical device of saying one thing while meaning another, available to kind and cruel alike; sarcasm is its mocking verbal form, an instrument the cynic often uses but is not identical with; nihilism is a philosophical denial of value and meaning altogether, far more radical than cynicism, which still cares enough to mock; world-weariness is tired disillusionment without the active contempt and accusation that cynicism carries.
  • Register and connotation: modern cynicism is usually pejorative — it marks a failure of trust and a souring of generosity — though it can be worn as a badge of hard-won realism. In academic prose the philosophical sense (Sense 1) is neutral and historical. The adjective is cynical, and the person is a cynic.

Antonyms and contrasts: idealism, trust, faith, naïveté, credulity, optimism, sincerity, openness. Where the cynic curls a sceptical lip, the idealist credits the motive; where the cynic sees self-interest, the trusting reader sees love and grief. A full account of cynicism must therefore hold together the lamp-carrying philosopher of Athens and the worldly sceptic of the modern marketplace — two figures joined by one bark.
