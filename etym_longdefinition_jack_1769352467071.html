jack (noun, slang) — money, cash, or financial resources; also, more broadly, "a common man" or an ordinary fellow. In the source passage from John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, George dreams aloud: "Someday we're gonna get the jack together and we're gonna have a little house and a couple of acres an' a cow and some pigs" — here "the jack" is the pool of saved money, the working man's stake, the cash accumulated through back-breaking labour toward a future. The phrase "get the jack together" means to collect and amass sufficient funds, and the word's whole flavour is Depression-era American working-class speech: plain, unromantic, and rooted in wages earned by hand. In this context jack is money that is saved, not spent — the opposite of the pocket-spending money of "dough" or "scratch"; it is the fund, the stake, the nest-egg of the poor. Primary senses in this card:
  • Money, cash, or savings — "get the jack together," "he needs to earn some jack," "no jack left." (American slang, late 19th–20th c.)
  • A common man, an ordinary fellow, a labourer — "every man jack," "jack of all trades," "I'm all right, Jack." (The generic-name sense from which the money sense grew.)
Secondary and modern senses — the polysemy in full:
  • Mechanics: a portable device for lifting heavy loads, especially a vehicle — "a car jack," "a hydraulic jack," "to jack up the car." The sense is named for the labouring servant-boy whose work the machine replaced.
  • Card games: the playing card between the ten and the queen, worth eleven in some games — renamed from "knave" in the 16th–17th c.; the "jack" shows a court page or servant.
  • Electrical engineering: a socket, plug, or connector for carrying signals — "a headphone jack," "a jack plug," "an audio jack," "to jack in" (to connect). The word moved from lifting to connecting in twentieth-century hardware.
  • Maritime: the small flag flown from a ship's bow, especially the "Union Jack" of Britain; the flag is the servant-flag flown in the "jack-staff" at the forepeak.
  • Animals: a male donkey (from "jackass," the male ass) and a male rabbit; a male bird in some dialect uses. The "jack" is the male of the species, the common male.
  • Games: "jacks" (the children's game played with small six-pointed counters and a ball) and "jack" (the target ball or jack in bowls and lawn bowling).
  • Verbs and idioms: "to jack up" — to raise or increase sharply ("jack up prices"), or to abandon ("jack it in"); "jack in" — to connect; "to jack" — to lift with a jack. "Jack-o'-lantern," "jackdaw," "lumberjack," "steeplejack," and "jackrabbit" all extend the common-fellow name to things and creatures.
  • Apparatus: "bootjack," "turnspit jack," "screwjack," "jack plane," "apple jack," "jack hammer" — a vast family of labour-saving tools and devices, each named because it does the work of a human "Jack."
Historical semantic bridge. The word begins as the personal name Jack, the affectionate diminutive of John (via Jankin), and by the mid-14th century it was so overwhelmingly common among English peasants that it lost its status as a specific name and became a generic noun for any ordinary man or low-status labourer. From "the common man" the meaning travelled in two directions at once: toward machinery — the lifting jack, the turnspit jack, the bootjack, devices that replaced the labouring boy — and toward money — the American working man's slang for wages, stakes, and savings. To "get the jack together" is to collect the common man's earnings, and the word still carries the earthy, democratic flavour of the man it is named after.
