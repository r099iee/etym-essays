Tantallon is Tantallon Castle, a great red-sandstone fortress on the cliffs of East Lothian in Scotland, looking out over the Firth of Forth to the Bass Rock. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita remembering his Scott-fed childhood — Tantallon was there; but no Archibald of Angus: — Stirling, but no Knight of Snowdoun means the boy visited the real stones and found the romance gone: the curtain wall, the towers, and the sea-wind remained, but the Douglas earl who held them in Scott's Marmion did not ride out, just as Stirling Castle remained without Scott's disguised King James. The capital T marks a proper noun; the word has no common-noun meaning, only the castle and by extension the lordship and the moment in literary history it stands for.

Physically Tantallon is one of the last true medieval curtain-wall castles in Scotland. Built in the mid-14th century by William Douglas, 1st Earl of Douglas, it consists of a single massive landward wall — some 15 metres high, nearly 4 metres thick — blocking off a headland, with a great central tower, two flanking towers, and the cliff and sea behind doing the rest of the defence. Held by the Red Douglases, Earls of Angus, it was besieged by James IV in 1491, by James V in 1528 when he broke the power of Archibald Douglas, 6th Earl of Angus, and finally slighted by Cromwell's artillery under Monck in 1651. The roofless ruin passed to the Dalrymples and in 1924 to the nation; it is now in the care of Historic Environment Scotland, with kittiwakes on its ledges, a dovecot in the outer court, and visitors walking the wall-walk where Marmion's pursuivants once stood.

  • Head sense: Tantallon Castle itself (source sense): e.g. to visit Tantallon, the siege of Tantallon, Tantallon above the Bass. Always definite and singular, often without Castle: Tantallon was there means the building stood. Guidebooks stress the great wall, the sea-girt rock, the gun-ports adapted for artillery, and the view to the Bass and to Fife.
  • Douglas-lordship sense: Tantallon as Angus power: e.g. Tantallon of the Douglases, Angus held Tantallon against the king. From the late 14th to the early 16th century to say Tantallon was to say Douglas defiance of the Stewart crown. James V's enforced minority under Archibald of Angus, his escape, and his revenge-siege of 1528 made the name shorthand for overmighty subject versus king.
  • Scott sense: Tantallon in Marmion: Canto 5 of Marmion (1808) lodges Lord Marmion at Tantallon Hold on his way to Flodden, describing its battled walls, its towers, its sea-mews and the Douglas earl metering hospitality to an English guest. For Ruskin's generation Scott fixed Tantallon as the type of border fortress: gloomy, honourable, fated. The child expected garrisons because Scott had peopled them.
  • Tourist-picturesque sense (19th c.-present): e.g. a sketch of Tantallon, Tantallon at sunset, Tantallon in the mist. Turner sketched it, Wordsworth versified it, Victorian steamers brought day-trippers from Leith. The ruin became a test of the sublime: red stone against grey sea, jackdaws in empty chambers.
  • Heraldic-botanical echo: Tantallon as emblem: East Lothian signposts, the Tantallon dove, hotel and golf-course names, and the Tantallon rose borrow prestige from the ruin. To name a thing Tantallon is to claim antiquity, strength, and Lothian roots.

Linguistically Tantallon is a place-name of debated Celtic origin, first recorded in the 14th century in forms like Tantallon, Tantalloun. No satisfactory Gaelic or Brittonic etymology commands assent; proposals involving dun (fort) plus an obscure second element, or Gaelic teine-talamh (fire-ground, beacon-place), remain conjectural and are honestly marked as such in place-name books. What is certain is the pronunciation in Scotland /tænˈtælən/ with stress on the second syllable and a reduced final vowel, and the spelling with double l. Register is proper-noun historical: outside Lothian the word occurs almost only in Scott discussion, siege histories, and tourist literature.
