denizen — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *h₁en “in” (particle/preposition, reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE) — source of Latin in “in, into.” 2. PIE *h₁én-tero- “inner” (comparative formation, reconstructed) — source of Latin intus “within, on the inside” (attested in Old Latin; the form intus is classical). 3. Latin de intus “from within” (de “from” + intus “within,” vulgar and late Latin collocation) → Vulgar Latin *deintus. 4. Old French denz, deinz “within” (from deintus, attested 10th–12th c.) → Old French denzein, denzain “one who dwells within, resident, inhabitant” (12th c.), formed with the suffix -ein/-ain. 5. Anglo-French and Middle English denisein, denizein, denisen “citizen, inhabitant, foreign-born resident admitted to rights” (attested 14th c.); the ending was reshaped under the influence of citizen. 6. English denizen “inhabitant; naturalised foreigner” (modern form settled 15th–16th c.). Debate note: the shaping influence of citizen on the Old French ending -ein is the standard explanation of the modern -en, but the exact path of the reshaped suffix in Anglo-French is debated; the derivation of Old French denz “within” from Latin de intus is secure. dwelling — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *dʰwel- “to go astray, be dimmed, deluded” (reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE) — the base proposed for the Germanic “wander / err / delay” group; cognate with Gothic dwals “foolish,” Old Norse dvǫl “delay” (debated in exact shape). 2. Proto-Germanic *dwelaną “to err, wander, go astray” (reconstructed) and its causative *dwaljaną “to mislead, hinder, delay” (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–100 CE). 3. Old English dwellan “to mislead, deceive, hinder, cause to wander” (attested c. 725; c. 1000 Ælfric). 4. Middle English dwellen “to remain, stay, abide, reside” (meaning-shift from “to delay, linger” to “to remain, live” attested from c. 1200; the verb dwellen “to reside” is common by the 13th c.). 5. Middle English dwellyng “a place of residence, abode” (noun formed from dwellen + -ing, attested 13th–14th c.). 6. English dwelling “a place of residence” (modern form settled 15th–16th c.). Debate note: the semantic leap from “to mislead / delay” to “to reside” is usually explained through the intermediate sense “to linger, tarry” reinforced by Old Norse dvelja “to delay, tarry, stay” — the Scandinavian cognate almost certainly helped fix the “remain” sense in Middle English, though the precise mechanism is debated.
