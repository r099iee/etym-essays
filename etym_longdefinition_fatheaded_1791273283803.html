fatheaded is an informal, contemptuous adjective meaning stupid in a thick, dull, self-satisfied way, and by extension obstinate in that stupidity: unable or unwilling to grasp what is obvious to others, uttering or doing things that show poor judgement. In P. G. Wodehouse's line, Tuppy's fatheaded words are the tactless, blundering remarks that wound the narrator and keep festering. The comedy lies in the bodily image: the head is imagined as stuffed or swollen with fat instead of brains, so sense cannot get in or out. Register is colloquial and insulting but usually jocular rather than vicious, belonging to school, club, and comic-novel banter rather than medical or formal criticism.

Morphologically it is fat + head + -ed (having a fat head), parallel to thick-headed, blockheaded, boneheaded, muttonheaded, pigheaded. It can describe persons (a fatheaded fellow, don't be fatheaded), remarks and behaviour (fatheaded words, a fatheaded decision), and attitudes (fatheaded complacency, fatheaded optimism). The noun fathead and adverb fatheadedly accompany it. Tone is superior and exasperated: the speaker claims clear sense against another's fog. In American English fathead as noun is common; in British English the adjective thrives in Wodehouse, Waugh, and cricket-pavilion abuse.

Understanding fatheaded requires hearing both stupidity and smugness. A merely slow person might be dim; a fatheaded person adds a layer of swollen confidence, talking big while thinking small. Hence its frequent collocation with words, talk, idea, scheme, grin, complacency: it judges utterance as symptom of constitution.

  • Core intellectual sense: dull-witted, slow to understand. The primary meaning is cognitive deficiency presented as thickness: failing to see jokes, implications, or consequences. The semantic bridge is humoral and folk-anatomical: fat clogs, thickness blocks, so a fat head cannot be quick. This is not IQ measurement but social judgement of cloddishness.
  • Behavioural sense: showing bad judgement in speech or action. Fatheaded words, jokes, purchases, or risks are those a sensible person would have avoided. The bridge is metonymy: the act reveals the head. Wodehouse exploits this constantly, letting well-meaning drones utter precisely the wrong thing at dinner.
  • Attitudinal sense: obstinately conceited and unreceptive. A fatheaded attitude will not listen, learn, or admit error, because the fat insulates it. The bridge from stupidity to obstinacy is natural: dullness that thinks itself clever becomes stubbornness. Here fatheaded overlaps pigheaded but keeps the emphasis on folly rather than will.
  • Affectionate-comic sense: lovably foolish. In comic fiction fatheaded friends (Tuppy, Bingo, Gussie) are censured but loved; their folly drives plot without villainy. The bridge is class and context: among idle gentlemen, fatheadedness is a venial, even endearing vice, softening insult into teasing.
  • Extended impersonal sense: systems or ideas as fatheaded. Modern colloquialism allows fatheaded rules, fatheaded bureaucracy, fatheaded policy, personifying institutions as big stupid heads. The bridge is the same bodily metaphor widened to collective mindlessness.
  • Distinction from clinical terms: Unlike intellectually disabled or learning difficulty, fatheaded is never neutral or diagnostic; it is always a moralised insult implying the victim should know better. That ethical sting explains why it rankles: to be called fatheaded is to be told one's words wound because they were thoughtless.

In short, fatheaded packages anatomy, intellect, and manners into a single comic condemnation: a head too fat to think and too swollen to listen, whose words therefore linger and rankle in others' bosoms long after they are spoken.
